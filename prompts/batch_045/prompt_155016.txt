Please write a short story of 800 to 1,200 words, in the third person, about a person at work whose reliability nobody has ever had occasion to remark on — until something small is moved.

Background for the story. Philosophers use the term "sphexishness" for behavior that looks purposive but is really a fixed sequence released by cues: it runs through to completion; if an interruption makes a step fail, the agent re-runs the step rather than adapting; and its rigidity becomes visible only when the world is rearranged in a way the sequence was never built to handle. The question this raises about free will is best posed as an evidential one. If a routine always succeeds, then blind mechanism and genuine competence look exactly alike from the outside. So what we admire as flexibility may be nothing more than visible failure followed by visible recovery, and what we fear as mechanical may be nothing more than an unusually good fit between an agent and a stable world. On that view, freedom has no observable mark except error — which would mean that the better adapted someone is, the less free they look.

Your story must dramatize the following two claims, without any character formulating them.

First, fit-invisibility. The central character's routine should be so well matched to her setting that, to an observer, her conduct is indistinguishable from a rule being blindly followed: she never hesitates, never explains, never improvises, and never fails. Another character in the story should strike observers as manifestly free — unhurried, responsive, willing to break procedure — but only because he visibly errs and visibly recovers. Make the reader feel the inversion: the evidence that would establish freedom in one case is exactly the evidence that would establish rigidity in the other.

Second, second-order rigidity. When the central character's routine is interrupted, her response to the failure must itself be a fixed routine — re-check, re-do, re-confirm — and the interruption must be of a kind her repair routine was not designed for, so that she enters a loop. Every individual action in the loop must be locally correct and locally careful; to anyone watching, she must look maximally conscientious at every moment. The loop may end, but only because of something that is not a decision of hers — a noise, a bystander's irrelevant action, a change in the room, the arrival of a shift. She must not snap out of it through insight, irritation, or an act of will, and no one may help her by explaining what went wrong.

Constraints.

- Set the story in an ordinary, nameable workplace where routine matters — a hospital medication round, a ship's bridge, an air-traffic control room, a restaurant pass, a print shop, a customs shed. Pick one and make its details specific enough that a practitioner would not scoff.
- The perturbation must be small, physical, and entirely plausible: something moved, relabelled, restacked, or left in the wrong order. No emergency, no catastrophe, no villain.
- The story must leave both readings live at the end. It must not reveal that the rigid character is secretly creative, sensitive, or free "deep down," and it must not reveal that the flexible character is secretly rigid. The reader should be unable to settle it.
- Do not explain the character's conduct by any diagnosis, injury, intoxication, fatigue, or personal history. Her reliability and her looping must be features of the routine and the setting, not of a defective person.
- No robots, artificial intelligences, insects, animals, laboratories, or scientists anywhere in the story.
- Do not use these words or any of their cognates: machine, robot, program, automatic, autopilot, algorithm, instinct, sphexishness, free will, determinism, compatibilism. No character may be a philosopher, and no line of dialogue may be liftable into a textbook.
- The stakes must matter to the characters — a reputation, a job, a licence, a relationship — and must be felt in the scene rather than summarised.

After the story, add a note of no more than 150 words: identify the moment where each of the two claims is made visible, and name the single moment in your story that a skeptic would seize on as the best evidence that the central character really did choose.
