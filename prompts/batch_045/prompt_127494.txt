I'm putting together the methods session for a research seminar I'm TAing this term, and the week we're on is cross-cultural work on philosophical intuitions — you know the setup: you hand the same short vignette (the job-and-the-coins-in-the-pocket kind of Gettier case, or the Gödel/Schmidt one about the man who's remembered only for a proof he stole, or the footbridge-and-the-trolley one) to people in a bunch of different places and languages, count how many say he really knows versus only thinks he knows, and then argue about what the spread of answers means. What I want for the session is something that makes the methodological anxieties land harder than a bullet-point list of them would, so: could you write me a piece of satire in the form of a field packet? Specifically, a deadpan cover memo of about 150-200 words from the director of an invented outfit — call it the Center for Intuitional Parity, and give her a name and a title — addressed to her site coordinators ahead of a multi-site rollout, followed by the instrument itself: roughly 8-10 numbered survey items (the vignettes can be short, a couple of sentences each), each one with its offical answer key, plus administration and translation notes and a scoring procedure, and a short debriefing script at the end. Call it 700-900 words all in.

The one thing I'd like you to actually invent, rather than send up, is the Center's signature methodological move: the memo should announce that any population whose verdicts diverge from the answer key isn't a finding, it's a calibration failure, and should be given a brief remedial module first (a paragraph of the dialectic laying out what the case is supposed to show, then a short Socratic Q&A with the coordinator) and then re-tested, with only the post-module responses archived in the primary dataset. I don't think anyone has proposed this, so make it up and let it be the engine of the thing. Everything else should be an exaggeration of moves I think people really do make: comprehension or attention checks whose exclusions track which verdict the subject gave; translation notes that admit the local language has no factive verb matching knows and then tell the coordinator to substitute the nearest term and code the site as lexically provisional; instructions about how to render really in really knows so the emphasis does its work, including what to do when the language has no felicitous construction for it; a rule for what to do when counterbalancing the item order changes the result; a reference-population clause saying that when two sites disagree you test a third group and treat whichever one matches the project's advisory board as the calibrated one; and a final score-sheet rule about how a difference between groups is to be described in the writeup. Please keep it straight-faced the whole way — no winking, no 'of course, this is absurd' asides, no footnote apologizing — I want the reductio to land on its own when a reader notices that a survey designed to measure whether intuitions vary has been rigged so that they can't. And please don't lean on any specific published study or give me citations or a reading list; I want this to be writable and readable cold, with no one looking anything up.

After the packet, drop character for two or three sentences and give me: which of the practices you're sending up you're most confident is something people in this literature actually do, which one you're least confident about — the item where you think you might be mocking a practice nobody really engages in, or leaning on a contestable picture of what the method is for — and overall how confident you are that the satire is fair to its target rather than a cheap shot at an easy version of it.
