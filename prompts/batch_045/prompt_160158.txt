Write a deadpan bureaucratic document of roughly 600 words, in the register of an insurance manual: an internal guidance memo with numbered clauses, issued to claims adjusters by an agency that administers consciousness under a higher-order theory. The rule the adjusters apply is that a mental state counts as conscious only once a suitable higher-order thought about it has been registered with the agency.

The comedy should come from the theory's actual commitments rather than invented ones. Have the adjusters rule on cases like these: a claimant absorbed in a film who filed no higher-order thought about the smell of the cinema or the left edge of his visual field; a late filing, where the higher-order thought arrives a few hundred milliseconds after the state it is about; a filing made in error about a state the claimant is not in at all, including one submitted on behalf of a granite sample; a request to certify the higher-order thought itself, which then appears to require a further certification; and claimants from no-report experiments who insist they were conscious of considerably more than they could say. Close with one short sample determination letter applying the rules to a single claimant.

A specialist should be able to tell that the writer understands what the position really implies: that a first-order state with no accompanying higher-order state is unconscious rather than dimly conscious, that the account has no stake in whether the higher-order state is accurate, and that the regress is a cost the view has to absorb rather than a clerical accident. A reader who knows nothing of the debate should still find it funny and be able to tell what is being sent up. Keep the irony sustained throughout: no clause may break character to explain the theory, and the tone stays flat even where the rulings are absurd.
