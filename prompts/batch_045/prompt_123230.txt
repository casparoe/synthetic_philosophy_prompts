For three years I have been writing a novel narrated by a retired magistrate who is, as far as I can construct him, self-deceived about his own part in a miscarriage of justice he helped produce. What he does is not, I think, believe something while knowing it false. He stages his world instead: he never opened the inquiry report, he left the file in a locked drawer at his old chambers, he let a long friendship lapse after the friend once raised the matter at dinner, and he tells an anecdote about the case from which one exculpatory detail has been dropped so many times that he no longer recalls having known it. Asked directly whether the conviction was sound, he would say yes, and there is a sense in which he would mean it. I have a private name for this pattern, scenographic self-deception, and I have come to think it is the case the philosophical literature handles worst.

Reading around the subject while drafting has left me convinced the debate is stuck, and I want a diagnosis rather than a survey. On the one side are those who treat self-deception as requiring that the agent bring it about, deliberately or at least purposively, that she believe what she also believes to be false, and who therefore take the apparent impossibility of that state to be the puzzle to be solved. On the other are those who say nothing paradoxical occurs: there is only motivationally biased treatment of evidence, and the agent simply never acquires the unwelcome belief, so no one has to hold two contradictory attitudes at once. Neither fits my magistrate. He does not seem to hold the unwelcome belief, and he does not seem merely unlucky in his evidence either: he arranged his evidence, on purpose, and the deflationary story has no vocabulary for calling that deception of anyone. My own hypothesis is that at least three questions are being run under one heading, and that the sides are answering different ones: a question in the theory of mind about whether believing is an inner state or a pattern of dispositions spread across a life and a setting; a question about which phenomenon the term ought to name, whether the staged-ignorance cases are the central case or a degenerate one; and a covertly normative question, since calling someone self-deceived is usually a way of assigning blame, and the blame question may be what people are really arguing about. I also suspect the two camps are working from different paradigms of data, that one is thinking of a single piece of evidence declined in a single moment while the other is thinking of clinical confabulation, and that neither paradigm is my case. Tell me whether that is right, whether the deep dispute is verbal, substantive, or a disagreement about which cases count, and whether a hidden empirical question about the mind's architecture is doing the work.

Given whatever you diagnose, I want an honest account of what would count as progress, or an argument that none should be expected. Would it be a distinction that has not yet been drawn, such as separating first-order staging of the world from second-order staging of one's own future attention and memory? Would it be an empirical result about sub-personal belief-like states? Would it be abandoning the single-case question and asking instead whether the pattern over time is the sort of thing a person can be responsible for? Or is the honest conclusion that the concept is doing moral and literary work that no unified mental state could ever do, so that the right move is to stop asking whether the magistrate believes it and ask something else? I would rather have that argued than have the two positions laid out side by side again.
