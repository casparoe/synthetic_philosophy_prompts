So I was out for drinks last night with two friends who work at the same quant-adjacent consultancy, and they were describing their employer's actual product, which as far as I can tell is just Solomonoff induction with a sales team bolted on — universal prior, shortest-program-wins, Occam's razor as a managed service — and I can't stop thinking that the entire business model must really be the choice of reference machine. I want to write a satire about it and I'd like you to do a first pass.

Form: a blameless internal incident-review memo (the postmortem genre — Summary, Timeline, Root cause, Contributing factors, Dissenting views, Action items) at a fictional firm I'll call Universal Prior Partners, about 700–900 words, deadpan corporate register, no footnotes winking at the reader and no character ever explaining what Solomonoff induction is. The incident: two rival clients (say a pensions fund and a hedge fund shorting the pensions fund) both bought five-year forecasts of the same quantity, got forecasts with opposite signs, and both were certified in writing as within bound. Root cause: each engagement shipped with its own Rosetta Reference Machine, a universal Turing machine whose instruction set had been extended with primitives drawn from that client's in-house jargon, so the client's preferred hypothesis compiles to a very short program. The jokes have to come from getting this stuff genuinely right: the prediction bound is only ever relative to a reference machine and only up to an additive constant that the theorem throws away, and that constant is unbounded, so every machine is "as good as" every other in exactly the sense that makes the guarantee empty; the bound is on total expected error over an infinite run and can be astronomically large, so within bound means nothing at week three; convergence is only in the limit, with no guarantee about any finite stretch of time (the firm should track a metric like estimated time-to-dominance that nobody has ever been able to compute); Solomonoff induction is uncomputable, so what actually runs in production is a time-bounded approximation — have the memo treat that as a trademark and marketing question rather than a math one; the prior is a mixture over all programs with the short ones dominating, so all the real content of Occam's razor lives in the machine, and there is no principled way to pick the machine, only encodings that differ by a constant, which is the one constant the convergence result discards; and the domination guarantee is only over computable environments, so include some lawyerly language about whether a particular client's world qualifies. The dissenting voice should be a newly hired formal epistemologist (call her Dana) quoted in Dissenting views: at any finite time two machines can say anything at all, so the product is the machine; and even a perfect sequence of forecasts isn't a decision, since somebody still has to attach a loss function and an action space before it's worth anything to a client — the firm should be selling that separately as a Decision Layer in beta. End on the remediation: a Machine Neutrality Pledge committing the firm to one published machine for all clients, which is at once the only honourable fix and commercially fatal, since under a neutral machine both clients get the same forecast and both churn.

The fairness condition matters most to me: nobody in the memo should be wrong about the mathematics, and nobody should be a cartoon villain — the firm's position really is defensible given the theorems, which is what should make it funny. It should also still read as comedy to someone who has never heard of algorithmic information theory, and it shouldn't decay into generic AI-hype or "quants are evil" satire; every laugh has to be a real feature of the view.
