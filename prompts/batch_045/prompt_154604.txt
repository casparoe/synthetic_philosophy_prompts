I write historical fiction, and before I fix the premises of my next novel I need the shape of an argument, not a reading list — no names, titles, or suggestions for further reading, please. The book follows one landowning family over three generations in a western Mediterranean province: the grandfather serves on his town council and complains about the assessment; the son negotiates with officers of a field army that no longer arrives when raiders do; the grandson takes service with a king who issues commands in Roman forms and collects revenue through the same registers. I can make their choices feel reasonable only if I know what a good explanation of the end of Roman government in the West is supposed to do. So please write me the specification.

Give me the requirements an account would have to meet to count as adequate: the phenomena it must explain, the distinctions it must respect, the cases that must come out right, each with the concrete situation that makes it a requirement. Include at least the following as candidates to be accepted, refined, or rejected: the divergence between the two halves of the empire, which shared institutions, enemies, and a common political language yet came out differently; the mismatch between a slow reshaping of fiscal and military arrangements and the punctual events people remembered, such as the sack of the city and the deposition of the last western emperor in 476; the stubborn continuities — senate, law, bishops, estates, kings governing through Roman forms — which any account must say enough about for the claim that this was transformation rather than collapse to be testable rather than merely reasserted; and the fact that a single story cannot treat Britain, northern Gaul, and Africa as the same story. Also tell me what an account owes to the evidence problem itself, since our texts are overwhelmingly the works of literate Christian men while the material record — coin hoards, pottery distribution, settlement size, field systems — sometimes points another way, and someone has to say which wins when they disagree.

Two further things. First, a requirement of my own that I suspect is a novelist's prejudice rather than a real one: I want an account that stays legible from inside the period, in the sense that a well-informed person living through it could in principle have drawn the same distinctions the historian draws, and that the explanation is not available only with hindsight. Tell me whether that belongs on the list, and if it does, what it costs. Second, mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are only preferences of a particular camp, name any pair or triple that I should stop hoping to satisfy together, and say which single requirement eliminates the largest share of the familiar explanations. A one-line verdict on each of the four or five standard families of explanation may follow, but keep it to that; the criteria are what I am paying for.

Please keep the entire answer under 800 words; a compact table is welcome if it helps.
