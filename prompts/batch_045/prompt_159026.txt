Longtime lurker here. I finally sat down and worked through the accuracy-first epistemology stuff (Joyce, Greaves, that whole literature) and I think I've extracted the practical upshot, but a friend says I've got it exactly backwards, so I want a second opinion.

My read: the framework says accuracy is the only epistemic value, and we measure accuracy with a proper scoring rule. The Brier score decomposes into a calibration term plus a refinement term, and calibration is the part that actually tracks whether your credences line up with the world — refinement just measures how spread out or extreme your numbers are, which sounds like a stylistic choice to me. So the rational policy is to maximize calibration: for any proposition, set your credence equal to the base rate of true propositions in it's reference class. I've been doing this for maybe six months and calling it base-rate calibration. Concretely, someone pitches me a startup, I say 10%, because roughly 10% of startups succeed; someone tells me their flight got delayed, I go with the airline's on-time rate; and so on. I've stopped individuating cases, since any case-specific adjustment can only push me away from the base rate and thus away from calibration.

My friend says this is precisely the forecaster accuracy-first epistemology is meant to rule out — that I've reinvented the lazy weatherman who predicts the climatological average every single day, ends up with a gorgeous calibration curve, and still gets beaten by anyone who bothers to look at the sky. He also says I'm treating a decomposition of a score as if it were a norm, and that calibration isn't even the kind of thing you can aim at case by case.

Is he right? And if so, what is refinement actually doing in the decomposition — is it just bookkeeping, or does the framework have anything to say about how sharp my credences ought to be?
