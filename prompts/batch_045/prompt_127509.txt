I host a long-form interview series on moral and political philosophy, and my next guest is the author of a recent book defending the hinge of history hypothesis: the claim that we are living in a brief, unusually dangerous, and unusually consequential window at the very beginning of human history, in which a small handful of near-term choices may determine whether our species ever gets to enjoy the astronomically large future that is otherwise available to it. What makes her an interesting guest is that she is a dissenter on the practical upshot: although she accepts the hypothesis, she denies that it settles any policy question on its own, and her stock move when pressed is to say that whatever has been raised is a question about policy, not about the hinge claim. I want the interview to make that reply progressively harder to make. A friend who reads along with me has been pushing two variants of the idea that I would like her to confront directly. Call the first the carousel model: if every generation that survives its own crisis faces a fresh hinge, then the argument never terminates — we are permanently in an emergency, and the notion of arriving on the far side of the hinge, which does a lot of rhetorical work in the standard presentation, becomes incoherent. Call the second the lock-in hinge: my friend's claim is that the real danger is not premature extinction but the durable entrenchment of a bad or merely mediocre trajectory — a stable regime of surveillance and control that survives into space settlement, or an early freeze on which values get to shape the rest of history — and that on this version the choice is not between nothing and everything but between an astronomical amount of value and a somewhat smaller astronomical amount of value, which seems to deflate the more fanatical conclusions people draw from the hypothesis.

Draft me eight to ten questions for her, each with the answer she is most likely to give sketched in a clause and then the follow-up that actually bites. I need at least one each on: whether the hypothesis does any independent argumentative work at all, given that the raw expected-value reasoning about vast future stakes looks like it survives without it, so that the hinge claim may be nothing but a vivid redescription of stakes we could compute anyway; the temporal arbitrariness my friend presses with the carousel model, including why these centuries rather than a rolling window; the lock-in variant, and specifically whether the hypothesis has to be reframed as a claim about the shape of the future rather than its duration in order to survive it; the non-identity worry, since any serious near-term policy changes which future people come to exist and so makes it unclear in what sense we can owe them anything, together with the question of whether the argument therefore requires an impersonal rather than a person-affecting axiology; and the symmetry problem, which is the one I most want to hear her on, namely that if the highest-leverage act at a hinge is to influence which values the long run locks in, then every rival faction can reason the same way and the hypothesis seems to license a race for lock-in rather than anything anyone would call prudence. Include one question on the option of growing up first instead of acting now: if moral concern has in fact widened over time and our institutions may yet catch up with our technology, then buying time and improving the quality of the decision-makers look like competitors to reducing risk now, and I would like her to say what within the hinge framework decides between them. Close with a single question about what evidence or argument would get her to abandon the hypothesis. No scene-setting or biographical questions: assume the audience already knows the basic argument and that she does not need to be asked to restate it.
