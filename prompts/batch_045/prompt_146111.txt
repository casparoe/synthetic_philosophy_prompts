I'm a senior in high school, and I host a small philosophy podcast for my school's philosophy club — eight episodes so far, mostly teachers from my school and one graduate student from the university nearby. Next month's guest is a philosopher who defends reductionism about personal identity: roughly, the view made famous by Parfit that personal identity is not what matters in survival, that what matters is instead psychological continuity and connectedness however caused, and that once you see this you should stop caring about your own future in the specially self-concerned way most of us do. He has evidently given this interview before, so I don't want questions that let him restate the view. I want questions that take the view seriously enough to find where it is genuinely strained.

Please write me eight to ten interview questions, in interview voice — one or two sentences each, spoken, not essay prompts — together with a follow-up after each one keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. At least six of the questions should be built around the variants below rather than around the classic teletransporter and fission cases, which my listeners have already encountered in the standard telling and which he will have answers polished for.

The variants:

A. The delayed push. The scanner sends the pattern to Mars, where a replica wakes up, but the original body on Earth is not destroyed until a week later, so both are conscious and both remember stepping into the booth. Is there a fact of the matter, during that week, about which of them I am? (He will probably say that the question is empty. Press him on what the Earth one should do with the week, and on whether that one's destruction at the end of it is a harm to me.)

B. The slow build. The replica is assembled one small batch of cells at a time over a month, while the original remains continuously conscious throughout and gradually diverges. At what point does a second person come into existence, and does the continuity of the original matter to the answer?

C. Lossy transmission. The machine can be run at differing fidelities: 99.9 percent of the relevant synaptic detail, 97 percent, 90 percent, 60 percent. Where does what matters fail, and is the answer a threshold or merely a matter of degree? If a matter of degree, what happens to the clean contrast between identity and the thing that supposedly matters?

D. The daily commute. Someone teleports every morning for a year at 99 percent fidelity. Compare the person who arrives at the end of the year with someone who stayed home and simply changed psychologically over that year in the ordinary way. If the two differ only in degree, what work is the machine doing?

E. The unauthorized backup. A scan is taken from someone while she sleeps, without her consent, and stored; the original then lives another fifty years and dies; the replica is made only after that death. Does what matters obtain here, does the original have any reason to care about the replica during those fifty years, and does it change anything that the cause was unauthorized?

F. The coincidence. A perfect psychological duplicate of someone comes to exist by an enormous accident of physics, with no scanner and no causal link to the original at all. If his account says that what matters is the relation with any cause, he seems committed to saying this counts as full survival — and I want that commitment drawn out rather than assumed.

Two further things I'd like included. One question should ask what would count as evidence or argument against the view, framed so that he can't answer by redescribing the cases. And one should ask what the view actually recommends to a seventeen-year-old: if identity is not what matters, should I be less frightened of my own death, and should I redistribute concern between my own future and other people's — and if he says yes, follow up on whether the view has thereby become a piece of ethical advice rather than a piece of metaphysics.

Finally, please keep the questions answerable without any reading: the club's listeners won't have background, and I don't want anything that asks him to summarize the literature or point us to books, since he won't have anything to hand in the studio. I would be grateful if you could also order the questions so that the episode builds, with the most abstract commitments saved for the second half.
