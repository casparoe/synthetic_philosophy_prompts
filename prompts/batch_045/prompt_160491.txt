I am closing a graduate seminar on historiography and would like to end the final session with a poem rather than a summary. I want the poem's form to carry the argument, so I am asking for a sestina.

Where the seminar has arrived: we spent the last two weeks on the fight over objectivity — Ranke's injunction to show the past as it actually was, against the scepticism of Carr (the historian selects; the facts do not assemble themselves; what he catches depends on where he fishes and what net he carries) and of Hayden White (that what turns a bare series of dated entries into a history is the giving of a story-shape, and that the choice among the available shapes is not dictated by the documentary record but is finally aesthetic and moral — though not for that reason arbitrary). At our last meeting a student put what I take to be the strongest objection: if the shape is not dictated by the facts, then nothing in the record can rule out any shape, and we are left with either an unarguable relativism or the quiet claim that our own taste happens to be good. I want the poem to meet that objection — not by refuting it in argument, but by making a reader feel what is right in it and what is wrong.

Form and constraints:

- A sestina: six unrhymed six-line stanzas in a steady ten- or eleven-syllable line, using the standard retrograde permutation of end-words (6-1-5-2-4-3), followed by a three-line envoi.
- The six end-words should all come from one documentary source and one documentary register: the kind of item an annalist enters without comment. I have in mind the annals of a single religious house for four successive bad years of the fourteenth century — plague, failed harvests, a disputed succession. Suggested set: bread, bell, bier, heir, rain, year. Substitute freely if you find six that bear the permutation better, but keep them concrete, ordinary, and all drawn from the same leaf.
- The permutation should do the conceptual work. The reader watches the same six bare entries return in a new order each stanza, with nothing added, and sees that the order alone changes what they mean. That is the sceptical point enacted rather than asserted. I am also attached to one mechanical feature of the form: the word that closes a stanza is the word that opens the next. That is precisely the mechanism I want — one account's closing event becoming the next account's point of departure.
- One stanza, early, should read as close to bare transcription as the syntax allows: the Rankean temptation, the hope that if the entries are simply set down the past will show itself.
- By the fourth or fifth stanza the poem should have caught itself selecting. It should be legible, without being stated, that the poem has been choosing which entries to keep, which to drop, and where to begin.
- At least two stanzas should emplot the same six facts into recognisably different story-shapes — one tragic, one comic or satiric — without naming either mode.
- The envoi should gather all six words a last time, two to a line, and should land somewhere a historian could accept: the reader should be left seeing both that the poem chose its shape and that the choice was answerable to the entries — that the record did not dictate the story, yet not every story fitted it. Do not resolve the tension into either confidence or shrug.
- No jargon and no name-dropping in the poem itself: avoid emplotment, trope, narrative, discourse, objectivity, relativism, construction; name none of Ranke, Carr, or White.

It should work on the ear, since I will read it aloud, and it should stand as a poem for someone who has never opened a book of historiography. But a specialist should be able to say which stanza is doing which job, and should not find the poem wrong.
