Me and my friend got into like a three-hour fight about Rorty last night over drinks and neither of us budged, so on the way out I grabbed this pop-philosophy paperback off his shelf that's got a chapter on him, hoping it would settle it. Now I think the book might be the actual problem. Here's roughly what it says (I'm paraphrasing, but not much):

Rorty's big idea is that truth isn't a matter of our sentences matching up with how things really are. "Truth" is just a compliment we pay to claims that are useful to us, or that nobody around us can think of an objection to, so really it's about what your conversation partners will let you get away with. His target is what he calls the "mirror of nature" — the picture of the mind as something that represents the world — and he wants us to drop it completely, which is his way of saying that reality doesn't constrain what we say, only our habits of talk do. So on his view there's no such thing as being wrong about how things are, only being out of step with the people around you, and no vocabulary, science included, gets you any closer to how reality really is than any other, poetry say. Critics pointed out that this leaves him no way to explain why our best theories predict so well, and since he never really answered that one, neopragmatism is mostly treated as a dead end these days.

I'm definately inclined to think the book has him right, and that my friend was splitting hairs when he insisted Rorty straight-up denies being a relativist — I mean, what else would you call the thing described above? But he also threw two things at me that I couldn't answer and the book doesn't touch.

One: imagine a civilization that predicts eclipses to the day for four hundred years off a theory that is, as it happens, completely false — they just got lucky that the math lines up on that one phenomenon. My friend says Rorty is commited to calling that theory true, since it survived every objection the community could raise, and that any position that has to say that isn't a position about truth anymore. Is that fair? Does he actually have to say it, and if he bites the bullet, what's left for him to object to?

Two: he says the whole thing rides on whether Rorty can keep "the world causes your belief" apart from "the world justifies your belief" once representation is off the table, that every time Rorty gets squeezed he reaches for that distinction, and that if antirepresentationalism eats the difference then the critics win. Is that actually the load-bearing move, and does it survive his own premises?

Then the two things from my friend. And one more: he keeps insisting he isn't offering a theory of truth at all, just a recommendation about vocabulary we should stop using — isn't "stop asking whether your beliefs correspond to reality" itself a claim about truth?
