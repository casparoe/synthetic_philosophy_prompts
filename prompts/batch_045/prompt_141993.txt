For as long as I can remember, everyone in my village has taken it for granted that the Well Blessing on the second Sunday of June is medieval, older than the church itself. Last month, clearing my grandmother's house after her funeral, I found a parish magazine from 1923 in which the curate describes writing the ceremony the previous winter — the procession, the hymn, the dressing of the well — out of a book of customs from a county two hundred miles away, because our village had no festival of its own and he thought it ought to have one. My grandmother, aged eight, walked in the very first procession; by the time she told me about it seventy years later she was entirely sincere in believing she was describing something immemorial, which I now find the most unsettling part of the whole discovery. I have been asked to give this year's opening address in the churchyard, ten minutes or so, to roughly two hundred people: the families who take the antiquity on faith, the incomers who now do most of the organising, the vicar, and two folklore academics who have been invited this year and who will recognise a 1920s revival in my first hedged sentence. I have been reading Hobsbawm on traditions being assembled and then backdated, and Anderson on communities imagining themselves backwards in time, and my difficulty is that the standard version of that story is a debunking one: it makes inventedness sound like fraud and continuity sound like an illusion, and it gives me no language for the fact that this invented rite is now nearly a hundred years old, that my family has sung in it for four generations, and that the newcomers who revived it in the 1990s did so in full knowledge of where it came from. I will not debunk it from the churchyard steps and I will not say that our forebears handed it down to us, so I need a speech that can be strictly truthful about the 1923 curate and still honour the thing, and that lets the audience feel that a false pedigree and a real hold on us are not in competition — one that lands as affection rather than as a lecture, that names no theorists and cites nothing, and that the two academics will recognise as doing something deliberate without the vicar's mother feeling she has been lied to all her life. Please write the address, and add a short note naming the single sentence in it that I should expect to be challenged on afterwards, and why.
