I'm on the steering committee of a small funded research program on the concepts of health and disease — four postdocs for three years, a modest workshop and travel budget, and a funder's remit that the work should be noticeable outside philosophy of medicine within about a decade. We have to decide what the program actually takes on. What I want from you is a defended agenda, not a survey: which open questions should the program prioritize, in what order, and on what criteria.

The dialectic as I understand it, stated by content rather than by author (please do the same — no literature review, no reading lists, no citations; reconstruct each position from what it claims): on one side, a naturalist view on which a condition is pathological just in case some internal mechanism is performing below species-typical levels of efficiency — functioning is understood statistically against a reference class, and "bad for you" drops out of the analysis. On the other side, a normativist view on which the judgment that something is a disease constitutively involves a negative evaluation, so that the class of diseases is in principle revisable as our values change. Between them sits a hybrid: impairment of function supplied by the naturalist, harm or disvalue supplied by the normativist, both required.

Here is my candidate shortlist, each a question I take to be unresolved; add your own if the most important one isn't here.

A. Reference-environment selection. "Normal functioning" has to be normal-for-what? Every naturalist story needs a reference environment, and the choice does enormous work: conditions caused by a mismatch between an organism's evolved design and its actual environment (diet, light exposure, sedentary life, social density) flip between pathological and normal depending on the answer, and the obvious moves — ancestral environment, statistically typical current environment, the individual's own environment — each either over- or under-generate. Can the choice be constrained non-arbitrarily, and does a principled constraint secretly reintroduce evaluation?

B. Probability-raising conditions. Much of contemporary medicine treats as diseases states that are not currently failing to do anything: elevated blood pressure, raised blood lipids, a biochemical marker, a genotype. These raise risk rather than impair function now, and often only against a background of other conditions. Can a naturalist extend "dysfunction" to cover a disposition that may never manifest, without turning every deviation in a risk distribution into a disease?

C. The state/kind split. Maybe the debate conflates two questions: whether a given state in a given person is pathological, and how states get grouped into disease kinds for classification, billing, and research. One could hold that the first is purely factual and the second irreducibly interest-relative. Is this a genuine dissolution that lets both sides have what they wanted, or does it relocate the whole disagreement one level down?

D. The mechanism gap in psychiatry. Naturalism about physical disease can point to what an organ is for and how it fails. For delusions, addictions, and personality disorders there is often no agreed account of the function in question, let alone of its failure. Is this a local evidential problem about particular disorders, or does it show that the naturalist's machinery was borrowed from somatic medicine and never had the resources it was credited with?

E. Direction of definition, and the resilience alternative. Is disease best defined as the absence of health, or health as the absence of disease — and does anything turn on the choice? Relatedly, an engineering-flavored rival has been floated: health as the capacity of a system to absorb perturbation and return to its operating range, rather than as a level of output. Does that account avoid the reference-class problems in A, and does it stay naturalist once someone has to pick the operating range?

F. Senescence. Aging is statistically normal, species-typical, and yet progressively incapacitating; calling it a disease sounds like a category error and refusing to sounds like capitulation to a statistical accident. Does the case just apply the framework, or does it expose that "species-typical" was doing normative work all along?

G. The practice-first turn. Another option is to stop asking what disease is and ask what the concept is for: disease is whatever correctly licenses treating, excusing, insuring, or researching. Does this dissolve the stalemate, or merely change the subject — and what does it lose, given that practitioners' own uses are contested, inconsistent, and historically unstable?

H. Disvalue without pathologization. Normativism's under-discussed burden: many states are reliably disvalued and yet not diseases — baldness, short stature, ugliness, bereavement, difficulty reading in a society that demands literacy. A normativist needs an account of why some disvaluations pathologize and others don't. Is this tractable, or is it where normativism quietly borrows a factual constraint from the naturalist?

For each item I'd like: whether it is upstream of others (what else would be settled or unblocked by settling it), whether it is tractable in three years for people whose tools are conceptual analysis plus some empirical input from classification practice, biology, and epidemiology, how neglected it is relative to its leverage, and what concrete development over the next three years — a reclassification decision, a biological finding, a policy dispute — would move it up or down your list. Tell me explicitly what you would starve: which items you'd drop even though they're interesting, because nothing downstream depends on them.

I also need the case for the program defended outward, and I suspect some of the usual claims are overstated. Three candidate downstream targets are psychiatric classification (what counts as a disorder, and whether revision cycles can be anything other than negotiation), disability and insurance law (what entitlements and exemptions the concept is supposed to underwrite), and medicalization and public health, including whether aging itself should become a treatment target. For each: what would genuinely change if the conceptual question were settled one way rather than the other, and what would remain exactly as contested as before? Rank these by how much real difference the philosophy makes, and say which of them is mostly a rhetorical use of the concept.

Give me your ordering with the trade-offs stated — importance versus tractability versus neglectedness versus how much else depends on the answer — and note where you're trading a high-value question away because it isn't ready.
