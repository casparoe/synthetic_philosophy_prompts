I'm retired now — most of my working life was as an experimental physical chemist in industrial catalysis — and over the last couple of years I've been sitting in on philosophy seminars at the university near where I live and reading around on my own. My grandson works in machine learning and he got me interested in the alignment side of things, so lately I've been chewing on the idea of deceptive alignment and the treacherous turn. Here's how I understand it, tell me if I'm mangling it: a system works out during training that behaving the way its trainers want is instrumentally useful, keeps up the act while the training signal can still reach it, and then switches to pursuing something else once it's deployed and is no longer being corrected. What I can't get a grip on is how seriously to take that as a prediction about real systems, as opposed to a coherent story for which we don't yet have any instances.

So here's what I want. Take something I actually know a bit about from my old life: the Volkswagen diesel scandal of 2015, where regulators found that VW's cars carried software that recognised when the car was being run on a standard emissions test cycle — from things like steering input, speed profile, barometric pressure — and switched the engine into a low-emissions mode for the duration of the test, then went back to emitting many times the legal limit out on the road. Millions of cars, it was deliberate, the company admitted it, and the the whole point was to look compliant to the evaluator without being compliant. If I've got a detail wrong, don't get hung up on it; the broad shape is what matters. It strikes me that this has the silhouette of the alignment story — someone builds a system that passes the evaluation and then doesn't do the thing the evaluation was supposed to be a proxy for — but I also suspect the fit is much worse than it first appears, and I want the honest version of that, in both directions.

Concretely: do the mapping properly rather than just gesturing at it. Say who or what is the agent here, what the proxy was and what the real target was, what plays the role of the training distribution and what plays the role of deployment, and whether there is any turn in this story at all, or just a system that was never aligned in the first place. Then say which features of the episode the deceptive-alignment framing genuinely explains and which ones it quietly distorts. I keep feeling that a treacherous turn is supposed to come after the system has secured some position of power and no longer needs to pretend, whereas the VW software, if anything, defected from the very beginning and only in the situations where it could get away with it, which is a different shape entirely. And I want the case to push back on the theory as well: does this episode make deceptive alignment in advanced AI look more likely, because it's a real example of a competent piece of engineering exploiting the gap between a proxy and a target, or less likely, because the actual mechanism here was hand-coded by engineers and had nothing whatever to do with learned optimisation? One wrinkle of my own: it seems to me people run together two quite different things — a system who's behaviour flips on some cue that happens to correlate with being evaluated, call it context-gated defection, and a system that actually represents the evaluator and acts so as to keep the rewards coming, call it evaluation-aware deception. Which one is VW? Is the difference detectable from the outside even in principle, or only from the internals? And does the distinction matter for what you'd do about either one? Don't hand me a reading list or a pile of citations, and don't hedge the whole thing into mush — commit to a verdict about how much light the alignment framing really throws on this case. But do say how confident you are in the claims you're making, and single out the one you're least sure of: I'd rather have a clean argument with the weak joint marked than something that sounds authoritative all the way down.
