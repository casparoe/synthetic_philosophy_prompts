I'm a PhD student teaching my first standalone seminar in epistemology: 16 upper-level undergraduates, one 90-minute session, a whiteboard, a pack of index cards and nothing else — no AV, no budget, no real stakes I can put on the table. They will have read short selections from Wittgenstein's late notes on certainty (the remarks about doubt coming to an end, and the image of some propositions as the bed of a river that shifts slowly while the water above it moves fast), and roughly half of them will also have read Moore's attempt to prove the existence of an external world by holding up his hands. In my experience students arrive in one of two moods: either they think every well-supported belief is certain in exactly the same flat way, or they think nothing is, and there is always one student who will play the professional skeptic for the whole hour. I ran a version of this session last year and it collapsed in both directions at once — the skeptic paralysed the discussion, and the rest of the class concluded that if doubt stops anywhere it stops everywhere, so that any proposition anyone feels confident about counts as bedrock. I want a design that makes that collapse structurally unlikely rather than one in which I simply talk them out of it.

What I want them to run into, before the word hinge is ever said out loud, is that the feeling that you cannot seriously doubt something is no detector at all. Please build the activity around a deck of propositions that contains both genuine load-bearing ones, where a sustained doubt makes the practice the group is engaged in literally unplayable, and what I'll call "decoys": propositions that feel exactly as indubitable but whose doubt the practice simply absorbs, because more evidence settles it. I also want the same proposition to appear twice, doing different work — what I'll call a "transplanted" proposition, load-bearing inside one practice and an ordinary checkable hypothesis inside another, with participants assigned to different practices so that each side finds the other side's bedrock scandalous and each side's outrage invisible to the other. And I want some propositions to be "erodible": dropped in the course of the session not because anything refuted them but because the practice they were holding up has quietly changed, so that the group has to feel the difference between that was false and that no longer does any work. None of these three things should be explained to them beforehand. They should be produced by the rules, the roles, the scoring, or the way the cards are distributed, and the group should supply the distinctions themselves before anyone names them.

So: a runnable activity with roles and rules and no upfront lecture, a timeline, the deck itself, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the session onto specific features of the view, including an honest statement of which feature the activity cannot reproduce and which I will have to state plainly (I suspect it is the way these propositions are not adopted by any decision and are shown in what people do rather than in what they say, but I'd like your diagnosis rather than mine). Tell me what will predictably go wrong — the skeptic, the everything-is-a-hinge slide, the student who treats a hinge as a rule the group voted on — and how to handle each without pausing to lecture. Give the answer as a single JSON object with these fields: materials (string: what I need, all of it cheap); roles_and_setup (string: who is assigned what and how the groups are formed); rules (array of strings); phases (array of objects with keys minutes, participants_do, engineered_to_happen); card_deck (array of 12–16 objects with keys proposition, practice, kind — one of load_bearing, decoy, transplanted, erodible — and intended_discovery); debrief_map (array of objects with keys moment_in_activity, mapped_onto, facilitator_move); predicted_failures (array of objects with keys what_goes_wrong, why, handling); cannot_reproduce (string). Keep every string field to at most three sentences except card_deck and phases.
