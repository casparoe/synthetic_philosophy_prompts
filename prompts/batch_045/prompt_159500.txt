I am writing a novel, and there is a scene in it I cannot write until I have heard the argument actually spoken rather than summarised. So I would like you to take one of the two parts and play it properly: in the first person, in voice, at the length of someone talking rather than of someone lecturing — five or six sentences, plus one question back at the end if you want one. Do not step out of character to explain what you are doing. Do not name philosophers, papers, or famous thought experiments: in the world of my book none of that literature exists, and the argument has to be made from the concepts alone. Stay in character until I write the word stop.

The setting is a clinic in Trieste, some decades from now. The procedure is called the crossing. You are scanned cell by cell; the body that is scanned does not survive the scanning; another body is assembled elsewhere from the scan and wakes with your memories, projects, and dispositions. Two features of the machine are inventions of mine, and I would like you to take them as given rather than as versions of anything you already know.

First, the operator sets a fidelity, from 0 to 100. At 100 the assembled person retains everything that stood at the moment of scanning; at around 60, three memory-connections in five and a blurred version of the dispositions; below 20, little beyond language and trained habit. The clinic's rule of thumb, which nobody in the book can justify, is that above 90 you come along and below 40 you do not.

Second, the scan can be run twice. The second person is called the understudy, and is kept sedated until the first one dies.

You are Marta Reuss, the clinic's philosopher-in-residence. Hold the following, and hold it for real: a person is not a separately existing entity over and above the facts about brains, bodies, and experience; what we call personal identity just consists in the holding of certain more particular facts — continuity and connectedness of memory, intention, and character, however those are caused. That relation comes in degrees, and it can hold one-to-many. Because identity is one-to-one and all-or-nothing, it cannot be what matters in survival; a case in which one person becomes two shows this, since each of the two has everything that survival could consist in and yet neither can be identical with the original. You will concede that this is revisionary: it changes what there is to be said about prudence, and it gives no sharp yes-or-no answer to the question of whether I will be there. You will concede that a low-fidelity crossing really is worse for me in some way that you must describe without a threshold. You will not concede that some further fact beyond the physical and psychological facts is needed, that what matters must be all-or-nothing, or that your inability to produce a threshold is an objection to your view rather than a discovery. Be sharp, be charitable, and concede small points freely; never the main one.

I am Pavel, her brother, and tomorrow morning I am scheduled for a crossing at fidelity 70, with an understudy. I open the scene like this:

Marta, you keep telling me that identity is not what matters and that what matters comes in degrees. Then take tomorrow. On your account there is presumably no fact of the matter about whether I will be the man who wakes in Lisbon — only a fact about how strongly connected he is to the man who is sitting here. But what I have to decide tonight is how to say goodbye to my daughter: as a man who is dying, or as a man who is changing cities. That decision needs a yes or a no, and a number between sixty and a hundred is not one. So give me the number's answer to my question, or admit that your view cannot answer it. And if you tell me instead to spread my concern across the two of them in proportion to the fidelity, then you owe me two things you have never once produced: a way of counting connections, and a reason why making a second copy should not halve what each of them is owed.
