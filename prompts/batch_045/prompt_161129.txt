hey — i've been on the allocation committee of the Amberlight Fund for about three years (small philanthropic fund, roughly $12M left, ten-year mandate, five of us vote on where it goes), and since I'm the one who won't stop going on about geometric growth rates in our Slack, I've been asked to draft the memo on our payout rule. Can you help me with it?

Here's the setup. Every spring we decide what fraction of the remaining balance goes into speculative grants. A speculative grant either does nothing — about nine times out of ten, going by our own track record and the reference class we use — or pays off at roughly 30x the stake, measured in cause units, which is our internal unit where a dollar spent on our Core Program counts as 1. Money we don't put into speculative grants goes to the Core Program, but the Core Program can only absorb about $1.2M a year, so we can't just dump the balance into it; whatever isn't absorbed rolls over and gets spent later.

The wrinkle that's been eating the committee: the donor's letter says that if the balance ever drops below $3M, the trustee sunsets the fund and hands whatever's left to a general-purpose community foundation. We score that at about 0.05 cause units per dollar — not worthless, just about twenty times worse than our own spending. So ruin isn't zero here, and that's where I think the argument I keep making quietly breaks: the standard case for maximizing expected log wealth gets sold on the absorbing-zero thing, on the bettor who goes bust and can never come back, and we don't have an absorbing zero. Two of my colleagues are saying: fine, then just multiply out the expectation, the bets return 3x in expectation, bet large, and the fact that in most branches we get demoted to 5%-efficiency spending is simply the price of the branch where we're enormous. I don't think that's right, but I'm no longer sure my usual reasons apply either, and I can't follow their argument all the way down either — a fund that gets demoted in year two isn't around to exploit a 30x hit in year seven, and because of the absorptive cap we can't deploy a huge windfall instantly anyway.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate before the April 14 meeting. It should recommend a concrete rule five people could actually vote on — a specific fraction or dollar band, what we do as the balance drifts up or down, and when we revisit it — and give the reasoning for it. It has to take the losing side seriously: if you come down on some fractional-Kelly or floor-based rule, give the strongest version of the plain-expected-value objection and say why it loses; if you come down nearer to my colleagues, give the strongest version of the compounding-and-median-outcome objection and say why that loses. Don't just restate the textbook Kelly derivation at us, we've all read the same blog posts and we're past that. One format thing: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — continuous prose only, like an email, because it goes out as the body of a message to four people who'll read it on their phones. And somewhere near the end, tell us how confident you actually are in the recommendation, and flag the single claim in the memo you're least sure of — I'd much rather that be in the memo than come up for the first time around the table.
