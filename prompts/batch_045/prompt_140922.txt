I write about science and ideas for a newspaper, and rather rashly I've agreed to give a forty-five-minute guest session next month in the weekly reading group of a machine-learning lab. They asked for "the private language argument," by which I take them to mean something like: if a system's internal states are in principle unobservable, in what sense can they be said to mean or represent anything? I don't want the argument explained to me. I want a plan for explaining it to them, and I want the plan shaped by the fact that this particular audience will arrive holding a picture of meaning as something computed inside a head, or a network, and then at best signaled outward.

What I need, first, is the order the pieces have to go down in and why each piece depends on the one before it. My guess is that something about a sign's having a use has to be secure before anything about a private sign's having no use can bite, but I want the dependency structure argued rather than asserted, including the places where my guess is wrong.

Second, the one example that should carry the whole session. My instinct is to take it from their own work: an engineer who keeps a private quality score for her model's outputs, defined solely by how the output strikes her, and who tries to keep that score honest by writing it down and rereading it later. Tell me whether this example has what the argument needs it to have, what it should deliberately lack, and why it is better or worse, at this length, than the diarist who marks a recurring sensation with a sign, or the case of the box whose contents no one can see into.

Third, the misconceptions, in the order you'd expect them to surface in a room like this, with the point in the sequence where each should be headed off: that Wittgenstein is denying that people have inner experience; that he is a behaviorist; that the argument is just skepticism about other minds in a new hat; that memory can privately fix the standard for a sign; that the conclusion requires an actual community of speakers, so a castaway or a machine refutes it; and the one I'm certain they'll raise before I've finished setting anything up, namely that if the argument works at all it shows their models can't mean anything either. For each, tell me what to say at that moment, not what is true in the abstract.

Finally, one question I can put to the room at the end whose answers would show me the argument has landed rather than merely been admired, and the things I should openly decline to settle in forty-five minutes, saying out loud that I'm declining them.

Answer in British English, in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a considered email to a colleague; I'll be turning it into a talk, so please keep it to roughly nine hundred words.
