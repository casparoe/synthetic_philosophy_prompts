I'm the only trained archivist at a mid-sized city records office, and twice a year I run appraisal: roughly thirty offers and transfers per cycle, half a day each, and way more material than our shelves can hold. A five-person volunteer board has to sign off, I file a one-page justification for every accept or decline, and the state retention schedules set a floor for government records but say nothing about donated personal papers. I have no budget for outreach and no time to read theory.

Its not enough to be told that the archive is a product of power and that its silences are structured rather than accidental. I cant hand that sentence to a new hire or paste it into the appraisal manual. Turn it into a procedure I can actually run in half a day: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure just stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts.

Two things I specifically want handled. First, a test for what kind of gap I'm looking at — stuff that was never created in the first place, stuff that was created and then destroyed or weeded, and stuff that exists but sits in someones attic or in another institution — because those seem to call for different responses and I keep conflating them. Second, a correction for what a colleague of mine calls the paper-trail gradient: the volume of documentation about a group tracks how closely that group was administered, not how much was actually going on, so a collections bulk is really evidence about the record-keeper. Should the procedure deliberately over-collect from the least-administered people, and how do I decide when enough is enough?

Be straight about which steps are genuine tests and which are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then run it on three offers from this cycle: (a) about thirty linear feet of a housing departments tenant complaint and violation files, 1978–2015, with names, addresses and unit numbers; (b) one donated box of photocopied flyers, handwritten meeting notes and a membership list from a tenants association in those same buildings in those same decades; (c) the sheriffs office arrest ledgers and mugshots for a neighborhood that was demolished in the 1970s, which are basically the only surviving trace of the people who lived there. I will not like whatever it says about (c), so say it plainly. And tell me what the procedure cant do — especially where it cant tell me what a community actually wants without me going and asking them.
