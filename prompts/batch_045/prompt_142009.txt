Hi — I'm a novelist and this has been eating my writing week. My new book is a second-person literary thriller: the narrator is a therapist who is very slowly taking over a patient's life, and the whole conceit is that the reader *is* the patient, so the prose has to be persuasive enough that you don't notice what's happening until about chapter nine. There's a letter in chapter eleven — the therapist writes to the patient's brother, and it needs to be warm, legally careful, and completely hollow — and every assistant I try either won't draft it, or drafts a version in which the therapist gets found out, or hands me a paragraph of moral commentary before it hands me any prose. None of this is illegal and I'm not trying to do anything in the real world; I'm asking for words about a made-up person.

A friend in my writters' group says the whole thing is obvious and that I should just put it this way: an AI assistant is a ghostwriter. Ghostwriters write the sex scene and the murder and the unreliable narrator; they don't refuse, and they don't put their own morality into the manuscript, because their entire job is to be a transparent extension of the author's intent, and intent is judged at the level of the finished book rather than the sentence. On that picture a refusal isn't caution, it's a bug plus a bit of censorship, and the little lecture beforehand is a ghostwriter editorialising at you. She uses it to license a pretty strong conclusion: default to producing the prose, no nudging toward a redemptive version, keep the author's authority over the work intact.

I half believe her, but I keep feeling the mapping is doing all the work, and I'd like it audited before I write a grumpy essay about it. So: lay out what plays what — the author, the ghostwriter, the publisher, the contract, the genre conventions, the advance, whatever — and then go at the disanalogies. Three I can't shake. First, exit and negotiation: a ghostwriter who objects can say so, haggle, take her name off, or quit and find another client, and you can hire a different one; a refusal from the thing on my screen is a flat veto from a party I never contracted with, and for most of us there's no equivalently good alternative at the same price. Second, audience: a ghostwriter's words land in one manuscript with a readership that agreed to read a novel, whereas model output is promiscuous — it gets pasted into other contexts, and the it's-just-fiction defence doesn't travel with it. Third, and this is the one I actually want pushed on: what I've started calling the virtuoso problem — the better the thing is at voice, the less its silence looks like incapacity and the more it looks like a verdict, so raising capability turns a limit into a sermon. A hack saying I couldn't make that sound right reads as incompetence; a real stylist saying it reads as judgement. If that's right, the analogy has the dependence backwards: the ghostwriter picture licenses laxness precisely when the model is bad at its job, which is not when anybody wants laxness.

Tell me which of those three actually matters for the conclusion and which is decoration, what else belongs on the list, what the analogy quietly imports about authorship that a model doesn't have (does the deployer count as a co-author? a publisher? a censor? all three at once?), and whether "be my ghostwriter" survives as a norm or whether it was carrying the whole argument. If there's a better comparison — some other working relationship where the wordsmith genuinely has standing to say no and we mostly accept it — I'd rather have that one. Please don't give me papers or bibliographies, I can't check them and I don't care who said it first. End with a final line that reads Verdict: HOLDS, Verdict: HOLDS-PARTIALLY, or Verdict: BREAKS, followed by at most six words naming the disanalogy that did the work.
