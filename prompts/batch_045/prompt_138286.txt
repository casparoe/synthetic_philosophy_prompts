For a podcast series I produce on moral philosophy and the arts, my next guest is a philosopher whose new book argues that Bernard Williams's writing on opera — his entry on the nature of opera for the New Grove Dictionary of Opera, and his essays on Mozart's comedies, on Don Giovanni, on Così fan tutte, on Don Carlos and on Tristan — is not a divertissement from his real work but the workshop in which the central commitments of his later moral philosophy were tried out. As I understand her position, two claims carry the weight. First: learning to hear an operatic convention — the accompanied recitative, the exit aria, the concerted finale — is structurally like acquiring a thick ethical concept, because in both cases descriptive uptake and evaluative response are inseparable, and because competence is a practical, historically local attainment rather than a body of information one could be told. Second: the reconciliations with which Mozart's comedies close are, for Williams, the aesthetic analogue of an external reason — a resolution imposed on the action from outside the agents' own motivational sets, and therefore suspect in something like the way the morality system was suspect to him.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions, ordered as an interview arc, with a sentence after each on the answer she is most likely to give and a follow-up keyed specifically to that answer. The questions should neither let her restate either claim at length nor let her fall back on the thought that programme notes and BBC talks are occasional journalism rather than theory. Among them I need at least:

- One that forces her to say what plays the role of the world in her analogy: thick concepts are, for Williams, answerable to how things are, whereas a convention looks answerable only to a practice. Her likeliest move is to say that the tradition itself is the object the response tracks; I want the follow-up to press on what happens when the tradition changes, so that what once counted as a tragic cadence no longer does.
- One on whether the suspicion she finds in Williams's treatment of the lieto fine is really a moral-philosophical judgement smuggled into aesthetics, or whether it generalises: if a formal resolution imposed from outside is suspect, is every supplied ending suspect too, including the sense of an ending that tragedy provides?
- One that puts her first claim against his late book on truth and truthfulness, with its defence of accuracy and sincerity as virtues. A form in which every expression is mediated by convention looks like a machine for producing artifice; is the pleasure Williams takes in it a counterexample to his own genealogy, or does the genealogy have room for it?
- One that connects the sense of an ending supplied by form to the retrospective intelligibility a life can have in his discussions of moral luck and of the painter who abandons his family for his art. Does her reading commit her to the claim that the intelligibility of a life is likewise supplied by something outside the agent?
- One counter-case: composers whose idiom is built against inherited conventions — Janáček's speech-derived melodic lines, Debussy's refusal of set-piece aria. Does her account predict that such works are barely intelligible until their conventions sediment, and is she willing to accept that prediction?
- One question that turns on the fact that Williams sat on the board of English National Opera. If uptake of convention is a trained practical competence rather than information, what follows for the democratic remit of a company like that — and can she say anything more specific than that education is needed?
- One closing question designed to make her state what would count against the thick-concept claim, so that she cannot leave the interview having made it unfalsifiable.

Write the questions in British English, and phrase each so that a listener who knows Williams but not opera can follow it: gloss any technical term inside the question itself rather than in a preamble from me. Do not include questions asking her to summarise the book or to recommend further reading.
