I'm in a small reading group on the ethics of political leadership — we've worked through Walzer's "Political Action: The Problem of Dirty Hands" (Philosophy & Public Affairs, 1973) and Williams's "Politics and Moral Character" — and I keep circling back to a case that looks like dirty hands but isn't shaped like any of the examples those pieces trade in (the bombing of German cities, the ticking interrogation, the compromised minister). I'd like a disciplined case analysis rather than a survey of the literature, and I'd rather you worked from the facts as I give them than try to recall or reconstruct the history.

The episode is Nelson Mandela's clandestine opening to the apartheid government from prison. From the mid-1980s, while serving the life sentence imposed at the Rivonia trial, with the ANC still banned, its leadership abroad, armed struggle still official movement policy, and mass mobilization at home, Mandela initiated and sustained secret contact with government figures — a justice minister first, later the head of the intelligence service and a small secret committee — and kept it up until F. W. de Klerk unbanned the organizations and released him in February 1990; those contacts are widely credited with making the negotiated transition and the 1994 election possible. He had also, as I understand it, refused earlier offers of conditional release tied to renouncing armed struggle. One variable I want handled explicitly: accounts differ over whether the exiled leadership knew of the talks and had given some general authorization for them, or were kept in the dark and effectively presented with a fait accompli. Run both versions and say what the difference does to the analysis, because I suspect that authorization variable is doing more work than anything else in the case.

Some of what I want is the mapping. Who, exactly, is the principal here — the prisoners, the exiled leadership, the internal movement, the Black public, the white public — and which principal's constraint is the one being violated? What is the content of the violation: deception of comrades and constituents, acting without or beyond a mandate, illegitimate recognition of a regime whose legitimacy the movement denied, or unilateral exposure of unconsulted third parties to risk? What is the good invoked, and how probable was it ex ante, given that Walzer's own supreme-emergency reasoning is probability-sensitive? Then two things I find genuinely hard. First: Walzer's decent politician is supposed to know that he has done wrong and to be marked by it; Mandela, as far as anyone can tell, was not tormented by guilt about these talks and was not criticized for that absence either — so is the guilt condition an essential structural element of dirty hands or a psychological rider, and what should we say about an agent with dirty hands and a clean conscience? Second: I want you to test a distinction I've been toying with, between an authorization-relative reading of dirty hands, on which the dirt consists in doing on a principal's behalf what that principal has not and would not authorize, so that prior authorization or subsequent ratification reduces or dissolves it, and a constraint-relative reading, on which the dirt is fixed by the norm broken and is untouched by anyone's consent, so that ratification cannot launder it and only acknowledgment, punishment, or forgiveness can address it. Which reading does this case support, and does the authorization-relative reading turn Mandela's action into a mere mandate defect — he had no right to act — rather than the morally tragic violation the doctrine is supposed to describe?

Then run it in the other direction and tell me what the case does to the doctrine. Dirty hands is normally built for office-holders acting for a political community they represent and are accountable to; Mandela had no office, no electorate, and no mandate, and was acting for a fractured movement whose parts disagreed about whether talking to the regime was permissible at all — so does the doctrine transfer, or does it turn out to depend essentially on authority and accountability, which would make it less a universal structure of political action than a morality of office? Does retrospective success launder the dirt, and if we say it doesn't, what follows about the 1994 settlement — should there have been some accounting, some sanction, something like the acknowledgment Walzer thinks we should be slow to forgive? Is the Truth and Reconciliation Commission's trade of amnesty for truth best understood as a dirty-hands institution, an institutionalization of the doctrine, or as an evasion of it? And where the theory is simply silent about an episode like this — no office, no single community, no contemporaneous acknowledgment of wrongdoing, eventual vindication — say so plainly rather than forcing it to speak. Don't write me a hagiography and don't write me a debunking; assume I know the outline of the transition, skip the chronology and the historiography, and don't cite anything.
