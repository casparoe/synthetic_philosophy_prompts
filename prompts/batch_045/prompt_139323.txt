I run a fortnightly film night in the back room of a pub in Leeds — usually ten or twelve of us, about half of them current or lapsed philosophy students — and we're doing Kurosawa's Rashomon next month (the 1950 one, plot and characters taken from Akutagawa's story set in the grove, title and framing from the other one). I said I'd put together a page of notes and I want to try one particular angle rather than the usual "ooh, isn't perspective slippery" hand-waving we always end up doing. So: read the film through Jain anekāntavāda. Here's roughly where I'm at with the idea so you can tell me if I've mangled it. My understanding is that anekāntavāda — usually glossed as non-absolutism or many-sidedness — says reality is complex enough that any single finite claim about it is partial, and that the remedy is systematic qualification: syādvāda, the sevenfold conditional predication thing, and nayavāda, the claim that every assertion is made from a standpoint. Crucially it's not supposed to be relativism: it isn't that every account is as good as any other, it's that they're all incomplete, and there is a way things fully are that they're incomplete about — a standpoint, maybe only an omniscient one, from which they'd all be seen as partial. And it's meant to have an ethical payoff: if you really take in that the person you're arguing with is looking at a real side of the thing, you stop clubbing each other over doctrine, which I gather is why it gets tied to non-violence. Someone always wheels out the blind men and the elephant at this point. My mate Dave's line on the film, which I think is bollocks but can't quite refute, is that Rashomon's point is that there's no fact of the matter at all.

So here's what I want, and it's the bit I'm stuck on. The four accounts — the bandit's, the wife's, the dead husband's through the medium, and finally the woodcutter's own, after he's caught out — don't feel like four genuine standpoints on one event to me; three of them feel like self-serving lies, and at least two of them can't both be true about who actually killed the man. I keep wanting a distinction I'll call a standpoint versus a screen: a standpoint is a real, partial grasp of a many-sided thing, whereas a screen is something you put up to hide it. Does the Jain framework actually have the resources to make that distinction, or does anekāntavāda end up having to treat even the self-flattering versions as a naya, in which case it collapses into exactly the relativism its defenders say it isn't? If it does have the resources, say how, and then tell me which of the accounts you'd read as a genuine naya and which as a screen, and what you'd do with the dead man's testimony, which is the odd one out since it arrives via a medium and so isn't obviously shaped by any living witness's interest. I'd also like you to take seriously the ending at the gate — the abandoned baby, the commoner, the woodcutter taking the child home, the priest's faith coming back — because it looks to me like the film quietly swaps an epistemological problem for an ethical one, and if anekāntavāda is meant to be as much an ethical discipline as a metaphysical thesis, that swap might be the real point rather than an evasion. But be an honest critic in both directions: if the truth is that Rashomon is about shame and self-deception and has nothing much to do with the limits of knowledge, and the Jain lens is a bad fit that flattens the film into an illustration, say that plainly and tell me what angle I should run instead.
