I am the philosopher on our university's Committee on Engineered Biological Materials, which is finalizing the definitions section of a new containment-and-disposal policy: any material we classify as living goes into the higher containment tier and must be autoclaved before it leaves the building, so the classification has real consequences for how labs here operate. Over the past two years the committee has issued a series of short classification memos, and I would like your help with the item now on the table. The precedents, as best I can reconstruct them: cultured human neurons, living; mature human erythrocytes, which have no nucleus or DNA, cannot divide, and do little beyond glycolysis, living; a mule, living, with an explicit note that sterility is irrelevant; bacterial endospores held dry for decades, living; a tardigrade in a cryptobiotic tun, living; HeLa cells, living; a bacterium whose genome was chemically synthesized and which grows and divides in the ordinary way, living; purified ribosomes and a purified enzyme preparation, not living; influenza virions, not living, though the cells they infect are; prions, not living; a DNA fragment amplified in solution, not living; a spreading forest fire, not living. The memos give reasons only briefly and not always in the same vocabulary, so I am not confident that I know what we have been doing.

The new item is a cell-free replication system: a population of RNA molecules in a buffered solution of activated nucleotides, where the RNA catalyzes its own copying; the copies accumulate errors, the population adapts as the experimenters shift conditions (it has been selected for faster replication and for resistance to digestion), and it persists indefinitely provided the experimenters keep supplying feedstock and removing waste. There is no membrane and no compartment, and no encoded metabolism; everything except the copying is done by the apparatus and by the people running it. My own suspicion is that our memos are not really about metabolism or about evolution at all, but about whether the entity in question admits a distinction between being killed and merely ceasing to be — whether there is a way of damaging it that leaves its matter intact while destroying it, as opposed to simply disassembling it or cutting off its supplies. I have been calling this the killable test, and I would like to know whether it actually fits our precedents better than the obvious alternatives, or whether it merely looks as though it does because I am the one who proposed it.

What I need from you: the classification you would recommend to the committee if we want to be consistent with our own memos; the two or three rules that fit those memos about equally well, what each predicts about this system, and precisely where they come apart; your best guess as to which rule the committee is implicitly running, with the reasons for preferring it; which precedent is the closest analog and why; an honest statement of what we would be committing ourselves to with each of the available decisions, including what we would be committing ourselves to if we classify this system as living; and which further case we should put in front of the committee to separate the surviving rules. My candidate for that tie-breaker is a synthetic vesicle that maintains its own membrane from precursors, grows, and divides, but whose division partitions its contents so faithfully that nothing heritable varies and it never evolves — living on one of the candidate rules and not on the other, as far as I can tell. Is that the right case to run next, or is there one that separates them more cleanly? I would also be grateful for your view on whether the committee should be sorting entities into the living and the non-living at all, or whether our memos are covertly tracking something else — the distinction between being alive and being an organism, say, or between a property of an individual and a property of a lineage — which we have so far been running together.
