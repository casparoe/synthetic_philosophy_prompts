Two members of our hospital's quality committee spent an hour going in circles, and I've been asked to summarize the dispute. This year we randomized 800 patients to a new procedure (N) and 800 to the standard one (S). Overall, N succeeded in 80% of cases and S in 75%. Our analyst then split both arms by discharge track — patients are sent either to home recovery or to a rehabilitation facility, and that assignment is made at discharge, after the procedure and partly on the basis of how it went. N patients went home 80% of the time, S patients only 50%. Within the home track, S succeeded 95% of the time and N 90%; within the rehab track, S succeeded 55% and N 40%. So S wins inside both tracks and loses overall. Paraphrasing, but fairly:

(1) Rina: The trial was randomized, so it's the overall numbers that license a causal claim. If I'm offering a patient one procedure or the other tomorrow, N gives her an 80% chance and S a 75% chance. Adopt N.

(2) Theo: But every patient ends up in one track or the other, and whichever it is, S does better. Not knowing in advance which track I'll be in doesn't matter: by the sure-thing principle, if one option is better under each state of a partition, it's better, period. So S.

(3) Rina: You're conditioning on a variable that comes after the treatment. Randomization protects the marginal comparison; it does nothing for the within-track comparison. Track is affected by the procedure — N sends people home far more often — and probably by unmeasured severity as well, so the two groups inside a track aren't comparable. N's home group is padded with sicker patients who would otherwise have landed in rehab, which is exactly the direction that makes N look bad within the track.

(4) Theo: The sure-thing principle doesn't ask where the partition came from. I don't know my track in advance and I don't need to: conditional on home, S is better; conditional on rehab, S is better; exactly one of those will hold. That's a dominance argument, not a causal one, and it requires no randomization.

(5) Rina: It requires something. It requires that your choice not change which state you end up in. If taking N is what makes you likelier to land in the easy track, then "S beats N within the home track" compares people who arrived there by different routes, and it isn't the comparison that confronts you when you pick N. The correct version: take each arm's within-track rates and reweight them by the track distribution that would obtain if everyone got that arm, then compare.

(6) Theo: Fine, let's run your reweighting — but look at what it returns. Randomization means the track distribution under "everyone gets N" is just the observed 80/20 among N patients, and under "everyone gets S" the observed 50/50. So N comes out at 0.8 × 90 + 0.2 × 40 = 80 and S at 0.5 × 95 + 0.5 × 55 = 75 — the two aggregate numbers we began with. Your method took my within-track evidence, ruled it inadmissible, and gave it back as the very figures I was objecting to. Real observations about real patients can't be that inert.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the six moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, justify each rating briefly, say who is more right overall, and state what, if anything, both parties are missing. Please write your answer in American English.
