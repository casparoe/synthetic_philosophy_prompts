hey — you're a political philosopher who also knows something about market design and prediction markets, and I need you to think carefully here rather than reaching for the usual talking points. Please brainstorm a wide space of candidate precisifications first (ten or twelve, say) before deciding which are worth writing up.

Some context. I'm in a civic-tech reading group in my city and we've been drafting a pilot proposal: for one class of decision — say, which of two versions of a zoning ordinance to adopt — the council would first formally define a welfare measure, and then conditional betting markets would pick between the options; whichever version the market estimates raises the measure more becomes law. The design is basically lifted from the futarchy page on Robin Hanson's site at GMU, where the slogan is that in futarchy "vote on values, but bet on beliefs": elected representatives define and manage an after-the-fact measurement of welfare, while speculators say which policies they expect to raise it.

At our last meeting I raised two objections and someone shut both of them down with that one line, as if it were obviously an answer to each.

(1) The choice of welfare measure is not a clean "value" input that gets settled before the betting begins — it's most of the actual fight. And the measure is endogenous to the policy: if you measure something like "median resident reports being satisfied with the neighborhood," a zoning change that gradually changes who can afford to live there also changes the population being measured, so the index can rise because the people who were unhappy moved out. A market can be excellent at estimating a quantity and still be answering a question nobody wanted asked.

(2) I floated a different design: instead of betting on the measure itself, bet on what a randomly selected panel of residents would say two years later, after studying the outcome — bet on a future considered judgement rather than on a present index. Someone said that just smuggles values back into the betting and that's exactly what the slogan rules out. I dont think it does: as far as I can tell the slogan separates who fixes ends from who figures out means, and saying nothing about whether the thing you bet on has to be a hard number.

So: what are the different precise claims "vote on values, but bet on beliefs" could be making? I want the menu, from the platitudinous to the radical. For each, a verdict — trivial, substantive and defensible, substantive but dubious, or plain false — and I want you to be explicit about which readings would actually do the work my groupmate needed: which readings, if true, would answer objection (1), which would answer objection (2), and which merely look like they answer them because they're about a different contrast (markets vs. voters as forecasters, rather than markets vs. voters as evaluators). Flag anywhere the line is doing rhetorical work the argument hasn't earned — I suspect there's a slide between "markets beat votes at aggregating information" and "markets beat votes at anything governance-related," and maybe a second one between "the market estimates expected welfare as measured" and "the market estimates what's good." And if your brainstorm turns up a reading that handles the endogenous-measure problem cleanly, say so explicitly and say what accepting it would cost.

Please write in American English, and use these sections in this order:
