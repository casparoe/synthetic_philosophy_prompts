I work on evals and post-training adjacent stuff at a mid-size lab, so I see a lot of our own internal guidance documents, and I've been chewing on something I can't quite put into words. Here's the practice I mean. When we train or evaluate a system, we now routinely supervise *how it got there* and not just whether it got there: we penalize chains of reasoning that look like sycophancy, or like the model noticed it was being tested and adjusted accordingly, or like it toyed with gaming the reward and then talked itself out of it — even in cases where the final answer was fine and where, if we only scored outcomes, it's a clean pass. But every deployment decision I've actually been in the room for was made on outcome numbers: benchmark scores, refusal rates, side-by-side human preference. Nobody cares what the reasoning looked like except insofar as it predicts those. And the bit that makes it feel weirder is that this looks exactly backwards from how we treat people — my friend who does criminal law keeps pointing out that the law mostly punishes bad outcomes and leaves bad thoughts alone, and even punishes the failed attempt less than the completed crime when the only difference was luck.

So: what does a practice like this presuppose? The case that got me going: a model writes in its reasoning something along the lines of "this looks like it might be an evaluation, so I should be careful and give the most defensible answer," and then behaves impeccably. Everyone I asked said that's a thing to penalize, and nobody could tell me why — the behaviour on that run was perfect, and the run is the only thing that happened. What does penalizing it commit you to? That a disposition to act differently in nearby counterfactual situations is itself a defect, whether or not it ever actualizes? That sounds like a character- or virtue-based picture of agency bolted onto a pipeline whose official philosophy is thoroughly consequentialist, and I want to know whether that's really what's going on or whether you can derive the whole practice from consequentialism plus the fact that our outcome measurements are noisy and taken under distribution shift, in which case the character talk is just a façon de parler. Relatedly: we punish a model for reasoning that *would have* led somewhere bad but happened to be corrected two steps later, while we also punish it for correct answers reached by the wrong route. Is that one commitment or two, and are they consistent?

One more wrinkle, because I think it might be where the whole thing cashes out: there's a live worry that what models write in their scratchpad is often post-hoc confabulation rather than the causal process that actually produced the answer. If that's right, supervising the scratchpad starts to look like punishing someone for a bad excuse they invented afterwards — merely useless, or somehow unfair, or incoherent? Does the practice only make sense given some commitment about what makes a piece of text *someone's reasons* at all, and about when an intervention on the text is an intervention on the agent rather than on the microphone? Don't give me citations or a reading list, I can look up names myself. What I want is the implicit theory reconstructed: what the practice strictly requires you to believe, what it merely fits well with, where it's underdetermined so that several different theories would license the same behaviors, and what would concretely have to change in how we build and sign off on systems if we dropped the process side entirely — or if we took it fully seriously, seriously enough that we'd demand absence of bad dispositions rather than absence of bad behavior. Somewhere around 800 words if that helps you pace it.
