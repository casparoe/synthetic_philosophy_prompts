Hey — I'm a science-and-ideas reporter with a piece due next week on why the forecasting crowd and the Keynes-flavored "you mostly cant put a number on this" crowd keep talking past each other, and I want your help pinning down my own thinking before I file.

Setup. Imagine a real forecasting platform adds one field to every question. Alongside their probability p, each forecaster reports a wobble number: how many percentage points they expect p to move, up or down, over the next twelve months — expected absolute revision. Questions resolve after two years, on geopolitics, macro, and tech timelines. Say 300 questions and 200 active forecasters, and the platform pre-registers the analysis.

Call it the Weight Hypothesis: (a) the wobble number predicts absolute error better than the extremeness of p does, out of sample; and (b) within high-wobble questions, forecasters' errors are more correlated with one another, so crowd aggregation helps least exactly where people felt least sure. The thought is that wobble is picking up something like what Keynes was after with the weight of an argument in his 1921 Treatise on Probability — the amount of relevant evidence behind a judgment, which on his view is a different thing from the probability itself, can be low even when the probability is perfectly definite, and always goes up when new evidence comes in even when the probability goes down.

So: give me a single number for your credence that the Weight Hypothesis comes out true in that tournament. And decompose it — the sub-questions the number rides on, a rough probability for each, how they combine, and which one the bottom line is most sensitive to. The ones I keep circling are: whether Keynes's distinction carves nature at a joint at all, or whether the Bayesians are right that any genuine epistemic difference here collapses into a precise probability plus a preference about ambiguity; whether, if the distinction is real, forecasters have any introspective access to which regime they're in; whether my operationalization tracks weight rather than just how newsy or volatile the topic happens to be; whether 300 questions and 200 forecasters is enough power to detect a correlation in errors at all, given the usual researcher degrees of freedom; and the base rate for a brand-new stylized fact like this one surviving replication. Throw in whatever other terms belong, and tell me how much weight — no pun — you'd put on the Ellsberg-style results and on what we already know about superforecaster calibration.

I'm at about 0.55. A researcher at one of the platforms, who I talked to yesterday, put himself at 0.25 and said mine was inflated by wanting the story to be interesting. Where does the disagreement between us most likely live? Would you take even odds? And if the result came back null, would that be evidence against Keynes or only against my little measurement idea — I want to know whether I'd be entitled to say so in print.
