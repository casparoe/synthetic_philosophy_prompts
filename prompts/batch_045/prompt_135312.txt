I am a psychiatrist, fifteen years in practice and, for the last two, a patient as well, which has given me an interest in reduction and emergence that is no longer purely academic. At a departmental meeting last spring a colleague said, without malice, that diagnostic kinds like the one I treat and now carry are placeholders, and will be dissolved once the neuroscience is in, the way phlogiston was. I find that I can neither simply agree nor simply refuse: I do not want to be told that my illness is nothing but a mechanism, and I do not want to be told that it is not a mechanism at all. I am closing our department's annual research afternoon — twenty minutes, mostly clinicians, a few neurologists who will be listening for sloppiness — and I would like to end on a poem rather than a summary slide.

Please write me a sestina: six stanzas of six lines and a three-line envoy, with the usual rotation of end-words. If the strict rotation forces you to mangle a line, tell me where and why in a note rather than quietly abandoning the form; I would rather see the strain than have it hidden.

The six end-words should be items from the lower-level vocabulary — words out of a mechanism story rather than out of a life. I leave the choice to you, but I have in mind ordinary nouns that can bear repeating: salt, current, fold, dose, wire, number, weight, gene. The word the poem is actually about must never appear in the body of it: some higher-level kind that the six jointly realize and that none of them equals. In my case it is the name of what I treat and have; I would rather not see it in print, so use a stand-in — illness, sorrow, the self, her — and let it be named, if at all, only in the title. If you find you genuinely must say it once, say it in the envoy, and tell me in the note what that cost you.

I want the form to do the argument's work:

1. In the first two stanzas, let the dream of a successful reduction be felt as a dream of matching: every higher-level term paired one-to-one with a lower-level one, bridge laws, nothing left over. The six returning in fixed order should feel like the promise that one of them will turn out to be the word I will not let you say.
2. In the middle stanzas, let at least two of them be recognizably about the same higher-level thing while the six words arrive in different orders. That indifference is the point I most want made: that the macro is insensitive to which arrangement of the micro realizes it is not only the obstacle to reduction, it is the reason there can be a higher-level science at all.
3. Somewhere around the fifth stanza, give me the squeeze: if the lower-level story is causally complete and the higher-level kind is not identical with any of it, then either the higher-level kind does no work or it was never distinct. Do not resolve it. I want the stanza to feel like a dilemma, not like a conclusion.
4. The last stanza before the envoy should carry the point that some phenomena are stated only in a limit in which the lower-level description goes singular — a phase transition, strictly speaking, occurs only in the thermodynamic limit, where the finite-particle description cannot follow — so that what was to be reduced cannot even be formulated in the reducing vocabulary. Keep it to the modest version of emergence unless your note tells me otherwise.
5. In the envoy, where the six must pair off, let the pairing be the poem's verdict, or its visible refusal of one.

Two avoidances. No "more than the sum of its parts," which is not what emergence claims anyway; and no appeal to quantum mechanics as a licence for anything — whatever the poem does, it should not rest on quantum woo. The phrase "nothing but" may appear once, and only where it is visibly the claim under examination rather than the poem's own voice. The audience is clinicians: the poem should be intelligible without any philosophy, and right enough that someone who knows the literature would not wince. And no consolation, please — I do not want the difficulty soothed, I want it legible in the shape of the thing.

After the poem, a paragraph or two of prose. Separate what is settled or nearly so among people who actually work on this — I take multiple realizability to be settled, and the exclusion problem to be a live dilemma rather than a settled verdict — from what is genuinely contested: whether multiple realizability defeats reduction rather than merely type-identity; whether the singular-limit cases show ontological emergence or only that we need autonomous higher-level explanations; whether the exclusion argument is sound; whether "strong emergence" has any content. Then tell me where the poem leans on one contested reading rather than another, and where it fudges for the sake of a line. I would particularly like to know if you have quietly helped yourself to a claim I would be embarrassed to say out loud in front of the neurologists, and I would rather the poem not imply a consensus where there is none. One caution while you are at it: please do not hold up temperature-equals-mean-molecular-kinetic-energy as the clean paradigm of a reduction. My understanding is that the identity is exact only for an ideal gas, that in general temperature is fixed by the derivative of entropy with respect to energy, and that there are systems with negative absolute temperature. If you use it, mark it as the special case it is.
