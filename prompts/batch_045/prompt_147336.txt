I design the assessment for an advanced undergraduate seminar on folk concepts and moral judgment, and I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums since the Sequences days, so I have a low tolerance for answers that gesture at a position instead of cashing it out. I need one closed-book, 75-minute final essay question on the Knobe effect — the asymmetry in intentionality attributions between foreseen harmful and foreseen helpful side effects — together with a model answer of roughly 800 words and a marking scheme out of 25.

The question has to be unanswerable by reciting the standard menu of explanations. Assume students will arrive able to name, in their sleep, the pragmatic or conversational-relevance account, the normative-concept account, the reasons-based or agential account, and the performance-error or bias account. The exam must force them to use those accounts rather than display them.

The stem should run on a fresh vignette, not the chairman-and-the-environment chestnut: I want a deployment decision at a social-media company or an AI lab, where the lead engineer signs off on a new ranking system. Its foreseen side effect is harmful in one condition (say, measurably increased political polarization) and helpful in another (say, measurably increased turnout in local elections). Crossed with that, the side effect is either a reason for shipping — the team would have cancelled the rollout without it — or not a reason: they would have shipped anyway and never weighed it. Add a third, morally inert condition in which the rollout will merely force a datacenter migration, foreseen with the same confidence, as a baseline. The question should require students to say what the baseline is *for*, not just to note that a control exists.

Structure the question so that hindsight can't do the work: part one asks for the predicted pattern of intentionality attributions across all five conditions, written down before any data appear; part two supplies a stipulated empirical result (you choose the pattern, and mark it clearly as stipulated so that no answer depends on anyone's recollection of published numbers); part three asks which accounts survive it, and — this is the crux — invites students to construct a fourth explanation of their own if the stipulated result defeats all the standard ones, together with the outcome that would falsify it. A student who constructs a genuinely new account with a checkable prediction should be able to score full marks.

Part four goes applied and is worth roughly a third of the marks. A regulator, or an internal model-release review board, is drafting a rule that assigns liability or blocks deployment when a team has intentionally brought about a societal harm. Given whatever the student concluded about the asymmetry, should the rule track the folk concept, stipulate a technical one, or abandon the intended-versus-foreseen distinction altogether? Full marks here require naming the decision-relevant quantity — the taboo move, of replacing intentional with something checkable, such as how the agent's decision procedure responds counterfactually to the outcome — and then saying why that quantity is the one the rule should care about. The answer must not be allowed to stop at it depends what you mean by intentional, at the folk are just confused, or at intentionality is partly a normative concept; each of those is a conversation-stopper unless the student says what difference it makes downstream.

Fairness constraints, please respect them strictly: no part of the question may ask for researchers' names, papers, years, or citations, and no part may depend on web access or on knowing any legal doctrine in detail — if mens rea, recklessness, or the doctrine of double effect comes up, it should be in passing only. Every empirical claim the student needs must be in the stem.

Deliverables: the question exactly as it would appear on the paper, about 350 words; the model answer, which should steelman the folk respondents rather than dismiss them, and which should say plainly where the asymmetry, if robust, is evidence about the concept and where it is only evidence about the English word; and the marking scheme. Give me five must-hit points at five marks each, at least four of which must be: the discriminating prediction stated per account rather than a taxonomy; the correct identification of which manipulated factor does the work in the stipulated data and why; the construction of a new account plus its falsifier; and the applied move, naming the decision-relevant quantity. Add one line per point on how candidates characteristically fumble it, and one sentence stating where the A/B boundary falls — my standing assumption is that a B answer taxonomy is correct but noncommittal, while an A answer has made a prediction it could be wrong about. Finally, add a short honest note on where your own model answer is most likely to be wrong or to beg the question, since I would rather see the seam than have it papered over.
