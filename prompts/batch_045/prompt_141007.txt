**Borrowed flexibility: rigidity never disappears, it migrates**

**1. The target.** The standard illustration of mechanical rigidity is the digger wasp who drags her prey to the mouth of her burrow, leaves it there, goes inside to inspect the nest, comes back out, and drags the prey in. If, while she is inside, someone moves the prey a few inches away, she comes out, drags it back to the threshold, and goes in again — and can be made to repeat the whole cycle many times. I don't want to rest anything on the literal accuracy of that anecdote; treat it as a model. What it's a model of, I'll call sphexishness: an agent initiates a structured, goal-directed sequence on a coarse trigger, and then runs it to completion without being able to take up information generated by its own progress.

The usual lesson drawn is about determinism or instinct. I think the interesting part is elsewhere, and is about the *structure* of the failure rather than its cause.

**2. Three things people mean by rigidity.** Output-rigidity: the same behavior across widely varying conditions. Process-rigidity: the same internal route to behavior every time. Feedback-rigidity: insensitivity to information that the agent's own ongoing activity creates.

Output-rigidity and process-rigidity are, I want to claim, bad proxies for what bothers us about the wasp. A chef improvising from whatever is in the fridge is output-flexible and process-flexible, but if the oven dies and she keeps plating the course as though nothing had happened, something wasp-like is present. Conversely, a creature that does the same handful of movements every time (a bird's song, my morning routine) needn't be sphexish in the offensive sense at all. So feedback-uptake is the load-bearing notion, and it is not the same as variety of behavior or variety of internal route.

But feedback-uptake alone is too weak: a thermostat has it. What seems to matter is feedback-uptake at more than one level — about whether the current move is working, but also about whether the current *policy* is the right one, and whether the current *goal* is worth pursuing. Call the number of levels at which an agent can be interrupted the height of its interruptibility. My suspicion is that freedom-talk tracks height more closely than it tracks openness of the future.

**3. The compensation principle.** Here is the claim I most want tested. Flexibility at a given level of description is not manufactured; it is *borrowed* from rigidity at another level. Improvisation in jazz is funded by rigidly automatized fingering patterns that are not themselves being chosen. The generativity of ordinary conversation is funded by rigid norms about turn-taking and relevance that nobody in the conversation is currently deciding. A general-purpose computer's flexibility is funded by a fixed instruction set. Scientific inquiry's flexibility about what to believe is funded by rigid norms about what counts as evidence. In each case the flexibility becomes more impressive exactly when the funding layer becomes more rigid and more abstract — which is why the funding layer is nearly invisible, and why we tend to describe the agent as simply free rather than as free-at-this-level-on-credit-from-that-level.

If the compensation principle is right, then becoming more flexible overall isn't a coherent ideal, since there may be no such thing as total flexibility. The useful questions become local: which layer is carrying the rigidity, is that layer one the agent could in principle revise, and has the rigidity been moved somewhere worse?

**4. Second-order sphexishness.** A consequence worth dwelling on: you can be sphexish about flexibility itself. Someone with the standing maxim *when I notice myself settling into a routine, break it* is running a fixed sequence; an adversary who wants to control them need only present them with routine-shaped situations on a schedule. Compulsive spontaneity, the person who can never order the same thing twice, the institution that mandates innovation every quarter — these look like the opposite of the wasp and share its structure.

This also shows why adding randomness to behavior doesn't help. A dice-rolling agent is maximally output-flexible and not one bit freer. Which suggests that the relevant notion isn't responsiveness to any perturbation but responsiveness to perturbations *in proportion to their bearing on the agent's reasons*: the difference between being knocked off course by a noise and being moved by a consideration. I don't have a clean way to say what that proportionality consists in, and I think this is the weak point of the whole essay.

**5. A proposed measure, and what I think is wrong with it.** Relative to a goal, define an agent's plasticity depth as the size and variety of the class of perturbations under which it still does something appropriately goal-directed, where the perturbations can be to the environment, to the agent's own effectors, to its feedback channels, or to its second-order policies. Then instead of asking whether an agent could have done otherwise, ask for its *rigidity profile*: its plasticity depth, level by level.

Three things I like about this. It is plainly compatible with determinism, since a deterministic system can have an arbitrarily deep profile — so it belongs to the compatibilist side of the house without needing the could-have-done-otherwise machinery. It separates two agents that the usual framing lumps together: the agent with deep first-order plasticity but zero depth at the level of its own meta-policies, versus the creature with a rigid first-order routine that it can, over months, notice, dissect, and replace. My strong intuition is that the second is the freer of the two, and that this means diachronic revisability — what you can do to your own routines over time — is doing more of the work in our concept of freedom than synchronic flexibility is. And it suggests a concrete diagnostic: to find out how free an agent is, don't vary the world once, vary the *level* at which you vary it, and see where the behavior stops adapting.

Three things I don't like. It looks suspiciously like a measure of intelligence or robustness under a new name. Profiles across different agents may not be comparable in any principled way, in which case it gives no ordering. And the compensation principle might just be false: learning-to-learn systems seem to get more flexible at one level without visibly paying for it anywhere, though I suspect they pay for it in rigidity of the learning rule itself and I can't say why that doesn't just push the question back.
