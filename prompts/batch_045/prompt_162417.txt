My friend Lena used to be really into campaign finance reform and now she has become a full-time sortition person — she consults on citizens' assemblies, and whenever somebody floats a "why not just pick them by lottery" idea she has an instant yes or no. I've been writting her calls down because they don't line up with any clean slogan I can find:

- Absolutely yes to a binding redistricting commission drawn by stratified lot, single non-renewable term, no legislative override.
- Yes to a 100-person assembly drafting the options for a constitutional amendment on a right to housing, with the final choice going to a referendum.
- Yes to citizens' juries advising the council on the ten-year waste and recycling plan.
- Yes to filling half the seats on the state ethics commission (the body that can sanction elected officials) by lot, for non-renewable terms.
- Yes, but only if the sampling frame is widened, to a panel setting water-allocation rules for a river basin that runs into a neighbouring state and across a tribal nation's land.
- No to replacing the elected school board with a randomly selected one with the same powers.
- No to staffing the city's seats on the regional transit authority by lot — those people have to build relationships and be answerable over years, she said.
- No to a standing "citizens' cabinet" that would set the whole city budget each year by binding vote: a budget is the one thing that has to be somebody's responsibility at election time.
- A reluctant no to a lot-drawn panel deciding which drugs go on the state Medicaid formulary: they'd just ratify whatever the clinical staff put in front of them, so what are we actually buying?

Here's the case I actually care about. A state with a few hundred million a year in public research money is considering a "Research Directions Chamber": 150 residents drawn by stratified lot, staggered three-year terms with a third replaced every year, permanent staff and a budget for expert testimony, binding authority over how the fund is split between broad areas (climate, biomedicine, fusion, ag research, whatever) but not over individual grants, which still go through peer review; deliberations and written reasons get published, and the only thing elected officials still do is raise or cut the total.
