Me and a friend got into a three-hour argument about objective chance last night and it ended with us both just repeating ourselves, so I want something concrete to throw at it. His position, as far as I can reconstruct it, is that chance is basically an expert: if you've got chance facts, you defer to them, full stop, and this whole business of an admissibility clause is a fudge invented to stop the principle from giving obviously wrong answers. My position is that you defer to the chance that's in force *now*, and that the restriction on which evidence you're allowed to conditionalize on is doing real work rather than being a patch. We stalled completely on one case and I think we were also quietly disagreeing about what chance even is, which is why we couldn't settle it.

So: build me a decision tree that sorts someone from "I accept that rational credence ought to track objective chance somehow" through to a named position on chance and on deference to it. Details on what I want:

Start from that one shared commitment (no pure subjectivist or chance-skeptic branch at the root, though the skeptic should show up as a leaf later). Give me eight or nine branch nodes. Every node has to be one concrete case or one concrete claim, phrased so that a person can answer it without having any idea which position it leads to, and no node is allowed to name a theory or a theorist or use a label like 'Humean'. If you need the word 'admissible', build its meaning into the case rather than defining it in a glossary.

These cases have to be in there:

1. The one we stalled on. Today's objective chance of rain on Thursday is 0.3. You also learn, reliably, that tomorrow the chance of rain on Thursday will be 0.9, because a front is rolling in and fronts don't dissolve. What's your credence in rain on Thursday, and why that number rather than 0.3 or 0.9?
2. The undermining case. You know the present chance of heads on this coin is 0.5. Separately, and without being told any individual result, you're told by a source you trust that over the next million flips there will be about 90% heads. Do you move your credence on the next flip? What exactly is doing the work if you do?
3. A laundering case I made up while failing to sleep, and I want to know whether it's a real problem. You are not allowed to look at the coin, but your phone tells you, every minute, the current objective chance that the coin has landed heads — and of course by midnight that number is 1 or 0, since by then it has landed. If you defer to chance facts unconditionally, you seem to get to learn the outcome purely by deferring. If that's cheating, say precisely which step is illegitimate; if it isn't, say what your credence is at 11:59pm and whether that's a cost.
4. One node where the chance itself is deterministic: the laws are deterministic and pin down the entire future, and yet there's a respectable chance of heads being 0.5. Is that coherent, and if so what are you deferring to?

If there's room, add a fifth on uncertainty about the chance itself (it's either 0.2 or 0.8 and you have no idea which) and/or a sixth where two independent chance set-ups both produce the same outcome.

Leaves I want to be reachable: the original chance-credence principle with an admissibility clause; the Humean best-system package where chances are just the probabilities that earn their keep in the best description of the world's history; the no-admissibility expert view; propensity-style or otherwise primitive chance; the view that a deterministic world can still have non-trivial chances; the view that you should defer only to the chance function at the beginning of the universe, conditionalized on everything you now know (the cosmological flavor of view); the analytic-functionalist move that chance is whatever makes the deference principle true; and somewhere a leaf for someone who thinks there's nothing there for the principle to be about.

Two structural things matter more to me than the leaves. First, half these positions are claims about what chance *is* and half are claims about what credence should *do*, and my friend and I were sliding between the two all evening: so build the tree from the logical space first — list the questions, the answer-combinations they allow, then put the named positions in the cells — and mark the empty cells, telling me for at least three of them whether they're empty because the combination is incoherent, because it's just unattractive, or because nobody's bothered. Second, flag at least two pairs of leaves your tree genuinely can't separate, and say what further question would separate them or whether nothing clean does.

Every leaf gets one sentence on its most awkward commitment. At the end tell me which single node actually decides my argument with him and what I'd have to answer differently on it to end up on his leaf.

Constraints: no citations, no paper titles, no bibliography, no 'see so-and-so 1994' — we can't look anything up and neither can you, and the tree has to work without them; if a proper name is genuinely the fastest way to point at a leaf, put it in parentheses but don't let anything depend on it. Aim it at two people with about one undergrad philosophy course between us. Under 1200 words if you can manage it.
