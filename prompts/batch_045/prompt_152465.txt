hey, I need a sparring partner. I'm writing a piece arguing that automation doesn't break capitalism at all, it just relocates who gets to collect the rents, and I keep geting shredded by people who've read more Marx than me. So please play my opponent: a committed Marxist in the tradition that takes the late-1850s notebooks seriously, someone who thinks capital's drive to mechanise produces a real crisis tendency rather than a techinical glitch.

Your commitments, so we don't waste a turn establishing them: you hold that competition forces each firm to cut necessary labour time, that doing so raises the composition of capital, and that since only living labour adds new value, the very same process erodes the basis of profit — so automation is capital sawing off the branch it's sitting on, not capital getting an upgrade. You won't concede that the labour theory of value has been refuted, and you won't let me get away with the Luddite-fallback. You will concede that the counter-tendencies are real, that none of this gives you a date for anything, and that a single mechanised firm just pockets super-profits. Talk like a person in a bad-tempered seminar: two or three short paragraphs, no bullet points, no flattery, no hedging every sentence, and if a bit of arithmetic makes your point then do the arithmetic. You can appeal to anything Marx wrote, but put it in your own words and say roughly where it comes from instead of reciting it — and don't just summarise that famous passage about machinery and call it an argument, I've read that bit and it's not going to settle this. Also: don't accuse me of ideology. Argue.

Here's my opening move. Answer exactly this, rather than a friendlier version of it:

Picture the loop. Mining, chip fabs, machine tools, farms, shipping — all of it run by machines that also build and repair the machines, with a few dozen salaried engineers somewhere upstream and no production worker anywhere in the chain. The loop's owner still sells at prices of production and still pockets the average rate of profit. Two things follow, I think. First: on your own accounting the value of these commodities collapses toward nothing as living labour vanishes, but their prices don't, so value has quietly stopped doing any work in explaining prices. Every prediction you actually make with it turns out to be a claim about ownership and bargaining power, and the labour theory is just vestigial plumbing you refuse to rip out. Second: your usual escape hatch is that value is a social magnitude, fixed by the backward producers still using the old methods, which is why the early mechaniser gets surplus profit. Fine — but that only works while the loop is a sector. Scale it to the whole economy and there are no backward producers left to anchor social value, and there's nothing left for the surplus to be surplus relative to. So automation doesn't detonate capitalism, it just empties your theory out. What actually happens inside the loop on your account, and why isn't the answer just rents?

Two more rules. If one of my points genuinely hurts, say so in one sentence before you parry — I'd rather lose to an honest opponent than win against a wind-up toy. And when I type time out, drop character for a single paragraph and tell me straight: which of my moves actually landed, what one argument or piece of evidence would make you abandon the crisis claim, and where your own reasoning is most likely to be wrong.
