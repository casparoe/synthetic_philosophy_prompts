After forty years in isotope geochemistry — most of it spent reconstructing past climates from marine sediment cores — I have retired and begun reading seriously in a field that is not my own: the literature on what is loosely called societal collapse. I have worked through the three cases that seem to anchor every general treatment — the eastern Mediterranean at the end of the Late Bronze Age, the Classic Maya of the southern lowlands, and Rapa Nui — and I find that the more I read, the less certain I am that they are instances of anything. They may simply be three episodes that share a label because they share a certain drama.

I have perhaps two productive years of reading and thinking ahead of me, and I would like to spend them where the argument is genuinely unsettled rather than consolidating what everyone already agrees on. So I am asking for a defended agenda, not a survey: three or four questions, ranked, with the ranking argued rather than asserted. For each, say how much other work depends on it, whether it can be advanced by a lone reader working from published syntheses rather than from excavations (that is, whether it is conceptual and methodological rather than empirical), how neglected it is, and how much difference settling it would make to how one reads the three cases. Then say what evidence or argument over the next few years would move each question up or down the list.

Below are the candidates my own disciplinary habits have suggested. Treat them as proposals to be criticised, reordered, replaced, or kept — I would rather be told that one of them is a cul-de-sac than have it developed politely.

First, the unit-of-analysis problem. A palace-based redistributive economy, a writing system, a settlement hierarchy, a subsistence regime, and a biological population can come apart. In the eastern Mediterranean the administrative and literate layer appears to go while a great deal else continues; among the Maya the dated monument tradition stops while the people plainly do not; on Rapa Nui the once-standard account of self-inflicted ecological ruin has been substantially qualified. So: collapse of what? And if there is no privileged unit, is 'collapse' a family-resemblance term, or a disguised value judgement about which losses count? Is this worth two years, or is it terminological housekeeping?

Second, the detection and null-model problem. Before asking why a society collapsed, one should ask how one would tell: what statistical signature in the proxy record would distinguish a genuine discontinuity from the ordinary flickering of any complex polity? I also suspect a sampling artefact — abandonment leaves an archaeologically loud signature, while continuity leaves a quiet one, so our case list may have been selected for legibility rather than for instructiveness. Is that suspicion well founded, and does it cut deep enough to matter?

Third, the deflationary thesis that societies never fail but only change. Is this a substantive claim with falsification conditions, or a rhetorical refusal to be impressed? If the former, what threshold of magnitude, speed, and reversibility separates a transformation from a collapse, and what is the status of such a threshold — measured, stipulated, or moral?

Fourth, the transfer of early-warning machinery from physical and ecological systems to social ones: critical slowing down, rising variance, flickering between basins of attraction. I need to know whether this is a real analogy or a metaphor wearing mathematics, and whether the vocabulary of resilience is doing explanatory work or merely redescribing in more forgiving language what used to be called decline.

Please also say plainly which questions I should drop — those whose answers are empirical matters I would only be reading other people's conclusions about — and, for each of the three cases, which of your ranked questions it bears on most directly. One constraint: I have no interest in a reading list or bibliography, and I will be reading your answer where I may not have access to a library. Do not cite; where a claim rests on particular work, describe it in a sentence I can recognise without the reference. Argue. About twelve hundred to fifteen hundred words, formal in register, and close by naming the single question on your list you would bet is still open in twenty years and the single one you think is near enough settled that it does not deserve my time.
