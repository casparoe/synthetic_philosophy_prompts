I got into all this through an argument with a friend about whether it was already true, three billion years ago, that the earth would one day have oceans. He said yes, I said the question doesn't even make sense until there's someone around to say it. I've since spent a few months reading about this on my own (blogs, whatever I could find that wasn't paywalled) and I've ended up more or less converted to the deflationist / minimalist side: as I hold it now, there's no substantive property shared by all and only the true claims, "true" doesn't describe anything, and its whole job is logical — it lets you generalize ("everything the manual says is true"), it lets you endorse in the dark (agreeing with a sentence you can't evaluate yourself: an oracle's prediction, a claim in a language you don't speak), and it's a shorthand way of saying "that's right" without repeating the whole thing. Correspondence, coherence, "what it's useful to believe" — all either versions of that or an invented property dressed up as a discovery. What I can't shake is that this has a definately-too-easy feel: the view never seems to disagree with anybody about anything substantive, so nothing you could go out and find out would refute it. So: what would actually count against it? I want defeaters, and I especially want the ones a deflationist would themselves concede, not just what its critics shout about.

Two things I made up that I want taken seriously as candidates. First, call it the shmue test: imagine a community that accepts every instance of the equivalence schema and uses its word in all the same generalizing and dark-endorsement contexts we do, but treats "true" as carrying no licence to assert and no reason to keep investigating — or, at the other extreme, one that always infers from "p is true" to "p is worth acting on". If the schema-plus-expressive-role story can't tell our word apart from those, it hasn't captured what our word actually does; if it can only tell them apart by appealing to norms of assertion or to some property, it's smuggled in exactly what it said it didn't need. Second, the conservativeness thing: I've gathered some version of the claim that bolting a truth predicate plus the schema onto a theory lets you prove nothing new about what the theory was already about, which deflationists like because it shows truth adds no content — but it seems to cut the other way too, since we do seem to get somewhere new with truth, e.g. going from "everything provable in the theory is true" to a conclusion about that theory's reliability or consistency. If that move is real and can't be reconstructed with a purely contentless device, the position is in trouble. I may well be garbling this; say so if I am. I'd also like to know whether the view generalizes out of control — shouldn't I be deflationist about reference, existence, meaning, even logical validity, and is that a reductio or fine?

What would move me most is anything about why truth is normative (why "aim at the truth" isn't just "believe what's so" in a fancier hat) and anything where deflationism and a substantive theory make genuinely different predictions; "but surely truth matters" said in the abstract moves me least. And tell me honestly whether the view has been qualified into something unfalsifiable, and whether that's fatal for a view of this kind or just what you'd expect from a claim about a word. Answer as a numbered list of exactly seven items, ranked from would-sink-it to merely-awkward, each item two or three sentences, no intro and no conclusion, each with a rough percentage for how likely you think that item is right. If my framing is confused — if "deflationism" is really several different positions and my question mashes together things that should be kept apart, or if I'm asking something that can't be answered as posed — use one of the seven items to say that plainly instead of being polite about it.
