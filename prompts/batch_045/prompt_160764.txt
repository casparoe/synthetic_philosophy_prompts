I'm giving a talk in about three weeks — one of those social science seminar slots, an hour, maybe twenty minutes of questions at the end — and I want to be ready for the Q&A. Can you draft me a prep sheet?

The thesis I'm defending: generations aren't kinds we discover, but that doesn't make them arbitrary either, and "are they real?" is the wrong question to be asking. I split what people lump together into three. First, bracket cohorts: birth-year ranges with negotiated edges — the standard newsroom version has millenials running from the early eighties to the mid nineties. Second, exposure cohorts: people who were a given age at a given event — sixteen during a financial crash, thirteen when the smartphone showed up, twenty-two in a hiring freeze. Third, self-constituted groups: people who come to think and act as a "we" and organise around it. My claim is that only the exposure cohorts do real causal work, because there membership is causally upstream of the traits we use to spot members, so they support counterfactuals and interventions; bracket cohorts have no shared cause at all, and nearly all the credibility of generational talk comes from quietly borrowing the exposure-cohort story while using bracket definitions; and the self-constituted "we" is real in a different, thinner sense, but it's downstream of the labelling rather than what the labels pick out. So: stop asking whether generations exist, ask which of these three you're holding and what you want it to do.

The room, which matters more than the thesis: Ive been told it'll be about forty people, mostly not philosophers. A cluster of demographic methods people who think in age-period-cohort terms and will say you can't identify all three without an assumption. Two political-behaviour types who work on generational replacement in voting and think cohort is doing fine as a predictor. A couple of quantitative personality-and-psych people who publish small cohort differences in things like narcissism or neuroticism, and who care about measurement equivalence across cohorts. One quiet realist about social kinds who'll be the only person actually interested in the metaphysics.
