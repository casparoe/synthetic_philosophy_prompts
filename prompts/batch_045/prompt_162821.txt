So I've more or less talked myself into a version of the higher-order theory of consciousness, and I want someone to try hard to talk me out of it. Here's my version, because the plain vanilla one seems too easy to kill:

A mental state is conscious iff (1) it's occurrent, (2) the system carries a higher-order state that non-inferentially targets it, (3) that higher-order state indexes the target as the subject's own and as current — not a detached thought that somebody somewhere is in such a state — and (4) that higher-order state earns its keep: it has to make some difference to what the system does beyond what the first-order state alone would predict (report, confidence, volatility-driven control, whatever). Crucially I drop the usual requirement that the higher-order state be conceptual, belief-like, or introspectively accessible. A subpersonal "monitor" signal counts. That's deliberate: it's how I keep infants and most mammals on the inside of the circle without having to say they're having thoughts about their own thoughts. Call it the tagging-and-audit view if you want a label.

What I want is a map of its exposure. Concretely:

- Which findings, arguments, or thought-cases would count against this specific package, ranked from genuinely fatal down to merely awkward? Don't just hand me the greatest hits — I know the regress worry and the "one thought too many" style complaint and I think my subpersonal-monitor move handles them, so tell me whether you agree and why.
- Here are two hypotheticals I've been chewing on; tell me how much damage each does. First, a "split-monitor" intervention: first-order sensory processing and ordinary reporting behavior are left intact, but the monitor signal is knocked out (or scrambled so it no longer tracks the first-order state). If subjects in that condition still insist they have rich experience, and their confidence ratings go flat while their first-order discrimination stays sharp — how bad is that for me? Second, the mirror case: the monitor pathway gets driven directly with no first-order state to target, and subjects report vivid experience of something. Which of the two hurts more, and is the damage symmetric or lopsided?
- I also want a verdict on a behavioral paradigm I half-invented: hold first-order discriminability fixed between two stimuli while varying only the reliability of the higher-order signal (a "decoy-match" design). My view predicts reported experience should track the monitor signal, not the first-order discriminability. If it tracked discriminability instead, is that decisive or just awkward? Is the paradigm even coherent, or is there no way in principle to vary the one while holding the other fixed?
- Be honest about insulation. The thing that worries me most is that my evidence base is entirely report-shaped, and report is exactly what a higher-order state is supposed to explain — so is there any route from evidence to consciousness here that isn't already routed through the very thing the theory posits? Have I quietly qualified this into unfalsifiability with clause (4)? And if I have: is that actually a problem for a theory of this kind, or is some insulation fine given that the theory is making a partly constitutive claim rather than an empirical prediction? I'd like you to be willing to say either "yes, this is now unfalsifiable and that's fatal" or "it's insulated and that's OK, here's why."
- What does my view predict that a first-order / global-broadcast view, or a self-representational view, doesn't? And at what point does my version collapse into one of them — would that be a defeater or a friendly merger?
- One more: if I build a machine with a genuine monitor module that passes the audit test, does my view say it's conscious? Is that a reductio or a bullet I should bite?

Please also say which of these a committed higher-order theorist would concede as counting against them, versus which they'd dismiss as question-begging (I suspect a lot of the intuition-based objections just presuppose what's at issue, but tell me if that's a dodge). And tell me which single one of these you'd personally lean on hardest if you were trying to move me.

American English please, and be casual — no need to be formal. For each substantive claim, give a rough confidence level, and single out the one point you're least sure about, including where you're just guessing. Don't give me a reading list; I want your own reasoning.
