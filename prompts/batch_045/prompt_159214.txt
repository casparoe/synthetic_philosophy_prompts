I help run a volunteer project that puts together deliberative mini-publics for our city: we pull about forty residents at random, they sit through two days of briefings and expert testimony, and then they vote on a recommendation that the council has promised to take seriously. One of the other organisers has started leaning hard on the Condorcet jury theorem in our design meetings. His pitch is basically that the residents are jurors, the recommendation is the verdict, and the theorem says that if everyone is even slightly better than a coin flip and their errors are independent, the majority's chance of being right goes to 1 as you add people. From that he draws two conclusions: (a) we should scale up to 150 or 200 participants, because bigger is just strictly better, and (b) the two days of briefing are useless or actively harmful, since deliberation is exactly what wrecks independence — people share reasons, defer to whoever sounds most confident, and become correlated — so we'd do better to cold-poll 200 residents and take the majority. When I pushed back he swapped analogies and said it's like the old story about the crowd at the county fair guessing the weight of an ox: nobody in the crowd knows much, but the aggregate beats nearly every individual guess, so why are we paying experts at all.

I'm sure something is off in both moves but I can't get the shape of it, so I'd like an audit. Can you lay out the mapping explicitly — what plays the juror, the verdict, the defendant's guilt, the competence parameter, the independence assumption, the size of the jury — and then where it breaks? Things I'm personally chewing on: real juries are pushed toward unanimity and they hang when they can't get there, whereas our mini-public happily reports a 21–19 majority, so we're already unlike the thing the theorem is named after; the ox story is about averaging continuous guesses at a quantity, not about majority rule on a yes/no; a lot of what we actually ask them isn't fact-shaped at all ("should the city prioritize the flood defences over the bus lanes?"), which may mean there's no pre-existing correctness for a majority to track; and adding people can't be free if the marginal resident is worse than a coin flip. Also, one of our members insists that with mixed competences the majority just tracks the average competence, and another insists you can build a group whose majority does worse than its average member and even worse than its best member — I genuniely don't know which of them is right, and it matters a lot for (a).

Two design tweaks we're toying with, and I want the theorem's verdict on them: a "split room" version where three sub-juries of about fifteen never talk to each other and we take a majority of the three verdicts; and a confidence-weighting scheme where everyone says how likely they think they are to be right and we either weight votes by that or drop the bottom quartile of self-rated confidence. Does either of those buy us the independence the theorem wants without paying for it in competence? Finish by telling me whether (a) and (b) survive once the disanalogies are on the table, which disanalogies are actually doing the work versus which ones are just pedantry, and whether there's a better analogy for what a mini-public is doing — my guess is something like a forecasting panel, or a set of measuring instruments with partly shared error, but I can't make that precise.
