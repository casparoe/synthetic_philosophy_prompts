I do backend work — data pipelines, nothing philosophical — and I run a little monthly movie night with a few people from the office. We watched Run Lola Run last week, the 1998 German one where the girlfriend has 20 minutes to come up with 100,000 marks or her boyfriend gets killed by a gangster, and the same 20 minutes gets played three times with slightly different outcomes. Two friends got into it afterward and I somehow got volunteered to write something up for the group chat, so here I am. One of them (he did a history degree before he switched to product) said the movie is basically the for want of a nail idea with a techno soundtrack: tiny differences early — the dog on the stairs, whether she trips, a few seconds on the clock, whether she is late enough to overhear her father's mistress — cascade into completely different endings, and then those quick still-photo montages of the bystanders she bumps into show whole lives getting rerouted. The other one, who does ML stuff with me, said it isn't chaos at all, because each run Lola keeps information from the last one: she jumps the dog instead of getting bitten, she knows how the guard will react, she prays instead of arguing with her dad. So by his reading it's a retry loop with state, closer to gradient descent than to a butterfly flapping its wings. I think they're both half right and I also think they're both using chaos to mean whatever they want, and I don't have any philosophy background so I can't tell which is which.

What I actually want is a careful reading of the film against the for want of a nail debate, and I'd like you to keep apart three things that I suspect the proverb runs together: amplification, that a small difference early can produce a big difference later; fragility, that the outcome that actually happened was one of several nearby possibilities rather than something overdetermined that was going to happen anyway; and steerability, whether anyone could have aimed at the better outcome. My guess, which I can't really defend, is that these don't go together the way people assume — that you can have amplification without fragility, since a system can be wildly sensitive in its details and still land in the same place in every way that matters, and that you can have fragility without steerability, since if tiny unknowable differences are what decides things then no amount of effort or intention buys you any control over which one you get, which would mean the third run, where everything works out, is the film quietly cheating on its own premise. So which of the three does the movie actually show? Does the third run earn its happy ending, or is it just luck stacked on luck, or does the fact that Lola remembers across runs turn it into a different kind of story entirely? And is my coworker right that the reruns are doing search rather than contingency?

A few other things I'd like you to get to if you can. First, the lock-in feeling: in the first two runs it seems like once the money is on the train and the clock is running, nothing Lola does downstream can fix it, she has to go back to the top of the loop and start over — is that path dependence in the economics sense, where the cost of leaving a path rises the further down it you go, and is it a different claim from the chaos one or the same claim in different clothes? Second, the bystander montages: is it fair to read those as the film saying ordinary lives are fragile in exactly this way, or is the movie doing something dishonest by making every tiny nudge produce one big legible change in somebody's biography, when real sensitivity to initial conditions would mostly just give you noise you can't predict? Third, whether the film has anything to say about the experience of contingency — the way you only notice the hinge moments after the fact and then build a story around them — that the arguments about it leave out. And please, no reading lists or papers or names I should look up; I'm definately not going to read them, I just want the reasoning in plain language I can paste into the chat.
