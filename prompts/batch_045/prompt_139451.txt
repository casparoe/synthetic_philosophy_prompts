A friend lent me a recent popular book of arguments for atheism. Its chapter on the argument from divine hiddenness closes with a summary paragraph, and before I repeat any of it to anyone else I would like to know how much of it is fair. The paragraph reads as follows:

"The argument is short enough to state in a sentence. If there were a perfectly loving God, he would want a personal relationship with every human being; you cannot have a personal relationship with someone whose existence you do not believe in; so God would see to it that everyone who is honestly open to him believes; but there are people who are honestly open to him and do not believe; therefore there is no perfectly loving God. Notice what this really demands: that God make himself as obvious as the people in the room with us, since that is what it takes to be in a relationship with someone. The argument is best seen as a branch of the problem of evil — both say that the world is not what we would expect a loving God to bring about, and both are answered in the same way. Its author is cautious about the conclusion, but the argument plainly establishes atheism: a God who leaves sincere seekers in the dark is no God at all. The standard reply is decisive. Skeptical theism reminds us that we are in no position to say what reasons an infinite mind might have for remaining hidden, and once that is granted the argument collapses. There is also an older reply: God stays hidden so that love for him can be freely given, since no one freely loves a being whose reality is as unmistakable as the sun. The most interesting development, however, extends the argument from God's existence to God's will. People who all believe in God still disagree, apparently intractably, about what he requires of them and about which religion, if any, he endorses. If hiddenness about his existence counts against a loving God, then hiddenness about his requirements counts in exactly the same way, and it is hard to see how the replies above could answer one and not the other."

I have several half-formed suspicions and would rather not trust them. I had thought the argument was specifically about people who are not resisting God in any way, and that "honestly open" and "sincere seeker" were doing more work than the paragraph lets on. The claim that this is a branch of the problem of evil seems to me both right and wrong: neither argument mentions suffering as such, yet both do seem to have the same shape. And the closing move about ambiguity in the divine will struck me as either a genuinely powerful extension of the argument or a bait-and-switch, but I cannot tell which.

Please work through the paragraph claim by claim and tell me what someone who takes the hiddenness argument seriously would accept as stated, what they would regard as a fair compression, and what they would call a misrepresentation. In particular:

Does the argument require that God be obvious to everyone, or only that belief be attainable by those who are not resistant, and does the distinction matter? Is the argument really a version of the problem of evil, or does it rest on different premises about love and relationship, and are those premises answered by the same considerations? Is the conclusion strictly that no perfectly loving God exists, or that no God exists, and does the difference affect how much work the argument does? Are the two replies genuine refutations or responses that carry a cost — does skeptical theism have as much traction here as it does against the problem of evil, and does the freedom reply, taken seriously, predict that God should be hidden from believers as well? And does the extension to the ambiguity of the divine will follow from the argument's own logic, or does it quietly introduce a new premise that a defender of the original argument might reject?

Finally, if you can, give me a single sentence I could use to state the argument in a way that neither its defenders nor its critics would object to as a summary.
