I'm taking a political theory seminar and we just did a two-week unit on moral revolutions — Williams's argument that nobody in the ancient Mediterranean world could have been argued out of slavery, because the concepts you'd need to even mount the argument weren't in the repertoire yet, and then Appiah's book on moral revolutions (the one with dueling, footbinding and Atlantic slavery) saying that what actually ends practices like these is a shift in the honor code rather than anybody being persuaded. My roommate, who does econ and loves being insufferable, took those two together and built what he calls the argument-proofness test: a practice is argument-proof at a time if there is no argument from premises its participants already accept that gets you to the conclusion that the practice is wrong; on that definition both slavery and dueling were argument-proof right up until the moment they collapsed; therefore moral argument never does any causal work, it's just the noise people make while interests and honor shift underneath, and if you want to change something the rational move is to stop arguing and go straight to making the practice unprofitable or embarrassing.

The problem is I half believe him, and it's messing with something I actually care about. I used to march in a college band, and the hazing there is defended entirely in honor terms — we're a family, what we put the new kids through is what makes the bond real, anyone who complains is weak — and every safety or dignity argument I make just bounces off, because from inside that little honor world the premises aren't available. So, before I spend the next couple of years either arguing with my old section mates or instead just trying to get the thing banned and uncool: is the argument-proofness test actually Williams's claim, or have we smuggled something in?

Concretely, I can't tell whether no argument was available from inside their outlook really implies that argument did no work in the change — surely somebody has to build the new premises, and doesn't that count as argument doing something? And I can't tell whether Appiah's honor mechanism means reasons are epiphenomenal, or whether argument can be one of the things that rewrites an honor code — and if it can, who has to be listening for it to do any work, the people actually doing the practice or the bystanders who's respect they want? Also, my roommate's version seems to slide toward saying slavery was fine for the people who practiced it, which I really don't want to be committed to, but I can't put my finger on where exactly the slide happens. And last thing: if interests, honor and argument all show up in every one of these cases, how would you even tell which one did the work, or is that question just badly posed?
