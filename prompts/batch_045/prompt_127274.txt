Draft the joint statement that a defender of the Identity of Indiscernibles and a haecceitist could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on Black's two-sphere world: a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar spheres, a fixed distance apart, with no landmark, boundary, or external object to break the symmetry.

The defender's line is that the spheres are at least weakly discerned: the relation of being two miles from holds between each sphere and the other, and between neither sphere and itself, and that irreflexive, symmetric pattern is enough to make them two rather than one; there is no further fact of which sphere is which and no primitive thisness attaching to either. The haecceitist's line is that the case is a counterexample: two things could agree in every qualitative and relational respect, so what makes them two cannot be a difference between them; identity is primitive and ungrounded, distinct possible worlds can differ by nothing but a permutation of the spheres, and either sphere could have been created without the other.

Give me, in this order: a numbered section of claims both can sign, including about what is coherent, about what the two spheres share, about what a purely qualitative description of such a world can and cannot distinguish, and about whether the disagreement is really about identity or about counting; then a table of the disagreements that remain, each stated twice, once in the vocabulary its own side would accept, and sorted into genuine disagreements and disputes about words such as property, quality, grounds, or sameness of possible world, with your reasons for each sorting; then, for each genuine crux, the single case or argument both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

Three cases must be handled explicitly, because I think they are where the two lines actually come apart. First, a continuous series of otherwise identical worlds in which the distance between the spheres shrinks toward zero: at the limit, one sphere or two, and on what grounds. Second, a world containing exactly one sphere qualitatively just like them: is there one such possible world or two, and if two, in what does their difference consist. Third, whether the dispute has consequences anywhere else, in particular for how indistinguishable particles are counted in statistical mechanics and for whether there is a fact about which of two computationally identical uploads is you. Where a case turns out to cut neither way, say so instead of manufacturing a prediction.
