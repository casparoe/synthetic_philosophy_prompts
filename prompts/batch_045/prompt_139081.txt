Professional historians are forbidden to invent. No invented dialogue, no ascription of unrecorded thoughts or motives, no composite minor characters — and the ban holds even when the invention is scrupulously in character, is the only way to make a sparse record legible, and arguably conveys more truth than the documents do. Biopics and historical dramas do exactly that as routine craft, are praised for it, and answer to nothing but a based-on-a-true-story card. Same activity, opposite conventions about the same device. And as I understand it the law lines up with the drama rather than the history: in the common-law world defaming the dead is not a tort at all — the action is personal and dies with the plaintiff — while a dead person's name, image, and likeness are increasingly treated as inheritable, licensable property.

I want the implicit theory of what we owe the real dead, and I want it split into what the practice strictly presupposes and what merely fits it comfortably. Grant for the sake of argument that the dead have no welfare-level interests: no experiences, no frustratable preferences, nothing a portrayal could be *bad for*. Something still seems to go wrong in some of these cases, and I can see two candidate accounts. One says the duty is really owed to the living — an anti-deception obligation whose object is the audience and the integrity of a shared record, so that nothing is owed to the dead at all. The other says what matters is answerability: a person is entitled to participate in the making of their own public image, and the dead uniquely cannot consent, correct, or reply.

The case that makes me doubt the second story is a living person who also cannot answer back — someone permanently unconscious, or with advanced dementia, or simply someone who will never learn the film exists and whose standing is unaffected by it. Practice treats those people as *more* protected, not as free material, which looks like a vulnerability principle rather than an answerability one. So: reconstruct the theory the practices actually presuppose, say whether the two halves of it are consistent with each other, tell me where the practice is underdetermined by several theories, and tell me what a coherent practice would look like under the view you defend — on-screen provenance markers, disclaimers, nothing, or something else — especially given that audiences form most of their historical beliefs from dramas and visibly do not discount them.
