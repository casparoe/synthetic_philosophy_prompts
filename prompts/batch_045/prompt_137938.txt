I'm taking an undergraduate seminar on archives, evidence, and power, and two analogies keep doing all the argumentative work in our discussions, usually in combination. The first is the net: the archive is a net with a mesh of a certain size, records are caught or slip through depending on how coarse it is, and the lesson drawn is that the remedy for a slanted archive is a finer mesh — appraise less, collect more, digitize everything, and the record converges on what actually happened. The second is the Sherlock Holmes dog that did not bark: when a body of records is silent about something — no petitions from a district, no mention of a group in the correspondence — the silence itself is read as evidence, and the skilled historian's job is to hear it.

Please audit both. For each, set out explicitly what is supposed to map onto what (a table is fine), then the disanalogies, and separate the ones that matter for the work these analogies are being asked to do from the ones that do not. Two complications from our readings seem to me to bear on both. First, silence has several quite different sources: nothing was ever created; something was created and then destroyed; something survives but was never collected, or was later discarded by an archivist; something sits in the boxes but is uncatalogued, unindexed, or invisible to search. Do the analogies track these differences, or do they flatten them into a single kind of gap? Second, most state records are by-products of administrative procedures rather than reports of events, so the density of the archive tracks the reach of paperwork-generating institutions — tax registers, courts, property titles, licensing — rather than the density of human life. What does that do to the mesh picture, given that a finer mesh in the same water catches more of the same fish? And for the dog: the Holmes inference works because we know the dog was present and how readily it barks at strangers, whereas in an archive the expected baseline of documentation is usually the very thing in dispute, since what counted as worth recording was itself a decision made by someone with power.

I would also like you to say what, if anything, both analogies leave out about who gets recorded. Then give your bottom line: does the practical conclusion our seminar draws — that more collecting and more digitization closes the gap — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the net carrying it the whole time? If both analogies fail, propose a better one.
