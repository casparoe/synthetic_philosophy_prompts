I chair the Priorities Committee of a regional statutory health plan with about two million members. Our charter says we may recommend a change only where it is a Pareto improvement in the ex ante sense: the change must raise each member's own expected wellbeing, where the expectation is taken over the outcomes that member personally faces, including their uncertainty about whether they will turn out to be the one who needs the service. The charter adds a single further qualifier, that the improvement be a safe one, glossed as holding under any reasonable way of resolving what we do not know. We have never had to say what that covers, because until now none of our cases has turned on it. In practice we do not observe members' attitudes to risk over health outcomes, we estimate the size and severity of effects only within a confidence interval, and we can sometimes but not always identify in advance who is at elevated risk.

Four decisions from our last two years:

1. Approved unanimously. Moving every member on a branded inhaler to a clinically equivalent generic, with the savings reinvested in a service that improves everyone's prospects. No member's benefits were reduced.

2. Rejected. A reallocation of funds away from a very costly, minimally effective last-line treatment for patients with terminal disease and toward a prevention programme that would have produced a substantially larger total health gain. The losers were identifiable; the gainers were a different and diffuse group.

3. Rejected. A new benefit that would have raised the expected health of the average member, but whose costs were to fall entirely on one identifiable subgroup, older members in rural districts, who would have paid the same premium increase while using the service at far lower rates. That subgroup's own expected health would have fallen.

4. Approved, over a dissent. Adding a vaccine to the schedule, although a small number of people would be seriously harmed by it and could be named afterwards. Our reasoning was that no one could be named in advance, that at the time of the decision each member's own expected prospects improved, and that the arithmetic was favourable by a wide enough margin that the verdict did not depend on how averse to risk we took members to be.

Now the proposal in front of us. Condition C is silent until it kills; undetected, it kills about one member in a thousand each year, and a death from C costs about twelve years of good health. Annual screening with early intervention would prevent nearly all of those deaths. The following numbers are the ones we have; take them as given rather than as an invitation to go looking for better ones. The programme costs 620 dollars per member-year, financed by a uniform premium increase, and at the plan's marginal cost-effectiveness threshold of 50,000 dollars per QALY the health displaced by that spending is about 0.0124 QALYs per member-year (we treat the forgone health as spread across the membership rather than as falling on any identifiable group). Averaged over all members the expected benefit is about 0.012 QALYs per member-year, so on a risk-neutral calculation members are very slightly worse off on the whole. The trial evidence on effectiveness is unusually tight, so severity is not where the disagreement lies. What is not settled is risk attitude, and also this: a first-degree family history identifies about 2 per cent of members whose annual risk is close to 1 in 100, which leaves the remaining 98 per cent facing about 1 in 1,225. The plan's calibrated health utility, a mildly concave transform of QALYs fitted to how members have actually traded off small risks elsewhere, puts the certain equivalent of a one-in-a-thousand chance of losing twelve QALYs at about 0.020 QALYs and of a one-in-1,225 chance at about 0.016.

The proposal reaches us in three forms: universal with no opt-out; universal with an easy annual opt-out; and targeted at the 2 per cent with a family history, financed either from the common premium or from a premium charged only to them.

What I want from you. Set out the rules that fit our four decisions, and say what each of them implies about each of the three forms, including the two ways of financing the targeted version. Give me your best recommendation for what we should do if we want to be coherent with ourselves, name the closest precedent, and tell me plainly where our record underdetermines the answer and exactly what principle we would be adopting with each of the available choices. Tell me what case we could put on the agenda next month that would separate the surviving readings of the charter's qualifier more cleanly than this one does, and over which dimension: risk attitudes, effect-size uncertainty, or what can be identified in advance. Finally, say whether the opt-out version changes anything: whether a member who could have left and did not has thereby revealed enough for the improvement to count as safe, or whether inertia and misunderstanding undo that, and what the committee would be assuming about its own members if it relied on either answer.
