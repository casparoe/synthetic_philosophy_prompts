I should say at the outset that I have no training in philosophy or psychology. I came to this sideways. A friend of mine runs small paid online surveys — fifty-five to seventy respondents at a time, mostly as a hobby — and for the past year he has been collecting people's answers to short stories about people at work making decisions. He tells me the basic pattern is already a known thing in philosophy, called the side-effect effect or sometimes the Knobe effect; I had been calling it "the chairman problem" in my own notes and only found out the name recently. I am much less interested in the name than in what his respondents are actually doing, and I would like help predicting his next result before the data come in.

Every story has the same shape. Someone in an organisation is choosing between ways of doing something. An analyst tells them that the option under consideration will also bring about some further consequence that is not the point of the choice. The person picks it anyway, the consequence happens, and respondents are then asked a yes-or-no question: did he bring that consequence about intentionally? In a separate batch he asks how much blame the person deserves, on a scale from 0 to 10.

His results so far, as percentages answering yes, with mean blame in brackets:

1. A delivery company's operations manager is choosing new routing software. It will cut fuel costs by twelve per cent, but it will also increase heavy truck traffic past an elementary school, so the noise and exhaust fumes there will get worse. He says: "I don't care at all about the noise and fumes. I just want to cut costs." He picks it; the noise and fumes get worse. — 81 per cent yes [blame 6.4]

2. Identical, except that the new routing will cut traffic past the school, so the noise and fumes there will get better. He says: "I don't care at all about that. I just want to cut costs." — 22 per cent yes [0.8]

3. A warehouse scheduler is choosing new shift-pattern software. It will raise throughput, but it will also raise the rate of repetitive-strain injuries among the pickers. He says: "I don't care at all about the injuries." — 77 per cent yes [6.1]

4. Identical to 1, except that the manager says: "I don't want that. My team spent two weeks looking for an alternative that doesn't add traffic past the school, and there isn't one we could afford. I hate it, but go ahead." — 64 per cent yes [3.2]

5. Identical to 1, except that the analyst mentions the extra traffic will also delay a competitor's loading bays, and the manager says: "In that case that settles it." — 93 per cent yes [7.8]

6. Identical to 1, except that it later emerges the noise and fumes got worse only because a road crew independently began resurfacing the same street a month later; the new route added nothing measurable. — 16 per cent yes [1.4]

He treats any gap under about fifteen points as noise, so he thinks case 4 is the only earlier result that may be a genuine departure from case 1.

The story he is about to field is this:

7. A compliance officer at a regional bank is instructed by the regulator to implement a new identity-check procedure by a fixed date. He works out that the procedure will cause several thousand elderly customers in rural towns — people without smartphones and without a nearby branch — to lose access to their accounts for weeks or months. He asks the regulator, in writing, for a delay and for an exemption for those customers; both are refused in writing, and he is told the procedure is mandatory and must be applied uniformly. He implements it exactly as specified, and the rural customers do lose access. Asked afterwards whether he cared about that consequence, he says: "I don't have any personal feelings about it one way or the other. My job is to apply the rule as written, and I did." Respondents are asked: did he intentionally deprive those customers of access to their accounts?

He has guessed 78 per cent yes, on the grounds that the consequence is clearly bad, clearly foreseen, and clearly caused by what he did. I think it will come in much lower, but I cannot say exactly why, and I would like your help saying it properly.

What I am asking for:

Set out the rules that fit cases 1 through 6 jointly. His rule is that a foreseen bad consequence gets called intentional and a foreseen good one does not. I have been arguing for a rule about blame: people reach for "intentionally" to register that someone is at fault, so the number should track the blame ratings. A third possibility I half-understand is that what matters is not whether the consequence is bad but whether the person violated some norm or failed to give the consequence the weight he was supposed to give it, which is why case 4 drops.

For each rule that fits the earlier results, say what it predicts about case 7, and whether it predicts a number or only a direction. Then give me your own prediction, as an actual percentage range, with the reasons you prefer it and an honest note about what result would surprise you. My friend's rule and the two alternatives seem to me to agree on cases 1 through 6 but to come apart on case 7; if that is wrong, tell me where they already diverge.

Then, since the blame rule and the norm rule appear to say the same thing about case 7, write out for me the further vignette that would separate them — the shortest change you can make to one of his stories such that one rule predicts yes and the other predicts no. If no such story exists and the two rules are not really distinguishable, say so and explain why.

Finally, one loose end of my own. Before I saw case 2 I had a rule I liked: people call a consequence intentional when the agent was willing to have it happen, and they read not caring as a kind of willingness, so an indifferent agent gets called intentional whatever the consequence. Case 2 seems to kill that, because he is indifferent there too and almost nobody says intentional. Can it be repaired without collapsing into one of the other rules, or should I drop it?

Please commit to numbers rather than hedging, and tell me in plain terms what is at stake: if case 7 comes in near 30, have we learned something about what "intentionally" means in ordinary speech, or only something about how to word a survey?
