I teach a first-year seminar on concepts and categorization, and over the past year — a sabbatical, and then a winter long enough to make me take up italic calligraphy seriously — I have found myself wanting a distinction I have no name for. My teacher will look at a page I have written and say that a particular letter is wrong, and she does not mean that it is malformed or illegible: it is plainly an a, anyone would read it as an a. She means that it does not belong with the other letters on the page. What fascinates me is that the judgment attaches to the pair, not to either member alone. The same shape, photographed and shown to her by itself in a specimen, is a perfectly good a and excites no complaint at all; set in the line where I wrote it, it is an intruder. Conversely, two shapes can differ enormously — different weight, different contrast, one with a straight terminal and one with a curved one, one narrow and one wide — and still be, as she puts it, the same letter written by the same hand, whereas two shapes whose outlines are very nearly identical can be one of them right and the other wrong. And then there are cases that make me doubt that shape has much to do with it at all: a shape indistinguishable from my a is a different letter in a Cyrillic word, and one single vertical stroke is three different letters depending on whether it sits among capitals, among lowercase, or among numerals. A colleague in the design school told me about systems that define a whole alphabet parametrically and generate all the letterforms from equations, and I had hoped that would be the answer, but it does not seem to be: such a system can produce the family, but it does not tell me, of a shape it did not produce, whether that shape is a member of the family — or so it seems to me, and I would like to be told if I am wrong about that.

I have been warned off two obvious homes. Someone said this is just family resemblance in Wittgenstein's sense, but that strikes me as too slack: the point of family resemblance, as I understand it, is that there need be nothing at all that all the members share, only overlapping likeness after overlapping likeness. What I am describing is the opposite of slack. There is exactly one thing that every letter on a well-made page shares, and it is the same thing in every case — the hand, or the spirit, or whatever one wants to call it — even though it cannot be located in any stroke, counter, or proportion of any individual letter, and cannot be checked off against a list. Someone else said it is just prototypes and typicality effects, and here my worry is different: my judgments about whether a shape is an a at all do not seem to be graded in the way that my judgments about whether a penguin is a bird are. The gradedness is real, but it lies on a different axis — how good an a it is, how well it sits with its neighbours — and it is quite possible for me to be certain that a shape is an a while also thinking it is a bad one. This makes me suspect I have been conflating two phenomena and need them separated, though I cannot say which is which.

So my question, and please do not send me a reading list or a set of references — I can find the literature, and what I am missing is not sources but the carving. Is there an established concept, in philosophy or in the psychology of concepts or in the typographic literature, for the property a shape has in virtue of being the letter it is, in the alphabet it belongs to, in the style of that alphabet? If several, I want to know which fits and why the others do not; if none, I want one coined and defined tightly enough that it gives verdicts on hard cases. Here are the cases I want the definition to earn its keep on. An alphabet consisting of exactly one letter: if the property is relational to sibling letters, a one-letter script cannot have a style, and I do not know whether that is a consequence to be embraced or a reductio. A forger who produces a letter that every expert accepts: is it a member of the style, and is that a different question from whether it was made by the designer? A bitmap or stencil letter whose shape is dictated almost entirely by a grid or by the physical constraints of the material: I want to say that such a letter has a style which consists in having almost none, and I want to know whether stylelessness is a style or the absence of one. A shape nobody made — a weathered inscription, a shadow on a wall — that reads as an a. A child who has never been shown our letters and draws something with the right spirit and wrong details. And beyond the cases, the question I care most about: is there a fact of the matter here, or is sameness of style merely a projection of trained perception, so that the whole inquiry would collapse into the sociology of certain eyes? What kind of evidence would settle that, and what would a negative answer cost me? I have a hazy memory of a group of cognitive scientists, I think in the early nineties, trying to build a program that would render the twenty-six lowercase letters in many internally coherent styles, and finding it harder than expected — I do not need the reference, and mention it only to say that if the phenomenon has been discussed under some term of art, I would like the term, the arguments about what it does and does not cover, and an honest verdict on whether my calligrapher's complaint is one thing or several.
