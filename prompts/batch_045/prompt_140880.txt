Ok so our lab makes a fake welcome booklet for each incoming cohort of PhD students, and this year someone talked us into doing it as a spoof wellness-retreat brochure. I somehow volunteered to write the part that's actaully about the science. Can you do it for me?

The setup: a luxury silent retreat called The Null Room, marketed at people who have read a pop-science version of predictive processing and taken it completely literally. If the brain is a prediction machine whose whole job is to minimize prediction error / free energy, the rational move is obviously to put yourself in the smallest, darkest, quietest, most predictable room you can afford and stay there. The brochure is 100% sincere about this. It sells the dark room as a premium experience, and every worry a guest might raise gets answered, in the brochure's own voice, with exactly the move a real predictive-processing person would make:

- prior preferences: your generative model predicts that you are the sort of creature who eats at seven and reads in good light, so a dark room is not obviously what your model is minimizing toward at all — the brochure has to handle this, and it does, by charging extra
- precision weighting: you can't just switch prediction off, you can only downweight the errors, and that should be something you can buy
- epistemic value and expected free energy: the optional day excursion where you go and look at something specifically because resolving uncertainty about it is worth something
- the gap between surprise in the technical sense and variational free energy: the fine print should quietly admit the retreat cannot evaluate your surprise, only the bound on it, and that this is fine
- the Markov blanket: an architecture section about how the suites are blanket-separated from the world, with all causal traffic routed through sensory and active states, and about the passive-sensing-only wing they're very proud of
- active inference: the moment where the brochure explains that you can either move your eyes or change your mind about what you were seeing, and that a stay here is essentially a subscription to the second option

The comedy has to come from getting all of this right, so I need it to be fair, not a strawman. It should never say anything about the view that someone who actually holds it would disown — it should just take real commitments about two steps too far. The dark room is a genuine reductio and I want the brochure to follow it cheerfully all the way down, including the degenerate endpoint where the guest stops being an agent in any interesting sense (maybe that's the top-tier package); that's the one place where I want the reader to flinch rather than laugh. And I want at least one joke that lives down in the formalism rather than at the level of vibes — something about the bound being computable while the quantity it bounds isn't, or about ergodic assumptions being smuggled in, or about how minimizing expected free energy over a future is just not the same thing as minimizing error right now, so the do-nothing-forever reading is a confusion before it's a conclusion. Your pick, but make it a real one.

Also please include one straight-faced testimonial from a guest whose priors were badly miscalibrated in a specific direction, and have the brochure offer a precision re-tuning service for it — precision is the concept I most want doing comic work.

Somewhere around 700-900 words, formatted like an actual brochure: a hero pitch up top, three or four named packages with prices, a sidebar FAQ, a couple of testimonials, and a block of fine print at the end. Keep it deadpan the whole way — no winking, nobody breaking character, and a reader should still be able to tell what's being sent up. Don't bother with citations or a further reading list, I know the literature, I just want the bit I have to read out loud at the welcome drinks. Casual writing is good, typos and all.
