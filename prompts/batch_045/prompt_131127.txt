I moderate a fairly large online community — volunteer work, no training in philosophy or law — and after yet another argument about where the line on speech should be drawn I started reading about Mill's harm principle on my own: the claim that stopping someone from harming another person is the only thing that justifies forcing them to do what they'd rather not. Everyone seems to accept the slogan and then disagree about every actual case, so before I read any further I want the specification rather than another tour of the positions. My purpose is fixed and I should say it up front: I need a principle that could be written into a real moderation policy, applied by whoever happens to be holding the pen that week including people who actively dislike the speaker, and applied without anyone being able to establish what would have happened if the post had never gone up. So please don't give me reading recommendations or point me at books; I have no library access and I want the argument itself. And if a requirement only makes sense for a principle used to morally criticise speech rather than to remove it, flag it as such instead of quietly dropping it.

Here is the inventory of situations I keep running into, which I take it any candidate principle has to say something about, and I would rather you reorganised this list than answered it item by item: something is said that revolts nearly everyone but changes nothing about anyone's prospects; the real damage is done later by a third party who voluntarily acted on what was said and who would probably have found some other excuse; a single utterance does nothing at all, but a million of them make a place unwelcoming to a group, so the harm seems to live in the practice rather than in any instance; a damaging false statement is filed where no human being will ever read it, and yet an automated system nobody can explain starts rejecting the subject's applications, so their prospects genuinely worsen with no mind ever registering the content; after the speech exists, the target has to spend money and weeks of effort guarding against what it makes likely, even though nobody downstream does anything wrong; after the utterance the target can still say everything they could say before, but it will now be heard differently and cost them more to say it, so they have lost nothing they possessed and yet have fewer live options; the text is harmless where it is written and predictably damaging where it will be read, and the speaker has no control over the spill; the speech is damaging only because the audience already believes something false which the speaker did not create but knowingly leans on; and finally the rule itself, which will be enforced by people with sympathies of their own, whose mere process of deciding is already a burden on speech, and which will sometimes be wrong about whether there was any harm. For each, tell me what it is a requirement *of* — which distinction it forces the principle to respect, which phenomenon it is the test for — and then do the sorting work I can't do: merge the ones that are secretly the same requirement, split the ones that are really two, and tell me which of them are genuine desiderata and which are only prejudices of one camp. I suspect things like harm has to be tangible or physical and the rule must never look at content are both factional preferences rather than requirements, but I'd like your reasons rather than my guesses back.

The part I most want is the tension. Tell me which pairs of requirements you think cannot be jointly satisfied — my own guess is that a rule tight enough to be applied by unsympathetic officials without discretion cannot also be a rule that catches harms of the cumulative kind or harms with no audience, and if that's right then the unsatisfiability is itself the interesting result and I want it stated plainly, along with which single requirement knocks out the most candidate principles. Only after that, and briefly, score the obvious contenders against the list you've built: the bare Mill line, adding a separate category for offense, views that appeal to dignity or to equality, views that appeal to the health of public discourse or of democracy, and the answer that says the remedy for bad speech is more speech. Finish by telling me which one requirement you would drop if you had to, and what breaks when it goes.
