I chair the acquisitions and collections committee of a regional museum: roughly 40,000 objects, fine and decorative arts, a small holding of material from living craft traditions, and an archive. We meet monthly and handle eight to twelve objects a sitting at about twenty minutes each — offers, bequests, and objects already in the collection whose catalogue record has been challenged. We have no conservator on staff. We can buy one outside technical opinion per object, and only when its insured value is above $8,000; anything with a possible illegal-export history goes to our counsel; deaccessioning needs the full board. Every decision has to be defensible on a single page to the board, to the donor or the donor's family, and to our accrediting body. My committee is seven people: two curators and five lay trustees.

Two things have just changed. First, the museum has adopted a collections policy that describes our purpose as preserving and enabling encounters with objects as evidence of the human past, and that explicitly declines to vouch for the authorship of anything we display. Second, a donor has offered to endow a small acquisitions line for deliberate imitations and outright fakes, to be shown as such — a cabinet of the kind we have never had and for which we have no rules. So the procedure has to work in both directions: what we do with an object whose claim to be something is in doubt, and what we do with an object we already know is not what it was said to be.

What I want from you is a written procedure my committee can actually run, and I want it grounded in what aesthetics has worked out about the value of originals rather than in customs-of-the-trade platitudes. I know the standard material and don't need it explained to me: the distinction between arts in which any exact copy counts as a genuine instance of the work and arts in which it does not; the thought experiment of the visually and physically indistinguishable forgery, and the argument that what such an object lacks is a history rather than a look; the rival argument that the difference is merely informational or economic and that nothing of aesthetic relevance is lost; and the skeptical line that authentic is just what our records happen to say. Use these; don't re-teach them. I don't want a survey of celebrated forgery scandals, and I don't want a reading list.

The procedure needs: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the fixed tier vocabulary we assign (by; attributed to; studio or workshop of; after; imitation; forgery by a named maker where known; of uncertain origin), with who has authority to sign off on each tier; the points at which it terminates in a no, and which no — decline the gift, return the object, reclassify and retain, deaccession; the points at which it stops and hands the decision to a named person or to the board because the question is one of values and not of fact; and the default to apply when the evidence is unavailable, inconclusive, or simply unaffordable, including when a default comes up for review again. It should fit on two pages, be runnable in twenty minutes by a non-specialist, and produce a one-page record. Build in the branch for works whose identity does not run through the maker's hand, and show that the branch is not empty by running it on case D. Tell me which distinctions you had to keep in order to stay faithful to the theory, which you dropped to make it usable by five trustees with twenty minutes, and — this matters most — where the procedure quietly encodes a philosophical choice that a reasonable person would contest: name each of those, and say which you would defend and why.

Then run it in full on three objects, and briefly on the fourth.

A. A small oil landscape on canvas, 46 by 61 centimetres, bought in 1974 for very little from a local dealer and catalogued ever since as by a woman painter, dead since the 1960s, with a thin catalogue raisonné and a following in two other regional collections. In 2023 we paid for infrared reflectography and one paint sample. The signature sits in an area where the varnish was abraded and renewed; the craquelure over the signature is finer and less continuous than that of the surrounding paint; ground, canvas and pigments are consistent with materials available from the 1920s to the 1950s and inconsistent with anything later. No alternative author has been proposed. The 1974 invoice describes the picture as by her and records no earlier owner. The handling is close to hers, but hers is not distinctive enough to identify a painting on style alone; our only comparanda are two securely documented works by her in our own collection.

B. A bronze statuette of a classical subject, bought in 1911 as a Roman antiquity and exhibited as one ever since. Technical work in 2021 established that the alloy and the casting method are nineteenth-century, and that the piece appears in an 1887 founder's catalogue as a reproduction of a Roman type in another museum, sold openly as a copy and stamped with the founder's mark. The area of the stamp has been tooled away; the tooling is old but not datable to a decade. As an object it is well cast, 140 years old, the product of a workshop whose output is itself collected, and it is far more legible to our visitors than the worn genuine Roman fragment displayed beside it.

C. A painting offered as a gift. It was made in 2016 by a living artist in the manner of a modernist who died in the 1960s, placed on the market through a dealer as a newly rediscovered work of 1948, sold for a large sum, and later exposed. The maker was convicted of fraud, now exhibits under his own name, and the imitations from that period are collected as his work and sell for serious money. The defrauded buyer has offered it to us on condition that we display it, and wants it shown as a warning about the market. The donor is not asking us to call it a 1948 rediscovery; the disagreement in the room is about what the object is now and what we should be willing to say on the wall next to it.

D. A portfolio of ten etchings printed in 2019 from the artist's own plates by the printer who had worked with her for twenty years, in an edition she had planned and for which she had signed a maquette, but which she died before printing; the plates were cancelled afterwards. The estate maintains that impressions printed after her death are not by her.

Give me, for each case, a tier and a disposition — not a menu of options. I expect at least one of them to come out somewhere my committee will dislike; say so where it happens and don't soften the answer to spare us. Say plainly where the procedure will misfire: which kinds of object, which kinds of maker and which kinds of record it is systematically unfair to; whether the tier vocabulary is really a device for laundering doubt into a value; and what it cannot settle at any price.

Be committed. Take a definite position on every case and on every design choice, and give your reasons. Mark as you go which steps follow from the philosophical material and which are your own judgment — I want to be able to tell the two apart — and tell me where you think the literature is genuinely unsettled and you are guessing.
