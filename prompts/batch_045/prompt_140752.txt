I sit on the grant committee of a small foundation that has historically funded near-term global health and poverty interventions, and we are now being asked to move a substantial share of the portfolio into longtermist work — reducing extinction risk, steering the development of transformative technologies, and preserving option value for future generations. The argument that has most persuaded me is a version of strong longtermism: the expected amount of value at stake in the far future is astronomically larger than anything at stake in the near term; small probabilities of favourably influencing that future therefore swamp ordinary near-term goods in expected value; so across a wide range of decision contexts the option we ought to pick is the one with the best long-run consequences. Before I vote I want the case against it laid out as a map of the view's exposure, not as a catalogue of objections from people who never found it attractive.

A few of the doubts I keep circling are probably not the standard ones, and I would like them engaged on their merits rather than matched to something in the literature. First, causal attenuation: influence over the state of the world plausibly diffuses as it passes through successive branch points — wars, institutional collapses, technological paths, cultural shifts — so that the probability that any given present intervention still makes a difference to how things go many centuries out may decay at something like a fixed rate per branch point. If the payoff is astronomical but the chance of still mattering at the relevant distance is correspondingly infinitesimal, the product may be unremarkable, and I want to know what shape that decay function would have to have for the argument to survive. Second, control-shifting without value-shifting: conditional on humanity's surviving at all, maybe the broad character of the far future is set by physical limits and by convergent competitive pressures, so that what our actions change is who runs the future rather than how well it goes — in which case the value at stake is real but not something we can move. Third, sign symmetry: if the distribution over the value of the far future has heavy tails in both directions, and if the bad tail is dominated by the possibility of vast locked-in suffering while the good tail is dominated by the creation of vast numbers of good lives, then under an asymmetric population ethics the priority ranking flips, and the portfolio I am being asked to approve may be the wrong one even if the axiological premise is granted. Relatedly, if both tails diverge, the expected value of the far future is not merely hard to estimate but mathematically undefined, and the injunction to maximise it is not well formed. Fourth, timing rather than occurrence: much extinction-risk work may shift the date of a catastrophe rather than its probability of ever happening, and I want to know what the relevant hazard-rate or multipolar-scenario claim is and how exposed it is. Fifth, near-term work has long-run effects too — via growth, institutional quality, demographic transitions — so even granting that most of the value lies in the future, the instruction to pivot away from near-term giving may not follow.

So: ranked from fatal to merely awkward, what would have to be true for strong longtermism to be false, and what is the difference between the two? Which of these defeaters would a committed defender concede as genuinely counting against the view, which would they absorb by hedging, and which would they rightly dismiss? Does the argument that the far future holds most of the value actually deliver the practical prescription I am voting on, or does it collapse into an impartial consequentialism that adds nothing? Has the view been qualified — about which decision contexts, about the sign of our influence, about tractability — to the point where no observation could tell against it, and if so, is that a defect here in a way it would not be in, say, physics? I will say what would move me most: empirical findings about the persistence of human influence over centuries, and about whether risk reduction changes whether civilisational collapse happens or only when.
