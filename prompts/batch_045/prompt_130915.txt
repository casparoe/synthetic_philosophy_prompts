ive been asked to give the short talk at the dedication of the new banding lab at the field station in Cutler, Maine — they're naming it after Rafe Halloran, who ran the puffin census with me for forty-one years and who died in March. I retired from fisheries biology a few years back and have only got into philosophy since, so I know just enough to be dangerous, and I keep tripping over one distinction that I think is doing all the work in what I'm allowed to say up there.

The situation. We had two colonies: the Nubble, in cold water with good forage fish, and Outer Double Head, which sits in the warm, poor water. Fledging mass at the Nubble averages about 340 g; at Outer Double Head about 260 g. Within each colony, mid-parent regression on fledging mass gives a slope of roughly 0.6 — a solid, repeatable, high heritability, and that's Rafe's number, measured off his own forty years of bands and scales. Rafe was convinced the gap between the colonies was genetic, locally adapted, and he wanted to translocate chicks from the Nubble to build up the Outer Double Head birds — "improve the stock," he'd say. I thought it was the food. The season he died we finally did the cross-fostering, swapping eggs between the colonies: eggs from Outer Double Head parents raised by Nubble foster parents at the Nubble fledged at about 335 g, and Nubble eggs raised at Outer Double Head came off at about 265 g. The gap followed the water, not the genes.

So I need a seven-minute talk, no slides, maybe 900-1000 words, that does three things pulling against each other. It has to genuinely honour Rafe's forty-one years of measuring — the only reason the test was possible is that he never skipped a season, and I want the room to feel that. It has to not let anyone walk away thinking the colony gap is genetic or that translocation is a good idea, because his son Kevin has started telling people in town that his father has now been vindicated and that "heritable" means the difference is in the blood. And it must not turn into a debunking of heritability either: that 0.6 is real, it's his, and I don't want the grad students in the back concluding that the genetics doesn't matter or that mass is just environmental. There's a fourth thing, too: Rafe's other slogan was "if it's heritable it isn't fixable," and I'd like to quietly bury that one in a way a room of non-scientists actually gets, without it sounding like I'm correcting a dead man.

One more piece I'd like to use if you can make it work: at Outer Double Head the same estimate has slid to about 0.1 over the last decade, since the water warmed and the forage fish went and the chicks got wildly variable in what they were fed. I'd like that number to do something in the speech rather than sound like a statistics lecture — the point being that the estimate is a ratio belonging to a population in an environment, not a property of the trait.

Half the room is his widow Mary, Kevin, the harbormaster, the selectmen, the summer people; the other half is seabird folks and two quantitative-genetics postdocs who will absolutely be listening for whether I get the inference right. Plain spoken, a bit funny, concrete — guano, the hired skiff, the rain — and no jargon except where I translate it in the same breath. Please start right on the first line of the talk: no "here's a draft," no commentary before or after, and nothing I'd have to delete before saying out loud.
