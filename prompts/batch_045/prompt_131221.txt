Hey — I'm in a PPE-ish seminar this term and we're on this week's topic, "safe Pareto improvements." My tutor's gloss, not a real definition: a change that leaves everyone better off by their own lights, and that doesn't smuggle in some huge downside nobody counted. I've been going case by case and I honestly cant tell whether my gut reactions add up to anything, so here they are.

- Free flu shots at work, paid for out of a budget line nobody wanted anyway, nobody worse off: obviously fine.
- Everyone alive is made better off by their own lights, but because it changes commuting patterns, a completely different set of people exist in 150 years (all with lives worth living): fine. I don't feel I owe anything to people who only exist because of the policy.
- Winners could in principle compensate the losers, and the sums work out, but the government just never actually pays: not fine. That's not a Pareto improvement to me, it's a Kaldor-Hicks thing with better PR.
- Everyone benefits in expectation, there's an unavoidable lottery element, and one town ends up worse off after the fact: fine. I care about ex ante, not ex post.
- Everyone alive gets a solid benefit, at the cost of a really tiny increase in the chance humanity dies out early — say one in ten million extra risk, cleanly estimated: fine. I feel a bit squeamish but I say yes.
- Same present benefit, same tiny probability, but the downside is a permanent lock-in: an unchangeable global regime with awful values that lasts as long as humanity does: not fine. I know 5 and 6 are the same shape and that's exactly what I keep going back and forth on.
- Everyone alive benefits, and the people living 200 years from now are well off by their own lights, but the policy slowly shifts what people care about, so they'd look back and call the shift a loss. My tutor calls these "stealth losses" — losses that land on values nobody currently holds, so nobody alive is in a position to object. I say not fine, but I worry that's just snobbery about which values count.

My own attempt at a rule was: everyone who can actually be consulted has to benefit by their own lights, and expected value of the long-run future has to go up. But that seems to give me "no" on 6 and also "no" on 5, and I don't want to give up 5; and I can't see how it lets me say yes on 2 either. Is there a principle that gets most of these, which of my verdicts does it force me to drop, and what does it say about the one I can't settle — an AI administration that surveys everyone alive, gives them exactly what they asked for, and lets nobody review the plan? Also please answer in British English, and keep the jargon light-ish, I have to present this on Thursday.
