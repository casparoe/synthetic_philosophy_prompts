I have been reading a series of posts by a political theorist who blogs about rational choice and public policy, and one of them applies Harsanyi's aggregation theorem to hospital rationing. I want to use the argument in something I am writing, but I cannot tell how much of the post is the theorem and how much is the author editorializing. Here is the passage, lightly compressed but in his words:

> Harsanyi's aggregation theorem is the closest thing we have to a proof of utilitarianism. Take any group facing a choice among social options — a hospital committee deciding how to allocate a single remaining intensive-care bed, say. Assume only two things: that each person's preferences satisfy the axioms of expected utility theory, and that the group respects a bare Pareto principle, namely that if every individual is indifferent between two options then the group is indifferent too. Given those, the theorem shows that the group's own preferences must be representable by expected-utility maximization, and that the group's utility function is the sum of the individual utilities, with each person counted once and with equal weight. The committee must therefore rank policies by total patient benefit: more QALYs is better, and nothing else about the distribution matters directly. Two consequences are worth stating plainly. First, the committee must be indifferent between assigning the bed to a named patient for sure and running a lottery that gives each of two equally needy patients a fifty percent chance, since the expected total is the same either way; a committee that randomizes "out of fairness" is making an error of consistency, not expressing a rival value. Second, Rawls's difference principle is refuted by the result, because maximizing the prospects of the worst off is simply extreme risk aversion about which person you turn out to be, and risk aversion of that kind is exactly what expected-utility maximization rules out. Once you grant Pareto, utilitarianism is not a moral opinion competing with others; it is a requirement of rationality.

My instinct is that the fairness consequence is too quick, and a friend who knows more social choice than I do says the equal-weight claim cannot even be stated without a prior convention for making the individual utility scales comparable, since each person's utility function is defined only up to a positive affine transformation. But the indifference claim does seem to fall straight out of an unweighted sum, and I would like to know whether that part survives.

Please audit the passage claim by claim, telling me what someone who accepts Harsanyi's framework — the aggregation theorem together with the impartial-observer or equiprobability argument — would endorse as stated, what they would accept as a simplification, and what they would correct. More specifically:

1. Is the list of premises complete and are they correctly described? In particular, does the theorem derive the claim that the group's preferences are representable by expected-utility maximization, or does it assume it, and how much of the conclusion rests on that assumption?
2. Does the equal-weight conclusion belong to the aggregation theorem itself, or does it require a separate premise? If a separate premise, state it as precisely as you can, say whether it is a moral or an epistemic assumption, and say what the theorem still permits if it is not granted — including whether every person is guaranteed a positive weight, and whether the answer depends on what options the group's choice problem happens to contain.
3. Is my friend's point about affine transformations a real problem for the passage or a technicality? Note that the hospital example invites a natural but substantive convention, namely fixing each patient's utility for death at zero and for survival at one; say what that convention does and does not buy.
4. Does substituting QALYs for utilities change what the theorem delivers? Is the "more QALYs is better, and nothing else about the distribution matters" gloss something the theorem supports, and does it matter that the committee faces a fixed set of patients, so that summing and averaging order the options identically?
5. For the indifference consequence: under what assumptions does it actually follow, and is the author right that a committee which prefers the lottery is thereby being inconsistent rather than appealing to a different value? Give the strongest form of the opposing view, and say whether it attacks the theorem's assumptions or only its conclusion.
6. Is the Rawls consequence fair? Distinguish, if the distinction matters, between the claim that maximin is incompatible with expected-utility maximization under equiprobability and the claim that the difference principle has been refuted, and say what a defender of the difference principle would need to reject.
7. Finally, two practical questions. If you were rewriting the passage for hospital ethicists — competent, unsentimental, not specialists in social choice — what is the shortest version that stays true to the result? And what is the strongest claim the post could defensibly make about this committee's actual choice, given that the patients' attitudes to risk are unknown, that some of the patients have not yet arrived, and that the committee is selecting a policy rather than a patient?

I am not asking for references, reading suggestions, or citations; I want the assessment itself, and I would rather you flag where you are uncertain than fill a gap with an authority.
