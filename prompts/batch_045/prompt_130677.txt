And here is the student's answer, reproduced as written except that I fixed one spelling error:

Kant's essay is a reply to Constant, who had mocked the whole idea by saying that on this kind of principle you would have to tell a murderer the truth about where his intended victim is hiding. Kant bites the bullet: truthfulness is an unconditional duty, and you may not lie even to save your friend's life, and even if the lie would harm nobody. And since Kant's topic here is right (Recht) rather than virtue, the prohibition isn't prudent advice or a counsel of perfection — it's a strict duty that admits of no exception.

The reason lying is wrong is the Formula of Humanity: to lie to someone is to treat them as a mere means, manipulating their beliefs instead of respecting them as a rational agent who should form his beliefs on the basis of the truth. This is also why the murderer's own wickedness is beside the point. The wrongness of deception is a wrong to the person who is deceived, and that wrong is the same whoever he is and whatever he plans to do with the information.

When Kant calls truthfulness a merely formal condition of right, he means that it is a formal requirement — that is, a legalistic one: the condition that has to hold in official settings like contracts, oaths and testimony, where the law needs reliable statements to work with. It adds nothing to the substantive content of what we owe each other in ordinary life. That is why the lie in this case isn't really a violation of anyone's right in particular, and why Kant has to fall back on calling it a wrong to humanity as such — which really just means a moral fault with no determinate victim and no rights-bearer.

Kant then adds the case where your friend has actually slipped out the back, so that the murderer, acting on your lie, runs into him in the street and kills him: there you are legally accountable for the death and can be punished as its cause. That example shows that even Kant's argument comes down to consequences in the end — a lie is wrong because of what it can bring about and because you can be held liable for it. So the "unconditional" duty is less cut off from consequences than it is usually made out to be.

On the variants: all three are covered. (i) Silence, in a context where you know he will take it as confirmation, is deception by another name. (ii) The literally true remark is covered too, since it is engineered to produce a false belief. (iii) Blocking the door while saying you won't say is also a deceptive performance. What matters for Kant is not the content of what you assert but what you intend the hearer to conclude: the ban is really a ban on deceiving and on withholding, not merely on false assertion. So Kant does require you to answer the murderer truthfully — which is exactly Constant's objection, and Kant accepts it.
