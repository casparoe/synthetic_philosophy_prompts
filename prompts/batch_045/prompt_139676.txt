I am writing a problem set for an upper-level seminar on theories of causation, and I need one exercise that runs against the grain of the examples I already use. In my current notes, every case in which a Lewis-style closest-world counterfactual analysis and a Woodward-style interventionist analysis come apart leaves the interventionist looking better: symmetric overdetermination, where the simple dependence test fails but interventionism still delivers the intuitive verdict; or an in-principle unmanipulable cause, where the counterfactualist seems committed to claims no possible experiment could reach. I want a case with the opposite moral, so that students see interventionism's extra structure — exogenous intervention variables, surgical isolation of the cause, invariance under a range of interventions — as a liability it can sometimes incur, not only as an upgrade.

Construct one scenario, set in a hospital, in which a state or event C produces an outcome E by a mechanism a clinician could specify, such that:

1. A closest-world counterfactual analysis returns a determinate yes — C caused E — where the truth of the relevant counterfactual does not hinge on a contestable judgment about which world is most similar, and where the backtracking reading is not the closest one.
2. An interventionist analysis does not return that verdict: it either denies that C caused E or returns no verdict at all.

Constraints on the design:

- One paragraph, no more than about 150 words, for the scenario itself. Realistic medicine and ordinary hospital resources. No duplicate mechanisms, no backups, no preemption or overdetermination structures, and no cause that is unmanipulable in principle — the cause should be perfectly settable in the abstract, and the problem should arise from how it can be set in this scenario.
- The divergence must be generated by a single structural feature: in this scenario every physically possible route into C passes through one gateway which also has its own separate, direct effect on E, so that any intervention on C is fat-handed. Make the inseparability nomological rather than merely technological, so that no redesign of the experiment, and no move to a merely possible rather than actual intervention route, gets around it. Close off, in one clause each, the two obvious escape routes: trying a different intervention, and testing the relation by moving C in the opposite direction.
- Both verdicts must fall out of the theories rather than be written into the case. If I can substitute a different mechanism for yours and read off the same two verdicts, the case has failed.

Deliverables, in this order:

a. The scenario, with every piece of physiology that is a stipulation of the case rather than established medicine explicitly flagged as such.
b. The counterfactual verdict: state the counterfactual, identify the closest world, say why it is closer than the alternatives, and draw the conclusion.
c. The interventionist verdict: state the conditions you are imposing on an intervention, list the candidate interventions available in the scenario, show for each which condition fails, and then say honestly whether the account's output here is no, or no verdict available, and whether the failure is metaphysical or merely evidential.
d. One sentence naming the feature doing the work, and one sentence on what happens to the verdicts if that feature is removed.
e. The strongest reply available to the interventionist, with the counterfactualist's best answer to it. If the strongest reply is to allow conceivable but nomologically impossible interventions, say what is then left of the disagreement and whether that reply sits comfortably with the point of the framework.
f. One closing line on what a student who answers "interventionism is simply the more demanding, more fine-grained theory" has failed to see.

Keep the whole thing under about 800 words. Apply the two accounts rather than surveying them: I do not want citations, quotations, or suggestions for further reading, only the machinery working on the case.
