I run training for new volunteers on a crisis line, and we just had a rough review: a couple of them blurted out 'I promise' to callers in ways they hadn't thought through — one promised she'd be on shift next week, another promised a caller that nothing he said would leave the room. So I've got 35 minutes next month on what a promise actually does, and I want a plan, not the theory dumped on me. What I'm stuck on is the order: I think they need to see, before anything else, that the question isn't what happens if you break a promise but what makes the obligation exist at all, and that promising isn't the same as predicting your own behavior or resolving to do something. Which example should carry the whole session for people who've never done philosophy? My instinct is a case where nobody relies on the promise and nobody could punish them for breaking it — an anonymous caller, a promise on a train to someone you'll never see again — but I worry that case hides the thing I want them to get, since it makes promising look like pure self-binding. I also want them to hit, at the right moment, promising to do what you already had to do anyway, like promising to repay money you already owe, because that's where it should click whether the promise adds something or just lights up something already there. What will they get wrong? I'm expecting some version of 'promises bind because otherwise people get hurt', 'if they can't hold you to it you're free', and 'keeping a promise always means doing literally what you said'. Where in the sequence do I kill each one? Give me one question for the end that tells me it landed, and tell me what I should flatly refuse to get into in 35 minutes.
