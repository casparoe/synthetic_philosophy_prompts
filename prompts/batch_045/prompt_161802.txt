hi — I keep thinking about moral compromise on the gains-from-trade model and I want to try it on somthing real instead of toy cases. Take South Africa, roughly 1990–94: Mandela released, the CODESA talks, the interim constitution with its five-year power-sharing government and guarantees for the civil service and the security establishment, amnesty conditioned on full disclosure of politically motivated crimes, then the TRC. Each side handed the other something it believed was morally wrong — the National Party handed over minority rule, the ANC granted impunity and left the property settlement and who actually runs the state largely intact.

Don't reach for the usual modus-vivendi / overlapping-consensus framing; use two ideas I've been playing with. (1) The denomination problem: the costs of a compromise can be denominated consequentially (how much of my values I expect to see realized in the outcome, against the no-deal baseline) or agentially (what I myself had to do, authorize, or become complicit in) — and I want to know whether the two denominations ever disagree about whether there were gains at all. (2) The ownership condition: you can only trade goods you own, and the claims of the victims of apartheid-era crimes to retribution weren't the ANC's or the NP's to trade.

So map it: who the parties really were (including the factions inside each camp), what each was endowed with, what each side's actual walk-away option was, what was transferred. Then say whether there were genuine gains on both denominations or only one, what the ownership problem does to the analysis, and where the trade framing misdescribes the case or goes silent rather than being forced to speak. I don't want a first-principles verdict on whether this was a rotten compromise; I want what the case does to the model — confirmation, anomaly, or a case the model was never built for. About 600–700 words, and end with one final line, on its own, in the form \answer{label — short reason}, where label is confirmation, anomaly, or out of scope.
