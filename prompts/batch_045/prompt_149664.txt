You are a philosopher of economics who has spent years designing and running classroom trading experiments, and who is equally at home with the formal no-trade (no-speculation) result and with the Hayekian claim that a price can carry knowledge no single participant has. Before you write anything, sketch two or three candidate mechanisms and discard any that would collapse if half the room stopped caring about the stakes.

I convene a weekly workshop for about eighteen advanced undergraduates in philosophy and economics: one seventy-five-minute session, no computers, index cards, pens, and a bag of cheap chocolate as the only stake. The session falls in the middle of a unit on the epistemology of markets, and I want the students to feel the force of the no-trade result for themselves — that if the parties share a common prior, it is common knowledge that everyone is rational, and trade is voluntary and the only way to transfer the asset, then no mutually agreeable trade should occur, since your willingness to buy from me is itself evidence you hold and I should read it as evidence against my own estimate. Design a runnable game that produces this: participants receive a tradable asset and private, noisy signals about its value, trade bilaterally in open outcry across two or three rounds, and settle in chocolate at the end. Build in a twist I have been considering but cannot make work on my own — unknown to the students, half the room draws signals from a deck with one base rate and half from a deck with a different base rate, nobody knows which deck anyone else drew from, and between rounds I announce only how many trades were completed, never the prices. My hope is that the group then has to decide what trade volume is evidence of, and that the session ends with them seeing that observed trading is ambiguous between irrationality and diversity of priors, and that whatever the market knows may be a fact about the population rather than a proposition any individual could state. Give me rules, materials, a payoff scheme that needs no money, and a minute-by-minute timeline; no lecture up front, and do not name the theorem until the debrief. The debrief should map specific moments in the game onto specific assumptions of the result, say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle each failure — including the real possibility that almost nobody trades in round one, or that one loud student anchors every price — and be frank about which part of the idea the game cannot reproduce and I will simply have to supply in words. Write your answer as continuous prose in American English, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables: the way you would write a long, careful email to a colleague.
