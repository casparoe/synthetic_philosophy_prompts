For about a year now I've been the person who writes the marketing copy at a language-learning app, and our growth team has quietly moved our pitch from "learn to say new things" to "learn to think new things": number-word transparency as an arithmetic upgrade, grammatical gender as a way of seeing objects differently, languages that mark evidentiality as an antidote to misinformation, absolute rather than relative spatial terms as a permanent inner compass. I read into the background because I didn't want to write something a linguist would laugh at, and what I found is that the serious version of this is more interesting than the version in our slides — the strong thesis (that language determines what can be thought) is in real trouble from the ease of translation and from the cross-language universals in colour naming, so the live position is a weak, task-specific one, and the fights that matter are whether effects survive into genuinely non-verbal tasks, whether what's being measured is thought or only "thinking for speaking", whether the causal arrow runs from language to cognition or from a shared cultural praxis to both, and what to make of a replication record that is thinner than the TED talks suggest. Meanwhile my brother keeps forwarding me articles about languages with dozens of words for snow. I'd like something I can circulate internally that makes our team wince in recognition.

Write the launch press release for an imaginary startup — you pick the company name, the founder, and the invented academic advisor — announcing a subscription product that installs the cognitively transformative features of other languages one module at a time, without your having to learn the language. Give it a headline, a dateline, and roughly 700 to 900 words in the flat, buoyant voice of a Series A announcement, with a bulleted module list, a pricing section, and a short "notes to editors" FAQ. The target is our industry's neo-Whorfian overclaiming, not the underlying research: a fair-minded researcher who is sympathetic to weak relativity should find the piece fair and funny, while someone who thinks the strong version is true should not be able to tell why it stings.

A few things I'd like you to build the jokes out of, all of them real features of the literature or of its popularisation. Each module's evidence should be a measurement that quietly can't support the claim being made — an outcome measured with the same verbal task the module trains, a trial run by the company, a placebo condition that is just another language course. The marketing should lean on the two famous chestnuts (a language with a legendary number of words for snow, a language supposedly without time) without ever admitting they are chestnuts, and the fine print should contain a careful disavowal of the strong thesis in language that doesn't retract any of the marketing. The pricing tiers should monetise the retreat from strong to weak relativity — a cheaper "thinking for speaking" plan, a premium tier for effects that persist into non-linguistic tasks, an enterprise add-on that handles the replication question — and the piece should close with the one product the company won't sell, the one that would actually follow if the strong thesis were true: complete removal of your native language, available only to children, priced on application. Don't name or target any living researcher, don't have the narrator ever state an objection out loud, and keep it deadpan throughout.
