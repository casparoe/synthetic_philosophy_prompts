Construct a thought experiment for a discussion of whether a reinforcement-learning agent's reward signal could ever be the kind of thing that constitutes suffering — that is, whether the sign or magnitude of reward is the right hook for welfare ascriptions to a trained system. Specifically, build two minimally different setups, A and B, meeting the specifications below.

Both must use only ordinary RL machinery: a reward function, a learned value function, temporal-difference prediction errors (the gap between the value the agent predicted and the estimate it arrives at after taking a step and seeing the actual reward), a discount factor, and a policy updated from those errors. No exotic hardware, no brain-like architecture, and no stipulation that the agent is or is not conscious: whatever inclination we have to ascribe suffering has to be generated by the learning dynamics rather than assumed.

In A, reward should be negative on essentially every timestep, yet nothing in the description should incline us to say the agent suffers. In B, reward should never be negative, yet the description should incline us, at least somewhat, to ascribe suffering. Negative reward should thus come out as neither sufficient nor necessary. The two setups must differ in exactly one structural feature of the signal, and that feature — not the task, the embodiment, or how hard the environment is — must carry the difference in intuition.

Give each setup in one short paragraph, in a setting a practitioner could actually run (a recommender, a warehouse robot, a gridworld). Then state the verdict of the reward-as-valence view, on which suffering is tracked by negative reward or by the negative value the agent assigns to its situation, and of the rival view I want tested: that the functional role of valence is played not by the reward number but by the prediction error, since that is what forces reorientation and drives learning. Say which feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to each view's verdict if that feature is removed, and how a defender of the reward-as-valence view would attack the case — in particular, whether they can argue that negative reward in A still does the motivational work of aversion even when it carries no error, or that B's appearance of suffering is smuggled in rather than earned.
