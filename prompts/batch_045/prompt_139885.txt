So I've been arguing with a friend about hypocrisy and standing to blame, and we keep dragging Eliot Spitzer into it and then talking past each other. Setup as I remember it (just stipulate it if my details are off): as New York attorney general he made a public point of going after prostitution and trafficking operations, then in 2008, as governor, it came out that he'd been paying for sex himself, and he resigned within days. What I want is an actual mapping, not a vibe.

First, the mapping. Who are the parties here -- the women and men his office prosecuted, the women he paid, the voters who elected him twice, the press, his own staff -- and what were the options each of them actually had (prosecute, resign, apologise, stonewall, defend, forgive)? For each party say what the standing literature would have them be able to do and say after the exposure: who may blame whom, for what, and what the rest are supposed to do with that blame.

Second, I think 'he lost the standing to condemn' is doing about four different jobs and we keep switching between them without noticing, so please pull them apart and tell me which one is really doing the work:

- the uptake/role one: the blame just misfires, like a speech act performed by someone who isn't in the position to perform it, and the target is entitled to brush it off with 'you of all people'.
- the fairness one: he exempted himself from a burden he was happy to impose on others, and the complaint is about an unfair distribution of moral criticism, not about the content of what he said.
- the sincerity one: he never really accepted the norm he was invoking, so the condemnation is a kind of fraud on his audience.
- the epistemic-discount one: his blame is no longer good evidence that the conduct is wrong, because his judgement is compromised in a way he had an interest in not noticing.

Then the bits I haven't seen anyone handle, which is where I actually want you:

1. Domain. Does the loss of standing stop at the norm he violated, or does it leak? He was also the guy who went after Wall Street. If he'd given a speech about corporate fraud in 2007, were we entitled to discount that too? Is standing local to a norm, local to a whole area of conduct, or global?
2. Timing. He was prosecuting rings in, say, 2004 and paying for sex in 2008. Did the 2004 prosecutions lack standing *at the time*? I can see three answers -- standing tracks the facts about the blamer when he blames, standing is judged from the later standpoint once the full picture is in, or standing tracks what was publicly knowable at the time -- and they give different verdicts about whether the people he convicted have a complaint. Which is right and why? And does the answer change if the person he blamed was a trafficker, versus a client, versus someone being trafficked?
3. Official versus private. Prosecuting is a role-act done in the name of the state, not a piece of personal moral address. Does the standing of the office inherit the standing of the man? What about a hypocritical judge at sentencing? Or does the state's standing to punish float free of whoever is holding the pen?
4. The fix. Suppose a hypocrite is genuinely willing to take the punishment the law he enforced prescribes for himself -- he thinks he deserves it more than the people he prosecuted, say. Does willingness to submit to the same norm restore standing, or is that a different question entirely?

One more: if you think buying sex isn't wrong and the criminalisation is itself unjust, does the standing question even get off the ground, or does the whole example collapse? Say if that's where you land rather than pretending the case is neutral.

Please commit. Give me a definite verdict on whether Spitzer lacked standing, in which of the four senses above, and which account of standing the case supports -- and be explicit about where the theory goes silent instead of making it speak. Definitely flag anything you think is genuinely undecidable from the armchair.
