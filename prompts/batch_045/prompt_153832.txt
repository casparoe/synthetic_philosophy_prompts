I need a short proposal, roughly two pages / 900 words, for a four-month funded mini-project. Here's the half-formed thing I've been chewing on.

Decision-making under moral uncertainty is normally framed as: an agent is unsure which moral theory is right, and has to pick an action now. The standard moves are all one-shot rules for a single agent — weigh each theory by your credence and maximize expected choice-worthiness, or let each theory have a proportional say in a kind of internal vote, and so on. But a machine facing the same uncertainty has options a human agent doesn't: it can defer the call to a designated human, it can ask, it can act in a way that's cheaply reversible and revisit later, it can log the case and escalate, it can wait for a better version of itself. So my first suspicion is that the standard question is the wrong unit of analysis, and the real question is which *combination* of acting, deferring, asking, and acting-reversibly is right under moral uncertainty — and in particular whether there's something like "normative option value": reasons to keep a moral question open that aren't just instrumental to eventually getting it right.

Second suspicion, and this is the part I like more: deferral gets treated as costless, even humble. But when a system hands the call to a human it offloads the costs of deciding — the effort of deliberating, the risk of being the one who got it wrong, the moral residue of a bad call — onto someone who may never have agreed to carry that. Call it moral burden dumping, or rename it if you can do better. If that's a genuine moral cost, then a system that defers everything isn't cautious, it's exploiting its operators, and none of the existing frameworks seem to register this.
