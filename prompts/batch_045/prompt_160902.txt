I am writing as someone who has recently changed sides of a table, and I need help thinking clearly before a decision is taken that I shall have to defend.

For six years I have volunteered with a mentoring scheme that prepares sixth-formers from state schools for competitive graduate programmes. Much of that work has consisted of watching able candidates fail hurdles that had nothing to do with the capacities the programmes claim to be selecting for. This spring I was asked to join my employer's committee as it redesigns the selection process for its graduate intake, and I find that the internal debate is conducted in a vocabulary I recognise from the outside but have never had to operationalise.

The immediate question is whether to add something costly. The candidates on the table are: (a) a two-day unpaid residential assessment at our London office, with travel and accommodation at the applicant's expense; (b) a forty-hour take-home case study, completed in the applicant's own time; (c) a requirement that applicants already hold a particular professional certificate, the course for which costs about £2,000. The case for imposing one of these, as a colleague put it to me, is that a hurdle which does not hurt cannot tell us anything: make it cost something, and only the serious, or the good enough to be worth it, will clear it. I take the point, and I think it is more than a slogan. But I have two worries that I cannot bring into focus, and I cannot tell whether they are one worry or two.

The first is that a hurdle can be genuinely informative and still be the wrong thing to impose, because what it is informative about may be an applicant's circumstances rather than the qualities we are selecting for. My own first job came through a two-month unpaid internship in a city where I happened to have a spare room in a relative's flat. I have always been told that taking it 'showed commitment'. What I think it showed was that somebody else was paying my rent.

The second is that 'make it hurt more' and 'make it tell us more' seem to come apart, and I want to know precisely where. My colleagues assume that if we soften a hurdle — pay the travel, provide the laptop, run the case study as ninety supervised minutes on the clock rather than forty unsupervised hours at home — we destroy whatever it was buying us. I am not convinced. It seems to me that a cost can carry information only relative to what it costs the person bearing it, and that a hurdle whose cost tracks free time or disposable income rather than competence is measuring the wrong variable with great precision. But I cannot tell whether stripping out that component of the cost repairs the signal, merely weakens it, or relocates it somewhere worse. That crux — does paying people to signal destroy the signal? — is the thing I most need explained, including the case where the hurdle ranks candidates rather than measuring them, so that lifting everyone's budget leaves the ranking untouched.

To argue this in committee I have tried to give myself a distinction. Call a requirement *trait-elastic* if the marginal cost of clearing it falls as the quality we care about rises; call it *budget-elastic* if the marginal cost of clearing it falls as the candidate's resources rise. I want to know whether that distinction does real work or whether it collapses into something the theory already says better, and what it implies about each of the three options above.

Please assume I already have the popular version of the theory. I know about the peacock's tail and the stotting gazelle; I do not need to be told that a signal is costly and therefore honest. What I need is the machinery underneath: what exactly has to be true of a cost for bearing it to carry information at all; whether the signal has to be *wasted* or merely differentially expensive; whether, as I suspect, the original statement of the handicap principle and the later formal treatments disagree about that; and why an honest signalling arrangement can be one that nobody involved would choose, so that everyone in the system knows the hurdle is pointless and no one can be the first to drop it. I would also value a frank statement of which of my claims rest on a modelling assumption — that the costs simply add up, that the receiver's response is fixed, that the population has settled — rather than on the logic of signalling itself.
