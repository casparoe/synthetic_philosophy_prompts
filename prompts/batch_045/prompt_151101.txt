Rand died in 1982, so she never ran into effective altruism — the thing where people sit down with spreadsheets, work out which charity saves the most lives per dollar, and then either give away a big slice of their income or deliberately pick a high-paying job they don't actually care about (quant trading, whatever) just to funnel the money to strangers on another continent they'll never meet. One wing of it has since gone further and says today's poor aren't really the priority at all: the priority is cutting the chance that humanity wipes itself out this century, since that's where the trillions of possible future lives are. So what would she have made of it? Would she call it the purest version of the altruism she spent her career attacking — self-sacrifice with its sentimentality stripped out and replaced by cost-effectiveness curves — or would something in it have appealed to her: the rationality, the refusal to be guilt-tripped by whoever happens to be visibly suffering in front of you, the fact that these people don't even claim to be sacrificing anything? What would she say about the longtermist wing in particular, given that on her own view the standard is your own life as a rational being, and these folks are trading actual living people for statistically possible future ones? And is there any version of this she could have endorsed — say an egoist who just happens to value being the kind of person who saves lives — or does that move only work because her definition of sacrifice is doing all the heavy lifting?
