Hey — I teach a grad seminar in formal epistemology, and one of my students came to office hours with an argument I can't find the exit from. We'd just done Jeffrey conditionalization: your posterior after an experience is P'(H) = Σ_i P(H | E_i) · P'(E_i), over a partition {E_i}, with the rigidity condition P'(H | E_i) = P(H | E_i), so that the experience's entire impact on H goes through its impact on the E_i. The case was the usual one — a cloth in bad light, you end up at .8 that it's green, nothing becomes certain, and nobody has to say what the "real" evidence proposition was.

His argument, more or less as he wrote it on the board:

(1) The rule's only moving parts are your old conditional probabilities P(H | E_i) on some partition, and the new marginals P'(E_i) that the experience hands you directly; rigidity says nothing else budges.

(2) Nothing in the framework says which partition to use. The rule is stated relative to a partition and is silent about the choice.

(3) Take the trivial partition, {⊤}. Then P'(H) = P(H) for every H. So on that reading, JC says no experience ever changes your mind about anything — complete dogmatism.

(4) Take the finest partition, the atoms — complete worlds. Then P(H | ω) is 0 or 1, and so is P'(H | ω), and they're equal, so rigidity is satisfied automatically and constrains nothing; and P'(H) = Σ_ω P(H | ω) · P'(ω) can be anything whatsoever. On that reading JC lets you move to any posterior you like, since the "input" is now the whole answer.

(5) In between, different choices of partition give different posteriors for the very same experience, and the rule doesn't pick one. So the content of the update is fixed entirely by a parameter the theory leaves undetermined.

(6) A norm of rationality whose content is fixed by an undetermined parameter — vacuous at one end, anarchic at the other, arbitrary in between — isn't a norm of rationality.

(7) So there is no correct way to update on uncertain evidence, and "generalizing conditionalization" was never a real project to begin with.

I don't accept (7), and I'd rather give up (7) than (1). But I genuinely can't tell whether (2) is a real premise or just a fancy way of restating my discomfort, and whether (3) and (4) are fair applications of the rule or scarecrows dressed up as limiting cases. Every step looks fine to me except the conclusion, which is exactly the situation where I assume I'm missing something.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Tell me why the step looked fine in the first place — I need to be able to say why a smart person would walk into it — and, if the answer is that the partition isn't free after all, what fixes it? A colleague claims the version where you take the posterior closest in KL divergence to your prior among those satisfying P'(E) = q escapes the whole thing; I don't believe her, but I can't say why. And give me something to say to the student beyond "interesting, let's talk about it next week": has he shown that the uncertain-evidence program is empty, or only that JC needs a story about where the partition comes from, and are those different?
