Our group of eight has to pick one of two vendors for a piece of equipment we'll be stuck with for years. Everyone is carrying private scraps of evidence — who has talked to which lab, who has sat through which demo — and none of us wants to argue it out in a meeting. My proposal was to run an internal pool: people stake modest real money on which vendor will look better after a year, and the group then buys whichever one the pool's odds favor.

One colleague says the pool can't work. I should only want her money if I think I know something she doesn't, and she should only want mine under the same condition; so the very fact that someone is eager to take the other side of my bet is evidence that I have mispriced it. On that reasoning either no one trades and the pool tells us nothing, or someone trades and gets taken advantage of.

Her proposed repair is that the group pay a flat bonus into every matched bet, split evenly between both sides, deliberately fixed in advance so that it does not vary with the odds or the stake — the idea being to compensate people for the risk of being the one who turned out to be wrong.

Does a bonus like that actually answer her objection, or does it just pay us to be exploited? If it does work, how large does it need to be, and what does it cost us? And is her original worry sound at all, given that dispersed private information is the entire reason for running a pool instead of just voting?
