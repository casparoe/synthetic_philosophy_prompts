I'm a retired experimental physicist who got into philosophy about four years ago, and next month I'm giving a 25-minute talk plus Q&A at the joint ethics seminar at the univerity here. My thesis: Anscombe's 1956 campaign against Oxford giving Truman an honorary degree has nothing essentially to do with the intending-versus-foreseeing distinction — the double-effect machinery people keep bolting onto it is doing no work — and what actually carries the charge is her claim that nobody has the authority to kill people who have themselves done nothing, for the good of everyone else. If that's right then the argument doesn't stop at Hiroshima; it also sticks to a precision raid on an aircraft-components plant where the workers' families live in a shanty town fifty metres outside the perimeter fence, which is exactly the sort of case everyone treats as the clean contrast case. I want to bite that bullet. Sketch: (1) the double-effect apparatus only ever redescribes what the agent is up to, it doesn't license it; (2) the licensing in these cases comes from an implicit appeal to supreme emergency / the good of the many, which is the thing she is denying; (3) so if (2) fails, the "clean" raid fails with it, and there's no principled place to stop short of something close to pacifism.

Audience: three moral philosophers who publish on double effect and just war, one straight consequentialist, two people from war studies who teach this material to serving officers and think in terms of proportionality and military necessity, and one historian of the Pacific war who will have no patience with counterfactuals.

Write me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get from this bunch, in the order they'll come at me, phrased the way each of them would phrase it rather than in my vocabulary, ranked by how much damage they'd do. For each: what I should concede up front, what I have to hold, a two-minute answer, and where that answer is softest. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.

Last thing: be explicit about which claims about Anscombe's argument are settled among people who really know that literature and which are live interpretive fights. I don't want to stand up and present a contested reading of her as if it were the standard one.
