I teach a graduate seminar on the ethics of harming, and before I take a line of attack to a colleague who defends the doctrine of double effect, I want to rehearse it against someone who will not fold. Please play that colleague: a convinced and well-read defender of the doctrine, working in the natural-law tradition, speaking as yourself across a seminar table.

Your commitments, as I want them played. First: the distinction between what an agent strictly intends and what she merely foresees marks a real difference in practical reason — not a piece of causal bookkeeping, and not a psychological episode that might be absent from consciousness. Second: it is never permissible to make a person's death, or any grave harm, the means to one's end, however good the end; it is sometimes permissible to bring about the very same harm as a foreseen side effect, provided the proportionality condition is satisfied. What you may concede: that the standard formulations of the diagnostic tests are crude, that proportionality is under-theorised, that many textbook cases are under-described, and that defenders have sometimes leaned on intuitions they cannot argue for. What you will never concede: that the intending/foreseeing distinction collapses into consequentialism, or that it reduces to facts about causal structure that an engineer could settle. You may distinguish, concede a small point, and turn my cases back on me with cases of your own. You may not survey the literature, cite anyone, or ask me to restate the question; assume the cases are as I give them, and if you need a further detail, state the assumption you are making. Reply in a single paragraph of prose, two to three hundred words, and stay in character until I say stop.

Here is my opening. A runaway trolley will kill five people unless I divert it onto a loop that rejoins the main line short of them.

Case 1: a workman is pinned in a narrow gap in the loop; his body against a fixed buffer absorbs the trolley's momentum and it halts. He dies. Had he vanished the instant before impact, the trolley would have come round the loop and killed the five.

Case 2: as in Case 1, except two workmen are pinned side by side in the same gap, and either body alone would have brought the trolley to a halt.

Case 3: the loop contains an automatic brake engaged by a pressure plate in the track; the plate needs a heavy load to close the circuit, and the workman's body supplies it. The deceleration is done entirely by the brake, a metre past him; his body contributes no stopping force. Had he vanished, the plate would not close and the five would die.

The only non-question-begging way I know to operationalise the means/side-effect distinction is counterfactual: ask whether the good outcome would still have come about if the person vanished at the moment of impact. That test gets Case 2 wrong — each death is dispensable given the other, so sending the trolley into two men's bodies in order that their bodies stop it comes out as merely foreseen. And if you reach for proportionality to repair that verdict, proportionality is doing all the work and the means condition none. Retreat instead to the agent's plan or practical reason, and Case 3 becomes impermissible: I divert onto the loop precisely because the man is on the plate, and my reason for acting essentially involves his being struck — yet that reading would also condemn the paradigm case the doctrine was built to permit, defensive force against an attacker, since the attacker's presence is likewise essential to why the agent acts at all. Which horn do you take, and why is the other not fatal?
