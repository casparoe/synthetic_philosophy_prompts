Hey — I run a small reading group on national memory, and before we read anything I want to get straight what I should actually be demanding of an explanation of lost-cause myths. Not a general theory of nationalism, and not a history of any one case. I want something I could hand to someone looking at a fresh defeat — a lost war, a lost election that half the country says was stolen, a referendum that went the wrong way, a colony handed back — and have them say: will this harden into a myth, roughly how long will that take, and what shape will it take (moral vindication? a promised return? a rescue that never counts as a defeat?).

So write me the spec. What does a theory of how defeats turn into sacred national myths have to get right? The cases it has to handle, the distinctions it must not collapse, the patterns it has to predict rather than only accommodate afterwards — each with the case or consideration that makes it a requirement. Mark which requirements are genuinely binding and which are just the preferences of one camp.

Four things I want you to take a stand on rather than list:

- the lag. I keep noticing that the myth seems to harden a generation after the actual defeat, once the people who were there are dead and can't contradict the story. Is "explain the timing" a real requirement, or am I overfitting to the four cases I know — the Confederacy, Masada, Kosovo (the medieval battle, not the 1990s one), Dunkirk?
- the null case. Germany loses in 1918 and gets a stab-in-the-back story; it loses in 1945 and mostly doesn't get one. Same country, one myth, one non-myth. Must a theory explain non-occurrence, or is that just asking it to explain silence?
- triviality. Masada looks militarily insignificant, Kosovo was at worst a draw, Dunkirk was a successful evacuation remembered as a rescue. Does a theory have to say why the size of the loss doesn't track the size of the myth, and why some defeats get narrated as not-defeats at all?
- the one I keep going back and forth on: does the theory get to assume the myth is a distortion? If it builds falsity into the definition it's begging the question and can't handle a defeat that really was stolen; if it stays neutral about truth it has trouble explaining why the memories of people who were there get overwritten by people who weren't.

Also flag any pair of requirements you think can't both be met, and if you find one, drop one of them and say why. I'd rather have the contradiction than a tidy list. My own suspicion is that "fit the famous cases" and "make falsifiable predictions about new ones" pull against each other given how few cases there are and how they got selected, but tell me if the real conflict is somewhere else.

Then briefly — a couple of lines each, not a lit review — how the three stories people always reach for do against your list: it holds the group together, it protects the group's self-image, someone with power keeps it alive because it pays. And commit: tell me which single requirement does the most work knocking candidates out, and rank the requirements by how binding they really are, even where you're unsure. If you're guessing, guess, and say where the guess is.
