Corridor argument I keep loosing, and I'm teaching Austin next term, so I'd like to get it straight before I stand up in front of thirty undergraduates.

Our legal theory lot run everything off one analogy: an utterance is a move in a rule-governed practice, like chess. Force, they say, is constituted by the rules plus the players' recognition of the move – the old practice-conception of constitutive rules, the counts-as clause, all that – so all a speech act needs is a speaker with standing, a well-formed move, and uptake. They then use it to settle something we actually argue about in college meetings: whether an institution can apologise. On their telling, a vice-chancellor's authorised statement of apology is a full apology; the complaint that no one meant it is a category error, sincerity was never part of the move; and anyone calling it a non-apology is just muddling an abuse with a misfire.

I want the audit, please: what maps onto what (board, turn-taking, rulebook, umpire, win condition, illegal move), where the mapping goes wrong, and whether their conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table.

Three things I keep getting stuck on. One: I suspect the source already contains the target – in correspondence chess you make your move by writing it down, and a call in bridge or a bid at auction just is a speech act – so how much work can chess be doing as an analysis of saying something? Two: has the analogy even got room for Austin's void-misfire versus hollow-abuse split? An illegal move looks void, which would make every infelicity a misfire and hand them their conclusion far too cheaply; but you can also play a legal move for the wrong reasons, in a fixed game, which suggests the split survives after all. Three: what does it smuggle in – a codified rulebook, an umpire, an agreed point to the whole business, players who share a goal, a closed field you can step out of?

My test case, which I made up to break it: a college apologises for what a predecessor body did in 1892; nobody alive did it, nobody alive was wronged, the descendants are in the room to recieve it, and the words are read by an actor the college hired. Is that an apology on their picture, and is it a better or worse one than the vice-chancellor's?

And if the analogy won't carry it, what would – the conversational scoreboard picture, rehearsal and script, something else?
