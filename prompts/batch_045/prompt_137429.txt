I am putting together a special issue on Hannah Arendt's essays on truth and politics — the general essay on truth in politics and the later one on the Pentagon Papers, "Lying in Politics" — and I want to open it with a piece of documentary satire rather than another commentary. Write the piece for me.

The form is an internal field manual: an interim circular issued to senior staff by an invented bureau of an invented government, in the flat register of civil-service prose — something like "Interim Guidance on the Retention of Factual Material and the Management of Public Assertion." Give it 900 to 1,100 words, set out in numbered clauses or sections, one or two of which should be a completed specimen form or a short worked annex applying the guidance to a case. Voice: deadpan. Passive constructions, cross-references to clauses that do not exist, the occasional footnote, no acknowledgment anywhere that anything is amiss. No winking at the reader, no jokes about the reader's comprehension, no real person and no real government named. The one thing the manual treats as a disciplinary offence should be an individual's unauthorized assertion of something he personally knows.

The doctrine the manual has to work out is an extension of Arendt rather than a restatement of her, so let me set it out. On her account a lie is not merely a false statement: it requires someone who knows the truth and elects to deny it. The liar is an agent, a doer, who asserts a kind of freedom over the given, and his credibility with an audience comes from anticipating what that audience already wishes were the case. Factual truth is peculiarly fragile because it is contingent — it could always have been otherwise — which makes it easy to deny and oddly hard to defend, harder than a demonstrable rational truth. And sustained, organized lying does not end in people believing a falsehood; it ends in the destruction of the capacity to orient oneself in a shared world at all. Now notice what follows from the first claim. If lying requires a liar who knows, then an institution that systematically destroys the knowledge *before* the statement is made produces the entire damage of lying with no liar anywhere in the system: no one lies, and the common factual world is dismantled anyway. Call this the liarless lie. The manual's business is the manufacture of liarless lies — not asserting what is false but arranging matters so that no one is in a position to know, with the result that nothing anyone says can be a lie, and nothing anyone says can be refuted either. There is a second nicety I want used: on Arendt's own terms this is worse than lying, because the liar at least retains his grip on the truth he denies and can in principle be caught against the record, whereas an institution that has abolished the knowers has abolished the standard against which refutation does its work. The satire should let a reader feel that recoil — a straight-faced proposal following her logic to a place her framework did not reach and may not be able to speak to — without the manual ever naming it as a problem.

Two constraints on the content. First, every joke has to come from getting something right: either a real feature of Arendt's position as I have described it (paraphrase her throughout; I want no quoted passages from her or from anyone else in the finished piece), or a real feature of how large organizations actually manage what they know — need-to-know, oral briefings, records not created in the first place, drafts superseded, pre-decisional material, the gap between what was said and what was meant, deniability as an explicit design goal. The exaggeration must be of a real feature, not of an invented one. Second, and this is the constraint that should make the thing work: nothing in the manual may itself be false. Every sentence should be one a real administrator could sincerely write. The document is not lying; it is arranging for the truth not to arrive.
