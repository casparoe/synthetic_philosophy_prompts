Hi — I teach a final-year undergrad / MA option on justice after atrocity and I'm setting this year's take-home paper. 2,500 words, marked out of 25, students get a week. I need one essay question that isn't the usual "is amnesty a justifiable price to pay for truth?" one, because I've set that three years running and every script is the same: restorative vs retributive, a paragraph each on South Africa, Argentina and Rwanda, then a wobble and a fence.

What I'd actually like is a question built around something I've started calling *provisional amnesty* (my coinage — tell me if it already has a name and I'll rename it). The idea: immunity is granted up front and individually, but it's defeasible for a fixed period. It lapses if (a) the applicant's disclosure turns out to be incomplete or is contradicted by victims' testimony, or (b) the state fails to meet its own reparations, vetting and institutional-reform timetable. After, say, fifteen years it becomes final. So it sits between the South African model — individual application, full disclosure as the condition, but once granted it's final — and the Argentine one, where blanket legislative amnesties were repealed by Congress in 2003, declared unconstitutional by the Supreme Court in 2005, and prosecutions resumed afterwards.

The question has to force students into the weeds, not onto the tour. The things I want it to make them do:

- distinguish at least three different jobs amnesty might be doing — settling with actors who could still wreck the transition, inducing testimony that wouldn't otherwise be given given self-incrimination, and saying something about what kind of wrong this was and what the new political community is;
- notice that the 'truth for justice' framing only gets a grip if the epistemic job is the one doing the work, and that provisional amnesty shifts which job matters and why — including the nasty point that testimony bought with immunity is epistemically compromised, and that a corroboration requirement turns a commission from a truth-teller into an adjudicator;
- deal with the standing worry, that the state is trading away claims that aren't obviously its to trade, and with the credibility point the Argentine case seems to raise: if a successor government can just repeal an amnesty, then maybe what any state can honestly offer is only ever provisional, and the honest version of that might be better than a pretend-permanent one.

It'd be good if the question also gave them a way in on Rwanda — the gacaca courts going for punishment at scale with sentence reductions for confession rather than amnesty at all — and on the difference between amnesty and mere non-prosecution because the courts can't cope.

And: it should end in a forced choice. No "on the one hand" escapes. Someone who recites the trade-off cliche and stops there should cap at a 2:1.
