I sit on the methods committee of a national health technology assessment body as its one philosopher, and in three weeks I am presenting a paper to the full committee recommending that it change how it handles distributional concerns. I want a Q&A preparation sheet for that particular room, not for a philosophy department.

The setting, so that you have the facts rather than having to guess them. The committee currently scores programmes in QALYs and then applies a distributional adjustment drawn from an Atkinson-style social welfare function: a single inequality-aversion parameter, with QALYs treated as measured on a ratio scale with a naturally fixed zero. On top of that it applies a severity modifier that gives extra weight to treatments for patients with large absolute shortfalls, and it has so far resisted fair-innings style arguments that would weight lifetime totals. Nearly every decision we make compares programmes affecting different numbers of patients, and we publish cost-effectiveness thresholds that lower-level bodies then apply.

The thesis has two halves. The first is a complaint about standard prioritarianism. On the standard view a person's claim on resources is weighted by a decreasing function of their absolute well-being level. My complaint is that this rule is not invariant under positive affine transformations of the well-being scale: rescaling and re-zeroing the scale changes the ordering of options. Add the same constant to everyone and the weight differences compress, so that as the constant grows the ordering of any two distributions converges on the utilitarian ordering. How much priority you have therefore depends on where you put the zero, and I will argue that the QALY zero fixes a zero for health, not for the quantity the priority view is supposed to care about, and that in any case we score gains against a baseline rather than lifetime levels.

The second half is the fix. I propose attaching the weights to positions in the distribution rather than to levels: order patients from worst off to best off, fix a decreasing sequence of rank weights, and evaluate a distribution as the weighted sum of the ordered levels, with the weights set by a single parameter the committee chooses, namely the ratio between the bottom and top weights, with the intermediate weights filled in by geometric interpolation. This variant is invariant under any positive affine rescaling of the scale, because the weights sum to a constant and adding a constant to everyone simply adds that constant times the sum of the weights. It still satisfies Pareto, it still satisfies the Pigou-Dalton transfer principle, and it still cannot reward levelling down: if you lower one person's level, no order statistic rises, so with strictly positive weights the total cannot rise. So, I argue, it keeps everything that made prioritarianism attractive while dropping the artefact.

I also plan to concede, up front, that the weight a patient receives now depends on where that patient sits relative to others, so that priority becomes a relation rather than a property of the patient. My line will be that this is not egalitarianism smuggled back in, because the rule still cannot sanction levelling down, and I will try to explain away the pull of the levelling-down intuition as a conflation of two things: the badness of a gap and the badness of the institutions that usually produce gaps.

The room is as follows. There is a health economist who built the current Atkinson adjustment, thinks a parameter that cannot be estimated from survey data is unfalsifiable moralising dressed as method, and will want to know why the inequality-aversion parameter she already has does not do the job. There are two HTA methodologists who care above all about separability: our incremental cost-effectiveness machinery and our published thresholds both assume that the social value of a QALY depends only on the patient who receives it, and a positional rule makes the value of a QALY depend on the whole distribution, so that no threshold can be published and no decision can be delegated. There is a bioethicist of a broadly relational-egalitarian temperament who holds that the levelling-down objection was never a reductio of anything, and who will produce cases in which the inequality is expressive, or produced by stigmatising institutions, and say that my immunity to levelling down is a symptom of having no view about relations between persons at all, while my positional weights show that I do have one. There is a clinician who will ask why the committee should weight a patient's lifetime position rather than how sick she is right now, and who will raise the rule-of-rescue cases. And there are two patient and public members, one of whom will ask whether I am really saying that her claim on the health service rises when her neighbours fall ill and falls when a sicker group is folded into the reference population.

Please draft the preparation sheet. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get, in the order they will arrive, each attributed to the person who will make it and phrased the way that person would phrase it, in their vocabulary rather than mine. For each, give a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and a candid note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede in the first two minutes, before anyone asks anything, and identify the one reply that has to land if the proposal is to survive the meeting. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks.

Two specific things. First, I have a private worry that normalising the rank weights so that we can compare programmes affecting different numbers of patients destroys the very invariance my argument rests on, or else forces me into averaging rather than totalling, with the population problems that brings. Tell me whether that is the question I should be dreading, or whether I am overrating it. Second, I have not decided whether the weights attach to patients' expected lifetime well-being, with the expectation taken afterwards, or whether we take the expectation of the weighted sum; I am going to have to say something about it in the talk. Tell me which version this room will push me towards and what it costs me either way.

Please work entirely within the machinery I have set out, and do not pad the sheet with the objections I would get in a philosophy seminar: I need the ones this committee will raise, and I need to know which of them actually have force.
