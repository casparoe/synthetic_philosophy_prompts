I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy background beyond one book I read on holiday, so please keep it concrete. British English throughout ideally (colour, analyse, organised) — our internal docs are all en-GB.

Where I work we're halfway through a big re-architecture, and at every retro someone says "it's a paradigm shift" and then says the old and new systems are "incommensurable": you can't compare them, you just have to switch how you see the problem. Last week our staff engineer put it like this: it's exactly like that famous duck-rabbit drawing — the marks on the page don't change, nothing is added, you just flip between two readings — so there's no argument that could settle which is right and no point pretending the new one is "better", you either see it or you don't. He then used that to argue we should stop collecting the old metric: our new observability stack has no concept corresponding to the old "silent data corruption rate", so it isn't being logged any more, and he says that's fine because there was never a common scale to compare the two on in the first place.

I went and read Kuhn's The Structure of Scientific Revolutions (1962) hoping to settle it. As far as I can tell he does lean on the gestalt-switch comparison, and later on something about learning a second language, and he says things that sound like scientists end up working in a different world after a revolution. But the pop-version conclusion — no comparison possible, just flip — looks a lot stronger than what a picture of a duck-rabbit can actually carry, and I want to know where the analogy is doing the arguing.

The case I keep testing this on (completely invented, feel free to swap it for a better one): a field station has logged water-sample readings off the same instrument for forty years. Under the old theory the number is described as the amount of suspended stuff blocking the light; under the new theory there's no such stuff, and the same numbers get re-described as a scattering coefficient. Two wrinkles: about halfway through, the new team re-calibrates the instrument, so later numbers aren't on the same scale as earlier ones; and they stop recording three columns the old theory treated as the interesting ones, because the new theory has no name for those quantities.

What I'd like:

- A correspondence table. For each moving part of the duck-rabbit case — the marks on the page, the duck, the rabbit, the viewer, the flip, the bit where you can't hold both at once, the bit where nothing about the picture itself has changed — what's supposed to play that role in a paradigm shift? Flag which mappings are load-bearing and which are just decorative.
- Then where it breaks, split into the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion my colleague wants and the ones that are noise. My guesses are: the "fixed data" (in my invented case the marks on the page change, and some columns disappear entirely), the voluntary reversible flip (he has never once managed to un-flip, and nobody who joined after the migration even can), and completeness (a duck and a rabbit are both complete readings of the same lines; two paradigms don't even score the same problems). Tell me if I've got the important ones or if I'm missing the real one.
- What the analogy quietly imports. I think it smuggles in a neutral observation language that survives the switch, plus a single observer who has both readings on tap. - The verdict, and be precise here because it decides whether we keep logging that corruption metric. I vaguely gather the word incommensurable came from maths, something about the side and diagonal of a square having no common measure — if that's right, say whether that original sense is closer to one of those three than the duck-rabbit is.

Ones I've been kicking around: switching unit systems while someone quietly redefines the ruler; porting a codebase while the instrumentation gets rewritten; becoming bilingual rather than just moving house.
