I'm a novelist — my last book had a subplot about a woman walking away from a communist cell in Marseille just after the war, and in the course of researching it I read Simone Weil's short essay arguing that political parties ought to be done away with altogether. I made the mistake of bringing it up at a dinner thing last weekend. Two friends went at it for about forty minutes while I sat there eating bread: Rania, who translates essays out of French and has read Weil properly, and Bill, who spent fifteen years as a union organiser and then eight on the city council. I couldn't of told you at the time who was winning and I still can't, but I'd like to turn a cleaned-up version of their exchange into a scene between two characters, so I need it adjudicated properly rather than just remembered fondly.

The way it ran: (1) Rania said the essay isn't an empirical generalisation you can knock down with counterexamples, it's a definition plus a consequence — Weil characterises a party as, first, a machine for manufacturing collective passion, second, an organisation arranged so as to put collective pressure on the thinking of each of its members, and third, something whose first and, in the end, only purpose is its own growth, and that without limit. On that description, she said, asking whether there could be a good party is like asking whether there could be a benign version of an organism whose only end is to get bigger: the answer is no, and the remedy is abolition, not reform. (2) Bill said that's a rigged definition, you've smuggled the conclusion into the premise — parties have been the vehicles for abolishing slavery, for independence movements, for building welfare states, so whatever they are they plainly pursue ends outside themselves, and if the definition denies that, so much the worse for the definition. (3) Rania said it isn't about anyone's intentions, it's about what a competitive environment does over time to an organisation that has to recruit and win: the stated aims get quietly traded for growth, members who notice get brought into line, and this is a selection effect, not a conspiracy — and the fate of the socialist and communist parties of Weil's own century is confirmation, not a counterexample. (4) Bill said if that's the mechanism then it isn't specific to parties at all: unions, churches, universities, campaign groups and resistance networks all run on collective enthusiasm, all discipline their dissidents, all put institutional survival first when they have to — Weil herself worked inside organisations like that — and an argument that can't tell a party from a union isn't an argument about parties. (5) Rania said there is a structural difference: a party is constitutively competitive and claims to speak for the whole, so a rival's growth is its loss and it can never afford to say it was wrong about the thing it exists for, whereas a union officially answers to an interest outside itself and can actually settle. (6) Bill said then we're back to a factual claim about how parties and unions really behave, and the honest answer is 'sometimes yes, sometimes no', which means the dramatic conclusion was never going to survive — at most you've got a reason to watch parties suspiciously and build in some friction, not a reason to think they're illegitimate in principle. (7) Rania conceded part of that and said: fine, read it as a regulative ideal rather than a policy — you can't abolish parties, but you can treat them like something with a known tendency to corruption, weaken party discipline, protect dissent, keep them from writing the rules they compete under. (8) Bill said then say that, and stop saying 'abolish' — that the apocalyptic framing is doing rhetorical work the argument can't cashed.

So: can you go through it move by move and tell me who has the better of it at each step, where each of them is being unfair to the other, and what the strongest version of each position would be — the one neither of them actually articulated? I don't want a debate-club verdict; I need to know which character is entitled to sound more confident, and whether there's a place where one of them ought to concede something they didn't concede at dinner. And if there's a distinction, or a case, or an objection that neither of them thought of and that would make the scene sharper, I'd love that too.
