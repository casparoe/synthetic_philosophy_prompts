I spent a little over forty years as an instrument chemist, most of them worrying about whether a number means what the manual says it means, and in retirement I have been working my way into the philosophical literature on algorithmic fairness. I am now in the awkward position of being able to state the central impossibility results from memory and still not knowing which criterion I actually want to give up in a real deployment. So I have started recording my own verdicts on concrete cases, in the hope that a principle would fall out of the pile. It has not. I would be grateful for help.

Let me fix the setting so that we are arguing about one thing. There are two groups, A and B, differing in the base rate of the outcome to be predicted. A model produces a risk score. A threshold converts the score into a decision. I take the following as established, and I neither need to be argued into it nor want references for it: when base rates differ and predictions are imperfect, the score cannot at once be calibrated within each group — a score of 0.7 carrying the same seventy per cent chance of the outcome whichever group the person belongs to — and also have equal false-positive and false-negative rates across groups; and equalised error rates cannot be combined with equal predictive value of a positive score. Something must give. My question is what, and when.

Here are my verdicts.

One. A pre-trial risk score, displayed to a judge as a probability and combined with other evidence in a release decision. I want calibration, and I am willing to let the error rates diverge across groups. The number is being read as a measurement; a measurement that means different things for different people is, in my old vocabulary, a broken instrument.

Two. A large employer's first-stage screen. Four thousand applications are ranked and cut to two hundred interviews; the score is never shown to anyone and never interpreted as a probability; a candidate's fate depends only on which side of the cut they fall. Here I am close to indifferent to calibration, and I do want the interview cohorts to be roughly proportionate even where the raw scores would not deliver that. Cases one and two are the same machine doing the same arithmetic. I give them opposite answers, and that is what prompted this letter.

Three. Intensive care beds during a surge. The score predicts clinical deterioration, beds go to the top of the list, and there are not enough beds. I want no parity constraint at all: allocate to the highest scores. If the score is miscalibrated between groups I want that fixed as a measurement defect, but I do not want the allocation reshaped to compensate for it. Cases two and three are also nearly the same machine — ordering plus a cut — and I again give opposite answers.

Four. Mortgage approval, where the recorded outcome is default within twenty-four months. Approval itself makes repayment easier and denial degrades the applicant's credit file, so the outcome variable is partly manufactured by the decisions it is used to evaluate. My verdict is that none of the three criteria is even well defined here. What I want is to equalise the rate at which people who would have repaid are denied, understood counterfactually, and I cannot see how that is to be checked against data.

Five. A two-stage process: an automated screen passes candidates to a human panel whose own pass rates differ by group. What reaches a candidate is the composite. I think the constraint belongs on the composite and not on the screen alone, and I go back and forth on this, because that seems to licence deliberately skewing the screen to offset the panel, which I dislike.

Six. A renewable benefit re-scored annually, where a flag in year one enters the file and raises the score in year two. What I find myself caring about is the expected number of wrongful denials per person across a decade, not the error rate per decision. Every criterion I have encountered is stated per decision, so nothing I have read is at the right scale.

My own tentative principle is this: a fairness constraint should bind whatever does the work in the decision and nothing else — where the score is consumed as a measurement, require calibration; where only its ordering is consumed, constrain the composition of the selected set. I can see already that it handles one and two neatly, that three is a counterexample to it as stated, that four is not a case it can even be applied to, and that five and six raise a question it never asks, namely what counts as one decision.

What I would like is for that principle to be tested against all six cases and then repaired, or replaced by a better one: state the principle precisely, clause by clause, naming the case that motivates each clause; say plainly which of my verdicts it accommodates and which it overturns, and what revised verdict it would put in their place; give its verdicts on cases five and six, where I am undecided; and derive what it predicts for two cases I have not listed — a university admissions model predicting first-year performance where the groups differ systematically in which departments they apply to, and a system in which the group label is self-declared and the pattern of declaration shifts after the model is deployed. I would rather be told honestly that a verdict must go than given an ingenious way of keeping it. Please give no bibliography and no citations; I can find those myself.

Please return your answer as a single JSON object and nothing else, with exactly these fields:

"tentative_principle_diagnosis" — a string, at most eighty words, saying what is right and what is wrong with my principle as stated.

"candidates" — an array of two or three objects, each with: "id" (a short string), "statement" (one sentence), "clauses" (an array of objects, each with "clause", a precise formulation, "motivating_case", the case number that forces it, and "cost", what the clause buys and what it gives up), "verdicts_saved" (array of case numbers), "verdicts_overturned" (array of case numbers).

"recommended" — the id of the candidate you would defend, plus "why" (a string of at most sixty words).

"case_verdicts" — an array of six objects, one per case, each with "case" (the number), "my_verdict" (a short restatement), "principle_verdict" (what the recommended candidate says), "accommodated" (one of "yes", "partly", "no"), and "note" (a sentence).

"unlisted" — an array of two objects for the admissions case and the self-declared-label case, each with "case", "predicted_verdict", "clauses_relied_on" (array of clause identifiers), and "confidence" (a number between 0 and 1).

"residual" — an array of short strings, the questions the principle cannot settle and that I should decide for myself.
