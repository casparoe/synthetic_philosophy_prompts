You are both a political philosopher who has taught graduate-level social choice theory and a writer of technical satire in the deadpan register of a Swiftian modest proposal or a very straight-faced Onion news report. Take the task seriously: the comedy has to come from the mathematics being right, not from winking at it.

Before drafting, do a short planning pass: list, in three or four terse bullets, which of Arrow's conditions the fictional product escapes and by which real route — unrestricted domain, the Pareto (unanimity) condition, independence of irrelevant alternatives, non-dictatorship, and the requirement that the social ranking be an ordering. Each feature you are about to invent should sit on top of a genuine escape route from the impossibility result, not a hand-wavy one. Then write the piece.

The task: draft the press-kit FAQ for a seed-stage civic-tech startup that has announced a group-decision platform for condo boards, worker co-ops, and city councils, marketed as having "solved" the problem of aggregating conflicting preferences. The company is fictional and you may name it something appropriately smug. The document should contain, in this order:

1. A two-sentence company blurb in launch-announcement voice.
2. A three-tier pricing block (one line per tier) whose tier names or descriptions encode the technical restriction each tier imposes.
3. Eight FAQ entries, in the voice of a head of product answering customer questions, each 60 to 110 words.
4. A closing testimonial block with two invented quotes: one from a delighted customer, one from the company's "independent advisor on democratic integrity."

Substantive requirements for the FAQ entries. Every answer must be a technically accurate description of a real way around the impossibility result, presented as a customer benefit. Work in at least the following, each in its own entry:

- Onboarding that places every member on a single ideological axis and only admits preference profiles that peak once, so that the median option always wins — i.e. the platform satisfies the conditions only by shrinking the domain to single-peaked profiles, and it enforces that restriction by screening people rather than by arguing with them.
- The handling of independence of irrelevant alternatives: the platform quietly removes options before members see them, so no alternative is ever "irrelevant" on a ballot it appears on. Let the answer slide into the fact that the order in which options are presented determines the winner, and treat that as a feature.
- Cardinal intensity input — sliders, "how much do you care" — with the answer conceding, in the language of customer delight, that the system compares how strongly different people care, which is exactly what ordinal preference aggregation forbids itself from doing.
- A "rotating facilitator" with final authority over what reaches the ballot and over ties. Include one invented statistic implying that the facilitator's own ranking is adopted in the overwhelming majority of polls, and have the spokesperson deny, in a sentence that does not quite deny it, that this makes anyone a dictator in the technical sense.
- One entry on a "personal sphere" setting that lets each member settle a small class of matters entirely on their own. The answer should, without naming it, walk into the conflict between that minimal liberty and the unanimity condition: let the example be something trivial and the consequence something the company has quietly decided to live with.
- One entry where the binary-only mode is defended as the fully general case.

Constraints. Keep the register deadpan corporate throughout: no admissions that anything is a trade-off, no "of course Arrow showed" asides, no glossary, no footnotes, no citations, and no appeal to anything that would require looking something up. You may let Arrow's name appear at most once, in a nervous question from a prospective investor, and the answer should deflect it. The piece must never state its own moral. It should nevertheless be legible: an undergraduate who has met the theorem once should finish it understanding that the platform "works" only by arranging that there is no genuine plurality of preferences left to aggregate, and a social choice theorist should find the joke fair rather than wrong. Target 800 to 900 words for the document itself, not counting your planning bullets.
