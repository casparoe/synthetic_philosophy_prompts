Had a long, dumb argument with my friend last night about Dutch book arguments and I'm still kind of annoyed about it. Her line was that a Dutch book argument only ever gets you coherence — synchronic probabilism, plus maybe the update rule if you buy the diachronic version — so it can't do any real epistemological work: no norms on priors, nothing about evidence or confirmation, nothing about what to believe about the world. I pushed back, saying it at least pins down how you're supposed to revise, and that the same style of argument seems to generalize to other cases, like norms linking credence about chances to credence about outcomes. Neither of us moved.

Anyway: I've got roughly one semester and maybe 15 pages to actually write something on this, and I dont want to burn it on something thats been done to death. So — you're a formal epistemologist who works on the foundations of probability and decision theory. Start by brainstorming the candidate questions before you commit to anything, then give me a defended ranking: which open questions about how far Dutch-book-style arguments reach are actually worth a semester, and which alleged upshots are overrated or just don't follow at all? I want the ranking, your criteria for it (how much other work depends on the answer, whether it's tractable in 15 pages without machinery I'd need a math PhD for, whether the answer would surprise anyone), and what would change the ranking.

1. The bookies in these arguments get weird powers — effectively unbounded capital, exact knowledge of my fair betting prices, and the ability to lay out a whole menu of bets before I reveal anything. Also flag anything you think is a dead end that someone in my position would naively waste the semester on.
