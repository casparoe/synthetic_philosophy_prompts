You are a professional philosopher who has spent several years sitting on hospital resource-allocation committees, and who can write for lay trustees without condescension. Before you draft anything, think the problem through carefully: in particular, work out the strongest possible version of the recommendation you are *not* going to make, and deal with that version honestly rather than setting up something easier to knock down.

I sit on the Exceptional Therapies Allocation Committee of a regional public health plan. We administer a capped Fund that pays for a handful of very expensive one-time therapies a year. One slot remains this year, and there are two applicants for it.

Applicant A is 31, with advanced interstitial lung disease secondary to a childhood-onset genetic condition. No behavioural or occupational contribution. Applicant B is 46, at the same clinical stage, with disease that our clinical reviewers attribute with high confidence to about eleven years of work with degreasing solvents in a small metal-finishing shop (2005–2016). He took the job at 21; the solvents were legal and standard in the trade; he was never told of any fibrotic risk; no respiratory protective equipment was offered until 2014. The regional inspectorate's permissible exposure limit had been set in 1998 on a model that a 2018 revision concluded substantially understated the risk for that class of solvents. Our own occupational health unit inspected the shop in 2009 and 2013, issued no citations and sent no worker notification. The job paid roughly 15% more than the only other work available to him locally; at 21 he had no insurance and an infant to support. He left when the shop closed in 2016, and the disease has progressed since exposure ended. A class action against the solvent supplier settled in 2021 without admission of liability.

Two things constrain us. First, our clinical reviewers have scored the two applicants within the margin of error of each other on expected net benefit, so medical utility cannot decide it. Second, the Fund's charter says only that the Committee "may take into account whether an applicant's condition is materially attributable to choices the applicant made," with no definition of choice, attribution, or materiality. So the decision turns on that clause, and on what such a clause is even for. Please do not rest the memo on a tort-style argument about who wronged whom: the Fund is a treatment fund, not a compensation scheme, and nothing has admitted liability.

One of our members has circulated a proposed test, which I want you to evaluate as seriously as it deserves. Call it the fairly-presented-gamble test: even where an applicant did choose the risk-bearing activity, treat the outcome as brute rather than option luck when (i) an institution whose assurances the applicant had reason to rely on materially shaped his beliefs about the risk in a way it now concedes was mistaken, and (ii) his next-best alternative was not one he could reasonably have been expected to take. Her thought is that option luck can do its moral work only when the gamble was legibly and fairly presented: a person may be left to eat his losses only if he was playing with the odds he thought he was playing with and the house was not loading the dice.

Draft the decision memo I will circulate before Friday's meeting. It should contain, in this order: the bottom line in the first sentence — whom we fund, and whether we adopt the proposed test — followed by the reasoning. No preamble, no restatement of my question, no citations and no named literature: argue it in our own terms, since the trustees will not read footnotes.

Beyond the disposition, the memo should say whether the test, if adopted, should be narrowed, and what it does to the recurring cases we actually see — the long-term smoker, the man who stopped taking his antihypertensives despite free prescriptions, the home health aide who declined the vaccine her employer offered because of what she read on social media — and, if it makes the responsibility clause close to vacuous, say so plainly and propose the narrowing yourself. It should also dispatch, in a paragraph, the chair's rival view that we should ask only whether an applicant chose the *risk* and then treat everything downstream of a chosen risk as option luck, regardless of the magnitude or timing of the outcome. It should take a position on whether B's status as sole caregiver to a nine-year-old may count, given one member's objection that this is need smuggled in under the guise of responsibility. It should give the strongest objection to any responsibility screen in a health system at full force — that it expresses a judgement about how people live, and that even a softened test requires prying into applicants' lives. And it should end with what exactly we should record in the minutes so that future panels are bound consistently, plus a time frame: if you recommend deferring or gathering further information, specify precisely what and by when. Aim for about 1,000 words.
