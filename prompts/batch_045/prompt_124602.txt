Hey — I'm flying out in two weeks for a campus visit where I'm giving a 45-minute talk on the self, and I badly need Q&A prep; the paper itself I could now give in my sleep, it's the question period I'm dreading. The talk argues that Dennett's center of narrative gravity picture gets the direction of explanation backwards: the stories aren't the raw material a self coalesces out of, they're the audit trail. What really does the work, on my view, is what I call the fiduciary self — a posit, closer to a legal fiction or a promissory note than to an average, that an agent has to make and then keep re-making in order to have any future-directed commitments at all. Three moves: (1) a center of gravity is a free abstraction, you can compute one over any cloud of matter and it does real predictive work, but the narrative that's supposed to generate a self can't be just any cloud — it has to be the system's own, and it has to bind prospectively — so the metaphor is quietly hiding a normative condition; (2) a case I use throughout, a woman I call Marta who after a brain injury maintains two mutually inconsistent autobiographies depending on who's asking, and can switch between them mid-conversation, though she still makes and keeps promises across the switch — I claim the Dennettian picture has no principled answer to how many selves are in the room, while mine says the answer is given by where the actual commitments land; and (3) the payoff, that this is why the self has the emotional and moral weight it does, and why it's something you can lose without thereby ceasing to exist.

The room is what I need help with.
