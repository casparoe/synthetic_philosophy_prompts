I'm running a one-off session on scientific change for a mixed group — about 18 people, mostly philosophy grad students with a few history/STS folks and some advanced undergrads, in a room with a projector and movable tables, 2 hours with a short break in the middle. I want an activity where they *feel* incommensurability rather than hearing about it, ideally without anyone saying "paradigm" or "incommensurable" until the debrief.

Rough shape I have in mind, but please design it properly and feel free to change it: split them into two halves, induct each half separately into a different way of handling the same deck of invented specimen cards (learned by doing tasks, not by being handed the rule), then bring them together and force a collision over some scarce shared thing. What I care about is that the collision isn't a facts dispute and isn't a communication breakdown they can fix by talking longer — I want the moment where someone fully understands what the other person is saying, can restate it to their satisfaction, and still can't be moved, and can't say why in a way that bites.

So I need you to design the deck and the two schemes. Constraints: they have to be genuinely cross-cutting (each scheme splits the deck into kinds that the other one cuts straight through — no scheme is a refinement of the other), and each one has to be answerable to something checkable so that "which scheme is right" isn't settled by accuracy. My thought is that one scheme's payoff is temporal (predict the next card in a hidden series, spot the "reversions") and the other's is physical/interventive (predict whether a cut-out of the figure will balance on its base), with a held-out set where the two schemes come out roughly tied overall but lose badly on each other's home turf. Please also build in a deliberate "false friend" region of the deck where the schemes agree most of the time — I want them to overestimate how translatable the schemes are, get confident, and then get burned. And there should be one card that is a textbook exemplar for one scheme and a defective/marginal/noise case for the other, since that's where the real fight should land.

Mechanics I want spelled out: how the induction works in each room with no rule statements, what the shared scarce resource is (my guess: a reference collection with fewer slots than candidate specimens, so each scheme implies a different selection), a prediction-credit payoff scheme that needs no real money, a timeline in minutes for the whole 2 hours, and a cross-training phase near the end where each half learns the other's scheme followed by a quick re-test on their original tasks — I want an actual measure of how much learning the second scheme degraded the first, because I plan to use that number in the debrief to talk about gestalt switch vs. mere bilingualism.

Then the debrief: map specific moments onto the pieces of the idea, including what to do with the participants who end up genuinely fluent in both (that's the obvious objection to the whole exercise and I don't want to hand-wave it). Tell me what will definately go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. And be honest about which part of the real claim the activity can't reproduce and that I'll have to say out loud myself — I'd rather know now than discover it while 18 people are sitting there waiting for me to land the point.
