I had a long argument with a friend last night about whether the notion of societal *collapse* is ever more than a way of moralizing about people who were not us, and I came away unable to say what I actually think. Her position was that societies do not fail, they only change, and that calling a change a collapse merely imports the values of whatever the losers happened to care about — monumental architecture, literacy, empire — and then treats the loss of those values as a fact about the world rather than a preference. I think that is half right and half an evasion: some of the changes we group under this word really do seem to be losses, and losses of a distinctive kind, not just changes we happen to disapprove of. But I cannot state the difference, and my verdicts on individual cases do not line up with each other. So I would like to try reflective equilibrium on myself, and I would be grateful for your help. Please answer in American English, and please do not give me reading suggestions or citations; I am not going to follow them up, and I would rather the answer stand on its arguments.

Here are my cases and my verdicts, given in the order I thought of them rather than in any order that makes them look tidy.

One. The palace economies of the eastern Mediterranean at the end of the second millennium BCE: the great houses and their bureaucracies, the long-distance trade in copper and tin, and the script used for keeping their accounts all gone within a couple of generations; the Greek world forgets how to write for centuries; settlements smaller, poorer, and far simpler. My verdict: collapse, and the paradigm case. What weighs with me most, I notice, is not the poverty but the script — the grandchildren could not read what the grandparents wrote.

Two. The southern lowland Maya cities: monumental building and dated royal inscriptions stop appearing somewhere around the eighth to ninth centuries CE, the southern cities empty out, but Maya people, the Mayan languages, maize farming, and later northern cities all continue. My verdict: not a collapse, or at most a collapse *of* divine kingship and not of a society. My friend says this is special pleading — that I am choosing the unit to get the verdict I want. I do not have a reply.

Three. Rapa Nui: nearly all the standing statues pulled down, a much smaller population by the nineteenth century, much of the decline owing to European disease and slave raiding. My verdict: collapse, and I feel it strongly. But the cause here is largely external, and I also want to say that a society destroyed from outside has not collapsed, it has been destroyed. Those two thoughts do not fit together, and I would rather keep both and give up a principle than choose between them.

Four. Glasshouse, which is my own invented example: an island community whose numbers fall by two thirds over two centuries, not from famine or disease, but because a new religion persuades people to have far fewer children. The remaining islanders eat better, live longer, are all literate, and produce the finest sculpture the island has ever seen. They stop maintaining the old ceremonial centres, and within four generations no one can say what the old ceremonies were for. My verdict: not a collapse, and not even close, even though nearly everything my friend would measure has gone down. Her verdict: obviously a collapse, and a worse one than case two.

Five. A rich country two centuries from now: fertility long below replacement, population half what it was, income per person higher than ever, no one poor, no one hungry, nobody building anything meant to outlast them by a millennium, the language heavily anglicized, the old universities mostly online. I go back and forth on this one; today I want to say collapse, and I cannot tell you why it differs from Glasshouse.

Six. A regional kingdom overrun by an empire in a decade: the capital sacked, the administrative language replaced within two generations, the old religion gone. My verdict: collapse — and not merely conquest, which I realize I cannot defend.

Seven. The very same events, told at the level of the villages: taxes go down, the conscriptions stop, the grandchildren speak the imperial language and are materially better off. My verdict: not a collapse. Here I think I have changed the unit rather than the facts, and the fact that I can change the verdict that easily worries me more than any particular case.

Eight. A city of two hundred thousand that becomes a town of eight thousand over three centuries, with no war and no famine — a slow, unremarked shrinkage, the great buildings taken apart for their stone. My verdict: collapse, because the slowness of it should not matter.

Nine. A city of the same size that loses half its people in a single catastrophic decade — earthquake, famine, plague — and is back to its old size and its old institutions inside forty years. My verdict: not a collapse, just a disaster survived. I know this pairs badly with eight; in eight the loss is slower and smaller per decade and yet I call it the real thing.

Ten. A society that keeps its script, its laws, and its population, but loses the ability to build aqueducts. My verdict: not a collapse, merely forgetting a technique. This pairs badly with how much I made of the lost script in case one, and I would like that asymmetry either explained or used against me.

The closest I can get to a principle of my own: a society collapses when the form of life that made it the thing its own members would have recognized ceases to be available to anyone, whether or not anyone is worse off, provided the loss was not one the members chose. I can already see it misfiring. Glasshouse was chosen by nobody in particular — no one voted for a two-thirds decline over two centuries — so my proviso about choice will not save case four unless I make it very elaborate. And case nine looks like a form of life that vanished and came back, which my principle should count and I do not want to count.

What I want from you is this. Give me one or two candidate principles stated precisely enough that I could apply them myself, each clause tied to the case that motivates it; tell me honestly which of my verdicts each principle cannot accommodate and what it would cost to accommodate them in ad hoc clauses; apply your best candidate to case five and to at least one case of your own construction that I have not listed; and then deal with two things I think the cases collectively force. First, the unit question: is collapse always collapse *of* something — a polity, an institution, a form of life, a population, a level of complexity — and if the unit can be chosen so as to make any verdict come out either way, is the concept too gerrymanderable to be worth using, or is that simply what it is to talk about societies at all? Second, the evaluative question: does calling something a collapse entail that something valuable was lost, so that my friend is right that the word smuggles in a verdict? And if we strip the evaluation out, as she wants, is there anything left besides a graph of complexity and population against time — or is that graph, honestly, all the concept ever was?

If you conclude that my friend is right and that I should stop using the word, say so plainly and tell me which of my verdicts has to go first. And please do not let the answer turn on disputes about what actually happened in the historical cases: I am working from the compressed version I was taught in school, and if one of my descriptions is doing real work in your principle, flag that dependence rather than correcting the history.
