**Question 3 (20 marks).** Origin essentialism is the thesis that a thing could not have originated from something wholly different from what it actually originated from — in the biological case, that you could not have come from a sperm and egg other than the ones you actually came from.

(a) Set the thesis out carefully, and explain whether it's just a consequence of the standard Kripkean package (rigid designation, the necessity of identity, necessary a posteriori truths) or whether it needs independent motivation of its own. (8 marks)

(b) Now suppose that a *different* sperm had fertilized the same egg your mother actually released, but a sperm that happened to carry exactly the same set of chromosomes as the one that in fact fertilized it — recombination makes that wildly improbable, but not impossible. The resulting child would be genetically indistinguishable from you. Would that child have been you? Say what your answer shows about what, exactly, origin essentialism is a thesis about. (6 marks)

(c) Give your own verdict on origin essentialism, and state the strongest objection to it. (6 marks)

Here's one answer, typos and all:

Origin essentialism (OE) is the claim that, necessarily, any organism that originates from gametes originates from the very gametes it actually originated from. It's a de re modal claim about each thing, not the de dicto claim that every organism has some origin or other — that weaker claim is uncontroversial and gets you nothing, since it's perfectly consistent with my having come from a completely different pair of gametes.

Is OE just part of the Kripkean package? Not straightforwardly. The necessity of identity on its own is silent here: it says that if a = b then necessarily a = b, and there's no pair of objects whose identity is in question when we ask whether I could have come from different gametes. So OE needs its own argument. Here's the best one. Take the description "the human being that developed from gametes S and E". On Kripke's picture this rigidly designates me — it picks out me in every world in which I exist, rather than picking out whoever happens to satisfy the description. And a rigid designator picks out the same object with respect to every world in which that object exists, so with respect to every world in which I exist, I developed from S and E. That's exactly the de re necessity OE wants, and it's a posteriori, since which sperm and egg I came from is an empirical matter. That's why OE is a necessary a posteriori thesis rather than something you could settle from the armchair.

(b) In the duplicate-sperm case the child would not be me. OE is a thesis about a thing's causal-historical source, and a distinct sperm is a distinct source even if it's genetically identical, so we have a different origin and hence a different organism. What this shows is that the essential property OE postulates isn't a qualitative or genetic one: it's the haecceitistic property of having come from that very sperm and that very egg. So OE has to be read as a thesis about individuals rather than about genomes, and a version of OE stated in terms of genetic make-up would give the wrong verdict here. That's a real commitment, but it's one the view already has in the ordinary case, so the case doesn't add any new objection.

(c) I think OE is false. The strongest objection is the conceivability argument. I can describe, without contradiction, a world in which my parents conceive a child from a different sperm and that child grows up with my body, my memories and my life. There's no contradiction in the description, and conceivability is our only guide to metaphysical possibility, so such a world is possible — and I exist in it. So it's possible for me to have come from different gametes, and OE is false. The proponent's response, that the child in that world is someone else who merely resembles me, is just a restatement of OE, so it has no independent force. The one thing on the proponent's side with real force is the point about ordinary counterfactuals: it's sometimes objected that OE makes "I could have been born a year later" impossible, since a year later my parents would have used different gametes. But that objection is weak — that counterfactual is about timing, and we can hold fixed which gametes fuse while varying when they fuse, so OE is consistent with all the ordinary counterfactuals about ourselves.
