I teach a second-year rational choice / decision theory course — 22 students, mostly philosophy and econ majors, one 90-minute slot, and I've got a projector and a big table at the front but nothing else. They've seen expected utility but no real decison theory past that.

I want a game that makes them feel the Ellsberg pattern instead of me putting the two urns on a slide and announcing that people are weird. The thing I want them to run into on their own: the usual pattern (I'd rather bet on red from the 50/50 urn than red from the unknown one, and likewise for black) can't come from any single probability assignment plugged into expected utility. My idea is that they each price all four bets separately, and then notice that their price for red-from-known plus black-from-known, and their price for red-from-unknown plus black-from-unknown, are both prices for packages that pay off for sure. No lecture up front, and they shouldn't hear the words ambiguity aversion or Ellsberg until the debrief.

The wrinkle I want to add, which I can't find anywhere: run the ambiguous urn in three flavours at once, one per third of the room — composition set by a shuffle/randomiser, by a classmate who gets a bonus if the bettors lose, and by a classmate who is herself blindfolded and just pulled a slip from a bag, so she knows nothing but is visibly a person. My hunch is the aversion is way stronger in the second condition, and that this says something about whether the phenomenon is really about missing information or about being in a situation where someone might know more than you than you do. Build it in and tell me how to read whatever comes out.

Then a last stage where they bet on the ambiguous urn repeatedly, either reusing one fixed urn with replacement or drawing a fresh one each round — you tell me which is cleaner — so that the long-run frequency pulls toward 50%. I want them to feel the force of "over enough rounds this is just a coin flip," and also to feel why that argument might not settle the question.

Need: actual rules, materials, a payoff scheme with no real money (points, snacks, bragging rights, whatever), a timeline, a debrief that maps specific moments onto specific bits of the argument, and honest notes on what will go wrong the first time I run it and what the game simply can't reproduce so I'll have to say it myself. Also my students will instantly say they'd just take both bets, so the setup has to block that move without me tipping them off as to why.
