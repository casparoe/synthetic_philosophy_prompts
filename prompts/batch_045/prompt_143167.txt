I run a weekly reading group on language and mind — mostly graduate students in cognitive science and philosophy, plus two working literary translators and a speech-recognition engineer. We are about to spend two sessions on whether statistical and neural machine translation systems understand what they translate, using as our entry point Hofstadter's complaint that such systems are shallow: that they work on the surfaces of sentences without anything like situation-modelling, sensitivity to register and style, or a felt sense of the music of language, and that this is a difference in kind rather than in degree. (The 1997 book of his on translation, built around dozens of competing renderings of one short Renaissance French poem, is the background text several of us have read.)

The problem I want the handout to solve is that the people in the room who agree that machine translation does not really understand turn out to mean at least four different things: that it lacks any connection to non-linguistic experience; that it is insensitive to form, rhythm, and the way sound carries meaning; that it has no model of the whole text, the author, or the reader, so it cannot make the large-scale choices a translator makes; and that it has no self and nothing it is like to be it, so nothing is at stake in what it produces. Those who deny the charge also split: some hold that systematic reliability under variation is all that understanding could consist in, others that the question has no determinate answer. I want an instrument that sorts people by what they say about cases, not by which of these descriptions they find flattering.

What I would like from you is a ten-question diagnostic quiz with a scoring key, plus the map of the logical space it carves up. Constraints:

Ten questions, no more, no less. Each gives a short concrete case, then exactly three answer options. One question should be built around a formal constraint (a verse that must remain singable to a given tune, or must keep a rhyme scheme and syllable count); if a concrete text is needed there, compose an original four-line verse for the purpose rather than reproducing a real one, and state the constraint explicitly.

No question may name a theory, a position, a school, or a thinker, and none may ask whether the reader accepts a labelled view — including the Chinese Room, which should not appear as a question or as a leaf at all. The cases have to do the discriminating work that thought experiment usually does in these discussions; if a question turns out to be that scenario in costume, cut it. Every question must be answerable by someone who has used online translation tools but has never opened a philosophy of language syllabus.

At least three of the ten must be cases that do not circulate in the standard discussion. I have in mind: translating, out of a language with no evidential marking, into one where every past-tense statement must be marked as witnessed, inferred, or reported; translating a text that is itself the output of a machine translation out of the target language, so the system is being asked to render a translation of its own kind of output; a case where the author of the source text later stated an intention that the text on the page does not support; and intralingual updating of an archaic text into the modern version of the same language. Use at least three of those four, and add at least one case of your own construction.

The outcomes the key must be able to deliver, each with a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment: content-first or proposition-first views; purpose-relative views on which adequacy is fixed by the use the translation is for; a form-and-music view in something like Hofstadter's spirit, on which what is lost when form is lost is itself part of the meaning; a grounding or embodiment requirement; a whole-text and theory-of-mind requirement; a reliability-first, intentional-stance functionalism; and an indeterminacy-style deflationism on which there is no fact of the matter about whether a system understands. Add an eliminativist leaf about understanding itself only if the space genuinely supports it. Make the quiz maximally sensitive to the four complaints I listed, and where two questions test the same boundary, say so in the key rather than quietly keeping both.

Please structure the response in these sections, in this order:

1. The ten questions — around 1,000 words. Case, then three options, nothing else; no commentary or hints about where an option leads.
2. Scoring key — a table, roughly 300 words' worth, mapping each option to the leaf or leaves it leans toward, followed by about 200 words on how to score mixed or unstable patterns, which two or three questions carry the most diagnostic weight, and which questions are less discriminating than they look.
3. The logical space — about 350 words. Set out the three or four distinctions the ten questions jointly define, the cells they generate, and which leaf sits in which cell, rather than starting from a list of names.
4. Empty cells — about 200 words. Which combinations of answers no actual position occupies, and for each whether that is because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored.
5. What the quiz cannot separate — about 200 words. Pairs of leaves that stay stuck together on every question, and one further question each that would pry them apart in a longer version.
6. Running the session — about 120 words. A suggested order for discussing the questions, the split you would expect in a mixed group like mine, and one way the exercise is likely to go wrong.

Keep the whole thing under about 2,200 words, and write the questions in plain, non-technical language.
