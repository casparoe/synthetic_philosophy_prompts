I'm in the second semester of a political theory MA and I have to hand in a prospectus for an ~8,000 word term paper in about ten days, and I'd rather write it on something I actually care about than on a safe summary of a debate. Me and two people from my study group have been going in circles about this for weeks: everyone we read seems to treat alienation as basically the experiential face of exploitation — you get exploited, and the felt upshot is that your work doesn't belong to you — and I've got this suspicion that this has never actually been argued for, just assumed, and that it might be false. So the half-formed idea I want turned into a proposal is that we should prise them apart. Take what the 1844 Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts bundles together (estrangement from the product, from the activity of labour, from species-being, from other people) and split it into at least three conditions that could in principle dissociate: not controlling your own labour process; not authoring the ends your labour serves; and not being able to recognise yourself in what you do. Then ask which of them, if any, is a wrong in its own right, and whether any of them can show up in a case where nobody is being exploited in the surplus-value sense.

The two test cases I keep coming back to are a worker-owned coop where production decisions are effectively dictated by a platform's demand algorithm (no outside owner skimming surplus, but nobody would call the work self-directed), and on the other side a self-employed craftsperson or small contractor who genuinely loves and fully controls her work, whose entire surplus is creamed off by a monopsony client or a landlord. If that dissociation holds up, worker ownership and market socialism are a much thinner fix than the tradition advertises, and the real target is the organisation of labour rather than the property relation. If every apparent case of alienation-without-exploitation turns out to be exploitation in a cleverer disguise, my thesis is dead and I'd rather find that out now than in week ten.

What I need is the proposal document itself, addressed to a supervisor: the question stated tightly enough that it has a determinate-ish answer; why the standard reading plus the obvious escape routes don't settle it (just dropping alienation as philosophical mysticism, or just redefining it as republican unfreedom, or just saying it's a hangover from Hegel and Feuerbach); the actual method — what I'd do week by week over roughly three months, on the assumption I already know the basic texts and don't need reading time budgeted — and why that method rather than a straight conceptual analysis of 'labour'; what a finished deliverable would look like; and honest scope, including where this would balloon past 8,000 words. Don't give me a bibliography and don't cite anything, I'll fill references in myself — I want the argumentative architecture, not sources. Also flag confidence levels: tell me how much of this is genuinely open versus already handled in the literature, name the weakest link you see, and be blunt if my core distinction is confused or if I'm telescoping the early humanist Marx into the later one in a way that won't survive contact with the actual texts — say so plainly rather than politely building me a proposal around a bad frame.
