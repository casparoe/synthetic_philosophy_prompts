I sit on the program committee of a small foundation that is spending itself down and has, practically speaking, one grant cycle left: enough to support roughly eight researchers for three years, mostly postdocs and a few senior people on released time. The donor's mandate is narrow in a way that has made our job harder rather than easier. He wants work on a single problem: how meaning gets located in an object when we are not entitled to assume in advance that the object is a message, what code it is written in, or what it would be about. That is, the case where what is called the frame message — the fact that this is a message at all, produced by something with something to say — the outer message, the code or system you have to recover in order to read it, and the inner message, what it says, are all unknown simultaneously. Call it the triple-underdetermination case. His motivating examples are an extraterrestrial transmission, an undeciphered script with no bilingual informant, and — the case I suspect he actually cares about — the internal states of a trained neural network, treated as candidates for saying something rather than merely causing behavior.

Two constraints come with the money. First, no new data collection: the work has to be conceptual or computational over material that already exists, and it has to be evaluable without a Rosetta stone, that is, without any access to the source that would let you check your reading from the outside. Second, three years. Anything whose payoff requires a decade of accumulated case studies is not eligible.

I want a defended agenda, not a survey of the area. Specifically:

Name the three or four open questions you would fund, in priority order, and give the reasoning for the ordering. For each one: what other questions its answer would unlock or render pointless; how far eight people could realistically get with it in three years given the no-Rosetta condition; whether it is genuinely neglected or already crowded; and what concrete result in the next two years would move it up or down your list.

Then tell me what you would defund. There are lines of work in this vicinity that are currently fashionable and that I suspect rest on a claim that does not actually follow — for instance, the idea that a general-purpose detector of anomalous structure would deliver the frame message without any decoding, or the idea that once the outer message is cracked the inner message is essentially automatic, or the idea that a message can be shown to be self-revealing in the way mid-century proposals for a from-scratch cosmic language, bootstrapping itself from arithmetic up to everyday vocabulary, were supposed to be. Say which of these are real implications and which are non sequiturs, and why.

Finally, and this is the part I care most about: tell me whether the frame-outer-inner decomposition is the right scaffolding for the agenda at all. It may be that the three levels are not separable in the way the picture suggests — that in practice we never trust a frame detection until we have decoded something, and never finish decoding without having guessed at content, so that the ordering is an artifact of hindsight rather than a structure of the problem. Or it may be that the picture quietly assumes meaning has a single location, which is exactly what is at issue. If you think the scaffolding is wrong, say so, say what you would replace it with, and then redo the prioritization in those terms; that is a legitimate answer, but you do not get to skip the ranking by dissolving the framework.

Please be committed: give me an ordering and a defunding list with reasons, not a menu of considerations for me to weigh. Where your ranking depends on a guess about tractability rather than something you can argue for — where a question might in fact be uncrackable in three years, or might collapse into another question on your list — mark those places explicitly and say what you would switch to if the guess goes the other way.
