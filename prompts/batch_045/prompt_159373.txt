I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I joined a reading group at my office that is working through Jonathan Haidt's The Righteous Mind (2012). I am trying to work out what the psychology in that book actually entitles anyone to conclude about who is right.

The way people in the group summarize the theory is with a taste analogy, and they lean on it heavily. The moral mind is supposed to be like a tongue with a small number of innate receptors: care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, sanctity/degradation, with liberty/oppression added later and further candidates floated since. Culture plays the role of a cuisine, training the palate by determining which receptors get triggered by which events and how strongly. A political disagreement is then supposed to be like two people disagreeing about whether a dish is good. The analogy is used to license a conclusion: since the difference between left and right is a difference in which receptors are wired up and how readily they fire, there is no fact of the matter about which side is correct, moral argument is mostly post hoc rationalization of a gut response, and the only defensible posture is to suspend judgment and treat the other side as having a different palate rather than a false belief.

I would like an audit rather than a verdict on the theory. Please lay the mapping out explicitly — a correspondence table is fine — covering at least: receptor, the chemical that triggers it, palate, cuisine, the judgment that a dish is disgusting, and whoever plays the role of the food critic; and say what each is supposed to be in the target domain. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and separate the breaks that matter for the conclusion above from the ones that are cosmetic.

Five things I want addressed in particular:

1. Innate but culturally trained describes where a judgment comes from, not whether it is correct. Vision is also an innate, modular, culturally shaped system, and nobody infers from that there is no fact of the matter about what is in front of me. Is the analogy doing any normative work at all, or is it purely a claim about etiology?

2. The analogy seems to import a contestable thesis about taste: that taste judgments are bare appetite, about which nothing is right or wrong. But taste talk has critics, expertise, improvement, and reasons — a dish is oversalted, its acidity is out of balance — and it responds to facts about what is on the plate. Even granting the mapping, does it deliver no facts and no better or worse, or only something weaker: disagreement that no single argument from first principles will settle?

3. The foundations come as a fixed small set whose number has already changed once. What does the analogy inherit from the fixed-receptors assumption, and what happens to it if the set is not fixed — or if the foundations are better described as a derived clustering, with care and fairness on one side as individualizing and loyalty, authority, and sanctity on the other as binding, than as elementary receptors?

4. One member of the group pushed the analogy the other way: if morality is like taste, then moral progress should look like refinement of a palate — finer discrimination, better-trained critics, convergence among people who attend closely — which seems to reintroduce better and worse and to contradict the agnostic conclusion. Is that an internal problem for the analogy, or does the analogy simply have no resources for describing moral change, which would itself be telling?

5. The evidence behind the picture is largely self-report questionnaires plus the dumbfounding studies, where people condemn harmless consensual acts and cannot say why. Does the analogy inherit the limits of that evidence, and does dumbfounding support the no-fact-of-the-matter conclusion, or only the weaker claim that people cannot articulate their reasons?

Finally: does the suspend-judgment conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the taste picture carrying it the whole time? If it does not survive, is there a better analogy that preserves what the psychology genuinely shows — fast, intuitive, emotionally charged, culturally variable moral response — without smuggling in the metaethical claim? Please say plainly which parts of the analogy are doing the work and which are decoration.
