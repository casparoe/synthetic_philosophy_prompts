I'm writing a short paper on mechanistic anomaly detection, and before I revise it I want a reader who will argue with it rather than summarize it. The draft below states what I take the central problem to be, introduces four cases I intend to use throughout, and ends with a trilemma I am not confident is real. What I want from you is commentary on the argument, not on the prose: where it fails, where it is weaker than it sounds, where it is stronger than I have noticed, and what I should do with it. I don't need references or citations — I'll do the literature myself — and I don't need to be told what the standard views in the field are unless you think one of them already contains the move I'm missing.

Here is the draft.

Mechanistic anomaly detection asks, of a reference system M and a target system M′ trained or fine-tuned from it, which inputs are anomalous: inputs on which M′ does something M would not do, and does it because the mechanism M′ brings to bear is not the mechanism M brings to bear. My claim is that the word because carries all the weight here and that no available account of when two components are the same mechanism can carry it. Accounts divide into two families. Behavioural accounts say a component c of M and a component c′ of M′ are the same mechanism when they support the same counterfactuals: intervene on c and on c′ in corresponding ways and the rest of the system behaves correspondingly. Structural accounts say they are the same when they occupy corresponding positions and are built of corresponding parts. The two families fail, and they fail in opposite directions on cases we all want to handle.

Take the behavioural family first, and the problem of finding the counterpart. To run an interchange test you need, for each c in M, some c′ in M′ to swap it with. Every way of fixing that correspondence either presupposes the sameness being tested or makes the test redundant. Align by similarity of weights or of activations and you have assumed that similar internal states realize the same mechanism, which is the structural view the test was supposed to replace. Align by causal role, taking c′ to be whatever plays c's role as identified by what it receives and what it feeds, and you have already established what the test was meant to establish; the subsequent check that the two respond alike to intervention is then either confirmation of a tautology or a test of your alignment method. So the interchange test is circular or vacuous. I call this the alignment regress, and I think it is the deepest of the difficulties below, because it arises before any of the other choices are made.

The four cases are these. In the relabeled network, M′ is M with a symmetry applied: attention heads permuted together with the matching rows of the output projection, or the hidden units of a multilayer block permuted together with the corresponding rows and columns of the matrices around them. The two compute exactly the same function, but every unit-by-unit comparison between them is a mismatch, so a detector that compares internals directly reports massive anomaly where nothing has changed. In the split circuit, M implements a check with a single internal feature and M′ implements it with two features, each sufficient on the training distribution and both required off it; ablate either one alone and M breaks while M′ does not, so the two look the same under simultaneous interventions and different under single-unit ones. In the graft, M′ solves a new task by wrapping M's old circuit in a new front end that routes fresh inputs into it; the old circuit is intact, but the interventions that can now be performed on it are a different set, so what it does is no longer the same thing it did. In the degenerate twin, M′ matches M on the whole training distribution while reaching its outputs by a different route — a generalizing circuit in one, something closer to lookup in the other — so that the two are indistinguishable by behaviour on the distribution and diverge everywhere else.

The first lesson is that the relabeled network and the degenerate twin pull in opposite directions. The first demands a criterion insensitive to internal structure, since the internals differ and nothing else does. The second demands a criterion sensitive to internal structure, since the behaviour on the observed distribution is identical and everything else differs. The second lesson is that no single ordering resolves this, because sameness is not transitive once it is relativized. Call two mechanisms the same relative to a triple of an intervention class, a distribution, and a granularity. Then a coarse-grained mechanism can be the same as each of two fine-grained mechanisms that are not the same as each other, so the relation does not partition the systems into mechanisms and a count of anomalies depends on arbitrary choices of grain. The third lesson is that even the easy case is only easy relative to a purpose. The relabeled networks are the same mechanism for the purpose of predicting behaviour and, I think, for the purpose of predicting further training; they are not the same mechanism for the purpose of operating on them in weight space, since averaging the weights of a network with a permuted copy of itself destroys the function that both compute. So the question is not whether the mechanism is the same but whether it is the same for what, and the answer to that is not determined by the two systems.

That leaves me with a trilemma I cannot decide about. Any adequate criterion should be invariant under relabeling, should discriminate the degenerate twin from the genuine one, and should return a verdict determined by finite evidence about the two systems. Invariance under relabeling and discrimination of the degenerate twin jointly require internal structure to matter in some places and not in others, and the only principled way I can see to say where is by fixing the intervention class, the distribution, the grain, and the purpose. But nothing in the finite evidence fixes those four, and for any finite body of evidence and any choice of them I can, it seems, describe a pair of systems that agree on the evidence and disagree in verdict under a different choice that is equally defensible. If that is right, then anomaly detection without a stated purpose is not hard, it is ill-posed, much as asking what state a lamp is in at the limit of a supertask is ill-posed without a further specification of the dynamics; the description simply does not determine an answer. What I cannot tell is whether this is a real result about mechanisms or only the banal underdetermination of theory by data wearing a costume.

That is the draft. What I want from you is this.

First, whether the alignment regress is sound. In particular, is there a way of fixing correspondences that is neither similarity-based nor already causal-role-based, and if the answer is that alignment is established by causal role, does that make the interchange test redundant or does it leave real work for the test to do? I would like you to try to defuse it rather than to grant it.

Second, whether the non-transitivity point survives the obvious reply, which is that a working detector fixes the grain once and for all and then gets transitivity back by stipulation. Does that reply concede too much, given that the choice of grain is exactly what the anomaly count depends on?

Third, whether the trilemma is genuine or whether I have smuggled the conclusion in by way of the purpose-relativity, and if it is only underdetermination, how I would tell the difference.

Fourth, which of the four cases is the weakest link, which of them a hostile referee would press on, and which one you would build the paper around instead.

Fifth, whether I have conflated anything. I am most suspicious of my own use of anomaly: there may be a perfectly good notion of an anomalous input that never mentions mechanisms at all, in which case my because is optional and half the paper collapses, and I would rather find that out now. I am also unsure whether I am using mechanism to mean a component, a process, or a capacity, and I suspect I slide between the three.

Finally, tell me what you think the paper is actually about, in one paragraph, and which paragraph of the draft above you would cut or move. If you think the interesting claim is one I have buried in a parenthetical, say which.
