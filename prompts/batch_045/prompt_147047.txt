I'm in a small philosophy of mind reading group — we meet in the back room of a pub, so apologies if any of this is sloppier than it should be — and last week we got onto pain asymbolia, and I ended up defending a position I'm not sure I can actually defend. Roughly: pain has at least two separable components, a sensory-discriminative one (where it is, how intense, what kind) and an affective-motivational one (how much it bothers you, how much you want it gone), and the badness of pain lives entirely in the second. Asymbolia is my star witness: patients who can still tell you that yes, that's a pinprik, sharp, on the left forearm, fairly intense, and who still wince and pull away, but who report that it doesn't really bother them and who seem genuinely not to care. If you could keep everything else about the pain and just delete the unpleasantness, there'd be nothing left to regret — so the unpleasantness is where the badness was all along. A friend in the group says I have it backwards. She grants the dissociation but thinks it shows the badness is a matter of desires, or cares, or what the subject is disposed to do about it, not a felt quality: on her view unpleasantness just *is* your minding it, and I'm inventing a spooky extra phenomenal ingredient that does no work.

So: what would count against my view? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want at least as much attention to the ones a sympathetic separationist should concede as to the ones only a critic would push. Some things I've been chewing on, where I want you to take a position rather than just note that people disagree:

- The worry that asymbolia isn't the clean case I want it to be. If asymbolic subjects still show basically normal autonomic arousal to painful stimuli (heart rate, skin conductance, that sort of thing), and if whatever lesion produces it tends also to blunt other interoceptive alarm states like breathlessness or nausea, does that make asymbolia a deficit in something downstream of the badness — attention, or reporting, or action — rather than a deletion of the badness itself? What exactly would the data have to look like for me to have to give up the case? And what would "give up" even mean here: does my view *need* asymbolia, or is asymbolia just nice marketing for it?
- A mirror-image case I made up, which I can't tell is coherent. Imagine a subject with the exact inverse profile: no withdrawal, no avoidance learning, no autonomic response, no motivation for it to stop — but who insists when asked that it is in fact pretty unpleasant, they just don't mind. If that's possible, does it hurt me or my friend more? And if it isn't possible, is that because "unpleasantness" just is the motivational/attitudinal package (so she wins), or because you can't have the feeling without the urge to act for some independent reason?
- An "asymbolia for other things" test. If the same dissociation shows up on the pleasant side — the thing people talk about in addiction research where wanting and enjoying come apart, or in anhedonia where someone reports liking things fine but never gets around to wanting them — does a unified story, one mechanism of "caring" that can be knocked out across the board, favor her desire view over mine? Or is it neutral between us?
- The triviality worry, which is the one that actually scares me. If I end up defining unpleasantness as "that feature of the experience in virtue of which it's bad for you", haven't I made my claim unfalsifiable, just a relabeling? Is there any way to pin down unpleasantness independently — introspectively, or by its functional role, or by what I'd say about aversive states that aren't pains — so that "badness = unpleasantness" is a real claim running a real risk of being wrong? And if there isn't, is that fatal for the view, or just a limitation of how the argument can be conducted?

Please don't just tell me the empirical picture is messy or that intuitions differ. Assume I'm a committed hedonic separationist who wants to know whether I'm wrong, and tell me which of these should actually move me, which I'd be entitled to wave off, and where you think the burden of proof honestly sits.
