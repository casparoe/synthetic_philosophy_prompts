In the reading group on multimodal models that I sit in on, a move keeps being made that I think needs auditing before anyone builds on it. The claim is that attaching a vision encoder to a model trained on text alone is Molyneux's problem finally run as a real experiment rather than a thought experiment: the model is the man born blind who has learned to distinguish a cube from a sphere by touch, the arrival of image inputs is the sudden restoration of sight, and whatever we can get the model to do zero-shot across the two modalities is his answer to the old question of whether, seeing them for the first time, he could tell which was which before handling them. The reason anyone cares is that the human evidence — patients whose congenital cataracts were removed late in life, the handful of cases that get endlessly re-described — has never convinced anyone, so the model is being offered as the clean version of the test, and the conclusions being drawn from it are not cautious: if the model can align visual inputs with shape words and descriptions it has only ever met in text, then sensory concepts do transfer across modalities and the question gets a yes; if it cannot, then something in the nativist direction is vindicated and there is a genuine gap between what can be learned about a sense from language and what the sense delivers.

I would like a proper audit of that comparison, and I would like the mapping made explicit before it is attacked: a table of what plays the subject, the tactile learning, the first moment of sight, the cube and the globe, and the act of telling which is which. Then the disanalogies, sorted into the ones that actually matter for the conclusion people want and the ones that are cosmetic. The ones I keep returning to, in case they are useful starting points rather than instructions: the model has no continuity of subject and no first-person view, so nothing in it first has tactile experience and later has visual experience; its training corpus is saturated with the verbal reports of sighted people, which makes it less a blind man than a blind man who has read everything sighted people have ever said about seeing; whatever success it shows may consist in a geometry of a shared embedding space rather than in anything recognitional; and whatever failure it shows may be an artefact of how the probe is phrased. I would also like the question itself taken apart, because I do not think it is one question: there is visually discriminating the two shapes from each other, there is applying the right shape term, and there is judging the thing now seen to be the same kind of thing one previously handled — and it seems to me that the philosophically loaded one is the third, and that it is not obvious it is even well defined for something that has no experiences to unify.

What I want at the end is a verdict rather than a list of caveats. Does the model case support, refute, or simply fail to bear on Molyneux's question? If it fails to bear, is that because the system lacks the wrong feature — a single subject with a phenomenology persisting across the transition — or because the setup quietly substitutes a different question about statistical association in a corpus for the old one about a person's capacities? And if the analogy is beyond repair, is there a better one? My own candidate, which I offer only to have it attacked, is that the model is not Molyneux's subject at all but a Molyneux subject supplied with the entire visual testimony of the species, and that the nearest approach to a clean control would be to retrain it with the visual vocabulary of its corpus excised, so that it has the concepts only through touch-like descriptions and not through reports of seeing; but I would rather hear whether that gets any closer, or whether the whole project of settling a question about what a subject can recognise by appealing to a system that cannot be said to recognise anything is confused from the outset.
