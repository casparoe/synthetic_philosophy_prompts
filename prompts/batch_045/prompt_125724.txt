I'm a staff software engineer at a mid-sized logistics company, and for the last two years I've been teaching (voluntarily, and with diminishing confidence) an internal eight-week seminar for senior engineers called "Decisions Under Constraints" — half engineering judgment, half what I've managed to pick up on my own about bounded rationality and search. I have no formal philosophy background, so I'm writing to you for the philosophical substance of the final assessment I have to set and mark by the end of the month. My students are strong engineers who have read exactly one chapter of Simon plus whatever they half-remember from blog posts about explore/exploit; they are very good at optimization and will instinctively assume that any procedure worth using is secretly maximizing something. They can't consult the web during the exam, and I don't want them to be able to pass by reciting "satisficing is choosing a good-enough option relative to an aspiration level." The burden of the question should instead be the thing a colleague on my infra team threw at me over lunch: once you put a term for the cost of search into the objective function, a satisficer just looks like an optimizer with a bigger objective function, so either satisficing is a trivial redescription of optimization or it was never a genuine alternative.

The apparatus I want the question built around is a made-up agent I've been using in seminar, which I call Searcher-R. It faces options one at a time in an order it can't control; each option has a well-defined utility that it knows on inspection; inspecting an option costs a fixed amount, so it can't inspect everything; it holds an aspiration level starting at some A₀ and accepts the first option whose utility meets its current aspiration; after each acceptance it ratchets — the aspiration rises to a fixed fraction β of the value just obtained (with 0 < β < 1) and never slips below where it already was; and if it goes some number m of inspections in a row without accepting, it drops the aspiration by a fixed step δ, never below A₀. I want the question to drive students through three moves: first, to show with a tiny concrete example that Searcher-R can take an option in one presentation order and pass over that very same option in another, and to say why no agent maximizing a fixed ranking over options could behave that way; second, to face the obvious reply that this is no objection because we should judge *policies* rather than options — a stopping rule can be optimal once search costs are priced in, and in fact for any stopping rule there is some search cost that makes it optimal — and to say whether that reply rescues satisficing, trivializes it, or something in between; third, to say what "rational" would have to mean for Searcher-R to be genuinely criticizable or genuinely praiseworthy, teasing apart internal consistency of choice, fit between procedure and environment, and whether the optimizing standard being invoked is one Searcher-R itself could actually apply given its computational limits.

Please hand me three things. First, the exam question itself, written as I'd put it on the page for a 90-minute take-home: self-contained, no readings or citations required, with the specification of Searcher-R spelled out and the marks for each part indicated. Second, a model answer of about 700 words. Third, a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit moves — four marks each — where the differentiators include: the difference between representing behaviour and prescribing it; the difference between a ranking over options and a policy over sequences; the unfalsifiability worry about post-hoc search costs; the first-person/third-person distinction about whose computation has to be feasible; and the claim that rationality is a relation between a mind and an environment rather than a property of behaviour on its own. For each move, give me one line on how engineers typically get it wrong — I expect the characteristic failure is reaching for "the aspiration level adapts, therefore it's still optimizing" — and finish with a short band note saying what separates an A from a B from a C. Please define any term an engineer wouldn't know (as-if representation, reservation price, dynamic programming, ecological rationality, procedural versus substantive rationality) rather than assuming it, and don't lean on any author, title, or date I'd have to look up.
