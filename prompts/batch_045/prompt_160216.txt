I am applying for a twelve-month dissertation-completion fellowship in archaeology and need a two-page research proposal, which I will afterwards cut down to a one-page summary for the same panel. Two stipulations: I do not want a bibliography or a literature review organised by citation — I have my own reading list and will insert references myself — and I do not want invented site names, excavation reports, stratigraphic details or radiocarbon dates, since I will have to defend every concrete fact I put in the file. Descriptions of *types* of site and *types* of assemblage are what I want instead.

The topic is the inference from material patterns to institutions. My half-formed idea is this. When archaeologists attribute a pattern to an institution — a standing office, a treasury, a priesthood, a permanent storehouse — rather than to a sequence of episodes, the argument usually runs through scale and standardisation: large storage facilities, seal-impressed vessel stoppers, metrically uniform containers, redundant administrative markings. But it seems to me that scale and standardisation are exactly what a well-rehearsed *event* would also produce, or what many separate episodes would produce if each copied its predecessor. A recurring festival, a mobilisation for a building project, or a season of harvest-time pooling can all generate deposits that look institution-sized. So the question I want sharpened is whether there is any material signature that discriminates a standing office from a repeated episode, and what shape such an argument would have to take to be convincing.

My tentative answer, which the proposal should develop rather than merely assert, is that a standing institution has to solve a problem that an event does not: it must hand over its equipment, its procedures and its records to a successor who was not present when they were made. That constraint should leave traces of a kind I would like to call successor-legibility: redundant and conventionalised marking rather than context-specific marking, modular and replaceable components, and repairs that preserve function while sacrificing the integrity of the original object. An event's equipment is legible to its participants and is characteristically deposited intact, unmaintained, and never adapted for reuse by strangers.

What I need from the proposal:

1. A precise statement of the question, narrowed enough that it has a determinate answer within a year, including a decision about how many rival generative hypotheses are in play (I have in mind at least three: a standing office, a recurring event with no office, and a one-off episode, but you may restructure this).
2. Why the existing positions leave the question open — in particular, why the standard move from standardisation-plus-scale to institutional attribution is underwritten by an assumption no one has isolated, and why the sceptical position that institutions are simply invisible in the material record is too cheap.
3. The method, month by month. I want the core of it to be a forward-modelling exercise: specify the three generative processes, derive what each should predict about assemblage composition, repair, discard and deposition, then test whether the predicted signatures can be separated at the sample sizes and preservation conditions real assemblages actually afford. Say what I would do with existing published assemblage data as opposed to simulation.
4. What the possible outcomes are and which considerations would decide between them, including the honest possibility that the answer is negative — that no accessible signature separates the hypotheses — and what such a negative result would and would not entitle me to conclude about the specific debates over early storage and redistribution.
5. What would count as the project failing, as opposed to merely returning a negative result.

The proposal should be written in a register appropriate for a multidisciplinary review panel that includes non-archaeologists, and should say plainly why a project that is methodological rather than empirical is worth a year of funding.
