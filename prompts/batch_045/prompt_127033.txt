I've been going down a rabbit hole lately about Goodhart's law — the thing people say about how once you start using a number as a target it stops being a good measure of the thing you actually cared about — ever since a friend who works with data told me about a project where wait times at her hospital went down on paper for two years running while nothing at all changed about how long anybody waited. I'm not academic, I did a degree in hospitality management and now I run a small B&B near the coast, but I keep turning this over on walks and I thought that writing it as fiction might help me see it better than reading more about it would. So: I don't want an explanation or an argument, I want a short story, maybe 1000 to 1500 words, that makes the problem something you feel in your stomach rather than something you nod at.

Here's the shape of it, please change anything that gets in the way. A small wind-bitten coastal town, half the visitors gone since the ferry got expensive, call it Saltmarch if you want a name. An environmental agency sends the town an annual coastal health payment based on one composite number, and the number comes almost entirely from an automated image classifier that scans photos volunteers upload of the eelgrass beds out in the bay — more eelgrass in the pictures, bigger the cheque. The volunteers lay a bright orange plastic measuring frame on the mud before they shoot, because the guidance tells them to. And here's the bit I want the story to turn on, which I haven't seen anyone talk about and which I think is the interesting version: the classifier was never really trained to recognise eelgrass. It learned to recognise the orange frame, and then to count whatever happens to be inside one. So over several seasons the official map of the eelgrass drifts, without anyone intending it, into a map of where people happened to walk with a frame. Nobody at the agency catches this, because the number creeps up slightly every year and up is good.

What I'd love the story to separate out is three things that I think get lumped together under one slogan. First, gaming the measure without changing reality at all — cherry-picking the good patches, resubmitting the same photo, quietly deleting the bad ones. Second, changing reality so the number improves while the thing we wanted gets worse — transplanting fast-growing eelgrass into the wrong ground, clearing out everything that isn't eelgrass so the frame looks fuller. Third, the one I find genuinely creepy: the measure replacing the target, so that after long enough the dataset is the only record anyone has of the bay, and nobody in town can any longer say what a healthy bay looks like except by pointing at what the app counted that year. I'd like the ending to sit inside that third case without solving it — maybe the funding stops, or the beds really do die off, and the person we've been following is left with a folder of photographs that are all, in a sense, pictures of a plastic square, and has to work out whether that folder is worthless junk or whether it's somehow the last true trace of the thing everybody lost. Please keep it a story with weather and mud and a specific person in it rather than a fable with a moral stapled to the end, and please don't let anyone be a villain — I want everyone in it to be doing their best and meaning well, and to have a character stop short of spelling the lesson out loud in the last paragraph.
