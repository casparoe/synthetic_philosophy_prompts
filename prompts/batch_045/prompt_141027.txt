I write a column on science and ideas and have spent a good part of the last decade writing about forecasting, replication, and the ways expert communities change their minds. For two years I have also sat on the methodology board of the Lumen Forecasting Collaborative, a small nonprofit that publishes quarterly probability estimates on questions about emerging technology and funds a handful of outside research groups. Our output is deliberately blunt: one number per question, published under the institution's name rather than under any individual analyst's, on the theory that a single institutional number is what journalists and funders will actually use. Our founding documents commit us to a written set of Baseline Positions — house prior probabilities for forty-odd recurring question types — and our analysts are told to treat those as the starting point for any new question.

The immediate case is a fight between two of our people over whether at least one major AI lab will, before the end of 2028, announce a model that produced a novel research result its own developers could not have anticipated, with the claim confirmed by a credible outside party. Marta Reyes, our senior quantitative forecaster, sits at 22 percent. Devon Achterberg, hired eighteen months ago from area-studies journalism, sits at 71 percent, and part of his revision rests on a confidential source inside one lab whose identity and contents he will not disclose even to the board. Marta has proposed what she calls the Concordance Protocol: four rounds in which each analyst posts a current probability and the likelihood ratios behind it, with the board chair certifying that nothing material was withheld; if the gap still exceeds fifteen points at the close, the question comes to us with both numbers and written statements, and we publish the arithmetic mean as the institutional figure. Her further contention, which she has put to us in writing, is that two analysts with the same evidence base and the same house prior who still disagree after full exchange are not displaying legitimate diversity: on her reading one of them has simply failed to update, and the board should be willing to say so, resolving the question in favor of the analyst with the better Brier record rather than splitting the difference. Devon's reply is that the Baseline Positions document is itself a contested artifact and that the source he cannot name is the reason his number moved, so an averaging rule that treats his private information as noise will systematically discard exactly the sort of evidence our outfit exists to gather.

What I need from you is the decision memo I will circulate to the six other board members before we vote in three weeks: roughly 900 to 1,200 words, addressed to the board, recommending that we adopt the Concordance Protocol as written, reject it, or adopt it with specified modifications. Please decide, and say plainly, what number we should publish when analysts diverge — arithmetic mean, geometric mean of the odds, single best-calibrated forecaster, or a published range — and what we should do about information that cannot be disclosed: I have floated a mechanism under which an analyst files only the direction and the log-odds magnitude of an update with the chair, who verifies that a source exists without seeing its contents, and the published question carries a flag saying one estimate incorporates verified undisclosed information. State clearly what the agreement theorem does and does not license for an institution in our position, given that our people are not Bayesians, our exchanges are finite, and our house prior is an artifact rather than something they would have agreed to in advance of any evidence. The board knows the theorem's statement but nobody has read the literature, so no footnotes or citations — I want the argument carried by the reasoning. Finally, do not dismiss Marta's contention that persistent divergence is a performance problem rather than a diversity of views; it is the strongest thing on her side of the argument, at least two board members are sympathetic to it, and I want it stated as well as she would state it before you explain why we should not act on it.
