My mother retired in June after thirty-one years as the director of an overnight shelter — officially open eight to eight, in practice open whenever anyone turned up, because she was the one who covered the shifts nobody was funded to cover. The board is throwing her a dinner at the end of the month and I have agreed, rashly as it now seems, to give the toast. The room will be around ninety people: her staff past and present, several of the shelter's long-term guests, our extended family (a good number of whom are still faintly resentful about the Christmases she missed), and the board and the two donors who underwrite most of the budget. I'm three years into a PhD in philosophy and I work on supererogation, which turns out to be the single least helpful thing to have in my head here. My mother's own line is that she was just doing her job and that the scandal is that the job exists; she will hate being made into an example of anything, and she will hate being thanked in a way that lets anyone else off the hook.

What I can't work out is how to praise her without saying something false or unkind, and every option seems to have teeth. If I say she went above and beyond, I seem to be saying the extra was optional — which is the thing she has spent her career denying, which tells the donors that the gaps in the shelter were filled by her virtue rather than by the line item they never approved, and which the guests in the room will hear as: the care you depended on was something nobody owed you. It also may simply misdescribe her: much of what she did was arguably required of her, given her role, and it's a familiar thought that what would be supererogatory for a stranger is obligatory for the person whose job it is. But if I say she only ever did what was required, there is nothing left to praise her for — we don't praise people for meeting their obligations — and I'd also be implying that whoever takes the job next owes that much too, which is exactly the expectation that burned her out and which her staff, several of whom are single parents with second jobs and could not have done what she did, would reasonably hear as a rebuke. If I make her an exemplar of self-sacrifice I lose the rest of her: the allotment, the atrocious puns, the decade she spent playing in a bluegrass band. There is a well-known complaint that a life narrated wholly in terms of moral demand stops looking like an attractive life, and she would find that version of herself unrecognizable. And praising her at all seems to carry an implication that people who did less are criticizable, which I suspect has something to do with the asymmetry between praise and blame but which I can't get into focus.

So: write me the toast. Four to five minutes, spoken, affectionate rather than scolding — including toward the board, who are paying for the dinner — with no technical vocabulary at the surface (my aunt should not have to decode "supererogatory"), though I wouldn't mind one line my supervisor will quietly enjoy. She should come out of it as a specific person who did a specific job for a long time, not as a case study in anything. Then, separately, give me a page of notes on what the toast is committed to: which of its moves lean on a contested reading of supererogation, where you think the reasoning is most likely to fail, and what would change your mind about how to frame it — for instance, if the requirement that supererogatory acts involve a real cost to the agent is false, or if role-duties end up swallowing the category entirely, or if the institutional critique is right and the whole vocabulary of individual virtue is a trap in this setting. Tell me which line you're least confident in and what you'd put in its place under each of those alternatives.
