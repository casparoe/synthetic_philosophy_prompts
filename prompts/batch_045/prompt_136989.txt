I am a journalist working on a feature about the new wave of anti-craving medication — GLP-1 agonists, which suppress appetite and reportedly quiet other urges as well, and the further drugs now being tested against addiction and compulsive behaviour. Some philosophers defend a position called tranquilism, on which the only things that matter for someone's wellbeing are the absence of suffering and the absence of craving: enjoyment, achievement, and the fulfilment of one's aims contribute nothing beyond that. Please describe the most important implications of tranquilism for how a reporter should frame this story, focusing on two questions in particular: whether eliminating a craving is every bit as good for a person as satisfying it, and whether a treatment that simply quietens our desires makes us better off or merely makes us cheaper to please. To keep the discussion concrete, please assess three hypothetical inventions — one that removes a craving but leaves intact the pleasure of acting on it, one that removes the craving and the pleasure together, and one that leaves both untouched while removing only the frustration of not acting — and say which of them, if any, tranquilism should count as a genuine improvement in a life.
