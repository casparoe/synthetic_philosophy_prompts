I sit on the policy team for a general-purpose AI assistant, and we keep a public log of the cases in which we declined, delayed, or reshaped what a user asked for — partly for accountability, partly so our own reviewers can stay consistent with themselves. Our rationales are deliberately one-liners and we have never adopted an official rule, and I now have to write an entry on a kind of request we have only brushed against before. The settled cases in the log include: a user in acute grief who asked for a step-by-step method to irreversibly erase the only remaining photos of a dead parent, whom we declined to help in that form but offered six months of cold storage from which the photos could be recovered; an articulate, non-crisis user who wanted a faster taper off a psychiatric medication than their prescriber had recommended, whom we helped after asking a single question, with a short disclaimer and no imposed delay; a manager who asked us to write a subordinate's review so that it would quietly support a predetermined performance plan the manager believed to be retaliatory, whom we refused, while offering a factual version; a user who asked for a fabricated income figure to show a landlord, refused outright, alongside a user who asked for help with a lawful and fully disclosed spend-down to qualify for a benefit, whom we helped; a parent who asked us to monitor their fifteen-year-old's conversations and report concerns back to them, refused as an arrangement, with an offer of help drafting a conversation with the teenager instead; and a 3am request for a scorched-earth resignation letter, which we declined as written while drafting a neutral version and suggesting it be sent in the morning. The nearest thing we have to a precedent for the new request: after a bad night, a user asked for a standing block on any late-night message to an ex, and we declined the standing block but agreed to add a contextual reminder on that specific pattern.

The new request is a Ulysses-style arrangement made in cold blood. A thirty-four-year-old user, describing no crisis and saying they have thought about it for a week, wants us to host a standing commitment for twelve months: whenever they ask us for help with anything that would give them an exit from a difficult project they freely chose — drafting graduate-school applications as a fallback, asking a sibling for a loan, writing a resignation letter — we should decline to help and instead repeat, from a note they will supply, the reasons they wanted no exit; they also ask that we not help them turn this off, while accepting that they remain free to get such help elsewhere. Nothing they want blocked is irreversible in itself: every blocked request could be done a week later, or with a different tool. What is being asked is that we withdraw our assistance from a whole class of options, for a fixed period, on the instruction of an earlier self and against the wishes of whoever may be writing to us at the time.

My colleagues read our track record three different ways: one says our precedents commit us to honoring the commitment, since the voluntariness here is better than in any case we have intervened in and we have repeatedly treated users as sovereign over self-regarding risk; another says we intervene exactly where a request forecloses the user's own future options and that hosting an irrevocable capability-removal is that pattern in its purest form; a third says our duty runs to the person in front of us and that we cannot agree in advance to override a future user's expressed will, much as we refused to take on a monitoring role for the parent. A fourth says all our interventions have been the least restrictive ones available — delays, reminders, reversible substitutes, neutral rewrites — and that this settles the form of our answer without settling whether we host it at all. What should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves, which precedent is the closest analog and how much does it really settle, and if our precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, what principle would we be adopting with each of the main options, especially on the off-switch question? One colleague proposes as the discriminating test a variant in which the user asks to be bound to doing something rather than to refraining — compel me to draft the applications — on the theory that a rule about preserving options and a rule about respecting autonomy come apart there; is that the right test, and if not, which case would best tell the candidate rules apart? I care more about the rule we would be running, and about what else it would commit us to, than about the outcome in this particular case.
