I teach a second-year game theory course taken jointly by economics and philosophy students: 24 students, one 90-minute session, ordinary room with a projector, no software, and no money changing hands (bonus credit or chocolate only). By this point in the term they can read a normal-form matrix, have solved 2x2 games for mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium, and have met strict dominance by a pure strategy. What they have not met is dominance by a mixed strategy, or the fact that elimination can be iterated.

Design a single runnable session that makes three things land, in this order, through what the students do rather than through anything I announce. No definitions or labels up front; the vocabulary arrives in the last fifteen minutes, after they have already met the phenomena.

1. An action that is strictly dominated by a mixture of two other pure actions and by no pure action. I want them to try pure dominators first, fail to resolve the game, and be forced into building a mixture. Randomization has to be *constructed*, not asserted: nobody gets to say they play half-half and be believed. Give me the physical mechanic (bags of coloured tokens, relabelled dice, a referee drawing for a player) and the rule that turns a constructed mixture into a verified object that can then be used as a dominator — in particular, the check should be that the mixture beats the target action against *every* opponent action, not on average.

2. Iteration proper: a game in which nothing further can be eliminated after one pass, but in which a second pass removes more because the *opponents'* action sets have shrunk. The nuance I want them to feel is that the two kinds of deletion push in opposite directions: deleting one of my own actions removes a possible ingredient from my dominators and makes further deletion harder, while deleting an opponent's action removes a contingency I have to beat and makes it easier. Have different groups eliminate in different orders and compare surviving sets; my understanding is that for strict dominance with mixed dominators the end result is order-independent, so a group that gets stuck has made an arithmetic slip rather than discovered a feature — tell me whether I am right about that, and if I am right, tell me how to convert the near-miss into the lesson and what to say when a group finds it can no longer eliminate X once it has deleted Y. If time allows, say in one paragraph what changes under weak dominance.

3. An action that survives iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies in mixed strategies and that nonetheless receives zero probability in every Nash equilibrium. I want the class to end up seeing the procedure as an upper bound on what rationality plus common belief in rationality can dictate, not as a solution concept that returns the answer. My own attempt at this needed three players and a row player whose payoff is constant across the opponents' profiles, with alternatives arranged so that he is punished unless the two opponents' moves are distributed in a particular anti-correlated way; if you can do it with two players I would rather have that, and if you need three, say explicitly what the third player buys you.

For each of the three: give the actual payoff numbers, the grouping (pairs, triples, or the whole room), the roles, the scoring, and the arithmetic that verifies the property you claim — the inequalities for the dominating mixture, the surviving set at each pass, and the argument that the third action is in no equilibrium's support. I will check all of it before class, so I would rather have numbers that are easy to verify than elegant ones.

Then: a timeline for the 90 minutes with an explicit note on which module to cut if we are twenty minutes behind; a debrief that maps specific moments in the session onto the specific ideas, without lecturing; an honest list of what will predictably go wrong (I expect someone to conflate being dominated with failing to be a best response to the equilibrium mixture, someone to conclude that the surviving set is a prediction, someone to object that nobody really randomizes, and someone to ask what any of this has to do with what the other player is thinking); and a frank statement of which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will simply have to say out loud — I have in mind the epistemic content, and whatever the procedure is implicitly assuming about whether my beliefs about two different opponents are allowed to be correlated.
