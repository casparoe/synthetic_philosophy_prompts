Please treat this as a question in aesthetics and the philosophy of language, and answer as someone who has themselves translated verse under constraint: I would rather have a careful, technical answer than an encouraging one. Think it through properly before committing — try out at least three candidate diagnoses and discard the weaker ones — and say plainly if none of them is decisive. Please write in British English. Please give your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no concluding paragraph. I have no academic background and no access to a university library, so please do not point me towards books, articles or reading lists: I am after the shape of the argument, not sources. I have a rough idea of what I would like the five items to cover — the step you reject, why that step looks fine on a first reading, what rejecting it costs me, what the most attractive rival rejection would cost me, and one consequence for how I should carry on with my own translating — but I will leave the ordering to you.

Background. For a couple of years I have spent my evenings on an English version of a short sixteenth-century French poem: ten lines, rhymed abba cdc dcd, ten syllables to the line, with a pause after the fourth, and the same three rhyme-sounds running right through it. I came to this through Douglas Hofstadter's book-length meditation on translating a short poem by Clément Marot, and the thought I took from it is that a formal constraint is not a handicap imposed on the work but an instrument for finding things out: the rhyme drives you into a corner and you discover what the poem is really doing. I have turned this into a kind of game. I have produced versions that keep the rhyme scheme, then the metre as well, then the pattern of which lines get which rhyme-sound, then the distribution of long and short words — and each time the extra constraint did seem to teach me something about the French.

The argument. Lately I have written out why the game works, and the result now strikes me as broken, because it ends somewhere I cannot accept.

(1) A translation of a poem is better the more of the poem it preserves; it is hard to see what else could make one translation better than another.
(2) The form of a poem — rhyme scheme, metre, line lengths, the pattern of its sounds — is part of the poem rather than a wrapping around it, which is why a prose paraphrase is not a translation of a poem at all.
(3) So a translation that preserves the form is more faithful than one that drops it.
(4) The same reasoning applies feature by feature: rhyme and metre together are better than rhyme alone; adding the exact pattern of stresses better still; then the length of the words; then the vowel colour of the rhymes; then the placement of the pauses.
(5) If each further preserved feature makes a translation better, then the translation that preserves all of them is the best translation there could be.
(6) The only text that preserves every formal feature of the original is the original itself.
(7) So the best possible translation of a poem is the poem, and every actual translation fails in proportion to how much it translates.
(8) That cannot be right. People demonstrably translate poems, sometimes well, and there are English versions that strike me as better poems than the French ones they came from.

Two things make it worse. First, if the actual sounds are part of the form, then the poem as I pronounce it today is already not the poem its author wrote, since French pronunciation has shifted; so step (6) does not even hand me the original as the limiting case, but something indexed to a particular speaker at a particular date. Second, the same reasoning seems to rule out versions of a poem in a closely related language or in another dialect of the same language, and even heavily adapted English renderings of Persian quatrains, which everybody is happy to call translations.

Repairs I have tried and do not trust. (a) Distinguish the pattern of the form — which lines rhyme with which, where the stresses fall — from its material, the actual sounds, and require only the pattern to be preserved. This dissolves the argument, but it also seems to make my game pointless: if only the pattern matters, then any verse rendering that instantiates the pattern has preserved everything essential, and the constraint becomes trivially satisfiable. (b) Say instead that only the load-bearing features of this poem need preserving and that the rest are decorative. But which features are load-bearing appears to be settled afterwards, by what I happened to manage to keep, which makes the standard self-certifying; I have taken to calling this retrospective aptness, and I do not trust it either.

So: which numbered step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? If I must choose, I would rather keep (3) — that form-preserving versions really are more faithful — than swallow (7), and I would rather keep both than fall back on (a). But I want to know what I am paying, and whether the worry in (b) is a real defect in my reasoning or only a defect in me.
