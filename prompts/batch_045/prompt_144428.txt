(1) A person is the maker of a work only if the features that make the work good depend in the right way on that person's own judgments — their sense of what looks right, of when a thing is finished, of what the thing is trying to be.

(2) Depend in the right way has to mean more than mere causation. It means: holding everything else fixed, if your judgment had been different, the relevant features would have been correspondingly different. Call this the counterfactual test.

(3) With an image generator there is no such dependence. The route from what I want to what I get runs through a process I don't understand; tiny prompt changes produce wildly different images; I generally cannot obtain a specific visual feature on demand; I get what I get and then decide whether to keep it.

(4) So the valuable features of a generated image do not depend on my judgments in the required way.

(5) So I am not the maker of the image. At most I commission it, or curate it.

(6) But now take a painter with forty years behind her. She often cannot say why a stroke went where it did, cannot reproduce an effect when asked to, and sometimes says the painting seemed to decide for itself. Her judgments don't map onto the features in the sense of (2) either.

(7) So she is not the maker of her paintings — which is false.

(8) So (2) is the wrong test. What matters instead is this: that you set the process going, that your judgment governs what you keep, revise, or discard, and that you are prepared to answer for the result.

(9) Prompting satisfies that test. I choose the initial prompt, generate hundreds of candidates, reject nearly all of them, refine and re-run, and I can usually articulate why I kept the one I kept.
