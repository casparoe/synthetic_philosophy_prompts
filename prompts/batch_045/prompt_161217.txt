I'm the newest and least philosophical member of the clinical ethics consultation committee at a large teaching hospital — I joined in January, my background is internal medicine rather than ethics, and because I'm the one who usually ends up writing the note, I'm also the one who has to make our past reasoning sound coherent to the people who have to act on it. Next week we have a consultation that I genuinely cannot see our way through, so I've spent the weekend going back through the last year of our own write-ups to work out what we actually think, and I'd like a second opinion before I draft anything.

Our precedents, as I would summarise them: (a) we supported withdrawing ventilation from a competent patient with motor neurone disease at her request, on the grounds that stopping the ventilator lets the underlying disease take its course and that the cause of death would be her disease rather than us; (b) we refused a family's request to withhold antibiotics from a man with advanced dementia and a treatable pneumonia who would probably have lived years, saying that he was not dying, so withholding here would not be letting anything take its course but would be bringing about his death by omission; (c) we honoured a competent patient's refusal of a blood transfusion that would have saved her life, even though we thought her reasons mistaken, and she died; (d) at a single meeting we approved escalating morphine for terminal breathlessness at a dose the team conceded would probably shorten the patient's life, and refused a request in the next room for a lethal injection, explaining the pair by saying that what mattered was what the clinician was aiming at; (e) we said of a nurse who, out of resentment toward a difficult patient, deliberately ignored his call light and let him bleed out that this was a killing and not a letting die, and morally on a par with giving him a lethal injection herself; (f) we refused a request from a transplant team to take steps that would bring about the death of an unconscious patient so that his organs would be usable for five others, saying that we do not bring about one patient's death for the sake of others and do not use one patient's body as a resource for the rest; (g) during a ventilator shortage we did support moving a ventilator from one patient with a very poor prognosis to five with good ones, accepting that the one would die.

The case I have to write up is this: a man in his thirties is brought in after an armed robbery in which he shot five people. The five are in theatre and will die within hours unless they receive transplants, and he is the only tissue-compatible donor within reach. He himself has an abdominal gunshot wound, is unconscious, has no advance directive and no family we can find. The trauma team says an immediate operation would very likely save him — they put survival at about seventy per cent — and that without it he will bleed out within the hour. If he dies, his kidneys, liver and lungs go to four of the five, and they survive; the transplant team is asking us for permission not to operate, and they are asking only that — no sedation, no organ-preserving intervention, nothing done to him before death. The five will be transplanted at another centre, so there is no competition for theatre space or staff, and nothing about his care affects them one way or the other except the organs.

What I want from you is less the verdict than the reasoning I could defend in the note. What should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves, and which of (a) through (g) is really the closest analog — is it the organ case, the dementia case, or the withdrawal-and-refusal cases? Does the fact that nothing is being done to him make this a letting die, with the gunshot wound as the cause of death, or does choosing the omission precisely because his death is needed make it the same act in different clothes? Does the fact that he is the person who created the need for the organs change anything, and if so does that commit us to treating an innocent bystander who happened to be the only match differently? I keep oscillating between two readings of our track record — one on which the real work is being done by something like a no-using-as-a-means principle plus a consent carve-out, and one on which it is done by whether the death is already underway and whether we are aiming at it — and I suspect there is a third reading I'm not seeing. Tell me what each reading predicts here, which you think we are actually running, and what we would be committing ourselves to either way if our precedents genuinely underdetermine the case. Finally, name one further case, real or constructed, that would separate the readings, since I may be able to get the committee to answer it hypothetically at our next meeting and settle this for good.
