I'm a PhD student in philosophy writing a seminar paper on the substantivalism/relationism dispute, and I keep running into the slogan "all motion is relative" doing argumentative work I can't pin down. Most recently a visiting speaker used it to brush off Newton's bucket argument: if all motion is relative, she said, then the water climbing the sides is just the water rotating relative to the bucket, and nothing in that phenomenon forces us to posit absolute space. I want to hand her a menu of precise theses rather than simply disagree, so I'm asking for a disambiguation rather than a defense of one reading.

My own attempt to sort the candidates leans on a distinction I'll call observational relativity versus constitutive relativity: a quantity might be undetectable even in principle, or it might fail to be a genuine feature of the world at all, and I suspect the substantive readings of the slogan differ over which of those they assert, and over which relata are permitted — ordinary bodies, the bucket itself, an inertial frame, the totality of distant matter.

So please lay out the distinct precise claims the slogan could be expressing, ordered from platitude to radical. At minimum I want readings distinguished along these lines: whether the claim is restricted to uniform motion or extends to acceleration and rotation; whether it is a claim about what can be empirically detected or about what exists; whether the determination of inertial effects by the distribution of distant matter is meant causally or merely semantically; and whether the thesis is really about the symmetry group of the laws or about the metaphysics of space. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and — the part I most need — whether it would actually license the inference she made with it against the bucket argument.

Finally, tell me what the strongest surviving reading predicts for the case I keep going back and forth on: two balls joined by a taut elastic cord, rotating about their common centre in an otherwise empty universe. Does the best version of the slogan force me to deny that they rotate, and if it does not, what work is left for it against substantivalism?
