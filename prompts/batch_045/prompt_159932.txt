I teach a second-year undergraduate seminar on nationalism and historical memory — eighteen students, one eighty-minute slot — and we have just come out of a week on the modernist picture of nations: the thesis that a great many of the practices a nation presents as immemorial were codified comparatively recently and then retrojected backwards, and the companion thesis that a nation is a community whose members will never meet, hear, or even hear of most of the others, yet who carry a felt fellowship with those unseen contemporaries and with the dead and the unborn. My students assented to both in about five minutes, which is precisely the difficulty: they read the material as a debunking — everything is fake, then — and they are now applying it indiscriminately, including to a village festival my grandmother describes from her childhood in Crete, which they have decided, on no evidence whatsoever, was manufactured in the 1930s. What I want them to feel rather than assent to is two things. First, how fast and by what mechanisms a practice acquires the phenomenology of antiquity, the sense that it was simply always there. Second, why the right conclusion is not that nothing is ancient, but something more interesting about the relations between invention, authenticity, and the ordinary work of belonging. So I need an activity that produces that experience instead of announcing it: no lecture up front, and I would rather they never hear either of the week's two technical labels until after the thing is over.

Design me something runnable for that room and that clock. My own instinct, which you should improve on or discard, is a two-stage machine. In stage one, small groups act as the culture ministry of a freshly independent country — give it a name, a terrain, and a couple of rival neighbours — and are scored on producing, in about fifteen minutes, a portfolio of national traditions: a holiday with a fixed date, a colour or garment, a dish, a gesture of greeting, a founder story, and, for each item, a two-sentence account of where it comes from. In stage two the portfolios are anonymised and shuffled together with a handful of traditions I supply myself that are meant to stand in for genuinely old local practices — ragged, regionally variable, lacking any stated rationale, half-remembered and slightly embarrassing — and a second set of students act as visiting antiquarians who must rank the whole pile from most ancient to most recent, using a rubric I hand them that looks entirely sensible: internal coherence, uniformity across the country's regions, an explicit symbolic rationale, an origin story that explains the practice. The trap, of course, is that the rubric runs backwards: the twenty-minute-old inventions will read as immemorial because they were built to be legible, while the real survivals will read as forgeries because they are messy and local and nobody can say why they are done. I want that inversion to arrive as something the antiquarians discover about their own judgements, not as something I tell them. Somewhere in the machine I also want a mechanic for the second half of the week's argument: something by which each participant handles only an artifact — a single-page national gazette passed from hand to hand, say, each person adding one line to it and never addressing most of the others — and yet finishes the session with a live sense of membership in a body of eighteen people they cannot see. Give me the rules, the materials (index cards and a timer, ideally nothing else), a payoff scheme in points rather than money, roles if roles help, and a minute-by-minute timeline that still leaves twenty-five minutes at the end.

Then the debrief, which is where I most need help. Tell me which moments in the activity to point back at and which part of the week's argument each moment is meant to carry, including the moment that should carry the distinction between a practice's having been invented and its being fake, shallow, or easy to repeal. Tell me honestly what will go wrong on the first run: I expect at least one group that refuses to invent anything because it feels like lying, at least one portfolio that is a joke, and a strong pull afterwards toward the nihilistic version of the thesis, where every tradition anyone mentions is declared an invention. Say how I should handle each. And be frank about which feature of the argument the game cannot stage and which I will simply have to say in my own voice — I am thinking of the slow time scale of the real cases, the schools and censuses and conscription behind them, the print and literacy conditions, and the coerciveness of the states involved.
