I am setting the final paper for a fourth-year seminar on paradoxes of infinity and physical possibility, and I need one essay question together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The students have worked through the Thomson–Benacerraf exchange on supertasks and can all reproduce it: a lamp is off at t = 0 and its switch is flipped at t = 1/2, 3/4, 7/8, …, after which, Thomson argues, it can be neither on nor off, since every period of being on is followed by a period of being off and conversely; so the story is incoherent and supertasks are impossible. Benacerraf's reply is that the story fails to determine the state at t = 1, that no contradiction follows from that failure, and that supertasks remain at least logically possible. My difficulty is that any question that simply invites a comparison of these two positions is answered by transcription. I want a question that makes students do something with the material, and a marking scheme that separates those who have grasped the structure of the dispute from those who have memorised its labels.

Build the question around the following three devices, none of which the students have seen. Let the switch schedule be fixed for all t < 1: the switch is down on [0, 1/2), up on [1/2, 3/4), down on [3/4, 7/8), up on [7/8, 15/16), and so on, alternating at the times 1 − 2⁻ⁿ. What varies is only how the lamp responds.

(A) The ideal lamp: brightness equals the switch position, so it is fully on and fully off exactly as Thomson's story says, for every t < 1. The sequence of states at the flip times alternates and has no limit.

(B) The damped lamp: brightness b ∈ [0,1] relaxes toward the switch position at rate k > 0, i.e. db/dt = k(u(t) − b(t)) with b(0) = 0. State as given that for every k > 0 the brightness converges as t → 1 to a determinate limit strictly between 0 and 1, and that the limit varies with k: numerically it is about 0.25 for k = 1, about 0.38 for k = 2, and about 0.48 for k = 4. Candidates should be told explicitly that they are not required to determine the behaviour of this limit as k grows without bound, and the question should not turn on it.

(C) The dead-band lamp: a two-state device that ignores any switch position not held for at least δ > 0. It therefore stops registering flips once the intervals fall below δ, and its final state is full-on or full-off according to the parity of the last flip that was held for at least δ. Its final state is thus determinate, but which state it is depends on δ, and as δ is halved repeatedly the final state alternates between on and off, so that this family has no limiting final state.

Note, and have the question note, that for any chosen N, both the damped lamp (large k) and the dead-band lamp (small δ) match the ideal lamp to arbitrary accuracy over the first N flips. The three devices are indistinguishable by any finite amount of observation and differ only in the limit.

Here is what I want the question to force, and therefore what the marking scheme must reward. First, it must separate four questions that the literature runs together: whether an agent could perform infinitely many acts; whether the performance is physically realisable; whether the completed history is coherent; and whether the state at t = 1 is determined by the story. The candidates should have to say which of these the variants bear on. Second, it must force a verdict on which diagnosis the variants support — Thomson's incoherence verdict, Benacerraf's incompleteness verdict, or a third: that the story gives a merely kinematic specification (which switch position at which time) and no dynamics, so that nothing about supertasks, infinity or agency follows, only something about what a complete description of a process must contain. The strongest answers will say what would have to be true for each verdict to be correct rather than simply picking one. Third, the question must bring out the role of Thomson's excluded-middle premise that at t = 1 the lamp is on or off: a damped lamp with large k is observationally indistinguishable from Thomson's lamp over any finite number of flips yet ends determinately at an intermediate brightness, while a dead-band lamp ends fully on or fully off depending on a parameter the story does not fix. Fourth, I want the model answer to give the general condition in the background of all this, namely that a supertask of this kind has a determinate outcome exactly when the effects of the remaining operations vanish in the tail — summably, in the damped case, trivially in the dead-band case, and not at all in the ideal case, since there every flip has full effect and the alternation never settles. That condition is what distinguishes the lamp from unproblematic convergent supertasks such as Zeno's runner or a ball that bounces infinitely often in finite time and comes to rest, and the model answer should draw that contrast. Fifth, the marking scheme must address determinism: whether the lamp is a counterexample to it depends on whether a stipulation about flip times is the sort of thing determinism is a thesis about, and on whether the failure is a failure of lawfulness or merely the absence of a limit in a state function that is perfectly well behaved at every instant before t = 1.

Some design constraints. The question must be self-contained: no outside reading, no differential equations to solve, no numerical work, all three devices and their stated properties given in the rubric. It should be answerable in ninety minutes and should consist of a main part and one short second part; I would like the second part to ask what follows from the fact that both one-parameter families match the ideal lamp on ever longer finite prefixes while disagreeing with one another about the final state — whether that shows the final state to be underdetermined, or shows that the ideal lamp is not a well-defined object at all. The question cannot be answered well by summarising the Thomson–Benacerraf dispute, and a candidate who knows only that dispute should be capable of no better than a lower second.

Please give me: the question itself, written in the formal style of an exam paper, with the three devices and their properties stated; a model answer of 800 to 1000 words; and a marking scheme out of 20 organised around five named moves worth four marks each, each with a single line on how candidates characteristically get that move wrong, so that I can tell an A script from a B script by what it contains. Add two short paragraphs: one describing the best wrong answer — the most attractive script that still misses the point — and what it should be awarded; and one listing the moves that should earn nothing, including the reflex that the switch would have to move faster than light so the case is closed, and any answer that treats the intermediate brightness of the damped lamp as a discovery about what the lamp is really doing rather than as one admissible completion among many.
