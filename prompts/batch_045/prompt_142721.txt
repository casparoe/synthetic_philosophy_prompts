I sit on the incident-review committee at a hospital, and a friend of mine used to work as a safety driver for a company testing autonomous vehicles, so I keep encountering the same shape of thing from two directions and I want to know whether it has a name.

In our hospital, a prediction system flags a patient as low risk; a nurse or a junior doctor confirms the flag within the time the protocol allows; the patient deteriorates and we hold a review. The question we are told to answer is whether the clinician exercised appropriate oversight, the record shows a confirmation at 04:12, and the discussion effectively ends there. My friend's version: the vehicle did something the engineers could not fully reconstruct, she did not intervene, and the inquiry concluded that she should have been paying closer attention; meanwhile the drivers who did intervene were told, via the intervention rates the company tracked, that they were overriding the system too freely, and one of them was faulted for intervening when the system had been right. What I keep noticing is not just that the human gets blamed, but that she gets blamed whichever way she acts, and that this is structural rather than unlucky: the role exists in order that there be a human author of record. Once a human is in the loop, blame has somewhere to land, and so the upstream questions — whether this model should have been deployed in this form at all, whether the threshold was set where it was for defensible reasons, whether anyone in that position could really have second-guessed the output in the seconds the protocol allows — stop being asked, because the file already contains an answer. Nothing runs in the other direction: nobody credits the clinician when the flag is right. And nobody in any of these stories seems to be scheming. The oversight requirement was introduced in good faith precisely in order to make accountability possible, the formal conditions of human control are arguably satisfied, and yet the effect seems to be that a measure designed to secure responsibility shrinks the total amount of it, with the clicking itself doing the shrinking.

So: is there an established concept for this arrangement, in which a human is interposed so that responsibility will have somewhere to land, with the result that responsibility-bearing migrates downstream, contracts overall, and becomes independent of what the human actually did? I have read a fair amount of general discussion of responsibility gaps for autonomous systems, but as I understand it that worry is that there is too little blame to go around, whereas in my cases there is no shortage at all — if anything the problem is the opposite. Someone also suggested the problem of many hands, but that sounds like it covers situations where no individual can be singled out, and my worry is that an individual is singled out far too easily. It is not ordinary scapegoating or buck-passing either, since in both of those someone deflects blame they would otherwise have borne, and the person here had none to deflect. Please do not just hand me a label: I want the mechanism stated precisely, the conditions that have to hold for it to arise, what it is not, and where its edges are — is a minister signing a regulation drafted by civil servants an instance? a junior doctor countersigning a consultant's plan? a night security guard? a referee at a sporting match? And if there genuinely is nothing established for it, then name it, define it, and tell me which boundary cases any definition like that would have to get right, since I want the carving and not the word.
