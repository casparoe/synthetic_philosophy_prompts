I have no academic background in philosophy — I work in logistics, and I got onto this through a reading group that goes through popular books — and for about two years now I have been chewing on one argument that I can't get to sit still. I'd be grateful if you'd answer at the level of the argument rather than the literature: I don't want a reading list, since I have no access to a university library and couldn't work through one if I had.

The case is the utility monster, the thought experiment from Nozick's 1974 book about a being that draws vastly more happiness from each unit of a resource than anyone else does, so that any view which totals happiness seems obliged to feed it everything and let the rest of us go without. As far as I can tell, the debate around it settled into a fixed set of moves within a few years and has not moved since: that no such creature could exist, given how satisfaction actually behaves; that the example is too alien to tell us anything about real choices; that the theory should simply accept the result; that it shows only that distribution has to be built into the theory somewhere, by weighting the worse off or by some other device; and, from the other side, that a monster can be tailored to whatever aggregation rule you prefer, so no such patch really escapes. Everyone in the discussion appears to know all of these in advance, and the exchanges I read feel like a script being performed. I want to know why it has stalled.

My own hypothesis is that the utility monster isn't one argument but a proxy for a disagreement about the shape of the good, and that the two sides are answering different questions under one heading: one is objecting to the idea that welfare is a single quantity that can be summed across people at all, while the other is offering a technical repair inside a framework that has already granted that it can. But I can also see two other candidates, and I can't tell which is doing the real work. One is formal: perhaps the live question is which feature of the aggregation has to be given up, and the monster is best understood as a way of pressing on that. The other is empirical, and this is the one that interests me most: how much human beings actually differ in their capacity to be benefited by the same thing. If the spread is small, the monster is a fantasy and the objection is being carried by its extremity; if it is large and unevenly distributed, the case may be with us already in a mild form, and the philosophical argument has been resting on a factual guess that nobody checks.

Three variations I keep turning over, which I'd want a diagnosis to account for rather than set aside:

The gradient monster. Not a being a million times more efficient, but an ordinary population in which one person reliably gets, say, three times the benefit from any given good as another, with nothing else to weigh against it. My discomfort fades somewhere along that gradient, and I can't tell whether that means my objection was really to the aggregation, or only to how extreme the inequality was.

The mirror monster. The same disproportionate capacity, but for suffering rather than enjoyment, so that the theory seems to require us to spend everything on sparing it. This strikes me as notably less outrageous than the original, and I don't understand why. My suspicion is that the difference in my reaction tells us something about whether the original objection is really about fairness, or about something less creditable, like resentment at one person's enormous good.

The retrospective monster. Someone long dead who cared intensely about something only we can now bring about, at real cost to everyone alive. If the objection is to summing welfare across persons, this should be no different from any other case; if it is about who can complain, or about who has a claim on us, it should be different. That the two come apart here is my main evidence that two questions are being run together.

So: what kind of impasse is this — a verbal one about what the theory is committed to, a clash of bedrock intuitions about fairness that neither side can argue for, a hidden empirical question about the real distribution of benefit-capacity, or a formal dispute about which axiom to surrender? Is my two-questions hypothesis right, and if not, where exactly does it break? And given your diagnosis, what would progress look like: a distinction, a formal result, some empirical work, or an agreement to stop asking the question in this shape? Please also say where your own diagnosis is most likely to fail — which consideration, if I pressed it, would most plausibly force you to a different one — and use British English throughout.
