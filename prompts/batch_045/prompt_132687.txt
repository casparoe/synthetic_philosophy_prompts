The part I most want you to engage with is an objection I have been developing, which I will call the no-chance gap. As I read the literature, the serious derivations come in two families. Symmetry-based arguments (envariance-style) and separability arguments constrain what form a probability assignment over branches may take, given the structure of entanglement or given what an agent's uncertainty may permissibly depend on. Decision-theoretic arguments constrain an agent's preferences over acts whose outcomes are branch-indexed, and then read a probability off a representation theorem for those preferences. My worry is that both families establish at most a structural claim: any admissible weight function over branches must be additive, unitarily invariant, factor across independent subsystems, and render certain sequences of gambles rationally coherent. The further step, from the weights must have structure S to an agent ought to set her credences to those weights, is either not taken at all or taken via a bridge principle that presupposes what is in question. For in a branching multiverse every anticipated outcome occurs somewhere, the dynamics is deterministic, and there is no fact of the matter about which future descendant is the agent. So there is nothing of the right category for a rational credence to track: no chance, no frequency along a single actual history, no indexical fact to be uncertain about. The usual bridge principles — a Principal Principle for branch weights, a calibration requirement linking credence to long-run relative frequency, or an expected-utility reading of the representation theorem on which the derived weights just are credences — each import one of the missing items. If that is right, the derivations deliver at best a normative constraint on how to distribute concern or how to bet, and the gap between that and credence is not closable from within Everettian resources.
