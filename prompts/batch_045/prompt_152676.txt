I'm a journalist working on a piece about how scientific institutions police truthfulness in public communication, and I need help with a prediction — and, more than that, with working out what the institution actually believes.

The body I'm writing about is a national academy's standing committee on the public communication of science. It hears complaints about members' public statements and issues anonymized findings on whether a statement breached the duty of truthfulness, graded by seriousness. I have the gist of its last five published rulings. I've anonymized the institution and altered identifying details, and there is nothing to look up: I want the conceptual work, not the docket.

Its precedents, as I understand them:

One. A clinical researcher said on a broadcast morning show that his drug cut deaths by a third, when the trial's mortality result was null and he had the figures in front of him. Breach, unanimous, top grade.

Two. Asked at a press conference whether her institute took money from a pesticide manufacturer, a toxicologist replied that the study under discussion was funded in full by the national research council. That was true, and the institute separately held an unrestricted industry gift. The committee found a breach in the same grade as the first case, reasoning in substance that arranging for a true sentence to be heard as a false one differs from asserting a falsehood only in technique. This is the ruling in which the committee appeared to collapse the lying-versus-misleading distinction for cases of designed misleading — what some writers call paltering.

Three. At a specialist conference session that reporters also attended, a physicist stated a result with a hedge; a journalist cut the hedge and filed a story asserting certainty. She had anticipated that this might happen and did nothing to prevent it. No breach. The substance of the reasoning was that the duty is calibrated to the audience the speaker addressed, and that it does not extend to preventing other people's errors, however cheaply they might have been prevented.

Four. In a newspaper op-ed written for a general audience, a geneticist used a sentence that was literally true under its technical reading but that the committee found most non-specialist readers would take to assert a considerably stronger and false claim; a true and jargon-free alternative was available to her. Breach, one grade below the first two. The committee expressly declined to find that she had wanted the false reading: in substance, choosing to address a lay audience brings with it a duty about formulation, and the committee has never required proof of intent to deceive.

Five. A group leader's press office issued a release containing a false claim that he had neither drafted nor seen. After he learned of it he left it standing on the press office site and then featured a link to it on his group's own page. Breach, but the committee limited the finding to the featuring of the link, which it treated as adoption; the bare failure to seek a correction was found not to be a breach, consistently with the third case, with two members dissenting on the ground that this punished an omission.

Now the pending case, which is why I'm writing to you.

The researcher is a climate scientist whose group has published regional heat-mortality projections: a central estimate of roughly a fifteen percent increase, with an uncertainty range running from a small decrease to something close to a doubling. Her university's news office runs every release through an automated summarizer that generates the plain-language one-line headline, and the tool is known to strip hedges and quantifiers: in something like four cases out of five it converts a hedged central estimate into a flat assertion. She drafted roughly a dozen versions of her summary sentence and ran each through the tool. Two of them produced hedged headlines, which she judged clumsy; she kept the one that produced the headline Heat deaths to double in the region by 2050. Her own sentence, which appears verbatim in the release one click below that headline, is true and properly hedged. She did not write, preview, or approve the headline, and she could not have predicted it with certainty. Her defence is that she was optimising for readability, that she asserted nothing false to anyone, and that the tool is the news office's instrument and the news office's problem. The file also notes that she later saw the published headline and neither requested a correction — a process she has used before — nor amplified it.

What I want from you:

Predict the ruling, and state the rule the committee is implicitly running if it rules that way. Then set out the other rules that fit the five precedents at least as well, and say what each of them predicts here: a rule turning on whether the speaker designed the false uptake, a rule turning on fit between formulation and the audience addressed, a rule turning on whose error it is, and any rule I haven't listed that you think the record supports. Tell me which of the five precedents is the closest analog and which of them you think is doing the least work in its own rationale.

Then give me the variant of this case that would best separate the surviving rules — the further case I should watch for, or put to the chair — and say what the committee's answer to it would tell us. Alongside that, I want your view on whether the lying-versus-misleading distinction is doing any real work anywhere in this record, or whether the operative axes are other ones entirely: design versus mere foresight, the audience addressed versus whoever ends up reading, authoring a falsehood versus enabling one. There is an argument I keep turning over, which is that the machine case is not really a lying case or a misleading case but a third thing, in which a speaker gets the causal effect of a false assertion while keeping the deniability of having said nothing false, and that this is worse than either. Tell me whether that argument survives contact with the committee's own reasoning.

Finally: if the committee wants to be consistent with itself, what should it decide, and what principle would it be adopting either way? I have an interview with the chair, so tell me as well what single question would most quickly reveal which of the candidate rules the committee actually runs.
