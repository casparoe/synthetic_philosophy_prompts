I direct a long-running cohort study — the Kiruna Register: roughly 40,000 people born between 1955 and 2015, genome-wide genotypes, unusually detailed environmental records, and repeated measurements on a standardized apparatus of a composite I will call saccadic reacquisition latency (SRL), the time in milliseconds needed to re-fixate a target after an unpredicted displacement. Heritability estimates for SRL are constantly quoted back at me: by clinicians deciding whom to screen, by colleagues in education deciding whether a training programme is worth funding, and occasionally by people who want an argument that no intervention could help. I end up having to rule, case by case, on whether a given inference from a heritability estimate is legitimate. My verdicts are stable, but I cannot get them to fall out of the one-sentence definition I was taught, and two pairs of them seem to pull against each other. I would like the principle I am actually applying, stated precisely enough to return verdicts on cases I have not considered, together with an honest account of where it refuses to follow me.

My cases and verdicts.

A. A GREML estimate in unrelated cohort members puts narrow-sense heritability at 0.42. Inference: genetic differences among these people account for roughly two-fifths of the variance in SRL, given the environments this cohort actually occupied and the measurement procedure we used. I allow this.

B. Same number. Inference: two-fifths of any given person's SRL is genetic, the rest environmental. I disallow this.

C. We introduce a revised training protocol; mean SRL improves by 15 ms, the variance is essentially unchanged, and individuals keep roughly their relative standing. Inference: the 0.42 must have been an artefact, since the trait turns out to be trainable. I disallow this. I also disallow the symmetrical inference that the 0.42 shows training is pointless.

D. Mean SRL is 8 ms higher for men than for women; within-sex heritability is about 0.40. Inference: part of the sex difference is therefore genetic. I disallow this, though I grant it may well be true. My reason: the within-sex decomposition does not constrain the between-sex mean, and sex is not a genetic contrast of the kind the estimate partitions.

E. Mean SRL improved by 20 ms between the 1955 and the 2015 birth cohorts; within-cohort heritability stayed near 0.40; allele frequencies at the associated loci are essentially unchanged. Inference: the improvement is environmental. I allow this. I am aware this sits badly with D, and I would like to know whether one of the two verdicts has to go or whether the asymmetry is principled.

F. Nearly everyone in the cohort was given, in childhood, a then-standard medication with a known effect on vestibular development; within the cohort, genetic differences in how the drug is metabolised explain a large share of the variance in SRL. Inference: SRL is therefore substantially genetic, and withdrawing or replacing the drug would not change matters much. I disallow both halves, the second emphatically. My reason: a trait can owe its existence entirely to an environmental agent in the difference-making sense and still be highly heritable, because heritability partitions differences among individuals while holding the shared cause constant.

G. Two indirect-effect cases, which I think are the real problem.
G1. Infant SRL at six months is substantially affected by maternal genotype acting through the intrauterine environment. Regressed on the child's own genotype, heritability is 0.15; computed so as to include the maternal contribution, it is 0.55. My verdict: both numbers mean something, but only 0.15 is the heritability; 0.55 answers a different question, about how much the trait runs in families and how fast it would respond to selection.
G2. SRL in eight-year-olds is affected by whether a parent reads to the child daily, and that parental behaviour is itself heritable and correlated with the child's genotype. Structurally this is the same pattern: genes acting through an environment the parents provide. My verdict: I want to say this one is not genetic at all, and that the 0.55-style number here is misleading rather than merely different. I cannot explain why it differs from G1 except that the pathway is behavioural rather than biological, which strikes me as a prejudice rather than a principle.

H. A second register in another country, with different diet and schooling but broadly similar allele frequencies at the associated loci and the same apparatus, gives 0.20. Inference: heredity matters less there. I allow the comparison but only under a gloss — that the difference is owed to a wider spread of environments and to weaker genotype-environment covariance, not to genes having become less important to any individual's SRL — and I disallow the further claim that the trait is intrinsically more genetic in Kiruna than elsewhere.

A case I cannot settle. For the subsample that underwent the new training protocol we computed the heritability of the improvement itself, post minus pre: 0.60. Half of me says the gain is measured, it varies, and it plausibly predicts who will benefit from the protocol, so it has a heritability like anything else. The other half says the gain is a function of two potential outcomes only one of which was ever observed for any individual, so either it is not the kind of quantity that can have a heritability at all, or its heritability licenses a different set of inferences from a heritability of SRL.

A tentative principle of my own, to be tested rather than deferred to: heritability is the proportion of phenotypic variance in a specified population — under a specified distribution of environments, a specified measurement procedure, and a specified rule for which causal pathways count as genetic — that is statistically attributable to genetic differences among the individuals in that population; it licenses exactly that attribution and, for the selection-relevant variant of the quantity, the prediction of within-population response to selection via the breeder's equation; and it licenses nothing about the causes of an individual's trait value, nothing about differences between populations or between periods, nothing about what would happen under an intervention the population has not experienced, and nothing about whether a trait is genetic in any intrinsic or counterfactual sense.

What I want from you. Test that principle against A through H and repair it where it gives the wrong verdict or no verdict. Say explicitly which heritability your principle is about — narrow-sense additive, broad-sense, SNP-based, twin-based — and whether the principle itself changes when the estimator does, since in my data the SNP-based figure (0.42) and the twin-based figure (0.58) disagree in a way I have never been able to adjudicate. Make the pathway-counting rule in G1 and G2 explicit and tell me whether any non-arbitrary rule separates them, or whether I must drop one of those two verdicts. Tell me whether D and E can both stand, and if so which clause does the work and what ancillary premise is being smuggled in. Give me your verdict on the gain-score case. Then construct three cases of your own that I have not listed, state what the repaired principle says about each, and say plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which of those you would overturn. Argumerather than summarise; I am not asking for the literature or for citations, only for the principle and its consequences.
