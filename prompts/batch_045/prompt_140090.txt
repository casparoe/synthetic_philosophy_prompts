I run the country office of a small international health NGO, and I've been going in circles on a decision I have to make this week, so I'd find it useful to hear how someone who thinks about these things structurally would frame it. A shipment of vaccines and cold-chain equipment is stuck in a bonded warehouse at the port. Our logistics officer tells me the customs official has twice mentioned "the cost of the paperwork" and that everyone in the trade pays it; he also says that refusing outright reads as an insult and will make this official colder, not warmer, on the next eleven shipments. Separately, a well-connected former minister who sits on a couple of boards has offered to make one phone call. No money changes hands with him, but he has let it be known that he'd appreciate it if we found a place in our warehouse for a relative of his and listed his wife's small foundation as an implementing partner in our annual report. We do in fact have a warehouse assistant vacancy, and the relative is not the worst name on the shortlist.

What has made this harder to think about is a conversation between my deputy, who has been with the organisation for a decade and is from here, and a programme officer visiting from head office. My deputy says the ban on paying is really about our donor's legal exposure rather than about anyone we serve, and that our head office is happy to enforce a rule from a rich country while people here wait for doses. The programme officer says the payment is the corrupt thing and the phone call is fine because nobody is being paid. Neither of them seems to me to be pointing at what is actually wrong or not wrong here, but I can't articulate what the right description would be. My own half-formed idea is that the test isn't whether cash moves but whether the arrangement is one we could write into the official's published schedule of fees and duties and still defend to the clinics that don't get their supplies. But I notice this test may let the phone call off too easily, and I suspect the opposite is true: the payment buys one shipment, while the phone call buys a relationship of obligation that outlasts it.

So I'd like you to tell me what you think I should actually do, and how I should explain it both to my deputy and to head office. In particular: is the second option better, worse, or the same as the first, and is there a version of refusing both that isn't just letting the vaccines spoil? I don't need a policy memo and I don't need to know what the literature says — I need the reasoning.
