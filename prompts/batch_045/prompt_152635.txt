I sit on a committee struck by our university's senate to make a recommendation about Carrothers Hall, a lecture hall named in 1961 for a benefactor, Elias Carrothers, who made his fortune in the 1880s and 1890s recruiting indentured labour for the sugar plantations of the Pacific. The committee's briefing sets the facts out roughly as follows. The trade was lawful in the colonies where he operated; it was also condemned by a substantial body of his own contemporaries, including officials in the administration under whose licence he worked, and he wrote at least one surviving private letter indicating that he knew the mortality figures on his ships would not bear scrutiny. The 1961 naming was itself contested at the time, and was in part a gesture by the then board toward local donors who objected to the open admissions policy the university had just adopted.

At our last open meeting, a historian colleague who opposes renaming said: "You can't judge the past by the standards of the present." I cannot work out what work that sentence is meant to do.

Please set out, as a menu I can circulate to the committee, the distinct precise claims that sentence might be making, separating readings on which it is a platitude, readings on which it is a substantive and defensible moral or interpretive principle, and readings on which it is false or too imprecise to assess. In particular, I would like these ambiguities resolved into separate theses, since they seem to me to be doing all the work:

1. Whose past? The object of judgment might be Carrothers's own conduct in the 1880s, or the 1961 board's decision to honour him, or the university's continuing to honour him now.
2. Whose standards, and of what kind? "The standards of the time" might mean what most people then accepted, or what the best-informed and most severe critics of the time said, or what it was reasonable to expect this man, in his position, to have known and appreciated.
3. What exactly is being withheld? Withholding blame or mitigating it is one thing; concluding that a person is nonetheless an unfitting object of public honour is another; and a further question again is whether the continued honouring itself constitutes a present wrong to anyone.
4. What is the name doing? Fewer than one in ten of our undergraduates can identify the namesake, so it is arguable that "Carrothers Hall" now works as a bare address rather than as a claim about Carrothers, and that its commemorative force has lapsed. I would welcome readings that turn on what a commemorative name is for and on whether its meaning can drift or expire.

For each reading, say whether it is trivial, defensible or false; say which one someone in my colleague's position is most likely to mean; and — the part I most need — say whether, granted it is true, it would actually get him the conclusion that we should leave the name alone, or whether that conclusion needs a further premise the slogan does not supply. If any of the readings collapse into the related claim that renaming would "erase history", please mark that explicitly and treat it separately.
