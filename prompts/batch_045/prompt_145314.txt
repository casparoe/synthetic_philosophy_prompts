I spent most of last night arguing with a friend — a political-theory postdoc who describes herself as a contractualist of the Scanlonian sort — about whether the complaints of many people can ever add up, and I left convinced that she was running a rule she never stated. She conceded early and happily that her view cannot simply sum benefits or harms, and she insists that the only comparisons that do moral work are pairwise: what one identifiable person could object to, weighed against what another could. Her verdicts were these. Certain death for one versus certain death for ten: save the ten. Certain death for one versus a million people each getting a mild one-day headache: save the one, because a million headaches do not amount to a death. Certain death for one versus ten thousand people each getting a papercut: still the one. Two people each certain to lose a leg versus one: the two, and when the complaints on each side are exactly equal she is content with a coin flip, though she thinks the numbers settle it when one side is larger. So far this is the familiar picture. The argument got interesting when I pressed her on two cases she had not thought about. First, risk: a policy that exposes each of a million people to a one-in-ten-thousand chance of a painful death, she said, is obviously worse than one that lets a single identifiable person die — even though, taken pairwise, each exposed person's complaint looks like a ten-thousandth of a death while the identifiable person's is a whole one. She replied only that risk is different. Second, repetition within a single life: one person woken every night for a year by a neighbour's faulty alarm has, she said, a perfectly serious complaint, and you do not need a crowd for it to matter — even though no single night's waking is worth objecting to.

Here is the case I want to put to her next week, and I would like your prediction before I do. A hospital can fund one of two things. Option A: a single identified patient, Ms. Adeyemi, keeps the hearing in both ears; under the alternative she would lose the hearing in one ear, permanently, with no other effects. Option B: three thousand identified patients are spared a year in which each of them suffers, every day, a two-second muscle cramp caused by a piece of equipment; each cramp is individually trivial, nobody would bother to complain about any one of them, and nobody is told at the outset how long the cramps will continue — but by the end of the year each person's accumulated burden is, by any reasonable measure, very roughly as bad as losing the hearing in one ear. Nobody in either option is at risk of anything larger; the 3,000 are not statistical people, they are named on a list.

What I want from you is this: the rule she seems to be running; the other rules that fit all of her earlier verdicts at least as well as that one does, with what each of them predicts about this case; your own best guess at what she will say, with the reason you prefer it over the alternatives; and the single further case you would put to her that would best tell the surviving rules apart — I suspect something like the same case with the crowd removed, but you may think the discriminating case is elsewhere, perhaps in how she handles risk. I would also like your view on whether her set of verdicts can be made coherent at all, or whether the risk case forces her to give up something she would rather not. Please argue the thing out rather than pointing me at things to read: I have no library access this month, so citations and reading lists are useless to me. And write in continuous prose, the way you would write a letter — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
