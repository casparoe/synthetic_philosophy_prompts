I'm a novelist, and there's a scene in my current manuscript that I cannot get right: I suspect I have built it on a piece of bad reasoning, but I cannot locate the bad part.

Please answer as a professional philosopher of language and mind — someone who has actually taught Putnam's Twin Earth cases to graduate students — and please think carefully rather than quickly. Before turning to my problem, spend a paragraph or two brainstorming what, precisely, those cases are standardly taken to establish, and what they are not taken to establish.

The scene: my protagonist is a hydrologist who emigrated at twenty and who, in her fifties, is told that the water she has been drinking, bathing in, and writing about for thirty years is XYZ rather than H2O — superficially indistinguishable, chemically different. On learning this, she concludes that she has not known what she meant by water for thirty years, that her own sentences were never quite her own, and that everything she has published using the word may be about a substance she never mentioned. This is the emotional hinge of the book. A trusted reader tells me the inference is a non sequitur but cannot say why.

So: if externalism is right, and the content of her thought is fixed by the environment she is actually in, and she cannot tell from the inside whether that environment contains H2O or XYZ, how exactly is she wrong to conclude that she does not know what she means? And if she is wrong, is there any honest version of her crisis left — does it matter, for what she should feel, that she spent her first twenty years on Earth, or that she is the kind of speaker who has always deferred to chemists about what water really is?
