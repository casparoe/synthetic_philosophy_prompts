me and my friend got into a proper two-hour argument after rewatching Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind (the Gondry/Kaufman one, 2004) last night, and I want someone to actually settle it instead of us just repeating ourselves. His claim is that the film is secretly an argument for the extended mind thesis: Lacuna only deletes the internal traces, but Joel's memory of Clementine is also sitting in the Montauk beach where they met, in the bookshop she works at, in the apartment, in his diary and his habits and the routes he walks, in Patrick and Stan and Clementine herself — and sure enough all that world-stuff re-seeds the relationship in the last act, and the tape Mary mails out at the end is basically Otto's notebook with a romantic plot attached. My counter was that he's got the direction of the argument backwards. If the extended view were really true, then a working erasure procedure would have to be part brain surgery and part world surgery: repaint the house, edit the diary, get Clementine and the Lacuna staff to misremember, maybe relocate Joel's whole body of habits. Since the film treats deleting the head-stuff as sufficient, it's tacitly internalist about what a memory is. He says world surgery is exactly what the extended view predicts and the movie just doesn't show it because it would be unfilmable, which I think is a cop-out but I can't quite say why.

So read the film for me with that disagreement in mind. I don't need anything external — no sources, no reading list, no quotations, I just want the argument. A few things I'd really like taken seriously:

Run the film's central case against the criteria people actually use for the extended mind: the parity principle, the reliability/accessibility/trust-and-glue conditions, automatic endorsement, and the standing worry that the environment is merely causing rather than constituting. My suspicion is Joel's scattered traces fail those conditions in interesting ways — he doesn't consult them in the right way, he actively runs from the ones he stumbles into, and the tape is something he never endorsed at all until he's already a different person. Is the film then a case where the world does all the causal work and none of the cognitive work?

Take a modified procedure seriously: imagine Lacuna sold a premium package that also scrubbed the apartment, the photos, the diary, and induced forgetting in everyone else who was there. On the extended view, is that just a bigger version of the same operation — meaning the standard procedure is a half-measure that only looks complete because we're all latent internalists? Or does it become a reductio: once you have to edit the world to delete a memory, have you lost your grip on what makes a memory someone's rather than something's?

And the variant me and my friend really couldn't settle: suppose a client, before going under, deliberately builds a prosthesis to defeat the erasure — letters to herself, a rearranged apartment, a standing plan to be in Montauk on a certain day, a recorded message telling her to trust whatever she's about to feel. Would the post-procedure person literally be remembering, on the extended view? Or is that just a stranger being steered by a dead person's props? I like this case because it seems to split reliving a memory from merely acting on stored content reliably, and I want to know which of those the extended mind thesis is actually committed to, and whether committing to the second one is a feature or a symptom.

Also tell me where the reading breaks down, because I think there's a real risk he's just reading his thesis into a love story. The film is in love with the idea that something survives underneath the erasure — Joel hiding Clementine in childhood scenes, the way she tells him to meet her in Montauk as the memory crumbles, the sense that the memory itself is fighting to save her — and that's an inner-theater, inner-residue picture, not a distributed one. Where does the movie resist the extended reading, where does it genuinely support it, and which two or three scenes actually carry the argument? I'm writing this up for the little film-and-philosophy night I run, so I need the scenes I can point people at.
