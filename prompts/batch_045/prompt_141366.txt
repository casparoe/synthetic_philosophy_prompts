ok so I'm giving a talk next week at a one-day workshop called something like 'Representation in Brains and Machines' — philosophy department and an interpretability lab co-hosting, 40 minutes plus 20 for questions. My thesis is easy to state: the features that interpretability tools decompose a network into are genuinely *in* the network, not just summaries we find legible and project onto it. Realism about machine representations, defended on causal grounds rather than on 'it looks meaningful to us' grounds.

The audience is the problem. Roughly half are philosophers of mind who did their training during the 90s fights about whether a state needs a proper function or a selection history to have content at all; they'll want to know what makes a direction in activation space different from the mercury column in a thermometer. The other half are interpretability people who build sparse autoencoders and do intervention-style causal analysis; most are pragmatists who think 'but is it *really* a representation' has no answer, and they'll be annoyed if the talk turns into philosophy of science conducted about their objects. There's also one theoretical neuroscientist who relates everything back to place cells, and one cognitive scientist who works on human concepts and thinks categories are graded and prototype-y. Assume nobody in the room is hostile to interpretability as a project — they just think the realism question is either already settled or empty.

What I'm actually worried about is the pair of cases the second half of the talk rests on. First, the 'dormant feature': a model is pretrained on next-token prediction with an auxiliary classifier head bolted on, and the head is then removed and never used again. Some directions in the residual stream were load-bearing for the language objective as well as the classifier; others were load-bearing only for the classifier. Those classifier-only directions are still there, still cleanly separable, and still causally efficacious if you intervene on them within the classifier's computation — but nothing in the model's actual behaviour depends on them any more, because the thing that read them out is gone. I want to say they're still representations, like a book in a language nobody currently reads. Second, the mirror case: a direction that is clearly load-bearing for behaviour but that nobody can say what it's *of* — no description fits, it isn't anthropomorphically legible at all. I want to say those are my strongest evidence, precisely because they're the ones we never went looking for, and that taken together the two cases show the 'is it real?' question can't be settled by behaviour alone in either direction.

Give me a prep sheet for the question period: seven to nine objections, ordered by how much damage they'd do if I handled them badly rather than by how likely they are, each phrased the way that subgroup would actually phrase it — I want to hear the difference between how the teleosemantics person puts it and how the autoencoder person puts it — each with a rough two-minute reply and an honest note on where my reply is soft or where I should just fold. Tell me which one I should concede in the first five minutes to disarm the room, and give me the objection from the neuroscientist I probably won't have an answer for. And include the one I'm hoping nobody asks: my guess is some version of 'what would you do differently on Monday if you were an anti-realist', but if you think there's something worse coming, say so. Write it all in British English please — analyse, behaviour, colour, that sort of thing — I'll be reading bits of it out loud and my spelling is inconsistent enough already.
