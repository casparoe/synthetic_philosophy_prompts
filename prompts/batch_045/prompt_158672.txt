I recently became the person at my company who handles accommodation requests, and a distinction I now have to apply has started to bother me. Our policy, which I gather tracks the ordinary pattern in employment law, requires us to accommodate sincere religious belief, observance, and practice, and explicitly not to accommodate personal, philosophical, political, or ethical commitments, however deeply felt and however much they cost the person who holds them. The training guidance adds three things I had to use in my first month: that we must never assess whether a religious claim is true or whether anyone else holds it, so that a religion of one is still a religion; that we may ask how the belief connects to the request and, where there is an objective basis for doubt, whether the belief is sincerely held, so that sincerity is fair game even though truth is not; and that the reason religion gets solicitude and secular conviction does not is that religion is comprehensive, unchosen, and identity-constituting in a way that a mere opinion is not. My first two cases were a dispatcher who cannot take shifts past sundown on Fridays and an engineer who refuses, citing an absolute moral conviction he says he did not choose, to work on any codebase destined for a military customer. The first was easy to approve under the policy; the second, by the policy, was not.

At the same time I started reading about religion as a natural phenomenon, and the policy now looks strange from the inside. The material I have been reading treats religious cognition as an ordinary by-product of machinery we have for other purposes, treats the boundary around religion as something modern scholars drew around a messy family of practices rather than something discovered in nature, and in some versions treats public ritual as social technology whose function is to produce commitment and cooperation. That last theme is what unsettles me. If the features the guidance leans on — comprehensiveness, being unchosen, structuring identity and community — are features that plenty of secular commitments can have and that some religious ones arguably lack, then either the accommodation rule rests on a theory of religion that a naturalistic account does not support, or it does not really rest on a theory of religion at all and is quietly tracking something else, such as cost to the employee, administrative convenience, or the fact that a tradition is old and legible to managers.

So I would like the implicit theory reconstructed, not advice: I am not asking what I should do with my two cases. What view of religion does this practice presuppose — that religious conviction is involuntary, that it is comprehensive rather than modular, that it is communal and historically rooted, that it is tied to the supernatural, or some package of these? Which of those does the practice strictly require in order to make sense, and which does it merely fit comfortably with? Where is it underdetermined, so that several quite different theories of religion would license exactly the same rule? And what does the rule do when the features come apart: someone raised inside an all-encompassing secular political movement that they experience as unchosen and identity-constituting; a cradle member of a ritual tradition who treats its doctrines instrumentally and would abandon them at a price; a solitary, overwhelming, unchosen moral conviction with no supernatural content, no ritual and no community behind it; a religious objector whose own tradition regards the objection as optional. Finally, is the asymmetry between truth and sincerity coherent, given that assessing whether something is a religion at all looks much like assessing its content, and would the practice have to change — and how — if it were replaced by a test of conscientious conviction generally, by a role-occupancy test asking whether a commitment plays the part religion plays in a life regardless of supernatural content, or by a test that simply asks what the person would give up?
