I'm in the final year of my philosophy degree, and I'm writing my term paper for a fourth-year seminar on paradoxes of rational belief. We have spent the last four weeks on the lottery paradox and the preface paradox, and our seminar leader has now ruled on five cases in class. She has told us that a sixth will be on the problem sheet next week, and I need to know what she is going to say about it: my paper argues that her verdicts so far run together two claims she has not distinguished, and I would rather find out that I am wrong before I commit to it.

In each case the question she answers is the same. A subject fully believes each member of a set of first-order claims A1 through An, and also fully believes that at least one of them is false. Is that total state rationally criticizable, and if it is, which belief has to go? The cases, as I have them in my notes:

(1) A fair lottery with 1,000 tickets and exactly one guaranteed winner. The subject believes of each ticket that it will lose, and believes that some ticket will win. Verdict: not criticizable. She added that the mistake here is to insist that the subject's beliefs be closed under conjunction.

(2) The author of a 400-claim monograph, each claim individually researched, each one she would put at roughly 0.99, who also believes on the basis of base rates for monographs of that kind that the book contains at least one error. Verdict: not criticizable; she said this has the same shape as the lottery.

(3) The author of a 12-claim paper, each claim individually checked and at roughly 0.995, who also believes there is an error because roughly 30 percent of papers of that kind contain one. Verdict: criticizable, and the error-claim is what has to go.

(4) A subject who believes each of 1,000 claims and believes one of them is false purely on the ground that she is a fallible human being, with no base rate, no audit, and no statistics of any kind. Verdict: criticizable; a bare appeal to general fallibility is not a reason, so again the error-claim goes.

(5) A subject with 400 individually researched claims who then runs a check that detects, with 0.99 reliability, whether a document contains any error at all, though it never locates one. The check returns a positive result. Verdict: not criticizable.

The trouble is that every reconstruction of her rule that I try fits all five verdicts equally well, which leaves me unable to predict the sixth. I would like you to do three things about this.

First, set out the candidate rules that fit her five verdicts, and for each one say what it implies about the two cases below. Second, give me a definite prediction for each of them, with the rule you think she is actually running and your reasons for preferring it over the others. Third, tell me which single further case I should put to her in office hours to settle the matter if these two do not, and say what you would take to be evidence that your prediction is wrong.

The two cases are these:

(6) A subject produces a 20-item report under time pressure. Every item was individually checked against its source and sits at roughly 0.99 for her; the items are relevantly independent of one another, and she is not guessing at any of them. She then learns from a well-conducted study of reports of exactly this kind, produced under exactly this degree of time pressure, that about 70 percent of them contain at least one error. She has no audit, no check of the sort in case (5), and no way of targeting any particular item. She believes each item, and she believes that at least one item is wrong.

(7) A subject produces a 400-item report, every item individually checked and at roughly 0.99, with the items independent of one another. She has no external base rate, no study, and no audit. Her only ground for believing that at least one item is wrong is her own aggregation of her per-item confidence: she multiplies 0.99 by itself 400 times, concludes that the chance all 400 are correct is about 0.02, and believes on that basis that at least one is wrong, while continuing to believe each item.

Please commit to a single verdict on (6) and on (7) rather than laying out what each rule would say and leaving me to choose, and state plainly how confident you are in each prediction. A compact table of rules against cases is welcome if it helps. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question; about 700 words, excluding any table. Please write in American English.
