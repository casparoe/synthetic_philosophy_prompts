I'm in a small reading group that works through one chapter of the philosophy of mind a week, and this month we landed on epiphenomenalism and the so-called paradox of phenomenal judgment. The book we chose is a trade-press introduction — well written, but it has a habit of turning live controversies into settled verdicts — and its handling of the self-stultification objection struck several of us as confident in exactly the places where we were most confused. I'm reconstructing the passage roughly from memory, so the wording isn't exact, but the claims are: epiphenomenalism says that conscious experience is a mere by-product of brain activity, like the whistle on a steam locomotive, which is produced by the engine and contributes nothing to its motion; the brain causes experiences, and experiences cause nothing at all. The trouble, the book says, is that the view saws off the branch it sits on. If my experiences caused nothing, then my saying or thinking "I am now in pain" would not be caused by the pain, and the utterance would come out exactly the same whether or not the pain existed; so I could never have any reason to think I have experiences at all, introspection would be worthless, and the words "I am in pain" would not really be about anything. Worse, the position is self-refuting, since the epiphenomenalist begins by noticing introspectively that experiences exist and then ends up committed to the claim that this very starting point is bogus. That, the book concludes, is why epiphenomenalism has been almost entirely abandoned and why anyone who wants to avoid dualism has to identify experiences with brain states so that they inherit the brain's causal powers.

What I'd like is an accuracy audit of that passage rather than a general essay on epiphenomenalism, and I don't need any references, reading suggestions, or names to look up — I want the reasoning itself taken apart. Which sentences would a philosopher sympathetic to epiphenomenalism accept without complaint, which are standard simplifications that a specialist would let slide in a popular book, and which are genuine misrepresentations? In particular, I suspect something has gone wrong in the slide from "the pain does not cause my report" to "the report would be the same without the pain," and in the further slide to "the report isn't really about the pain," but I can't say precisely what. Please also tell me whether the passage's final verdict — that the view is abandoned and that the identity theory is the destination — follows even from the parts of the summary that are correct, and whether the argument as stated would establish that epiphenomenalism is self-refuting or only something weaker.

Two things from our discussion might help, since they were the points we got stuck on. First, we invented a distinction and want to know whether it does any work: call the version in which the physical cause of an experience metaphysically or nomically necessitates that experience "tethered epiphenomenalism," and the version in which the physical cause could have occurred with no experience, or a different one, alongside it "untethered epiphenomenalism." Our hunch is that the self-stultification objection only bites against the untethered version, but we couldn't say why in a way that satisfied everyone. Second, one member proposed what he called the same-shaft model: imagine the physical state P causing both the quale Q and the report R through two separate downstream pathways, so that R is a perfect indicator of Q even though Q causes nothing — as a speedometer needle driven off the same shaft as the wheels tracks how fast the wheels are turning without being pushed by them. Is the paradox really about causation as such, or is it about something else that the same-shaft model also lacks, such as counterfactual sensitivity, a causal-informational link, or the right relation between the truth-constitution of my judgment and the state it is about? And would it matter whether we frame the problem as epistemic (can I know, or cite evidence for, my own experiences?), semantic (could the term for a quale ever have come to refer to one?), or merely dialectical (can I assert the view without undermining my grounds for it)? Please tell us which of these framings carries the real force, and don't just report that every point is contested — say which replies are the strong ones.
