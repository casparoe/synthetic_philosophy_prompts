I want a number with its derivation, not a survey of the literature. My colleague and I have been arguing about whether deployed language models are best described as agents or as simulators, and I have come to think the argument is stuck for a structural reason. Before I say what that reason is, here is the proposition I want your credence on, stated as precisely as I can.

Proposition P: for systems of the current kind — transformer-based, trained by next-token prediction on a large mixture of human text, then instruction-tuned and preference-trained, then deployed behind a system prompt with tool access — the agent-versus-simulator dispute is level-relative rather than empirical. That is: for any such system, both descriptions are correct, but at different explanatory grains, and once the grain is fixed by specifying a temporal horizon and a unit of behavior, no behavioral disagreement remains. No experiment, however well designed, could produce a result that a competent proponent of either description could not accommodate without giving up anything they actually care about.

Resolution condition, so that P has something like a truth value: I will count P as confirmed if the disagreement turns out to be an artifact of unstated grain parameters — if the two camps were making claims about different timescales or different units of behavior all along, and specifying a horizon collapses the residual disagreement into something small or merely verbal. I will count P as refuted if, at some specified horizon, the two descriptions predict different behavior and one camp concedes on the basis of the result. If in ten years the dispute is in exactly the state it is in now, treat that as weak evidence for P.

My credence in P is about 0.6. My colleague's is about 0.25. He thinks the question has real empirical content and that it will be settled by engineering: give systems persistent memory, long horizons, and stakes, and we will find out whether there is anything there that acts for reasons or only something that continues text.

Give me your own number, decomposed. I would like you to use the following sub-questions, and to add, drop, or restructure them if you think I have the structure wrong — but say why if you do.

First, how much of the dispute survives the fixing of a grain? Your credence that agent and simulator make genuinely different predictions about one fully specified system at one fully specified timescale, after both camps have said what they would count as a result.

Second, a conjecture of my own, which I want priced separately. Preference training and instruction tuning, I take it, do not leave the space of characters a model can simulate free-ranging: they install a heavily reinforced helpful-assistant attractor, so that under long horizons and under degradation of the immediate prompt context, simulation does not wander but converges on a fixed point. Call this the fixed-point conjecture. If it is right, then at the horizon where it holds there is no behavioral difference left between simulating an agent and being one, and the simulator framing has quietly become the agent framing. Give me your credence in the fixed-point conjecture, and your credence in the conditional claim that if it holds, the dispute at that horizon is merely verbal.

Third, the training-objective term. How much of the current failure of these systems to show cross-horizon commitment persistence is due to next-token prediction over a mixture of speakers, in a way that no amount of scale would remove, and how much is due to contingent engineering facts — no persistent memory, bounded context, no stable cross-session identity, no skin in the game? Split your probability between those two explanations.

Fourth, the stance-dependence term. Your credence that agent has an extension fixed by the world rather than fixed partly by the explanatory purposes of whoever is doing the attributing, in the style of the intentional-stance tradition. Note that this term and the first one are not the same: the first asks whether the descriptions diverge after the grain is fixed, this asks whether there is a fact of the matter about which description is correct at all.

Fifth, whatever else you think belongs. If you think the moral or legal upshot of the classification is doing hidden work in how people argue about it, price that in as its own term rather than gesturing at it.
