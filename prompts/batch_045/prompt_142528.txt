The audience is four people: a senior research engineer and an evaluation researcher, both from a lab's alignment team, who think about proposals like this almost entirely in terms of whether they can be turned into a training signal and how you would measure success; a formal epistemologist from a philosophy department who works on testimony, peer disagreement and the regress of reasons; and a program officer at the foundation who will actually sign off on the money and who cares mainly whether any of this changes a decision anyone would make about a real system.

My thesis is the one I've been chewing on for about a year: that debate as a scheme for scalable oversight fails on obfuscated arguments, and that the failure is worse than it looks because cross-examination is supposed to be the fix. By obfuscated I mean an argument that is long, made of steps that each look locally fine to a competent judge, and that contains a single error or hand-wave whose location the honest debater cannot find within the handful of moves they are allowed. The judge cannot read the whole thing, the honest debater cannot locate the flaw, so the dishonest debater wins without ever having to say anything that looks false. My proposed patch is what I've started calling step-betting: instead of scoring debaters only on the final claim, have the protocol require them to attach a probability to every intermediate claim they assert and score all of those with a strictly proper scoring rule, so that producing a long obfuscated chain means risking your score on a great many intermediate steps while a short honest argument risks only a few, which should make obfuscation expensive rather than free. I know the two places I am soft: I have no empirical results at all, and I haven't shown that this failure mode actually arises for arguments produced by a trained model rather than by an adversary who is deliberately obfuscating.
