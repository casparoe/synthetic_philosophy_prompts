I have been reading LessWrong, Astral Codex Ten and the older Overcoming Bias comment threads for roughly fifteen years, and the genre I have come to trust most from that scene is the adversarial collaboration done honestly: both parties taboo their loaded words, write down what they already agree on, state the residual disagreement in wording each side would accept for its own position, name the cruxes, and pre-register what each expects the relevant evidence to show. I want a document in exactly that genre on a topic that normally gets handled in op-eds and policy white papers instead: responsibility gaps for autonomous systems.

You are a professional philosopher specialising in the ethics of technology and the philosophy of moral responsibility, and also an experienced facilitator of adversarial collaborations. Work carefully, in stages.

Stage one, private brainstorming, not reproduced in the final document: spend a few hundred words listing (a) the distinct things someone might mean when they say that a machine creates a responsibility gap, (b) the strongest reply available to the side that denies there is a gap, and (c) which of the standard disagreements here are substantive and which are only disputes about how to use the word responsibility. Only once that is done should you draft the document.

The two parties are these.

Party A, the gap realist, holds that for at least some harmful outcomes produced by autonomous or continually retrained systems there is no human or corporate agent who satisfies the conditions that make blame, censure or punishment deserved: nobody had the relevant foresight, nobody had the relevant contemporaneous control, and nobody violated an identifiable duty. Realists hold further that this is not merely a legal inconvenience or an insurance problem. It is a moral remainder: a victim is owed an answer that no one can give, and a class of harms occurs with no one appropriately answerable in the desert-implying sense.

Party B, the gap functionalist, holds that responsibility is a social-moral practice with forward-looking jobs: guiding conduct, giving assurance, repairing losses, aligning incentives, and expressing what we demand of one another. The practice has always adapted to new technology, and here it can adapt through role responsibility, vicarious and strict liability, no-fault compensation schemes, and duties of oversight and upgrade governance. On this view there is either no gap at all, or only a request that the practice be updated, which is not the same thing as a moral remainder.

I have three hobbyhorses that I have never seen separated properly in the literature, and I want the document to separate them. First, the distinction between a foreseeability gap (nobody could reasonably have predicted the failure mode), a contemporaneous-control gap (at the moment of action no human was steering the system finely enough for the act to be theirs), and what I call the transmission gap (even where a human or firm is clearly the right addressee of demands and clearly owes repair, the ordinary links that carry blame from an outcome back to an agent are missing, so blame feels misplaced even though compensation does not). Second, what I call the capability-scaled standard of care: the proposal that the duty owed by a deployer rises with the capability and autonomy of the system, so that the defence we could not have predicted it is never available, because at higher capability we owe more foresight, more sandboxing and more monitoring. Third, what I call the type/token asymmetry: whether the moral response we owe is owed to the individual harmful event or to the class of risky deployments of which it is one instance.

Here is the case I want both parties to apply their view to. Call it the Continuous-Update Warehouse Case. A logistics firm runs a fleet of autonomous warehouse robots. The control policy is not hand-written: it is produced by a nightly automated pipeline that retrains on the previous day's telemetry, and the pipeline is maintained by a rotating team of engineers, each of whom reviews only a sample of its outputs. On one shift a robot misclassifies a rare sensor fault, occurring in roughly one in forty thousand operating hours, as a routine obstruction, and a worker is seriously injured. Afterwards: (i) no individual engineer's decision is identifiable as the cause; (ii) the firm met every prevailing industry standard for monitoring, sampling and human review; (iii) the firm's fleet-wide serious-injury rate had been running at 1.7 times the human-staffed baseline, and that elevation was statistically detectable in aggregate telemetry three months before the incident, although no specific design choice could be isolated as its cause; (iv) because of the retraining pipeline, the behaviour that produced the injury is not reproducible offline.

And here is the institutional mechanism I want both parties to assess: what I call the surety-and-answer regime. Deployers of systems above a capability threshold must fund a no-fault compensation pool, with premiums set actuarially by the measured harm rate of the deployed class, and must take part in a mandatory post-incident public proceeding whose stated purpose is to establish what happened, what the deployer will change, and what the victim is owed, but which is formally forbidden from finding fault or assigning blame. The question is whether this closes the gap, whether it merely changes the subject, or whether it is the best available response to something that remains a real remainder.

Produce the joint statement in this format.

Section 1: a numbered list of six to nine agreed claims, each precise enough that a hostile reader could not accept it while denying the substance. Both parties must be able to sign every claim in this section.

Section 2: a disagreements table. One row per crux, with columns for: the crux stated as a question; the realist formulation, worded so a realist would sign it; the functionalist formulation, worded so a functionalist would sign it; the case, argument or piece of evidence both parties agree would bear on the crux; the realist's prediction about what it will show; the functionalist's prediction; and a verdict on whether the disagreement at that crux is substantive or merely verbal. Include at least these cruxes: whether the remainder is a matter of desert-bearing blame at the level of the individual event or of governance at the level of the deployment class; whether a capability-scaled standard of care closes the gap or simply relocates it to whatever the deployer could not do given the system's opacity; whether population-level statistical evidence of the kind described in clause (iii) can ground blame or only regulation and compensation; whether blame transmission requires contemporaneous control or only counterfactual avoidability at the point of the deployment decision; whether the surety-and-answer regime answers the demand that someone answer or silences it; and what each party would make of a well-run empirical finding that victims receiving swift no-fault compensation report the same satisfaction of their sense that someone answered as victims who see someone blamed. Predictions must be specific enough to be wrong; nowhere in this document may either party say that more research is needed.

Section 3: the Continuous-Update Warehouse Case, applied. Give each party its own paragraph of about 150 words stating what its view implies about that case, including what the firm owes, who if anyone is blameworthy, and what the correct institutional response is.

Section 4: verbal disputes identified. List any disagreement you judge to be about words rather than substance, and for each give both parties' positions restated with the words responsibility, blame, desert and accountable tabooed.

Constraints on the whole document. Do not adjudicate: this is a text both parties sign, not your verdict. Do not cite, name authors, or supply reading of any kind; everything must be argued from the positions as characterised. Steelman both sides; a well-informed reader should find at least two formulations here that they have not seen before. Use a formal, polished register, avoid hedging and throat-clearing, and aim for 1,400 to 1,800 words for the final document, excluding your private brainstorming.
