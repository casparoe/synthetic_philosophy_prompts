I write novels, and I've agreed to run a nintey-minute evening session at the writers' centre next month. About a dozen people, mostly novelists plus two poets, nobody with any philosophy, and at least two of them will arrive believing that theory is a machine for spoiling stories. The session is nominally about writing from the inside of an experience you haven't had, and I want the Mary case to be its spine: the neuroscientist who has spent her whole life in a black-and-white room, knows every physical fact about colour vision there is to know, and then walks out and sees a red tomato for the first time. My group already half-knows this one and half-misuses it, which is why I'd rather build on it than on something new.

So please don't explain the argument to me. Plan how I should explain it to them.

What I need:

The order the pieces have to go in, and what depends on what. I don't want a list of points that could be shuffled; I want to know which thing has to be in place before the next thing is even intelligible, and which bit will collapse the session if I put it too early.

The one carrying example, and what it has to have and what it has to lack. My instinct is that it should not be a character from my own work in progress, but tell me if I'm being precious about that.

My worry about completeness. If Mary doesn't know which physical state she's in when the tomato shows up, her surprise looks like she merely learned a boring fact, and then nothing is threatened. Where does that have to go in the sequence, and can it be done in fifteen minutes for people who have never met the word indexical? If it can't, tell me what to sacrifice.

The emotion swap. Someone will say within the first ten minutes that it's just like writing grief before you've lost anyone. My instinct is that grief is not like red, because having grief seems to require a pile of beliefs and a history, and that this difference quietly makes the case weaker rather than stronger, so I should hold the swap back rather than lean on it. Tell me whether that instinct is wrong, and if it is, what the right thing to do with the swap is.

Misconceptions, in the order they'll actually form. The ones I expect: that the case simply refutes physicalism; that it shows a machine could never be conscious; that its moral is that we can't imagine other people's experience; that Mary's surprise is just a broken expectation and therefore proves nothing. Correct any of those I've got wrong, tell me the order they'll arrive in, and say where in the session to head each off — I'd much rather defuse them at the moment they form than deal with them in a summary at the end.

Where I say out loud that the philosophers don't agree, without it feeling like a cop-out. This group will want a verdict and I don't have one.

One question to put to the room at the end that would show they'd got the argument rather than just absorbed its atmosphere, and a different one that would show they'd got something usable for their own manuscripts.

And tell me plainly what to refuse to get into at this level. I'm guessing the zombie thing, representationalism, and the phenomenal-concept business, but I want to know which one I'll be most tempted by, why it will eat the hour and a half, and what I should say instead when someone asks me about it.
