i work at a comparative cognition field station that also hosts a robotics group, and im on the panel that signs off on any protocol involving a subject that might have a mind. We recently rewrote our rules so that what we owe a subject scales with what kind of creature it is, in Dennett's sense of the four kinds — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian — and honestly the rewrite was the easy part. The hard part is that we have no procedure for deciding which tier a subject belongs to, so we argue it out case by case and recieve the same two objections every time.

What I need is a test battery the panel can actually run: the tests in the order we should run them, what counts as passing each one, where the procedure stops with a verdict of too low to bother with, and where it has to stop and hand the decision to a person because what's left is a values question dressed up as a factual one.

What we're working with:

- The tiers do genuinely different work for us. Below the Skinnerian line we only owe the population anything — habitat, population-level effects, nothing individual. Once a subject is Skinnerian we start owing it a learning history: no protocols that work purely by punishment, enrichment required, that kind of thing. Once it's Popperian we owe it something about its expectations: a thing that can be wrong about how the world works can be wrong in a way that costs it, so deliberately surprising it is a different sort of harm than hurting it, and we also have to let it gather information rather than just react. Once it's Gregorian the competence lives partly outside the animal, in the tools and signs it was trained up on, so stripping the scaffolding away counts as a deprivation.

- Only cheap non-invasive tests, about twenty subjects, two weeks, no surgery or neural recording. The panel chair, the station vet and a philosopher we keep on retainer all have to be able to read the result on one page.

- We've anchored the bottom already: everyone agrees a slime mold finding the shortest path through a maze sits below the Skinnerian line, since it changes its shape but nothing in it was shaped by consequences over its own lifetime. Use that as the calibration case for whatever your first test is meant to detect.

Two wrinkles we keep tripping on and that the procedure has to handle:

1. Contamination and ordering. A subject with a long training history will fake the higher tiers on anything it has been shaped on, so I assume tests have to be run on transfer problems it has never seen, and that some tests spoil the subject for later ones. Say which test you'd run first and why, and what you'd do with an animal that someone else has already been training for years.

2. Broken rungs. The kinds are supposed to stack, but we get proposals where a system looks like it uses cultural tools and does not look like it tests hypotheses inside its own head first. Give us a rule for a subject that seems to pass a higher tier without passing the one below it — measurement failure, classify by the highest tier passed, or admit the ladder is the wrong picture. And while you're at it, tell us what the procedure does when it isn't clear what the subject even is: the individual or the collective it belongs to.

Then run the whole thing on three cases and show the work:

- a honeybee swarm choosing a nest site, where scouts press their case by dancing and the colony converges on one option
- an octopus in our tanks that opens a jar after watching another octopus do it once, and has never been shaped on jars
- the robotics group's warehouse agent: a learned low-level policy at the bottom, a language-model planner on top that writes and revises its subgoals in words, and access to tools — a map, a lookup table, its own past logs

At least one of those should come out somewhere we don't like, and I want you to say which and why that's the honest result. Also say plainly what the procedure can't tell us. I'd rather hand the vet the untidy version than the tidy one.
