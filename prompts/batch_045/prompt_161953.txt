I build simulation software for a living and have no formal training in philosophy, so please define the distinctions you rely on rather than only naming them, and don't assume I know the standard vocabulary. I would rather have the machinery spelled out than be told which dead people argued about it.

The argument below is mine, assembled after an argument with two colleagues about whether a deterministic world is a predictable one. I run numerical models of physical systems, and the gap between the model being deterministic and the model telling me what will happen is something I hit weekly, so I thought I understood the gap. Then I wrote the argument down and it seems to show there is no gap at all, which I don't believe. Here it is:

1. A system is deterministic if and only if, for any state s at a time t, the laws together with s fix exactly one state at every other time. Nothing about anyone's knowledge or computing power appears in this definition.

2. If the mapping from initial state to later state is a genuine function, then an agent who possessed the exact initial state and the laws could in principle produce the later state. A mapping that could not be evaluated even in principle would not be a function but merely the description of one.

3. So determinism entails predictability in principle, and every instance of actual unpredictability — error bars, ensemble forecasts, we cannot say which way it will settle — is a limitation of instruments, of memory, or of clock cycles, never a feature of the dynamics.

4. Chaos, in the sense I use it: sensitive dependence on initial conditions, meaning that differences in initial state smaller than any bound you care to name grow to differences of macroscopic size within some finite time, at an exponential rate.

5. Sensitive dependence is exactly the mechanism by which finite measurement error becomes macroscopic forecast error. So the unpredictability of chaotic systems is an instance of 3: an instrument problem bolted onto a perfectly definite dynamics. Chaos is determinism viewed through a short ruler.

6. Therefore the popular claim that chaos shows a deterministic world can be unpredictable in principle is simply confused. Either the world is deterministic, and hence predictable in principle, or it is not deterministic. There is no third option, and the question of predictability collapses entirely into the question of determinism.

7. Corollary: for any deterministic system whose laws are known, and for any target accuracy e at any target lead time T, there exists some finite precision d on the initial state and some finite amount of computation that lands within e at T. Predictability in principle means only that this curve d(e, T) exists, not that anyone can afford to evaluate it.

8. Second corollary: a computer large enough, running inside the universe, could in principle forecast any later state of the universe, including the forecast itself and the machine producing it. I find this uncomfortable but cannot locate the break.

Step 6 is the part I cannot accept, and 7 and 8 look worse. But my attempts to locate the flaw disagree with each other, and I suspect none of them is quite right:

One colleague points at 2 and says something about functions that are perfectly well defined yet not computable, and about a diagonal argument, and about the initial state of a continuous system being a real number that would take infinitely many bits to write down. I follow the words and not the force. Premise 1 says there is exactly one future, and it seems to me that there is a unique answer and the answer can be produced cannot come apart — if they can come apart, I do not know what kind of thing the second claim is, or how it could be false while the first is true.

Another colleague says 1 through 6 are fine and the rot is in 7 and 8: she says that because the needed precision grows exponentially with the lead time, the measurement effort needed to forecast a chaotic system a few seconds further out grows beyond anything a device inside the universe could carry out, so my curve exists as mathematics while asking for its values is like asking for something no finite physical process can deliver. She also says 8 fails for a self-reference reason: a predictor that must predict its own output is in the position of a program asked to report whether it will halt. I do not see why either point touches predictability in principle, which is what 6 is about, rather than predictability on a budget.

A third reading I tried myself: 5 misdescribes chaos. Sensitive dependence is a claim about the dynamics itself — about neighbouring trajectories separating at a fixed exponential rate, about the stretching and folding of the state space — and not a claim about anybody's instruments. So the separation of determinism from predictability might be internal to the dynamics after all. But when I try to make this precise it turns back into 3, because a dynamics that merely separates nearby trajectories has not yet said anything about what anyone can know.

There is one more thing from my own work that I cannot place. In double precision my computed trajectory departs completely from the trajectory I meant to compute, yet it stays close to some genuine trajectory of the same system, and the long-run statistics come out right. So my simulation both predicts and fails to predict, depending on which question you ask of it, and I do not know which of the two is the one at issue in 6.

What I want from you: name the step where the argument actually breaks, and if it breaks by equivocation, say exactly which phrase is doing double duty and what the two readings are. Then price the alternatives — for each of the diagnoses above, and any better one you have, say what I would have to give up to adopt it. In particular: is there a coherent position on which determinism in the sense of 1 is true, predictability in the sense of 2 fails for reasons that are not about budgets or instruments, and chaos is still doing genuine explanatory work in accounting for why? Or does keeping that combination force me to weaken 1 into something that is no longer the plain claim that there is exactly one future?

One last question, which I think is where the answer lives. Suppose I restrict the whole argument to systems with finitely many discrete states, cellular-automaton style, where the exact state is a finite bit string and the update rule is a finite table. There, 2 looks undeniable: simulate the thing and you have the answer. If my argument is sound for those systems and unsound for continuous ones, that tells me the trouble is in what infinite state spaces do to the notion of an exact state — but I want you to say whether that is the right diagnosis, whether it also depends on whether the laws themselves are computable, and what it costs me to accept that determinism and predictability come apart only in some systems and not others.
