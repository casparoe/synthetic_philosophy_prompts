Im a second-year physics PhD student, mostly doing quantum-information-adjacent stuff, but I keep sneaking into the foundations reading group and it is getting me into trouble.

Last week at group meeting I made the mistake of asking whether "is the wavefunction real?" is even a well-posed question. My actual point was that people run together at least four seperate claims when they say psi is real: (1) psi is part of the theory's representational machinery — it's the object the formalism assigns to a system and then evolves; (2) psi is ontic in the sense that distinct pure states are never compatible with one and the same complete physical state of the system; (3) psi is an item in the inventory of the world, something like a field, awkwardly, a field on configuration space rather than on ordinary space; (4) psi does the work — it's what the Hamiltonian acts on, what entanglement is a fact about, what we cite when we explain interference or the Bell correlations. I wanted to argue that (2) and (3) can come apart: you can be psi-ontic in sense (2) without psi itself being a piece of the furniture, e.g. on views where psi is more like a law or a nomological object than a substance. And I wanted to argue that this matters, because the no-go results I have in mind (the Pusey–Barrett–Rudolph style ones — as I understand them, they need an assumption that independently prepared systems have independent physical states, correct me if I've got that wrong) really only touch (2). I also suspect "psi-epistemic" is itself three different views — psi is an agent's information, psi is just a compressed encoding of something deeper, psi is nothing but a device for churning out probabilities — and that people slide between them.
