I recently read a piece in a general-interest magazine on voting reform that summarizes Arrow's impossibility theorem roughly as follows:

"Arrow proved mathematically that no voting system can be fair. Take any procedure for turning individuals' rankings into a single collective ranking: at least one of a handful of minimal, obviously reasonable requirements has to fail. And the proof assumes nothing at all about the procedure — ranked ballots, approval voting, score voting, it makes no difference — because it only assumes that each voter has some ordering of the options. The requirements include: if everyone prefers A to B, the group must too; and the group's ranking of A against B must not depend on how anyone ranks some third, irrelevant option C — which is why a losing candidate dropping out of a race shouldn't be able to change who wins. The requirement that always gives way is the one saying no single voter should get their way regardless of everyone else: every such procedure hands some voter the role of dictator. The theorem therefore shows that settling a disagreement by voting has no rational justification. Nor does it help how voters' preferences happen to be structured: even when everyone's rankings can be lined up along a single left-to-right dimension, the paradox goes through exactly as before. So moving from plurality to any other method is rearranging deck chairs; a coin toss would be just as defensible."

I have no expertise here, but two things made me uneasy: I have heard that the result is stated for a particular kind of procedure with a particular axiom set, and I have also heard that when preferences are single-peaked along one dimension the cycling problem is supposed to vanish. I cannot tell whether either point actually dents what the article says, or where else it may be going wrong.

Please audit the passage claim by claim: which parts would a social choice theorist accept as stated, which are acceptable shorthand for a popular audience, and which are simply wrong? Say whether the article's practical conclusion survives once the errors are corrected, and mark which of your corrections are uncontroversial and which are themselves contested. If either of my two suspicions is misguided, or if my framing of the question is confused, please say so plainly instead of accommodating it.
