Hi — I work in responsible sourcing for a mid-sized food company, and the same shape of decision keeps landing on my desk, so I want something repeatable rather than me reinventing the argument each time.

The shape is this. We're being pushed to swap conventional proteins (fishmeal, soy) for farmed insects — black soldier fly larvae, crickets, mealworms — and separately we're being asked to make public claims about animal welfare. Every version of this runs into the same two facts pulling in opposite directions. First, the numbers of individual animals involved are completely absurd compared to anything else we deal with: someone on my team did a back-of-envelope suggesting a single tonne of larvae protein is on the order of a million-plus individuals, and our annual volumes would put us in the billions. Second, it's genuinely unclear whether any of them feel anything at all — "sentience" is the word people in the space use — and the honest probability could be anywhere from near-zero to fairly substantial. When I try to think it through myself I end up just multiplying, and then literally everything comes out as a five-alarm emergency, which can't be right and also isn't usable: if every warehouse fumigation is a moral catastrophe then nothing is.

So: write me a decision procedure my team can actually run. Concretely, I need:

- The questions in order, and for each one what would count as a good enough answer to move on. We are procurement people, not biologists — flag which steps genuinely need an entomologist and which we can do ourselves with a day per decision and normal internet access.
- Where the procedure just stops and says no, and where it hands the question to a human because it's a values call rather than a factual one. I need that distinction sharp, because I have to explain to our sustainability committee why something is their call and not mine.
- How to handle the fact that the key probability is itself something I don't trust. Experts disagree wildly, and I don't want to plug in a made-up number and let the arithmetic launder it. Tell me what to write down instead of a number when there isn't one.
- How to handle the sign problem: even if they do feel things, I can't tell whether a larval life in a bin of food waste is a good life or a bad one, and which way that goes reverses what the huge numbers are telling me to do. The procedure needs to not quietly assume one of those.
- How to score the indirect evidence when it points different ways. People cite reflex responses to being damaged, versus learning to avoid a place where they got hurt, versus the fact that they'll keep tending an injured body part, versus trade-off behaviour where they accept one bad thing to get another. I need a rule for what I'm allowed to count as evidence here and what's just a good story, and what to do when the proxies disagree.
- Whether there is any point at which we stop counting. I need a defensible place to say "this is below the line, site manager decides, no escalation", because otherwise the procedure will eat the whole company. But I also don't want the line to be a way of making the numbers disappear by fiat — tell me how I'd know if I'd drawn it in a self-serving place.
- How much the counterfactual matters. A lot of this stuff is raised on waste we'd otherwise send to landfill, so some unknown number of these individuals would have existed anyway, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's a defence, an irrelevant detail, or an extra problem.
- And an edge case: tell me what the unit of counting even is when the animal is a colony. If a supplier ever turns up with ant or termite protein, I don't think I can say how many individuals there are in any sense that means something.

Then run it on three real-ish cases. (1) Switching a farmed-salmon supply chain from fishmeal to black soldier fly larvae raised on food waste — this one goes to the board and marketing wants a claim on pack. (2) Routine pest control in a grain warehouse, stored-product moths and cockroaches, decided by a site manager in about twenty minutes, weekly, forever. (3) A startup pitching "no-kill" insect protein: they let the animals complete their whole life cycle and collect them after natural death, it costs roughly three times as much, and they want us to sell it as the welfare-friendly option in a premium bar. I have a feeling at least one of these is going to come out in a way that costs us money or makes us look bad — please tell me straight rather than softening it.

One more thing: whatever we produce has to be defensible in public. NGOs will read our policy and a journalist could ask about it, so "we ran the numbers" won't do if the numbers were invented. Please also say plainly what this procedure can't do.

Don't bother with a reading list or a pile of citations, we won't follow them up and I can't check them anyway. But do tell me how confident you are in what you're claiming, and single out the one point you're least sure of — ideally the one where you think a reasonable person who knows this literature would probably disagree with you.
