my flatmate and I got into a stupidly long argument last night about whether my confidence that the train will be late tomorrow even has an exact number attached to it. He says of course it does, I'm just bad at reading off my own state; I said no, sometimes the honest answer is a range and demanding a point value is like demanding a sharp border for a blurry shape. We got nowhere, and now I want to turn it into a post.

I write a small substack for a forecasting-adjacent crowd — people who are comfortable with Brier scores and calibration plots but have never heard of anyone arguing that credences might be genuinely indeterminate. I want to make them a "which kind of imprecise-probability person are you, actually?" decision tree. The whole point is that it should sort them by what they say about concrete cases, not by which label they'd pick, so no node should be anything like "do you think your credences are sharp?" — that just asks for the label.

Here's what I need:

- Start everyone from "I don't have a single number for this one" and branch with questions a reader can answer cold, without knowing any theory names.
- Six to eight leaves. It matters to me that the tree can land someone on: (a) the plain Bayesian line where the imprecision is just ignorance of your own sharp number and you do sensitivity analysis over priors; (b) views where the range itself is the real state; (c) worst-case / robust-decision-rule views that still pick one action; (d) permissive views where several actions are admissible and the choice isn't made by the numbers. If there's a respectable non-additive "mass on sets" view that fits, give it a leaf too, but don't force one.
- The nodes I care about, roughly: is the imprecision ignorance of a sharp value or is it the state itself; what you do when you're forced to act anyway; what happens when you're imprecise now but certain you'll be sharp after you see the evidence (this one came up last night and neither of us had an answer); what you do with two sources that give you 0.4 and 0.7 — take the spread, average, pick one; and whether you'd accept a bet at every price inside your own range.
- Please use these cases as the actual questions: an Ellsberg-style urn (known vs unknown composition), a case where conditioning makes your interval wider instead of narrower, the two-sources case above, the bet-at-every-price case, and one case where the imprecision runs only one way — you're confident the drug isn't harmful but all over the place about how much good it does.
- Every leaf gets a name, or "unnamed" if nobody defends it, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment.
- Flag any two leaves your tree can't actually pull apart, and flag the combinations of answers that the tree allows but nobody occupies, with a sentence on why nobody is there.

Keep the whole thing under 700 words, tree included — it needs to fit on one screen on a phone.
