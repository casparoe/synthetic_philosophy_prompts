I host a twice-monthly long-form interview podcast on political philosophy — the audience is mostly people who took a course or two and remember the standard menu of consent, fairness, and rescue-based accounts, but who haven't kept up with the literature — and in two weeks I'm recording with a political theorist whose forthcoming manuscript rebuilds political obligation around a fiduciary model. Her central move is that the state's claim to be obeyed is not a debt incurred by accepting benefits, not a necessity imposed by the perils of a stateless condition, and not something actual consent ever really generates; it is the counterpart of fiduciary standing. We owe compliance, on her account, to whoever is entitled to act on our behalf, and that entitlement holds only so long as the power-holder genuinely conducts itself as a fiduciary must — loyal to the interests of those it administers, transparent about its reasons, non-arbitrary in its procedures. Two secondary claims are where I expect the real argument. First, she treats civil disobedience not as a moral anomaly needing special justification but as ordinary self-help in response to breach: because authority tracks fiduciary performance, the familiar requirements that resistance be public and nonviolent and that the resister accept punishment get demoted from duties of fidelity to mere strategic advice. Second, she bites two bullets on purpose. Against the worry that her account cannot explain why we owe obedience to *this* state rather than to any well-governed power, she bites the bullet that the duty is dischargeable by whoever meets the standard — including a non-state or transnational body — and calls the result her "unbundling proviso." Against the worry that one serious injustice destroys authority wholesale, she proposes a "proportionality proviso": authority is forfeited piecemeal, so a state that administers some domains unjustly loses its claim in those domains alone, and citizens owe each institution a share of compliance keyed to how well it performs.

What I need from you is eight to ten interview questions that go beyond inviting her to restate any of that. My co-host is convinced the fiduciary framing is only a metaphor dressed as an argument, and I don't want to spend twenty minutes of tape on that; but I do want one question that forces her past the stock reply that the fiduciary claim is structural rather than metaphorical, and that presses on the fact that real fiduciary duties come with a settlor, an undertaking, ascertainable beneficiaries, and a forum that can enforce them, none of which ordinary law provides. For every question I also want a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give — the maneuver she has already made in the manuscript — and a one-line host's note on what a merely evasive answer would sound like, so I know when to push again.

Please make sure the set covers, at minimum, these pressure points: (i) whether the fiduciary apparatus does any work beyond the claim that the state must govern well, given the missing settlor and enforcer; (ii) the unbundling proviso and the particularity problem, including whether it really commits her to saying we could owe obedience to a competently run private protection agency or an occupying administration, and why territoriality would survive at all; (iii) the proportionality proviso, who is supposed to measure fiduciary performance, and whether obligation then becomes hostage to contested empirical questions — predictive policing, carceral conditions, budget priorities — that ordinary citizens cannot adjudicate, so that nobody can ever know what they owe; (iv) the subjectivization worry that if each person judges breach for herself, the "duty" collapses into a license to comply whenever one independently approves of the law, which starts to look like the anarchist conclusion she is trying to avoid; (v) whether forfeiture has a threshold at all, and whether a dimmer-switch conception of authority is coherent with law's characteristic claim to settle what must be done regardless of one's own assessment; and (vi) what is lost if willingness to accept punishment is only prudential — whether disobedience can still function as a form of address to the community, and whether those who take the law to be valid owe it any respect. Close with one question about what evidence or case would genuinely count against the view, framed so that "that's a research program, not an objection" will not get her out of it.
