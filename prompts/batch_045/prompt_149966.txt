Two friends of mine — Marta, who works in philosophical logic, and Devin, an electrical engineer — got into an argument at a dinner I was at, and I ended up refereeing it badly: I kept nodding at whoever had just finished speaking, which neither of them found helpful. I'd like a proper verdict.

The puzzle they started from was the classic one about Thomson's lamp: a lamp with a toggle switch is switched on for half a minute, then off for a quarter, then on for an eighth, and so on, so that infinitely many switchings are completed inside a single minute; the question is whether the lamp is on or off at the one-minute mark. (1) Marta opened by saying the puzzle is something of a dud: the setup simply fails to specify a final state, and failing to specify is not the same as being contradictory, so the example shows at most that the story is incomplete, not that supertasks are impossible. (2) Devin replied that this concedes too much: if the description admits more than one completion, then there is a coherent story in which the supertask gets performed, whereas the interesting claim was supposed to be that no coherent story is available. (3) Marta said he had changed the subject — he had not produced a completion of the original setup but only described a different object, a lamp plus an extra mechanism, while the original question concerned what the lamp's own state follows from given only the switching schedule. (4) Devin then proposed what he called a durational version: keep the same switching schedule, but suppose the switch also drives a capacitor that charges at a fixed rate while the lamp is on and discharges at the same rate while it is off. On Thomson's original schedule the lamp is on for two thirds of the minute in total and off for one third, so the capacitor's net charge has a perfectly well-defined limit as you approach the one-minute mark, and by continuity that is its charge at the mark; stipulate that the lamp's state thereafter tracks the sign of that charge, and the lamp ends up on. His claim was that the indeterminacy was never a feature of the supertask itself but of asking about a quantity — the bare on/off state — that has no limit along the approach to the minute, while other perfectly physical quantities defined by the very same history do have limits. (5) Marta granted that the capacitor has a determinate charge, but said the stipulation tying the lamp's state to the sign of the charge is doing all the work, and that the trick does not generalise: she offered a rescheduled version in which the lamp is on for a quarter of a minute, off for a quarter, on for an eighth, off for an eighth, and so on — each duration used twice before being halved — so that total time on and total time off come out exactly equal, the capacitor ends at exactly zero, and a rule of the form on-iff-charge-positive and one of the form on-iff-charge-non-negative give different answers; since nothing decides between them, she said, the durational fix buys determinacy only for some schedules, and buys it by stipulation rather than derivation. (6) Devin answered that an exact zero is a knife-edge case of no physical interest — perturb the schedule slightly and you get a definite answer again — and that her own position has the same feature anyway, since she is happy to call the description incomplete for every schedule; the two accounts, he said, differ only in how much they leave open, and his leaves open strictly less.

Could you go through the six moves in order and rate each one — clearly right, defensible but incomplete, or mistaken — with a brief justification, and then tell me who is more right overall and what, if anything, both of them are missing? I'd particularly like to know whether this disagreement is really about supertasks at all, or whether it is really a dispute about something else: what a physical description has to do in order to determine the state of a system at a limit point that the description itself never reaches.
