I work as a software engineer on a logistics forecasting team, and I sit in on a small reading group that a friend runs. We recently spent two sessions on the view that the whole point of a belief is to be accurate, and that there are formal ways of scoring accuracy which a forecaster cannot beat by reporting anything other than her real probabilities. I want to know whether something I keep tripping over at work has a name in that neighbourhood, because it seems to me to sit right next to that idea without being quite it. One constraint up front: I have no library access and no way to chase down references, so please explain things rather than point me at papers.

At work our models output a distribution over an ordered set of buckets (delivery delay of zero days, one to two, three to five, six to ten, more than ten). Two things bother me. First, our accuracy metric is entirely indifferent to how badly a miss misses: a model that put most of its mass on three to five days when the truth was six to ten is scored exactly as badly as one that put it there when the truth was more than ten days. I tried fixing this by giving partial credit for near misses, scoring a forecast by how much probability mass it placed within one bucket of the outcome that actually happened. A colleague pointed out that my fix is gameable in precisely the way the reading group said a good accuracy measure should not be: because the score is linear in the reported probabilities, the best thing to report is no longer your honest distribution but a spike on whichever bucket maximises the smoothed probability of landing within one bucket of the truth. What I then noticed is that this is not specific to my hack. Score a single point forecast by whether it hits the truth exactly, by its absolute distance from the truth, or by its squared distance from the truth, and the optimal report is respectively the most likely value, the median, and the mean of your distribution: change how much you care about being nearly right, and you change which state of mind counts as the accurate one to have. Second, even before I started tinkering, the two standard metrics disagreed about which of two models was better on the same run of data. Model A put slightly more mass on the bucket that actually occurred but dumped the rest of its mass on a distant bucket; model B put slightly less on the actual bucket but kept the remainder on immediately adjacent ones; the metric that looks only at the probability assigned to what happened preferred A, while the metric that looks at the shape of the whole distribution preferred B. A statistician I asked about this said there is a standard way to get near-miss credit without gameability, by applying the shape-based metric to every at-least-k version of the question and adding the results up, which sounds to me as though it must be double counting something, though I cannot say what.

So here is what I am after. Is there an established name for the dependence I ran into, where how accurate a forecast counts as, and which mental state counts as the accurate one to be in, depends on a choice of distance, of partition, or of scoring rule? I went down an internet rabbit hole and came out with the word verisimilitude, but that seems to be about whole scientific theories being nearer to or further from the truth rather than about per-question probabilities over ordered outcomes, and scoring rule looks to me like the name of the no-gameability property rather than of the dependence itself. I also want to know whether I have described one phenomenon or two: metrics disagreeing about who was more accurate on the same data, and metrics rewarding different states of mind. If they are different, tell me which one I actually ran into and how to tell them apart in a new case. And I would rather have a coined term with a definition and clear boundary cases than a bare label, because what I really want to know is whether what I found is an objection to the philosophical claim that accuracy is the single epistemic good with a privileged measure of it, or merely a fact about measurement that leaves that claim untouched.
