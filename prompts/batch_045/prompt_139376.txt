I teach an upper-level undergrad seminar on philosophy and economics and I'm writing the final exam. Last night I got into a long, slightly too-drunk argument with a friend (econ PhD, very pro-planning) about the socialist calculation debate, and I came away realising I don't actually know what would settle it. Her move was: Mises and Hayek were writing before computers and before mechanism design, so of course they thought the information couldn't be gathered; today a planning board could just ask every manager to report their cost curves, pay them for accuracy with a proper scoring rule, and then run a big general-equilibrium simulation on the reported data. My feeling is that this misses the point, but I couldn't say exactly which point it misses, and I also had a nagging worry that the Hayek argument, if it works at all, seems to work against planning inside firms too, which is definately wrong — firms plan internally all the time and mostly fine — so the argument must need some scoping condition I can't articulate.

So: write me one essay question for a 60-minute in-class final that makes students work out whether a mechanism-design reply of that kind actually answers the argument, and if not, what exactly is left standing. Concretely the question needs to:

- be self-contained — briefly set up Lange's trial-and-error procedure (board announces prices, managers follow rules like minimise average cost and expand output to where marginal cost meets price, board revises prices in response to shortages and surpluses) and the scoring-rule fix inside the question itself, since most of them haven't done any mechanism design;
- be unanswerable by reciting 'knowledge is dispersed' or 'no private property therefore no prices' or 'prices are a telecommunications system' — I want them forced to say what kind of knowledge, and what is supposed to be missing, given that Lange's board also doesn't know the firms' costs and doesn't claim to;
- force a decision about whether the scoring-rule version fails for a computational reason, an incentive reason, or a 'the relevant knowledge doesn't exist yet to be reported' reason, and why that distinction matters for whether the objection is Mises's or Hayek's;
- end with a short clause making them state what scoping condition stops the argument from proving that planning inside a firm is impossible too.

Give me: the question as I'd print it; a model answer of about 900 words; and a marking scheme out of 20 built on five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth and one line on how students typically mangle each one. I want the line between an A and a B to be a specific move a student either makes or doesn't, not 'more detail' or 'greater sophistication'. And please have the model answer end with a short paragraph on where its own argument is weakest and what the strongest Lange-style reply to it would be — I want to use it as an example of arguing in good faith, and I'm still arguing with my friend, so I also want to know what would change my mind.

No citations or reading lists needed; the names in the question are enough, and I'd rather the thing not depend on exact dates or paper titles. Keep the model answer at a level a strong third-year could aspire to rather than journal-article density.
