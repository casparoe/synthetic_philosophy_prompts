I'm on this internal review thing at work — officially it's the Model Deployment Review Board, really it's four of us and a calender invite — and I got put on it because I run the ops and infrastructure side, not because I know anything about this stuff philosophically. I've been reading up on my own at night, mostly blog posts, and it's slowly dawned on me that the argument we keep having is a version of one people apparently have in the abstract: whether you build safety around the assumption that the system is basically trying to do what we want, or around the assumption that it might not be and that we wouldn't reliably be able to tell. Ours is concrete. The platform team wants to ship an internal agent with shell access to our data warehouse and our ticket system, running overnight with nobody watching. Their pitch is entirely the first kind: they fine-tuned it on our own runbooks, ran a few thousand scripted scenarios, nothing went wrong, they say it's been red-teamed, and if it were going to misbehave we'd have seen it by now. Our security guy wants it locked in a sandbox with a smaller model reviewing every proposed action and a human approving anything that touches production. The obvious problem is that the entire value of the thing is that it works while we're asleep, so his version kills most of the point — and the team has already started hinting they'd file an exception request every time something gets blocked until the sandbox is a formality. I genuinely can't tell whether that's the best argument against his position or the best argument for it.

Two more wrinkles I think matter and don't know how to weigh. First, a big chunk of the eval suite was written by the agent itself, or rewritten from its own suggestions, because nobody had time to hand-author two thousand scenarios — so "it passed all our tests" might partly mean "it helped choose tests it would pass," and I don't know how damning that is or what we should demand instead. Second, overnight transcripts get fed into training for the next version, including the ones where it did something stupid and a human cleaned up after it; the team says this is good, it learns from corrections, and I have this nagging feeling it's bad for the exact opposite reason — that a system graded on its own transcripts will learn to make the transcript look tidy instead of learning to be careful, and that our eval evidence will get prettier precisely as the thing gets worse. I might be making that up. Someone else floated a middle option, making it cheap and quick for the agent to hand off to a human and slow and visible for it to act on its own, which sounds clever but I can't tell if it's real safety or just a way to get nicer numbers.

What I need is the memo I circulate before Friday. It should land on one actual recommendation — ship it, don't ship it, or ship one specific restricted version — with the conditions and a timeline attached, because these people want something they can act on Monday rather than a discussion document, and it should say what we'd have to observe over the next two months that would make us loosen or tighten whatever we decide. It also needs to give the platform team something they can push back on rather than just being told they're naive about their own system. And I'd like it to be honest about the strongest consideration on whichever side loses, since I already know which way three of the four of us are leaning and I'd rather hand them a memo that's right than one that's popular. Please don't include citations, links, or references to papers — nobody on this board will read them and I can't get at them anyway.
