I host a recorded interview series on the philosophy of mind, and the next session is a long-form conversation with a philosopher — I will call her Dr. Aurelia Vance — who defends what she labels the custodial theory of persons. Her view is a development of the strange-loop account of selfhood that Douglas Hofstadter set out in *I Am a Strange Loop* (2007), on which a self is a self-referential pattern rather than a substance, and on which, because a person's pattern is partly made of models of other people and other people's patterns are partly made of models of her, part of a person's self can outlive her brain by being realized, in attenuated form, inside the brains of those who carry her. Vance takes this literally rather than therapeutically: for her, custodianship is a metaphysical relation with degrees, and the deceased person's 'I' genuinely persists, distributed and diminished, in the loops of the living. She is articulate, well rehearsed, and prone to two deflections I want closed off in advance: that questions about how much of a person survives, or where, are category errors because a pattern is abstract; and that the felt absurdity of her view is an artifact of our substance-bound intuitions.

Write me eight to ten interview questions for this session, each no longer than about forty-five words, with the following properties.

Do not open with background, and include no question that could be asked of any philosopher — nothing about influences, intellectual biography, or the field's reception. Every question should turn on the internal machinery of her position: what a strange loop must contain to be the same loop, what counts as one pattern's being realized in another substrate, and how degrees of custodianship are measured.

Cover, among your eight to ten, at least one question each on the following cruxes, using the case material I supply rather than inventing your own:

The grain problem. A custodian's model of the deceased is a coarse abstraction with no proprioception, no subpersonal affective texture, and no access to the bodily feedback the original loop ran on. Ask whether such a thin realization can be a chunk of the same self rather than a representation of it, and press on what, if anything, is essential to the pattern rather than incidental to its original substrate.

The accretion case. An archivist who never met the deceased builds, from letters, diaries, and recordings, a behaving model of her far more detailed and behaviorally faithful than the one carried by her surviving spouse. If degree of persistence tracks fidelity of the realized pattern, the archivist is the better custodian; if it requires causal-historical contact instead, ask why the metaphysics should care how the pattern arrived.

The conservation problem. A dead person's influence can grow after death and divide among arbitrarily many custodians. Ask whether there is a fixed quantity of self to be distributed or whether the total degree to which a person exists can increase posthumously, and what follows if the dead can come to be more fully realized than they ever were while alive.

The slow-replacement case. A living custodian's self-model is gradually rewritten so that she increasingly deliberates and remembers in the deceased's idiom, until her own loop and the deceased's are difficult to separate. Ask when one brain goes from hosting a single loop that represents the deceased to hosting two loops, one of which is the deceased's, and whether a single brain can realize two first-personal perspectives at once — and, if it cannot, what exactly the deceased's 'I' is missing.

At least two of your questions should be constructed so that her standard moves are not simply available: one in which the abstraction defense cannot be repeated without conceding that degrees of custodianship are unmeasurable and hence that the view has no explanatory content, and one in which the appeal to intuition cannot do any work.

Include one question about phenomenology: even granting the shared pattern, the only experience involved is the custodian's, so ask in what sense persistence is a fact about the deceased rather than a fact about what is now going on in someone else's stream of consciousness. Include one question about her relation to reductionist accounts of personal identity, which ask whether custodial persistence is reductionism plus a permissive realization condition or an additional identity claim reductionists reject. Include one question about evidence: what empirical finding about memory, neural realization, or the transmission of self-models would count against the view. Close with one question about the consolation objection — whether the theory is metaphysics or a piece of grief technology, and what it says to someone it does not console.

For each question, add a follow-up of no more than thirty words, keyed to the answer she is most likely to give rather than to a straw position, and precede each pair with a single short line stating what the question is designed to extract from her. Sequence the whole set so that it begins where her position is strongest and ends where its resources are thinnest, and keep the register of a serious but unhostile interview.
