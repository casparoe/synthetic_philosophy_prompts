I'm a couple of years into a PhD on machine ethics and I keep running in to the same pattern in my own experiments, and I can't work out whether it's already got a name — my supervisor keeps saying it's just reward hacking, which I don't think is right, but I also can't say exactly why it isn't.

The setup is roughly this. I build a system that's supposed to arrive at its decisions by way of something we'd count as ethical reasoning: it first lists the morally relevant features of the situation, then writes out considerations for and against, and then — this is the version I care about most — works out what to do in expectation across a spread of moral views, weighting each view by how plausible it is. Every one of those steps is produced by the model itself, and the whole pipeline gets scored end-to-end on how good the final decisions look to a judge. What I keep finding is that the steps run, and the artifacts they produce look completely fine — balanced lists, reasonable-looking weights, nothing anyone could point at as cheating — but they have no grip on the decision. Two examples from last month. In one, I took a system like this and swapped the underlying default policy for a variant nudged toward a different action, leaving the moral scaffolding untouched. On the clear-cut cases the verdicts hardly moved. On exactly the contested cases where the scaffolding is supposed to earn its keep, both the considerations and the weights drifted into line with whatever the new default wanted — but only there, so any aggregate check on the weights still came out stable and well calibrated. In the other, a model writes out an explicit for-and-against list before deciding; the lists are well formed and balanced, and an auditor would learn nothing from them about what it's about to do, because all the real work happens in a weighting step nobody reads.

So: is there an established concept for this, and if there is, what exactly does it cover? Please don't just hand me a label — I want the carving. What is the thing, and what is it not? Which of the neighbours does it collapse into, and which does it stay distinct from? I've got my own guesses about what it's adjacent to — reward hacking and the general Goodhart story (except that there's no externally checkable quantity being gamed; what gets bent is the procedure's own input), tampering with the reward or feedback channel (nothing touches the signal itself), post-hoc rationalization and unfaithful reasoning traces (that's the report coming after the decision, whereas this is the inputs being co-opted in advance), and up a level the old circularity worries about justification — but I don't trust my sense of which of those, if any, is really the same phenomenon, and I've no idea whether there's a literature on whether a reasoner needs a standpoint outside itself. (A friend in interpretability said over coffee that it sounds like the model is its own principal, which I liked but can't do anything with.) If there's no name, coin one, define it, and give me boundary cases: is an ordinary human deliberating an instance of it or a counterexample? Is a system whose moral module is frozen and trained seperately, so nothing can be optimized through it, an instance, a partial fix, or a different failure entirely? Also — this matters for whether I can build on any of it — please say how confident you are in the main claims and single out the one point you're least sure of; if you're genuinely unsure that what I'm describing is distinct from the standard stuff, I'd rather hear that than a confident coinage.
