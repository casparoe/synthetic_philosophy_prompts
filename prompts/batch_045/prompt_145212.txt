You're a professional philosopher who works on the ethics of fiction and the ethics of technology — please think this through carefully rather than firing off a first reaction, and if you brainstorm your way somewhere different from where you started, say so, because I want to see where the cases push back. I don't need citations or a reading list; I want your own reasoning.

Here's the situation. I'm a philosophy professor, I teach an undergrad applied-ethics course, and the unit on whether virtual characters matter always produces the liveliest discussion and the worst arguments, mostly because everyone (me included) has strong gut reactions and no principle. So this year I tried to get my own house in order before class by writing down my verdicts on a pile of cases, and they don't line up at all. My verdicts: (a) mowing down hundreds of identical unnamed enemies in a shooter, with fairly realistic death animations and cries, is completely fine, no residual guilt, and I don't think worse of anyone for it. (b) In an RPG, a companion I've travelled with for forty hours begs me not to hand her over to the authorities; I hand her over because it wins the mission. I feel genuinely bad and I'd call it mildly wrong — not a big deal, but not nothing. (c) An immersive sim where you can torture a procedurally generated prisoner who pleads in generated sentences and has a detailed pain model: I won't do it, and I think it's wrong in a way (b) isn't, even though the prisoner is in every authorial sense less of a character than the companion — that's the pair I keep tripping over. (d) A game a student showed me last spring — let's call it Hollowmill — where each NPC keeps a persistent psychological profile across all players, so that if enough players are cruel to the blacksmith she becomes anxious and mistrustful for everyone, permanently. Being brusque with her once feels wrong to me, but I cant cash that out in terms of my own character (one rude line to a shopkeeper changes nothing about me) and it doesn't cash out in terms of effects on other players either, since my contribution to the aggregate is negligible. (e) Modding a game so the death animations are slower and more graphic, purely for my own amusement: worse than the base game, although the character suffers exactly as much in both, i.e. not at all — which makes me think something here is about what I'm enjoying rather than about the character. (f) Deleting the save file so that the companion in (b) never existed: totally fine, zero guilt, which is weird right next to (b) — I mind harming her but not erasing her. (g) Letting a keychain virtual pet starve through neglect: I feel bad and I'd call it a small failing, but I'm fairly confident that's about what it shows about me, not about the creature. (h) Running a tabletop scene where my character tortures an NPC that exists only in our shared imagination: fine, and less fraught than (c), even though in (h) I'm authoring the suffering rather than just triggering it. (i) A game that ingests a real, named, recently deceased person's writing and video and lets you kill a reconstruction of them: I think not fine, but I honestly cant tell whether that's about the character, about the dead person, or about the living people who loved them.

My own first pass was a hard-nosed view: nothing is owed to the character itself, and everything here is really about effects on real people, myself included. That handles (a), (f) and (g) beautifully and has no idea what to do with (c), (d) and (e). What I'd like from you is a principle, or a small set of competing candidates, stated precisely enough that I could hand it out to students, with a clause-by-clause note on which case motivates each clause. Then please tell me what your account says about two cases I haven't listed: a serious anti-torture game in which you play the torturer and the point is to make you hate it; and paying a low-waged worker in another country to log in and perform in-game cruelty for you, so a real human spends their shift doing it while you watch. And be straight with me about which of my verdicts your principle can't accommodate and which one you think I should give up.
