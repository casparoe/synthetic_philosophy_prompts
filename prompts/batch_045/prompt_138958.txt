Hi — I'm working up a feature on disjunctivism for a general science-and-ideas magazine, and I want an actual graphic in it: a ladder of hard cases, sorted by how much trouble they give someone who wants to say that really seeing a thing and merely hallucinating a thing have no interesting mental kind in common. My editor says the readers need numbers, so please: score each case 0 to 10 (0 = the disjunctivist waves it away without breaking a sweat, 10 = it basically forces them to grant a common factor), then group them into three or four tiers, and give me one sentence on why each lands where it does.

Here's the list, roughly the one Ive been kicking around:

1. The half-submerged stick that looks bent.
2. Doubling: you press a finger against the side of your eyeball and everything splits in two.
3. Afterimage: you stare at a bright window, look at the wall, and a glowing window-shape hangs there.
4. Full-blown hallucination with nothing there at all — the floating-dagger type case.
5. The coincidental match: you hallucinate a black cat on the sofa, and there is in fact a black cat on the sofa, asleep behind a cushion, and your hallucination matches it in every detail you could check. (If this has an official name, don't bother telling me it — just handle the case.)
6. Passthrough VR: you're in a headset whose outward cameras feed you the room you're actually in, in real time — call it 90 milliseconds late, colours slightly off. You reach for your mug and your hand closes on it. The object is real, the causation is mostly real, but what you're visually working with is a rendered image on a screen a centimetre from your eyes.
7. Restored sight: someone blind since birth gets a retinal prosthesis — a grid of phosphenes — and after two years of training reads large print fluently and says the letters look like letters to them now. Are they seeing the page, or interpreting a signal?
8. Pareidolia: you see a face in a brick wall. The wall is definitely there; the face is not.
9. The perfect simulation: your entire stream of experience is generated by something that tracks your movements so well that everything stays responsive — you can walk, look behind you, pick things up — but nothing external corresponds to any of it.
