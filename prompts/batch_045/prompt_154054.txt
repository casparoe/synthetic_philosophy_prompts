I'm doing a PhD in history and I also work part time on a digital humanities project where we take published monographs and tag every single sentence. Each sentence gets one of three labels: 'sourced' (there's a document underneath it), 'inferred' (no document actually says it, the author got there by reasoning, generalization, or common sense), and 'framed' (it's the author's interpretive choice — what to call the thing, who counts as an actor, where the story starts and ends, what kind of story it is). We also spot check footnotes and tag whether the cited source really supports the sentence it's attached to. The people on the project keep fighting about whether this is neutral metadata or whether it already commits us to a position about whether there is a fact of the matter about the past. I think it does commit us, but I can't say exactly where.

It's made me look differently at what ordinary academic history already presupposes. On the one hand the whole apparatus behaves as if there's a real past we're converging on: you go to the archive, you cite primary sources rather than secondary ones, reviewers spot check your footnotes, journals print corrections and sometimes retract when someone finds you got a date or a quotation wrong, and fabricating a source will end your career. On the other hand nobody in my department believes the Rankean slogan about showing the past as it actually was, everyone's read Carr on the facts of history always reaching us already selected by somebody, and since Hayden White people will happily say in a seminar that the narrative shape — tragedy, comedy, romance, satire — is something the historian puts onto the material rather than finds in it. And the discipline is wildly more relaxed about the second kind of thing: almost nobody is ever made to retract an interpretation, only a fact, and there's a strong norm that saying "that's my reading" settles the matter.

So: what does that bundle of practices actually presuppose about historical knowledge? Reconstruct the implicit theory, and separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with — I suspect footnote discipline and the punishment of fabrication commit you to less than people think, while the refusal to ever correct an interpretation commits you to more. Tell me whether the parts are consistent with each other. Two things puzzle me especially: we punish fabrication savagely and correct wrong dates, but a source that's been badly read is barely a problem and an interpretation is never withdrawn, which feels like it presupposes that the chain of attribution matters more than the truth of what's said; and we let a sentence resting on a self-interested document count as 'sourced' on exactly the same footing as one resting on a document nobody had a stake in. Then bring it back to my tagging scheme: which kinds of sentence should break a three-way split like ours — causal claims, claims about what someone believed, claims from silence or from absence in the archive, claims about what was typical, counterfactuals — and what would have to change in the practice of history if we took the Whitean view seriously enough to build it into the way we write, review, and correct books?
