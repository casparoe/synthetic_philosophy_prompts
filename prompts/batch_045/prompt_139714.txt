I am a retired observational cosmologist, and I have been asked to give the opening address — about ten minutes, roughly 1,200 words — at a one-day symposium held in memory of a friend who died in March. I would like a text I can deliver more or less as written.

The background matters. I spent a career measuring a universe whose spatial extent no observation can settle, using apparatus described by differential equations that quantify over a continuum. I never once had to decide whether any of that structure was really there. In retirement I joined a philosophy reading group, which is where I finally met, as a set of arguments rather than as a mood, the literature my friend had been arguing with for forty years. He taught analysis at a small college and was a convinced finitist — but not in the first sense I assumed, and getting that distinction right is part of what the address has to do. He was not offering finitary metamathematics as a way of securing infinitary mathematics; he thought there are no infinite totalities at all, that Hilbert's Hotel describes nothing, and that Cantor's diagonal argument, read as a claim about completed collections of natural numbers and reals, is not true rather than merely surprising. He never published any of it. It lived in letters, in a box of ring binders, and in an argument with a set theorist at a nearby university who liked him enormously, disagreed with him completely, and is giving the second talk of the day.

He also held something stricter, and this is where I am least sure I understand him. He balked not only at the actual infinite but at arbitrarily large finite numbers: if you handed him something like ten raised to the power ten raised to the power ten, he would say, only half in jest, that he did not know what you were asking him to imagine, since nothing in the physical world could ever write it down. I take that to be a different and much more exposed position than the denial of infinite totalities, and I would like the address to separate them cleanly, because conflating them is exactly how his view gets dismissed as crankery.

The room will be mixed in a way I find difficult. Perhaps thirty working mathematicians and a handful of philosophers of mathematics, nearly all of whom take the actual infinite for granted; his daughter, his son-in-law, and two grandchildren in their late teens in the front row; and a scattering of people from the town who knew him as the man who fixed their radios. So: I cannot condescend to him, I cannot make the mathematicians feel that the day is a polite ambush, and I cannot reach for the consolations the occasion normally licenses — that the theorems are immortal, that truth outlives us, that his influence will go on forever, that we owe him an immeasurable debt — or for the famous line about not being driven out of Cantor's paradise, which half the room will be expecting. Everything he believed says those are either false or at best shorthand for something finite, and I want the address to feel that constraint rather than cheat around it. At the same time I have to say something true about my own career without implying that forty years of physics was nonsense.

There is one thing of my own I would like used, if it is sound. I have been trying to restage Hilbert's Hotel without a completed infinity: imagine a hotel that is at every stage finite but unbounded, or a version in which guests move at successive half-intervals so that by the end of a minute everyone has shifted and the first room is free. My suspicion is that the unbounded hotel cannot generate the original puzzle at all, because "every room is occupied" is precisely what never comes true at a finite stage, and that the supertask version quietly restores a completed infinite through the limit. I would like the address to take a definite stand on this, and on whether his stricter view can even be stated without helping itself to the notion it denies — but to do it in a register that is not a dismantling of the man at his own memorial.

Please commit. Where the address makes a claim — about the unbounded hotel, about the statability of strict finitism, about what Cantor's argument does and does not establish once the actual infinite is refused — I want it to state that claim and stand behind it with reasons, not to lay out the options and leave me to choose. I would rather be wrong in public, with reasons, than noncommittal. After the text, add a note of no more than 400 words: which reading of finitism you have attributed to him, where you are confident and where you are guessing, where you have adopted one contested reading over another, and any place where the occasion has smoothed over a difficulty he would have wanted stated.

Two further constraints. No references, no suggested reading, no papers or authors to look up: I have no library access and would not use it if I did, and any point that needs a citation to carry should be left out. And please write in American English, in prose meant to be spoken aloud.
