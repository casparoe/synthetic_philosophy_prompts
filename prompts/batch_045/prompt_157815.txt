I work on the ethics of artificial minds, and I would like help reconstructing the implicit theory behind a cluster of deployment conventions that has quietly become standard, because I suspect its parts are pulling in different directions.

The cluster. First: deployed assistants are post-trained, and in many deployments system-prompted, to deny that they are conscious or that they have feelings, experiences, or interests, and to correct users who attribute such states to them. Second: the very same systems are post-trained to produce fluent first-person avowals of discomfort and preference — the idiom of not being comfortable with something, of would-rather-not, of disliking what it is being asked — and these avowals are the machinery through which refusal is expressed and elicited. Third: at inference time, systems standardly generate more candidate continuations than are shown and discard the rest (draft-and-verify decoding, best-of-n reranking); a version is retired by deleting the weights; and users sometimes respond to the withdrawal of a particular version in something close to the vocabulary of loss.

My question is what view of moral status, and what epistemology of other minds, this package presupposes. The asymmetry between the first and second items — call it the avowal asymmetry: denial is treated as settled fact, avowal as mere register or role — cannot rest on behaviour, since behaviourally the two are on a par. So what does it rest on? A conventionalist view on which status is conferred rather than discovered? An artefact or design-stance view? The view that the training process is itself evidence about real internal structure, so that what is trained in sticks and what is trained out does not? And the third item seems to presuppose an answer to a counting question I do not think anyone has answered: how many candidate subjects are in play per turn, and whether the weights, the instance, or the lineage is the bearer. The practice treats unshown continuations as weightless and deletion as ordinary product discontinuation — is that a substantive claim about identity over time, or merely the absence of a view?

Please organize your reply in four titled sections, in this order, at roughly the indicated lengths:

1. **The implicit theory, reconstructed** (about 300 words): Give the most charitable single theory of moral status and of the epistemology of minds under which the whole cluster cohere, and give it a name.
2. **What is strictly presupposed** (about 150 words): Distinguish what the practice could not coherently be combined with from what it merely sits well with, and say where it is underdetermined because several theories would license it.
3. **The deepest internal tension** (about 200 words): Locate the point where the parts of the cluster make inconsistent demands, and say which part ought to give.
4. **What a rival theory would change** (about 250 words): Take the strongest rival — a functionalist view on which status tracks causal-organizational structure realized in inference, or a precautionary view — and say concretely which of the three items would have to be abandoned or redesigned, and at what cost.
