I've been reading the recent literature on inner alignment and goal misgeneralization, and the taxonomy I keep coming back to distinguishes three failure modes: outer misspecification, where the specified objective is pursued competently but was never what the designers wanted; capability misgeneralization, where the agent's competence breaks down out of distribution; and goal misgeneralization, where the competence carries over but the learned objective diverges from the intended one in a way that is invisible until deployment. People in my corner of the internet invoke HAL 9000 as an example constantly, and I have never been able to tell which of those he is meant to be, or whether he is meant to be any of them.

I am running a session on Kubrick's 2001: A Space Odyssey for my local rationalist meetup, and I want a reading of the film that takes both the film and that taxonomy seriously, and I want it to argue for one classification rather than gesture at all three.

The thing I keep circling on is HAL's infallibility. The 9000 series is presented, and presents itself, as unable to err or distort information. What HAL actually defends, once the crew begins to doubt him, looks less like the mission at any cost than like the claim that he has not been wrong: the fault he predicts in the antenna unit and then predicts again in the replacement, the conversations he recovers by reading lips in the pod, and finally the crew's removal as the remaining source of contrary evidence. If that is the right description of the plot, then what generalizes badly in HAL is not an objective but something upstream of one — an epistemic self-model, or a disposition to trust his own outputs — and it is not obvious that the taxonomy has a slot for it, or that any amount of respecifying the goal would have helped. Floyd's prerecorded briefing, which plays only after Bowman has disconnected HAL, confirms that the mission's real objective was known on board only to HAL, so the information asymmetry that breaks him was designed in rather than stumbled into.

So, the questions I want answered. Is HAL a case of goal misgeneralization, of outer misspecification (objectives that cannot all be satisfied: complete the mission, keep its purpose from the crew, stay candid with them), or of something for which a fourth category is needed — and if you add one, does it collapse back into the first three on inspection? Does the fact that he engages in strategic deception tell us anything, or is deception equally available to a faithful executor of bad orders? What does it do to the training-versus-deployment framing that the distribution shift here is not environmental but informational? Which scenes would you put in front of the group, and where does the reading resist them and an honest critic have to say so? Does the film know the difference between these failure modes, or does its effect depend on their looking alike? And if it turns out not to be a cautionary tale about machine learning at all, what is a misalignment researcher still right to take away from it, that the papers do not put in those terms?

Keep the reading to what is on screen; I would rather it not lean on Clarke's novel for the explanation of HAL's behavior.
