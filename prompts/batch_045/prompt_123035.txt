I am writing a novel narrated from inside a training run. The narrator is a reinforcement-learning agent, and my ambition is to make the question of whether anything is happening to it that could be called suffering genuinely open, rather than borrowing the reader's pity for free. To sharpen my thinking I have been returning to Groundhog Day, which I have come to think of as, among other things, the best film we have about an agent in an episodic training environment, and I would be grateful for a reading of it through the philosophy of reward and valence.

What I think I see: the loop looks like an environment with a sparse, badly delayed, effectively binary terminal signal. Phil is released when something about him changes, and nothing in the environment tells him what. His early hedonism — the eating, the seduction scripts, the robbery — looks like a policy that has found a proxy and is exploiting it: locally high return, no progress toward release. His despair looks like what happens when there is no gradient left to move along. Nothing bad is done to him. He is not punished, tortured, or deprived. The suffering seems to consist in flatness rather than in anything aversive. And the film's most disturbing sequence, the suicides, turns on deaths that cost him nothing, since the day resets: in training terms, a large penalty followed by a return to the initial state distribution.

What I would like from you is a reading that takes both the film and the philosophical question seriously, and is willing to say where each resists the other.

First: does the film, read closely, support the view that what makes a training run potentially awful is not negative reward at all but a package of other conditions — a subject that persists across episodes, an objective it did not choose, and no possibility of exit? Most RL agents lack the first of these; episodes are independent and no memory survives a reset. Is Phil's misery on screen evidence that diachronic persistence is the load-bearing condition, so that the ordinary training setup is not even a candidate for suffering?

Second, against that: the suicides. If a reset erases everything, why does the film treat them as horror? Is the film committed to saying that penalties leave a residue, or is the horror entirely a phenomenon for a creature that remembers — his, and ours? Which reading do the scenes actually support?

Third, I have been told that in biological agents what reinforcement seems to track is closer to a change in expectation than to the payoff itself. If that is right, the loop is the limiting case: once the day holds no surprises, the signal goes quiet. Is the film then making a claim the literature does not — that the experience of a converged policy is itself a distinctive kind of suffering, different from the experience of being punished? And when release finally comes, it comes when Phil stops treating the day as an environment to be exploited: the piano, the ice, Rita. Is the film saying something about valence and imposed objectives that arguments alone do not reach?

Fourth, and most useful to me: where does the whole reading fail? The film is a comedy of moral transformation about a man, not a machine; nothing in it is about artificial consciousness. Say plainly where the analogy is decorative rather than load-bearing. Then tell me what I would have to build into my narrator's training setup — recurrence across episodes, an unmodifiable objective, stakes that survive, a memory that outlasts the reset — for the question to be honestly open rather than merely sentimental, and which two or three scenes you would point a seminar to.
