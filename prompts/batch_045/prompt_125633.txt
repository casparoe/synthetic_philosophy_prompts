Bit of background so you know where I'm coming from: I'm 71, spent my working life in physical chemistry — mostly phase transitions and hysteresis — and about a year ago I started sitting in on a philosophy seminar at the university near where I live. I'm auditing, not enrolled, so nobody's obliged to be polite to me. We did a fortnight on statues, street names and commemoration, and I came out of it with a pile of gut reactions and no idea what I actually think. I'd like the principle, if there is one.

My verdicts, as honestly as I can report them:

1. The big bronze of the slave trader in the city centre, paid for by public subscription decades after he died because he left money to hospitals and schools: fine that it came down. I was mildly pleased. I don't think it had to be smashed or chucked in the harbour, and I'd rather it hadn't been, but I'm definately not going to pretend I'm upset, and I don't want it back up.

2. That same statue, cleaned up and re-erected inside the city museum in a room about the city's role in the slave trade, with a plaque saying who he really was: also fine. Possibly better than destroying it.

3. The war memorial in my village — an obelisk with the names of the men who never came back from the First World War: obviously stays. And this even though I've come to think the war itself was a squalid mess and I don't believe those men were fighting for anything I'd of wanted to honour. Something makes it a different kind of object entirely.

4. A university building named after a chemist who did genuinely first-rate work and who, in his sixties, was a public supporter of compulsory sterilisation of disabled people: keep the name, add a plaque. I feel oddly firm about this and can't fully say why.

5. A street named after a surgeon who pioneered anaesthesia and did his experimental work on enslaved patients, without anaesthesia: rename. No plaque fix, no "adding context". Just gone.

6. The statues of Confederate generals in front of courthouses, put up long after the war, during the Jim Crow years: take them down. But the plain slab in the cemetery that just says "our Confederate dead": leave it alone.

7. A four-hundred-year-old tomb brass in a village church for a family who got rich off enclosures or slaving or whatever it was: leave it. It's a grave.

8. Statues of Lenin still standing in small Russian towns, where people still leave flowers: leave them. And I notice this one breaks my own rule, because plainly nobody would cast and install a Lenin statue today.

The thing I invented to explain myself to myself, before I'd read anything: ask whether we would put the thing up now, for the first time, knowing what we know — if not, it's on borrowed time and should come down. Call it the re-installation test. It gives me 1, 5 and 6a, and also 8, which is the answer I don't want. And it tells me to scrap 3, 4, 6b and 7, which I won't. So I've been bolting on a second condition: a monument only really offends when the bad thing is what it's honouring, or is bound up with the thing it's honouring, rather than being a stain elsewhere in the same person's life. That gets me the surgeon — his fame is built out of the very work that was monstrous — and it gets me the chemist, whose fame isn't built out of his politics. But then case 1 is a problem again: the hospitals were literally bought with the slaving, so the thing being honoured is entangled with the bad thing after all, and the chemist's name is on a building paid for by nobody's suffering. And 8 is just me overruling my own test, and I don't know what licenses me to do it.

There's one more thing I keep wanting to drag in from my old work. Hysteresis: once a monument's meaning has flipped — once enough people see it as an insult rather than as background — you can't get back to the state where it was just innocent architecture. It's got two stable states and it's fallen into the other one, and the only moves left are take it down or openly re-frame it. Is that a genuine feature of the ethics here, or am I just importing my own toolbox because it's the only one I've got?

So: is there one principle, or a short list of candidates, that gets most of this? State it precisely enough that I could actually apply it to something, say which clause is doing the work in which case, and be straight with me about which of my verdicts it can't save — and if you have to drop one, drop it and tell me why it's the weakest. Then run your principle on a few cases I haven't listed, which I'd rather you picked than me: something about a living person who's honoured now and turns out badly later, something about a monument that's become a joke or a protest site, and whatever third case you think actually separates the candidates you're offering.
