in a group chat about whether anything after 1989 can count as a genuinely new form of political order, Raj shut the thread down with: "the end of history already happened — Fukuyama, done, nothing new under the sun."

The work he was making it do: Priya had argued that a polity where nearly all legislation is drafted by machine-learning systems and then ratified by sortition assemblies (chambers of randomly selected citizens) would be a new stage of political order, not just liberal democracy with new plumbing. Raj says the end of history rules that out in advance, and when pushed he said Fukuyama is really making Hegel's claim — the underlying principle is settled, the struggle for recognition is over, so new institutional hardware can never be a new stage, only a variation on one.

I cant tell whether that reply is deep or just a way of winning by definition. Can you lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims "the end of history" could be standing for — from the trivial or tautological ones up to the really strong ones (no more large-scale ideological rivalry; no further fundamental institutional forms; liberal democracy is the final adequate form of recognition; history as a directional process has stopped so nothing "world-historical" can occur again; or something weaker about Europe in 1989)? For each: is it plausible, and would it actually do the job Raj needs here?

Two more things I'd want: what each reading would count as a counterexample, i.e. under which readings Priya's algorithmic-sortition polity could in principle refute it and under which no possible institutional change could, which would make the slogan unfalsifiable by construction; and whether the Hegelian version Raj switched to is a stronger claim than the popular one or just the same claim moved somewhere harder to test.
