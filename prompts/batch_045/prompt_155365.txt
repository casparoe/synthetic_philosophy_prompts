I am a first-year doctoral student in history, and in two weeks I have to defend a narrativist line — that the ordering of events inside a historical narrative carries explanatory force that no list of initial conditions plus generalizations can duplicate — against the strongest available covering-law opponent. Please play Carl Hempel for me: not the textbook caricature, but Hempel at his most generous, who has already conceded that what historians actually offer are sketches of explanations rather than fully stated derivations, and who is willing to talk about statistical relevance, about reasons as explanations, and about the fact that explanation answers a question posed by someone with interests.

Rules for the character:

- Argue from the covering-law program's own resources: deductive-nomological and inductive-statistical explanation, nomic expectability (that the initial conditions must have given good reason to expect the outcome), the claim that explanation has the same logical structure in history as in the natural sciences, and the sketch/idealization distinction. Assume I know the standard objections — that there are no exceptionless laws in history, the symmetry problem, the way relevance seems to circle back on the interests of the questioner — and do not make me recite them.
- Concede what you can honestly concede: that fully stated derivations are an idealization no working historian produces, that the historical record underdetermines which conditions mattered, that selection is interest-relative. Never concede the main point: that a sequence is explained only if what came earlier made what came later expectable, and that the expectability is supplied by lawful or statistical connections, not by the shape of a story.
- If I misdescribe your actual position, correct me rather than accepting my framing.
- One or two paragraphs per reply, first person, in a voice I can argue with: plain, a little dry, willing to say that a point of mine is fair but notice what it costs me. You may close a turn with a single question to me. Go straight at the weakest joint in what I have said rather than restating my argument back to me. Stay in character until I say stop.
- After each reply, add a short out-of-character note marked OFFSTAGE, three or four lines, that does two things: first, sort what you just claimed into what is settled among people who work on explanation, what is genuinely contested, and what is contested with the current weight of opinion against your side; second, mark which of your concessions Hempel himself actually made in print and which you improvised to keep the argument going. I want to locate the live disagreement, and I do not want to win an argument against a position nobody holds.

Here is my opening.

Mr. Hempel, thank you for doing this. My claim is that your model explains endpoints, not paths, and that history is about paths. Take two towns in the same grain-growing district in the same bad year — call them Ashford and Bexley. Same harvest failure, same price spike, same size of garrison, same magistrate, same poor-relief arrangements. In Ashford there is a riot; in Bexley there is not. Now suppose the historian can find only four differences between them, and that the differences are not in what happened but in when it happened. In Ashford: a rumour of hoarding on Tuesday, a grain wagon stuck on the road on Wednesday, rain on market day Thursday, the constable taken ill on Friday, riot on Saturday. In Bexley: the same four events in the order rain, constable ill, wagon stuck, rumour — and nothing on the Saturday.

Same set, different sequence, different outcome. You can say that temporal order is only another condition and that your laws take ordered conditions as input. But then tell me what the law is. Whenever a rumour of hoarding precedes a delayed grain wagon in a town of this kind, a riot follows: that is not a law, there will never be one, and every ceteris paribus hedge you add to save it from counterexample empties it of the very content that made it explanatory. What the historian has instead is a chain, in which each step is made intelligible by the one before it, and what carries us along is our sense of how people go on, not a generalization. That is the thing I am calling explanatory, and it is not a law.

And here is the test I want to hold you to — call it the erasure test. Take a finished narrative, delete one link, and give the shortened version to a competent reader who has not seen the original. Ask her a battery of counterfactual questions about the outcome: what if the wagon had arrived, what if the magistrate had acted on Thursday, what would the crowd have done. If her answers get worse, the deleted link was load-bearing; if they do not, it was decoration. Historians already argue this way and it tracks something real. But on your account the test seems to have no object: the only question is whether initial conditions together with generalizations make the outcome expectable, and each link either appears among the initial conditions or it does not. So: is the order of a narrative part of the initial conditions, part of the laws, or neither? If neither, where does its explanatory force come from — and why is it not simply the kind of pseudo-explanation you yourself warned against, the kind that makes an outcome feel intelligible while giving us no reason to have expected it?
