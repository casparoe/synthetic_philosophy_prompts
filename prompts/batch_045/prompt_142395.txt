Next month I'm presenting a paper to a joint seminar of a land-conservation NGO and a university environmental studies program: about twenty people, mostly field conservation biologists who treat habitat loss as the paradigm environmental bad and whose working currency is native-species richness, ecosystem integrity, and naturalness; two ecological economists who do natural-capital accounting and will want a decision-relevant number with uncertainty bounds and a defensible welfare weight; and one animal-welfare scientist.

My thesis is deliberately narrow: for many habitats, conversion or degradation plausibly *reduces* net wild-animal suffering, because most of the suffering in these systems is concentrated in the deaths of very young animals of high-fecundity species, and the annual count of those deaths tracks the energy a habitat makes available to animals (net primary productivity) far more closely than it tracks species richness or naturalness. So a preserved hectare is not automatically welfare-positive, and conservationists who invoke animal suffering against land conversion often have the sign backwards. I'll argue it in three steps: welfare in a population is a *flow* of births and deaths, not a stock of existing animals; suppression, substitution, and extinction differ in their welfare effects and are routinely run together; and the counterfactual land use determines the answer, so the relevant unit is suffering per unit of net primary productivity under each option.
