I teach a graduate seminar on moral responsibility, and we have just finished a unit on the standing to blame — specifically on the so-called non-hypocrisy condition, and on what a blamee is doing when he answers a blame with a "you too" rather than with a defense or an excuse. My students found the usual binary framing unsatisfying (either the blamer has standing or she does not), and one of them suggested we instead treat standing as a scalar quantity and score concrete cases from 0 (no standing whatsoever) to 1 (full, unqualified standing). I would like to put that suggestion to the test, and I have written up eight cases for the purpose.

Two of the cases are deliberately built around wrinkles that the standard treatments do not handle cleanly, and I would like you to engage with them as I have posed them rather than assimilating them to the familiar ones. The first is what I have been calling *counterfactual hypocrisy*: a blamer who currently complies with the norm in question but who would violate it under a very small change of circumstances — no one would ever find out, the opportunity is costless, her reputation is not at stake — so that the difference between her and the actual violator looks like nothing but luck. My student's claim is that this should cost her exactly as much standing as actual non-compliance costs; I am not convinced, but I want the case scored on its merits. The second is the *confessional preface*: a blamer who explicitly acknowledges her own violation of the very norm she invokes, says in so many words that she has no standing to speak, and then speaks anyway. Does such an acknowledgment restore some or all of the standing she would otherwise lack, is it merely good manners grafted onto an act of blaming that remains defective, or is it a further exercise of a standing she does not have? Here are the cases: (1) a father who habitually glances at his phone while stopped at red lights tells his seventeen-year-old daughter that she was seriously at fault for texting while driving; (2) a senior professor who has quietly skipped department meetings for years publicly blames a junior colleague for missing one; (3) a climate scientist who flies to two conferences a year blames a fossil-fuel lobbyist for deliberately misleading the public about climate risk; (4) a stranger with no prior relationship to anyone involved remarks, coldly and accurately, that a man she read about online deserves blame for cheating on his taxes; (5) a woman sober for three years blames her brother for driving drunk, having herself done so repeatedly before her recovery; (6) a magistrate who has herself underpaid her taxes sentences a defendant for tax fraud; (7) the counterfactually hypocritical case: a department chair who has never padded an expense report but who would certainly do so if the accounting system did not flag it and no one could ever trace it, blames a colleague who did pad his; (8) the confessional preface case: a novelist who once plagiarized a passage early in her career blames a peer for plagiarism, opening with an explicit statement that she has done the same thing and has no standing to say what she is about to say.

Please do three things. First, assign each case a standing score between 0 and 1, sort the eight into tiers (full, largely intact, significantly diminished, nearly absent, none), and give one sentence of justification per case. Second, say which considerations are doing the work in your ordering — the blamer's relationship to the blamee, her institutional or official role, the quality of her will (resentment, indignation, or detached judgment), the seriousness of the norm and of her own violation of it, her causal or structural complicity, the motivational purity of her blaming, and so on — and then tell me whether these considerations genuinely trade off against one another or whether one of them dominates the others in your scores. Third, tell me whether standing is really scalar at all: perhaps the scoring exercise is only a crude way of recording a binary verdict plus separate judgments about how harshly the blamer may blame, how much uptake she may demand, and whether what she says is true. While you are at it, please deal with the moral equality worry: almost everyone is implicated in some wrong or other, so if every violation cost standing, the practice of blaming would collapse; explain why your numbers do not collapse toward zero across the board, or, if you think they should, say why that is not a reductio of the view you are applying.

Finally, and this is the part I most want for my seminar: tell me what would change your mind. Which single argument or consideration would most plausibly reorder your tiers — perhaps a defense of the thought that hypocrisy concerns only the sincerity of the blamer's commitment rather than her conduct, or a defense of the thought that standing is entirely a matter of relationship and has nothing to do with clean hands — and where is your own reasoning most likely to fail? If a colleague convinced you that you had misdescribed one of these cases, which of your eight scores would you revise first, and in which direction?
