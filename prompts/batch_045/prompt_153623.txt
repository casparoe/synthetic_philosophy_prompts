I'm on the standards committee of the Coastal Aquaculture Certification Alliance — we certify farmed seafood for about forty mid-sized producers, mostly Atlantic salmon and farmed whiteleg shrimp — and we're rewriting the animal-welfare module of our standard. Two subcommittees have produced two basically incompatable documents, we vote in three weeks, and I'm the person who has to write the memo that goes out beforehand. The salmon people want a numeric index, something like a 0-to-100 built out of time-to-loss-of-consciousness at harvest, stocking density, and a few water-quality and fin-lesion measures, because a number is auditable and a buyer can print it on a bag. The shrimp people say that's empty for decapods: you can't run anything EEG-like on an animal that small, so every number would come from proxies like loss of appendage movement or righting reflex, which may be pure spinal/reflex noise after the animal is already insensible. Their counter-proposal is an input-based scheme: a list of prohibited practices (no eyestalk ablation, no ice slurry as the primary kill step) plus required equipment specs. My brother-in-law runs a shrimp hatchery in south Texas and thinks the whole exercise is theater, which hasn't helped.

The bit I keep going in circles on, and that I now think is the actual crux, is aggregation. A shrimp farm kills somewhere in the low hundreds of animals per kilo of product; a salmon farm kills a small fraction of one. So any standard that weights by number of individuals and any standard that weights by intensity-and-duration per individual will rank the same two farms in opposite directions, and I don't think anyone on the committee has said out loud that we have to pick. One member has proposed we just assign decapods an explicit sentience weight — she suggested 0.3, flagged as provisional, so a single scale can carry both taxa and we can express everything as welfare-years per kilogram — and another member says that's numerology in a lab coat and that we should decline to certify decapods pending better evidence, which would drop roughly half our producers from the program. The vet on our committee also keeps pushing the behavioral-evidence point: shrimp will groom or rub a chemically irritated body part, and that grooming drops when an analgesic is applied, which she reads as pain and the skeptics read as a nociceptive reflex with no affective component, and I have no idea how much weight a certification standard should put on that.

So: can you draft the memo I circulate? I need a genuine recommendation with a timeline — one unified index with a provisional weight, a two-track standard (outcome-based behavioral indicators for finfish, input-based requirements for shrimp), or a hold on decapod certification until the evidence is better — plus reasoning the losing side could actually respond to rather than a restatement of our mission statement. Two objections I specifically want taken seriously rather than waved off: first, that publishing any welfare score for shrimp converts a guess into a marketing claim that shoppers and retailers will treat as established, and second, the worry a few of us raised that certifying welfare for animals who are killed at a small fraction of their natural lifespan mostly functions to make buyers comfortable rather than to help the animals. And please tell us what to do in the meantime — commit to revisiting the weight on a fixed schedule, fund or commission our own behavioral work, or simply wait. I took one philosophy class in college and have been the annoying person in the room about it ever since, so I'm fine with a recommendation that says we can't honestly aggregate the two taxa; I just need something the committee can vote on that isn't mush.
