I'm writing a thesis on the concepts of health and disease — specifically on whether the biostatistical, value-free family of accounts can be reconciled with the "harmful dysfunction" style of account — and I also sit, as the token philosopher, on a working group advising our national health service on which conditions to add to the list of diagnoses that attract funding. We have been stuck on one case for months, and I keep hearing the same sentence doing three quite different jobs.

The case, stipulated as our group has provisionally described it: a trait, call it T, such that

- T is present in well over half of adults, so it is statistically typical in every reference class anyone has proposed (age, sex, population);
- T is produced by the ordinary operation of a mechanism that, on the best available evidence, was fitness-enhancing in the environments in which it evolved;
- in the current environment — artificial light at night, shift work, cheap calorie-dense food — T reliably causes a cluster of downstream harms and somewhat reduces species-typical functioning relative to that environment, though not relative to the ancestral one;
- there is a safe, cheap intervention; recipients mostly report feeling better afterwards, and their downstream risk falls only slightly;
- a patients' organisation wants T listed (access to treatment, research funding), while some of its own members strongly do not want to be told they have a disease (stigma, insurance).

The three uses. The chair used the line to stop me: I had argued that, on any view on which disease is a matter of statistical species-typical functioning, T is not a disease, and she replied that health is a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity — so the absence of dysfunction cannot settle whether people with T are healthy. A clinician on the group used the same line to argue for listing T regardless: medicine, he said, is in the business of promoting well-being, not only of removing disease. And a sociologist member used it to argue that the health concept is so thoroughly evaluative that we should drop it from eligibility criteria altogether and work with something like need or capability instead.

What I want is a disambiguation I can hand round at the next meeting. Please set out, as a menu rather than as one preferred reading, the distinct precise claims the sentence could be making: that absence of disease is not sufficient for health; that it is not necessary either; that health is a positive state rather than a remainder left by pathology; that health is a capacity or an ability to do or to cope with things; that health is a relation between an organism and an environment rather than an internal condition; that health admits of degrees while disease is bivalent; that health is an evaluative concept and so a value-free account of disease cannot be the whole story; that the proper aims of medicine outrun the elimination of disease; the maximal reading on which almost nobody counts as healthy; and anything else that belongs on the list. For each, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, a contentious one, or false, and say which of the three uses above it would actually support — and, equally, which it would not.

I would also like to know which of these readings a defender of a value-free, species-typical-function account of disease could simply grant, perhaps by distinguishing a theoretical concept of health from an ordinary or clinical one. If the chair's use depends on a reading of that kind, I need to be told, because then we may only be having a verbal dispute. Finally, please flag anywhere the sentence slides between a claim about what health is and a claim about what medicine should do or about how a concept ought to be used in allocating resources. British English, please, and no need for references or suggestions for further reading.
