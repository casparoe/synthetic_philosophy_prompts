I sit on a small advisory panel to a civic museum that is rewriting the interpretation for a permanent gallery on trade, empire and exploration; Columbus, Jefferson and Churchill all appear in it. We have now had three sessions in which the same sentence has stopped the discussion dead: "he was a man of his time — you can't judge him by the standards of today". What interests me is that it is being used by people on both sides. One member uses it to say that no verdict should be attached at all, and therefore that the celebratory framing should stay; another uses it to say that if his conduct was merely the output of his period then he was not an exemplar, and the commemoration should go.

I want a precisification rather than a verdict. Please set out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses the sentence could be expressing — I would expect somewhere between six and eight — and for each: the thesis stated in a form one could actually argue about; whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false; and what it would and would not licence in our decision. Please group them by the kind of claim they make: about the truth or the historical variability of moral standards; about what was knowable or reasonable to believe then; about excuse, blameworthiness and the alternatives open to the agent; about the point of commemoration; about counterfactuals concerning what we ourselves would have done in his place.

Two constraints I would like every reading tested against.

First, "his time" is doing unexamined work. For each reading, say what it takes "the standards of his time" to be: the prevailing opinion of the day, the best opinion then available, what was knowable from his social and informational position, what he could realistically have done otherwise, or the opinion of the people his actions affected. So that the answer does not turn on contested history, stipulate for the sake of argument that each of the three was criticised by at least some contemporaries for the conduct we now object to, and that each also did things we now admire.

Second, the sentence seems to travel in only one direction: nobody says "he was a man of his time" to explain why Columbus sailed, or why Churchill refused to surrender in 1940. It is produced only for the parts we condemn. For each reading, say whether it can account for that asymmetry, or whether the asymmetry shows the sentence is functioning as a selective excuse rather than as a general claim — and, where a reading fails, whether it fails merely as deployed or in itself.

Finally: given what the sentence is being made to do in the room, which reading is my colleague most likely reaching for when she says it settles the matter, and does that reading actually settle it? If it does not, tell me what question the panel should be asking instead. Please write in British English.
