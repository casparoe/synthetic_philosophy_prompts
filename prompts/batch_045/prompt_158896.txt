At a philosophy workshop I attended last month, two of the participants got into a dispute that I was asked to referee and could not. I would be grateful for an adjudication that goes through it move by move rather than picking an overall winner.

The background. They were discussing whether disposition ascriptions can be analysed conditionally: roughly, an object is disposed to manifest M in conditions S just in case, were S to obtain, it would M. Two familiar styles of counterexample were on the table. A fink: the object has the disposition, but the stimulus itself sets off a change that removes the disposition before it can manifest — say, a filament disposed to glow when current passes through it, wired to a sensor that detects the current and floods the chamber with coolant within microseconds, so that it never reaches glowing temperature. A mimic, as they called the mirror image: the object lacks the disposition, but something else produces the manifestation once the stimulus arrives — the same filament with an insulating core, where the glowing is supplied by an external heater that switches on when it detects the circuit closing.

The case they actually fought over was built by one of them, and I had not encountered anything like it before. Call it the stutter case. It is the fink case with the sensor deliberately slowed: the filament reaches glowing temperature and glows for three full seconds before the coolant brings it down, after which it is undamaged and would glow again if the current were restored. Owen, who built it, argued that it is not a fink at all, because the disposition does manifest, just briefly. On his reading the conditional analysis survives if "would M" is read as "would begin to M," or as "would M for some non-zero stretch of time," and the classic fink cases get their force only from the unexamined assumption that manifestation is instantaneous.

(1) Renata replied that "would begin to M" is hopeless, since in the mimic case the object also begins to M — the heated filament does start to glow — yet intuitively it is not disposed to glow when current passes. And "for some non-zero stretch" has no principled stopping point: three seconds counts, but so would a microsecond, and a microsecond of glowing is just the original fink described at a finer temporal grain, so the repair has swallowed the counterexample rather than answered it.

(2) Owen granted the point and changed strategy: drop the conditional analysis and identify the disposition with its causal basis. What makes the filament disposed to glow is its resistivity and structure; that basis is present in both the fink and the stutter case and absent in the mimic, and the conditionals are evidence for the presence of a basis rather than an analysis of it.

(3) Renata said this works only if the basis must be intrinsic to the object, and that either way the view fails. If bases must be intrinsic, ordinary relational dispositions stop counting, since a solute is soluble only given solvents and a key opens a lock only given the lock. If bases may be extrinsic, then the basis in the mimic case is arguably the filament-plus-heater system, and the mimic counts as disposed after all.

(4) Owen answered that both horns rely on an unstated assumption, namely that there is one privileged object to which the disposition belongs. Manifestation partners such as the solvent and the lock are one thing, he said; but consider a heart whose own conduction tissue is wrecked and which beats regularly only because of an implanted pacemaker. He claimed that the heart is disposed to beat regularly, that this case is continuous with the relational ones, and that the pacemaker is no more a mere partner than the filament's heater is; so which object an ascription concerns is not settled in advance of our interests, and once a system is fixed, the fink and the mimic come apart cleanly inside it.

They went round in circles after that and I could not tell who was ahead. Please give me exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no closing summary: items 1 through 4 should rate moves (1) through (4) respectively as clearly right, mostly right, mostly wrong, or clearly wrong, with a clause of justification for each; item 5 should state what both of them are missing.
