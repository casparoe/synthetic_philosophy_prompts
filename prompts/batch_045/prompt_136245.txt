so I've been going back and forth with a friend about ambiguity aversion and I think his argument proves way too much, but I can't put my finger on the step that breaks.

Setup: the usual Ellsberg urn, 90 balls, 30 of them red, the other 60 black and yellow in a proportion nobody will tell you. One ball gets drawn. Four tickets, each pays $100 on a colour: R pays on red, B on black, RY on red-or-yellow, BY on black-or-yellow. The standard pattern is that people take R over B, and BY over RY.

His argument, roughly in his words:

(1) Your attitudes under uncertainty are rational only if they can be written as expected utility against a single probability function — if you violate sure-thing, you're out.

(2) Those two preferences can't be written that way. The first says your p(red) > p(black). In the second, the yellow payoff is on both sides, so it cancels, and you get p(black) > p(red). That's just arithmetic and I grant it.

(3) And it isn't only a bookkeeping problem, it's exploitable. Here's the trade: you're handed the choice between RY and BY and you take BY. Then a referee peeks at the ball and announces only whether it is yellow or not. If she says "not yellow", you now strictly prefer RY and you'll pay a dollar to swap. And you knew that in advance — at the very start, before the announcement, you'd have paid to give up the option to swap, because swapping leaves you with something you liked less than BY. So your preferences cost you cash and buy you nothing, which is what "irrational" is supposed to mean around here.

(4) Nothing in the story in (3) cares *why* your preferences have that shape. It only ever uses the four preferences. So it convicts anyone whose preferences have that shape, whatever generated them.

(5) So now take what I'm calling the shadowed version. Everything is the same except: the black/yellow split is fixed by someone who sees which ticket you picked and gets a bonus if you lose. (There's a second version where the split is fixed in advance but the person offering you the bet knows it and only offers when the bet is bad for you — my friend says these are completely different and I don't see why, surely it's the same problem.) In both, the person who shrugs and says "I don't have a number for black, and this offer was picked with me in mind" does better than the person who forms a number and acts on it: whatever number the second person forms, the setter can pick the split that makes her ticket lose, and she loses.

(6) But by (1) through (4), the shrugging person is the irrational one. That's the bit I can't swallow. The argument now says the correct response to an offer you suspect was selected against you is to invent a number and bet as if you knew it — which is the exact opposite of what the winner's curse, adverse selection, and every card player's rule about not being able to tell who the fish is all say.

(7) So (1), (3) or (4) has to go.

His answer to (5) is that in the shadowed cases it isn't really uncertainty at all, it's strategy: put a prior over the setter's rule and expected utility handles it fine. I said fine, what if I don't have a number for her rule either — then my preferences have the same shape again, one level up, and the trade in (3) starts over. He said then put a prior over your priors. I said that's just the same move one more time and we looped.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you? If I have to choose I'd rather keep (6) than (1) — the winner's curse thing is the firmest item in the whole picture — but I also don't know what "rational under uncertainty" even means if I drop (1), so tell me what I'd be buying. Two more things I want: is my friend right that the two shadowed versions are different, and is there any version of ambiguity aversion that survives (3), or does (3) kill every version of it equally?
