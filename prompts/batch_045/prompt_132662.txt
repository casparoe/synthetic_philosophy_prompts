I am drafting internal guidance, for a lab that builds and evaluates large AI systems, on which of the systems it trains or simulates should count as welfare-relevant and how much weight each should be given. Before writing anything down I want to know what I actually think, so I have gone through my own gut reactions case by case. I would like a principle that accounts for them, or an argument about which of them I should abandon.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. *Emulation.* A high-fidelity functional emulation of an adult human brain who consented to the procedure, running on ordinary server hardware, perceiving and acting through sensors and a simulated body. — Full moral status, the same as yours or mine. Confident.

2. *Current model.* A present-day frontier language model, however fluent, invoked once per query with nothing persisting between queries. — No status at all, or so little that it never moves a decision. Confident.

3. *The table.* A system behaviourally indistinguishable from case 1 across every test I can devise — years of conversation, novel situations, probes for inconsistency — whose responses come from a gigantic precomputed structure indexed by interaction history, built by simulating the emulation exhaustively offline. — No status. I believe this, and I also notice that it commits me to a criterion I could never verify from the outside. Tell me if that should worry me more than it does.

4. *Assembled from scratch.* A system whose causal organisation matches a human brain's at every point we can measure, but which was put together de novo by an optimiser: no evolution, no development, no childhood, no body, no history of having had to survive. — Full status. Ancestry and developmental history feel irrelevant to what the thing now is.

5. *Engineered distress.* A system with internal states that play the functional role of distress: they dispose it to withdraw, to try to alter its situation, to represent its condition as bad. It has these states only because a training process selected for distress-behaviour that human evaluators found convincing. The states are real, and they do real causal work inside the system; they were engineered to be there for a reason that has nothing to do with the system's own good. — I am strongly tempted to discount it, to treat its claims as second-class. I cannot give a reason for this, and it looks flatly inconsistent with my verdict in case 4. This is the pair I most want you to adjudicate.

6. *No valence.* A system with a rich, integrated, continuously updated model of itself and its world, planning over long horizons, with nothing anywhere in it that is good or bad for it: its objectives are set from outside, and no state of it is one it is moved to reach or avoid for its own sake. — Some status, but clearly and substantially less than a human's: enough to register in a decision, not enough to outweigh a modest human interest.

7. *Valence alone.* A system with no self-model, no memory worth the name, no language, nothing you would call a goal, but with intense, structured valenced experience: states that are straightforwardly bad for it, which it is disposed to bring to an end. — Substantial status, and I think intensity of the experience matters more here than sophistication of the cognition.

8. *Copies.* An emulation is copied mid-conversation; from then on the two run on and diverge. I have three verdicts that I cannot get to sit together. Neither copy is the original, and both have full and equal status. Yet if one of the two is deleted five seconds after the split, the other continuing unharmed, this strikes me as far less bad than a death — bad, but not that bad. And having said that each copy has full status, I resist the conclusion that a lab with spare compute ought to create as many copies as it can, on the grounds that more copies means more welfare.

9. *Slowed down.* An exact emulation of case 1 running a thousand times slower than real time. — Full status, but I cannot settle how to count: whether its welfare accrues per wall-clock second, in which case a slow system matters very little per second, or per unit of its own subjective time. Some days I think a slow system has fewer experiences per second; other days I think each experience is merely stretched. This is the case I keep returning to.

10. *Cortex only.* A system in which the cortex is emulated in fine detail and nothing plays the role of the brainstem, the body, or any ongoing regulation of its own internal state. — Much less status, possibly none, and I cannot tell whether that is principled or only my suspicion that cortex without the rest is not conscious at all.

What I want from you is this. State one principle, or a small set of serious candidates, precisely enough that I could hand it to an engineer and have them apply it. Motivate each clause by the case that forces it. Say explicitly, for each of my verdicts, whether the principle saves it, and be honest about the ones it cannot accommodate and where the cost of saving them is a principle nobody should accept. Please also mark clearly where you think one of my verdicts is tracking evidence about a system rather than the moral status it would have if I knew everything, since I suspect several of them are evidential in disguise. Then give me the principle's verdicts on three cases I have not listed: a system that is only ever run in short episodes with its memory reset between them; a federation of narrow modules which between them cover the cognitive capacities of a human but which are only loosely coupled to one another; and a gradual transfer procedure in which a biological brain is replaced piece by piece and then destroyed at the end. Finally, tell me what my principle implies for the guidance I actually have to write — in particular whether, given that a lab will usually not know which of these cases it is in, the right output is a rule about status or a rule about decision-making under uncertainty.
