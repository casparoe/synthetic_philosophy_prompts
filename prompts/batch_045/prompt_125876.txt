Our reading group (a therapist, a retired classics teacher, a data scientist, and me) has been going in circles for a month about what emotions actually *are*, and I got stuck writing the "what question are we even asking" document for week one, so I'm asking you to write the hard part of it. The problem is that everyone has exactly one case they take to be decisive: the therapist keeps coming back to a client who knows perfectly well the spider is plastic and is still terrified of it, and says this kills the "emotions are judgments" view; the classics teacher keeps pointing out that people genuinely grieve at the death of characters who never existed, and thinks that kills anything that requires a real particular object; the data scientist says emotions can't be private inner episodes at all because he can predict them from behavioural traces better than people can report them. I don't think any of those arguments settle anything, because nobody has said what a theory would have to get right in the first place. So: give me the desiderata list for a theory of what emotions are, numbered, each with the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a taste, and each marked negotiable or non-negotiable.

I have my own pile of cases that I want represented or explicitly ruled out, and honestly half of my hope is that you'll tell me some of them are not the theory's job. (1) Emotions seem assessable — you can ask whether the fear was apt or the anger was out of proportion — so a theory has to say what correctness consists in, and I want to know whether that's really a requirement or just a prejudice of the judgment camp. (2) Recalcitrance: the plastic-spider case, but also the reverse, where someone's affect lags behind their own considered view for months and then catches up. (3) Emotions you can be *talked* out of but not *decided* out of, which seems to demand a weird intermediate status between belief and itch. (4) My friend with a rare condition that badly blunts her visceral feedback, who says she still knows she's anxious because of what she starts doing — rehearsing conversations, checking the door twice — and I don't know whether that's a counterexample to the bodily-feeling family or just a case of the evidence being rewritten. (5) Emotions with diffuse or missing objects: ambient dread about something huge and statistical that no single action would address, "nostalgia" for a trip that hasn't happened yet, relief about an outcome that was never really on the cards. (6) Emotions whose object is another emotion of your own — being ashamed of being jealous, being annoyed at yourself for still being frightened — and whether that recursion causes trouble for any of the three usual families. (7) A dog's fear, a pre-verbal infant's distress, and whether a theory is allowed to exclude those by fiat or has to earn the exclusion. (8) Emotions as things that can be *educated* — people who learn to feel indignation at something they used to accept quietly, which is the bit I personally care about most, since my real interest is whether emotions can be reasons for action and whether they can be better or worse in something like an epistemic sense.
