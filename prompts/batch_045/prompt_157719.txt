my value theory reading group has been stuck on spectrum arguments for whether "better than" is transitive for like three years, and i want a diagnosis, not another round of the same moves. Setup: you build a long series A1 to A100 where each item is a hair better than the one before it — a touch more of one good, a touch less of another — so every adjacent pair looks like a clean improvement, but A100 is clearly worse than A1 (A1 is a long calm life of modest well-being, A100 is a very short life of extreme intensity, or pick your own). Half the group concludes "better than" isn't transitive: the pairwise comparisons are the data, transitivity is just a formal prejudice we inherited from thinking about numbers. The other half says the series only shows our comparisons get unreliable below some threshold, or that there's a lexical break somewhere in the middle, or that a relation that isn't transitive doesn't deserve the name, because it couldn't settle a choice. Nobody has budged since about the third meeting.

My hypothesis, which I want you to push on: they aren't answering one question. One side wants to know what has to hold between two options for the comparison to settle a choice from a menu, and for that job transitivity looks non-negotiable. The other wants to know how outcomes stand in value full stop, and for that job there's no obvious reason the ordering has to be acyclic — everyone reaches for majority-preference cycles as the model. If that's right then most of the shouting is verbal, or at least it's two questions under one name.

Two cases make me doubt my own diagnosis. First: run the series past people in reverse and a lot of them will happily go the other way too — A100 better than A99 and so on down — because each pair is judged from whichever end they just came from. Second: the sequential version, where one agent is actually offered every step in order over time (a small upgrade every day, each one worth taking, after a thousand days you're worse off than if you'd refused). In the first case the intransitivity looks like it's in the judging, not in the outcomes; in the second it looks like it's real and it costs you something. I've also been floating a distinction nobody likes: "better than" is transitive at a fixed grain of resolution and only looks intransitive when the grain drifts across the series.

So what sort of stalemate is this? Verbal? Substantive but bottoming out in opposed starting intuitions about what a comparative verdict is for? A disguised empirical question about order-dependence and grain in comparative judgment? Two questions sharing a label? Or a sign the question is ill-posed and should be replaced by something like "what do we need a comparative verdict to do"? Argue for one reading against the others, engaging what each side in the group actually says rather than surveying positions, and then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — something I could actually test on my group next week, a formal result about what a chooser with cyclic values can do, a distinction, or a reason to expect no progress ever. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. Don't restate my question back at me and don't give me reading suggestions; I've read plenty, I want the diagnosis.
