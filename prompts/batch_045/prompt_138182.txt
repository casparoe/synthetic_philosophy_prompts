I have no academic background in philosophy and have been working through the essay Camus wrote against capital punishment on my own. In a discussion thread about it, someone closed the argument with a single line: "the punishment should fit the crime — a murderer took a life, so anything less than death is a discount." It sounds self-evident to everyone there, me included, but I can't tell what it actually rules in or out.

I also have a worry of my own that I'd like dealt with rather than set aside: even granting that the crime fixes the punishment, the person executed years later is not the person who killed — people change a great deal over that time — and a prison sentence can be shortened or ended in a way an execution cannot, so "fitting" a punishment to a past act seems to misfire for reasons that have nothing to do with being soft on crime. (I take this to be close to one of Camus's own points about irreparability, but tell me if I have him wrong.)

Please set out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims that one-liner could be expressing: what has to match what (the harm done, the offender's culpability, the type of act, the offender's character), in what respect it has to match (proportionality, deterrence, denunciation, something else again), and whether the "should" is moral or merely conventional. For each reading, commit to a verdict — trivial, substantive and defensible, or false — and say plainly whether it would actually get him his conclusion that death is the only punishment that fits murder, which reading he was most likely reaching for, and whether the slogan is simply the wrong instrument for settling the question. Where you are reconstructing rather than reporting, or where a claim about Camus's text is something you cannot check, say so rather than hedging everything.
