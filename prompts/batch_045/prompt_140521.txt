I got into an argument about this at board game night and it's been bugging me since, so here goes. My roommate works on evals at one of the big labs and described their setup roughly like this: the scoreboard only ever looks at the final answer. If the system writes two pages of reasoning that is complete garbage and then puts down the right answer, it gets full marks; if it writes a beautiful derivation and fumbles the last digit, it gets zero. The reasoning trace isn't even part of what gets delivered to the customer — it's logged, and nobody reads it unless something is already going wrong. Two systems with identical accuracy, one with clean legible steps and one with nonsense in the middle, count as tied, and then you pick whichever is cheaper and faster. But the moment something does go wrong, or somebody suspects the system of scheming, everyone drops everything and demands to read the traces and grade individual steps in them. And then there's a third thing she said that I keep turning over: they won't train on that step-by-step score. The moment the process grade starts pushing on the weights, the reasoning becomes theater — it learns to write steps that read fine while the actual work happens somewhere you can't see. So the rule is: audit the reasoning, never optimize it. (She also admitted, when I pushed, that nobody can really check whether the trace is *why* the model did the thing, as opposed to a fluent story it produces afterwards that happens to sound right.)

What makes it feel inconsistent to me is how differently we treat people in what looks like the same situation. My friend in med school says she gets marked down on oral exams for landing on the right diagnosis with no defensible route to it, and a resident who copies a correct answer gets in serious trouble even though the patient was fine. Judges reviewing a government agency will send a decision back because the agency didn't explain itself, even when nobody is claiming the outcome is wrong. A student who gets the right answer by working backwards from the answer key hasn't learned anything. So with humans we're officially process people: what matters is whether you followed a responsible procedure and could give reasons for what you did. With these systems we're outcome people — except during an incident, when we become hardcore process people for a week, and then we go build a process score that is specifically designed never to touch the thing being scored. I don't have the vocabulary for any of this so sorry if I'm describing it badly.

I'm trying to write a short blog post and I keep going in circles. What does outcome-only scoring actually presuppose — about what a reason is, about what makes a performance good or a belief justified? Is it committed to saying the reasoning is just a causal step on the way to the answer, worth exactly its contribution to accuracy, or could someone who thinks having reasons is the whole point of a rational performance still sign off on it, maybe as a temporary compromise? Does the incident-time audit quietly concede something the first practice denies, or can the two be made to fit together? Does the "audit but never optimize" rule amount to anything more than pragmatism, or does it presuppose that a trace is more like testimony than like a skill, something that stops being evidence the moment you start grading it? And which parts of all this are genuinely forced by the underlying view rather than just convenient — cheap to measure, easy to compare across labs, avoiding the fact that human annotators disagree about what good reasoning even looks like? Finally: if we actually dropped outcome-only scoring, what would evaluation have to look like, and what would we lose?
