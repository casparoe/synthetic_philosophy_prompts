I'm the guy in my friends' group chat that gets tagged whenever somebody posts one of those "what's the probability" puzzles, and last month two of them collided in a way I can't stop poking at. First: a point is picked at random somewhere inside a circle a foot in radius — what's the chance it lands within six inches of the middle? My brother-in-law (mechanical engineer, extremely calm about being wrong) said a half: the distance from the middle is somewhere between zero and a foot, six inches is the halfway point of that range, done. I said a quarter, because the little circle has a quarter of the area of the big one. We were both completely sure, and we weren't disagreeing about any fact or about any arithmetic, which is the part that got under my skin. Second, the same week: my sister texts that she has two kids and at least one is a boy, and the chat does the usual 1/3-versus-1/2 fight; then she adds that the boy was born in July, and the number moves, even though the month obviously has nothing to do with anybody's sex. Those two feel like different problems to me and I want to be able to say why. In the first one, the fight is about which quantity you spread your credence evenly over — distance, area, angle, the square of the distance — each of which is a perfectly good way of describing the very same point. In the second one the quantity is settled and the fight is really about how the information got to me: what my sister would have said instead if she'd had a boy and a girl, or two boys, or a boy born in March. My working names are "description-sensitive" and "report-sensitive", and I think there's a third pile too: puzzles where the setup already tells you how the thing was produced (I drop a needle on a floor with parallel lines ruled on it, I spin a pointer, I throw a dart) and where nobody argues at all.

So here's what I actually want, and I want the carving, not the label. Someone in the chat told me the even-spreading idea has a proper name and that there's a classic version of all this involving chords drawn across a circle with somebody's name attached to it — that was the least useful thing anyone said, because it doesn't tell me which of my piles a given puzzle belongs in, or whether three is even the right number of piles. My brother-in-law's suggestion, which I've half adopted, is a machine test: a puzzle like this is well posed only if you could actually build a device that does what the words say, and if two different devices both fit the words and spit out different numbers, then the words are just incomplete and the honest answer is "no answer". But I think it breaks in both directions. It seems to under-generate: I can build a device that sprinkles points evenly by area and one that picks a distance and then spins, so it declares my circle hopeless — fine, that's the right result — but there are also cases where there's one obvious device and people still argue, because the vagueness sits in the question rather than in the setup. And it over-generates in a way that bothers me more: for a "random chord" you can build a straw-throwing machine and a spinning machine, and now the conceptual question has been replaced by an empirical one about friction and how you hold the straw, which is not what I was asking. Is there an established version of the machine test, or an established replacement for it — something that asks instead whether the answer stays put under a family of re-descriptions, rather than pointing at hardware?

The other thing I can't place: some of these puzzles have a whole continuum of defensible numbers and some collapse to a single one. For a lot of setups every reasonable way of spreading credence gives the same answer, and I don't know what predicts the collapse. Does that property have a name, is it explained by symmetry in a way my machine test is missing, and is "average over all the admissible devices" ever a legitimate move, or is that just picking one more device and pretending it isn't? If nothing established fits my three-way split, give me your own: name the categories, define them tightly enough that the circle case, the two-kids case and the needle case land in different places, and give me at least one boundary case where you genuinely can't tell which category a puzzle falls under, plus what you'd need to be told to settle it. Please write in American English, and skip the history and the reading suggestions — I definately don't need who raised any of this first, I need to know what I'm looking at.
