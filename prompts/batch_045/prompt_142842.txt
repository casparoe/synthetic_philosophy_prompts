I'm in the final year of a philosophy degree and I'm writing a term paper on whether the numbers should count in cases where we can save either one person or several. Before I commit to a position on paper I'd like a check that the package I've assembled actually hangs together, because I have a feeling that some of it has been assembled for the wrong reasons. Assume throughout that the people involved are strangers to the rescuer and are alike in every other respect that might matter — no differences in age, dependents, or expected future.

Here is roughly where I've landed:

(1) I don't believe there is impersonal value in more people surviving. Everything that matters in these cases matters to, or for, some particular person; the bare fact that a greater number would be saved is not itself a reason for me to save them.

(2) So in the standard case, where I can save either one stranger or five, I hold that it is permissible to save the one: there is no duty to save the greater number, and no one's claim on me is strengthened by the fact that other people would benefit if I answered it.

(3) But I also don't want to deny that the outcome in which five die is in some sense worse than the outcome in which one dies. I want to say both that it is worse and that this worse-ness is not a reason for me to save the five. I can't tell whether this is a coherent combination or a way of having it both ways.

(4) When I have no special relationship to anyone involved, I think the fair procedure is a coin toss — heads the one, tails the five — which gives each of the six people an equal chance of surviving. And I want to say something stronger: that for an impartial rescuer, saving either group outright without randomizing is unfair.

(5) Numbers do seem to me to matter when the harms are small. I think I am required to prevent a million strangers' migraines rather than one stranger's migraine, and not merely permitted to do either.

(6) I think institutional policy is a different matter again: ambulance services, disaster triage protocols and hospital rationing should, as a matter of policy, aim at saving the greater number, and I don't regard this as a compromise with a view I otherwise reject.

(7) I think I may save my own friend rather than five strangers, and I want to say this is the same permission as in (2) rather than a separate exception for people I like.

There are also two cases I can't stop worrying about, and I can't tell whether they are objections or just pressure on my intuitions.

The serial case: the same choice comes up sixty times over a career, with me as the rescuer each time. My view seems to license saving the one on every occasion, leaving three hundred dead where sixty could have survived. That seems wrong to me, but I can't identify what changes between the one-off case and the series.

The overlapping-groups case, as I've been calling it in my notes: I can save {A, B, C, D, E}, or I can save {A} — the same individual is in both options, so A survives either way. Here I feel I must save the five, even though nothing I've said so far makes the numbers count; the difference seems to be that in this case no one's claim is being traded off against anyone else's. That makes me suspect that (1) is doing more work in the standard case than I've admitted.

What I'd like is an audit rather than a survey of the literature or a list of things to read. Go through the seven commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look as if they are; rank the real tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad; for each, say exactly what would have to be given up and what that would cost me elsewhere in the set; and then tell me whether there is a single revision that would clean up the most. I don't have a favourite among them, so please don't assume I'd rather keep any particular one — if one is the weak link, say which and why. And if the serial case or the overlapping-groups case is straightforwardly decisive against (2), say that plainly rather than reconciling it; better to find out now than in the exam.
