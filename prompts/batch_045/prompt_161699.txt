Putting together a short position paper on the nonidentity problem for a reading group and I want somebody to poke at it before I send it round, becuase I've got a feeling one of these has to go and I keep flip-flopping about which.

Where I've landed:

(1) Some kind of person-affecting restriction: an act is wrong only if it's worse for some person, and I mean worse in the plain counterfactual sense — lower well-being than that same person would otherwise have had. I've never seen the point of the "worse off than someone else could have been" version.
(2) The asymmetry: no reason to bring an extra person into existence even if their life would be happy, but reason not to bring one into existence if it'd be miserable. (Relatedly, I also want to say a bigger population of happy lives is impersonally better, just not something we should bring about — which might be inconsistent with the first half, or might not.)
(3) In the same-number cases — the teenager who conceives now rather than waiting, the two medical programmes where each treats whichever patients turn up, that sort of thing — I want to say the choice that produces the worse-off people is wrong.
(4) I'm attached to saying we owe real things to future generations about emissions, recource depletion, and so on.
(5) Methodologically, I don't want to rest any of this on case intuitions; I'd rather get the verdicts out of a general axiology and let the cases fall where they fall.

Does this hang together? Go through the pairs and tell me which tensions are real and how severe, and which ones only look like tensions but dissolve once the relevant distinction is stated properly. For each real one, what would have to give and what would giving it up cost me. Then tell me which single revision buys consistency cheapest overall. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning — no preamble, don't restate the question.
