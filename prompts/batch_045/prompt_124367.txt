Hi — I sit on the allocation committee for our university's Horizon Fund, roughly 120k a year earmarked for speculative high-upside work that normal panels won't touch. I'm the token plant pathologist on it, one of two non-economists, so keep the formalism light where you can. Two years ago we burned three whole meetings arguing about point estimates, so we rewrote our rules: each member writes down the widest probability range they'd personally defend for a proposal's key claim, we pool by taking the union of those ranges and deleting anything everyone agrees is "silly", and then we (1) drop any proposal whose resulting interval of expected value is dominated — every number in it below every number in some other proposal's — and (2) among the survivors, fund whichever has the highest lower bound. This year we recieved three finalists and the rule is behaving strangly, and I need to put a one-page recommendation in front of six people — one of whom is a statistician who will genuinely enjoy taking it apart — by Friday afternoon.

Finalist A is a 25k, six-month pilot from a group who want to field-test a cheap wastewater-and-metagenomics early-warning protocol for novel zoonotic spillover; their claim is that two to four months of extra warning would move a real fraction of the response earlier, which in their modelled scenario is worth something like 40,000 lives. Members' probabilities that this pilot specifically leads to an adopted system that actually changes a future response ran from 1 in 100 (the enthusiast, who is on the panel) down to 1 in 1,000 (our skeptic), so A's pooled EV interval is about 40 to 400 in lives. Finalist B is a very boring randomised trial of a tutoring intervention with an established effect size: EV interval around 8 to 15 in the same units, and everybody's ranges overlap almost exactly. Finalist C is a mid-sized data-infrastructure thing with a wide interval, 5 to 90. So under rule 2 A wins by three to five times and it isn't close — and yet half the committee thinks that's mad, because A's lower bound is only as good as the skeptic's willingness to be precise at that scale. She admits 1 in 1,000 is a gesture, not a measurement, and if the honest number were 1 in 100,000 (which nobody formally excluded, since "silly" was never defined) A's lower bound drops to 0.4 and it loses badly.

One member has floated a fix that I think is worth building the memo around. Call it the resolution floor: any probability the committee cannot distinguish from zero at the resolution our evidence actually supports — she proposes 1 in 500, roughly the finest discrimination our evidence base will bear — should not be treated as a probability at all but as unmeasured, and a proposal whose case rests on a sub-floor probability should be judged on non-EV grounds instead, mainly whether the money cheaply buys information that shrinks the interval (option value, reversibility, how fast we find out). On that way of looking at it we'd fund A, but as a measurement rather than a bet: 25k now, a hard decision gate at six months, and no renewal unless the update pushes the lower bound back above the floor.

What I want is the memo itself: bottom line up front — what we fund, how much, on what conditions and by what dates — then the reasoning, including a fair and specific statement of the strongest thing the losing side will say. My own guess is that the best version of that objection is that a resolution floor is arbitrary and description-dependent: how finely you carve up the event (adoption on its own, or adoption-and-a-spillover-occurring, or adoption somewhere with jurisdiction that matters) slides the number across the floor without anything changing in the world, so the rule doesn't track anything real; and that applied consistently it would defund every long shot this Fund exists for. Tell me how much of that you actually buy rather than just listing it. Keep the whole thing under 600 words, no heading longer than one line, and please don't point us at any literature — nobody on this committee will read it.
