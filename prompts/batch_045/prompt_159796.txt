i have no academic background in any of this, i went down this rabbit hole the way i guess most people do — read something popular about how kids pick up language, then found out the fight underneath it has apparently been running since the 60s and nobody has moved an inch.

The way i'd reconstruct it: one side (the Chomsky-ish one) says kids end up knowing extremely specific things about their language that you just couldnt get out of what they hear. The speech they get is messy, full of false starts and half-finished sentences, nobody reliably corrects childrens grammar, and yet they still converge on the same weirdly specific rules — the auxiliary-fronting example is the one everybody trots out. So some of the structure must be built in. The other side says: okay but (a) nobody has actually measured a real corpus and shown the crucial information is missing, you're just asserting the input is too poor; (b) kids turn out to be way better at picking up distributional patterns than anyone assumed; and (c) "innate" is a hopeless word anyway, everything is a mix of genes and environment, so the question as posed is broken.

What frustrates me is the shape of the thing. The same three or four examples get rerun forever. When someone says "how would the child ever rule out hypothesis H", the reply is "show me the corpus" — and the answer to that is "a corpus cant answer a question about whats possible in principle". Neither side seems to be addressing the other. I also half remember that the famous learnability theorem everyone gestures at doesnt quite say what people take it to say, which, if true, feels like its own little scandal sitting in the middle of it.

My own theory, which i definately cant test: the one fact both sides agree on — the input is incomplete, parents dont correct — only does any work given an unstated assumption about what learning is. If the child is testing discrete hypotheses and crossing off the ones that fail, missing evidence is fatal. If the child is doing something continuous and statistical, missing evidence is just noise you route around. So the same fact is decisive for one side and beside the point for the other, and nobody argues for the assumption. Maybe that's most of the disagreement. Or maybe they're simply answering two different questions: how is acquisition possible at all, versus what actually causes it in a real child.

So what kind of stalemate is this, really? A verbal one about "innate"? A genuine empirical question about how much information is in the input, which better data could in principle settle? A fight about what counts as an explanation in psychology? Or two questions unhappily sharing one name? And whatever it turns out to be, what would actually count as progress — because "wait and see what the big language models do" doesnt feel like an answer, since both sides already claim the models prove them right.
