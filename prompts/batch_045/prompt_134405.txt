Hey — I want you to build me a case in the philosophy of perception, and I care a lot about how it's built, so here's the whole brief.

What the case is for (so you know what counts as cheating): the standoff between disjunctivists and people who run the argument from illusion gets settled too cheaply on hallucination cases. On hallucination the disjunctivist has a clean story: the bad case is a degenerate, merely negative state, not a positive mental kind you also get in the good case. I want to push on illusion instead — real, mind-independent object right there in front of the subject, misperceived. That's where a relational, object-involving starting point is supposed to get dragged toward a common-factor conclusion. So: design an illusion case where the disjunctivist's usual exits are blocked.

Specs for the scenario:

1. Ordinary setting — a kitchen, a workshop, a hospital corridor, a night drive. No brains in vats, no evil neuroscientists, no VR rigs. Mundane causes are fine: bad lighting, tinted glass, a medication side-effect, a migraine aura.
2. One paragraph.
3. It has to be genuine illusion: not veridical perception, not hallucination. There's a real object, the subject really is in perceptual contact with it, and the subject is presented with a feature that the object doesn't actually have.
4. The illusory feature has to be perceptually present, not inferred — the subject isn't just layering a bad judgement or some wishful filling-in on top of an experience that's itself perfectly innocent. Make that come out of the story.
5. The experience should be introspectively indisitinguishable from both a matching veridical perception of an object that really does have that feature, and from a matching hallucination of one.
6. Two standard disjunctivist escapes have to be closed off: (a) "nothing is presented that isn't there — it's just how the object looks from here, a relational look-property, not a property the object lacks," and (b) "perception is innocent here; the missing feature is supplied by judgement or imagination." If you can think of a third escape hatch, close that off too and say what it was.

Then, after the scenario:

- Run the argument from illusion on your own case: lay out the indistinguishability premise and the conclusion about what kind of state experience fundamentally is, and say exactly which premise the disjunctivist denies and which one they can live with.
- Give the verdicts: what does the conjunctivist / common-kind theorist have to say this state is, what does the disjunctivist have to say, and why, given your setup. Both answers should fall out of the theories, not out of you stipulating them.
- Show the case meets specs 3–6, one short check each.
- Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to the argument if it's removed — e.g. if you turn the illusory feature into a mere look-property, or delete the object so it's plain hallucination again.
- Last bit, and the bit I care about most: what's the strongest thing a disjunctivist can say to reject the case, where "strongest" rules out just restating their view. Can they go for illusion as a seperate, third fundamental kind of experience rather than a mixture of a relational bit and a non-relational bit — and if they do, what does that cost them elsewhere?
