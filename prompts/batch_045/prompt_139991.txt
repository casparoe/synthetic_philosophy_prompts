I'm on the job market in political philosophy, and next month I'm giving a forty-five-minute talk, with a twenty-five-minute question period, at an interdisciplinary research centre for social and behavioural science. The room will be almost entirely non-philosophers: mostly quantitative and cross-cultural psychologists who work with large cross-national survey datasets, a handful of labour and education economists, one evolutionary psychologist who works on mate preferences, and four or five feminist science-studies and gender-studies faculty. The only philosopher present will be the host. The psychologists will care about measurement invariance, effect sizes, and whether the effect replicates; the economists will care about identification, what my counterfactual actually is, and whether I have an estimand at all; the science-studies people will want to know what my argument licenses anyone to do; the evolutionary psychologist will want to know whether I am simply an anti-nativist with better manners.

My thesis has three moves. First: the "gender-equality paradox" — the cross-national pattern in which countries scoring higher on widely used gender-equality indices show *more* sex-segregated educational and occupational outcomes, not less — cannot adjudicate between a "nature" and a "culture" explanation, because those are not rival values of a single parameter. They name different causal pathways and different counterfactuals, and country-level aggregates cannot separate them, so the pattern is evidentially inert with respect to the question everyone reads it as answering. Second: where the pattern is real, I argue that the best explanation is neither innate disposition nor unremediated barriers, but what I call the self-expression channel. As legal exclusion, material constraint and economic necessity recede, occupational choice comes to be experienced and socially legitimated as an expression of authentic identity, and gender — being the most culturally available template for who one "really is" — is recruited to do more work in choice, not less. Gender-egalitarian reform removes obstacles; it does not reveal a pre-social baseline, it hands gender a new job. Third, the normative upshot: if that is right, the site of gender injustice is not the blocked door but the formation of aspiration, and a liberal state committed to self-authorship cannot straightforwardly intervene there without undercutting the very self-authorship it exists to protect.
