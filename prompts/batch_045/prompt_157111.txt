I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for a long time, and the habit I've picked up there that I most want applied to this topic is the demand that a favored theory be cashed out on its hard cases rather than its easy ones. The view I keep half-returning to is constitutive panpsychism: a human mind is not merely realized by but composed out of the consciousness of the ultimate physical parts of the person, so that macro-experience is what you get when many micro-subjects stand in the right relation. Its hard case is the combination problem, and my sense is that both sides in that dispute tend to gesture at a mechanism — the micro-subjects "fuse", the cosmic subject "decombines" — without ever saying what the mechanism would have to do in order to count as an explanation rather than a label. I'd like a dialogue that closes that gap.

Stipulate the following scenario, set a few months after it has become experimentally routine; neither interlocutor is allowed to treat any part of it as impossible:

- A reversible procedure, n-fusion, links the brains of n volunteers so that during the link the system supports what all n later describe as a single stream of consciousness: one "I", one attentional focus, memories that none of them can assign to a particular body.
- After de-fusion there are always exactly n reports, and each reporter says they were the fused subject — not n observers watching one stream from outside.
- The fused subject's phenomenal palette is a blend rather than a union: presented with two different colours, it reports one intermediate colour experience, not two colours experienced together.
- Grain degrades with n. At n = 2 the fused subject reports experience as richly differentiated as any ordinary person's. At n around a thousand it reports something close to a single undifferentiated quality, and its ability to discriminate stimuli behaviourally degrades in step, even though the linked system's information processing has increased.
- A variant links a human to a cortical organoid that, on its own, yields no reports at all; afterwards the human reports a fused stream containing content that only the organoid's inputs could have supplied.
- A complementary fission procedure splits one subject into two, each of whom reports having been the original right up to the moment of splitting.

Requirements:

1. Both speakers are equally competent and argue in their strongest form; neither may be a foil. Each turn must engage a specific claim made in the previous turn rather than restating a position, and each speaker should concede at least one real point.
2. The micropsychist must address the three distinguishable difficulties: why there is one subject rather than n (subject-summing), why the macro-qualities are the ones they are given the micro-qualities (the palette problem), and why experience is not as fine-grained as the micro-level would seem to predict (the grain problem). The cosmopsychist must address the mirror-image difficulty: why the one fundamental conscious subject yields many human subjects rather than one, and why the cosmopsychist is not committed to saying that you and I are the same subject.
3. The stipulations above must function as a genuine stress test, not decoration: both speakers should use them, and neither may escape by declaring the reports illusory without paying a visible theoretical cost or explaining what independent reason there is for the dismissal.
4. No speaker may name, cite, or allude to any philosopher, paper, book, or school by name; every move must be motivated from the scenario and from conceptual reasoning about consciousness, composition, and subjects.
5. The final turn should land on a crux: one further experiment, specified concretely enough that it could in principle be run, whose outcome would differentially confirm one view over the other, with each speaker's prediction stated in advance.

Format: exactly five numbered turns, alternating between the two speakers, with the micropsychist speaking first, each turn between 100 and 150 words. Include no introduction, no conclusion, no epilogue, and no stage directions beyond the speaker labels; the dialogue should read as a transcript of a conversation that was already in progress.
