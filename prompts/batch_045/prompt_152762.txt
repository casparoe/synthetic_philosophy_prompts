Two people in my philosophy-of-science reading group spent most of last night's session going at each other over the problem of old evidence, and I said nothing the whole time because I genuinely couldn't tell who was winning. Can you go through it move by move and tell me who's right about what?

The setup, as I understood it: standard Bayesianism says evidence E confirms hypothesis H only if learning E raises your credence in H, but if you already knew E then P(E) = 1 and P(H|E) = P(H), so nothing can ever be confirmed by evidence you already had. Yet general relativity apparently got a real boost out of explaining the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion, which everybody already knew about. Here's roughly how the argument went:

1. Marco: The problem is fake. When GR explained the perihelion, nobody learned *that* the anomaly occurs — they learned the *link*: that this theory, with no knobs to turn, yields that number. Model that as a proposition L, H derivationally yields E. Beforehand L was improbable; after someone does the calculation L is near certain. Conditionalize on L and P(H) goes up. Old evidence confirms after all, and you don't need any amnesia or counterfactual trickery.

2. Dana: If L is just the material conditional H → E, this is stillborn. You already knew E, and given E, H → E comes out true no matter what H says. So E and (H → E) together are just equivalent to E, which means P(H | E and H → E) = P(H | E) = P(H) when P(E) = 1. Conditionalizing does nothing at all. Your move only works if you refuse to treat the arrow truth-functionally.

3. Marco: Right, and I do refuse. Treat yields as a primitive in an extended algebra: a derivation-link, not a material conditional. Nothing in Bayesianism says every proposition has to be a Boolean compound of H and E. Coherence just requires a coherent probability function over whatever propositions you actually have attitudes toward. So we conditionalize on L and get a real, nonzero boost. Call it the link account.

4. Dana: Then the size of the boost is hostage to a completely unconstrained number. The boost factor is P(L|H)/P(L), and P(L) ≈ P(L|¬H) when P(H) is tiny, so basically the boost is P(L|H)/P(L|¬H). Nothing in the framework pins P(L|¬H) — it isn't tied to how antecedently surprising the datum was, which is the thing that made Mercury impressive in the first place. And links multiply. Slice the anomaly into ten aspects, postulate ten links, and if they're independent given ¬H you get ten multiplicative boosts out of one datum. You haven't solved the old-evidence problem, you've turned it into a bookkeeping problem where confirmation can be inflated by re-carving the evidence.

5. Marco: The slicing worry overreaches. Independence isn't free — if the ten aspects all fall out of the same core of the theory, they're correlated given H and given ¬H, and the product doesn't compound; a well-set-up agent gets roughly one link's worth of boost. And the ratio isn't as unconstrained as you say. P(L|H) ≈ 1, so the boost is roughly 1/P(L|¬H): how unlikely it was that a *false* theory of that kind would reproduce that datum. A theory with adjustable parameters gets a high P(L|¬H) — you can fit anything — so it gets no credit. GR had no free parameters to fit the perihelion, so it got a big boost. That's exactly the verdict we want, and it's the one the amnesia/counterfactual versions can't explain.

6. Dana: But that concedes my point. All the work is now being done by P(L|¬H), which is just the old judgment — how surprising the datum was, from the standpoint of the alternatives — relocated into a prior over a new primitive. So the account hasn't solved anything, it's moved the hard part somewhere darker: a probability over what *false* theories would have derived. What space is that even? Which false theory? Unless you can say how to assign P(L|¬H) without already knowing which rival is true, the number is doing the work while wearing a disguise.

7. Marco: Bayesians relocate substantive judgment into priors all the time; that's not a bug, that's the division of labour. The framework's job isn't to eliminate judgment, it's to say where judgment goes and to get the update right once it's in. And the old-evidence problem wasn't that judgment was hard, it was that judgment did *zero* work no matter what — conditionalization gave you nothing. The link account at least makes it do work, and unlike the counterfactual-amnesia version, it moves your actual credence instead of telling you you're confirmed while your credence sits still.

8. Dana: It moves your credence for the wrong reason, and it won't converge. Work it out: P(H|L) ≈ P(H)/(P(H) + P(L|¬H)), so where you land depends entirely on your own P(L|¬H). Two people who both end up certain of the link land in different places with no pressure to meet, because each link is learned exactly once and is then frozen at 1 forever — there's no stream of data to wash priors out the way there is with genuinely novel evidence. Also note the timing: the boost arrives when somebody checks the algebra. So a theorist who was slow to see the consequences of their own theory gets more confirmation than one who saw them immediately. An account where being confused about your own hypothesis pays is an account with something wrong with it.

9. Marco: Fine, I'll bite on the non-convergence, because I think it's the truth rather than a defect. Agents with different P(L|¬H) disagree about how much room the theory had, and that's a real disagreement, not an error. If we want it to be tractable, constrain P(L|¬H) by how many adjustable parameters the theory brings to that particular datum — then the disagreement becomes an empirical one about how much freedom the theory really had, instead of a shrug.

10. Dana: Parameter-counting is precisely the kind of substantive methodological principle that P(L|¬H) was supposed to be *built* out of, not something you get to bolt on afterwards. Once you're counting parameters and weighing rivals against each other, you're doing model selection — and model selection never had the old-evidence problem in the first place, which suggests the link account was never carrying the load.

Two more things, offered neutrally. A third person in the group muttered something about comparing likelihoods rather than posterior changes and got talked over, and I've been wondering ever since whether that was the thing both of them were circling. And separately: I can't tell whether either account gives the right answer for a theory that was deliberately built to fit data you already had, where there was no surprise at any point.

Please rate each of the ten moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a brief justification for each, say who's more right overall, and say what — if anything — both of them are missing.
