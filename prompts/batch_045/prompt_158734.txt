I'm leading one 90-minute session of a grad reading group next week on the problem of old evidence — about ten people, half philosophy grads and half ML/cognitive-science people who mostly know Bayes from machine learning (priors as regularizers, updating as "seeing more data", evidence as a row in a dataset). Please don't explain the problem to me; I know the shape of it. I want a plan for how to get it across to that room.

Concretely, here's what I need:

1. The order the pieces have to come in and what depends on what. My worry: if I open with "confirmation is probability-raising," half the room will think the puzzle is an arithmetic accident; if I open with "P(E) is already 1, so nothing can be raised, so the framework is broken," the ML people will immediately say "fine, use the likelihood ratio / Bayes factor, the posterior-odds form doesn't need P(E) at all" and feel like they've dissolved it in ten seconds. So where does the Bayes-factor route have to sit in the sequence, and how do I stage it so it lands as a serious answer that then runs out, rather than as a heckle I have to bat away?

2. The one case that should carry the whole session. I was going to use the classic one — the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion and general relativity — but I think it's doing too many jobs at once and quietly hiding the structure: it's a novel prediction, it's quantitative, the datum was already known to be anomalous, and there's a rival theory humming in the background. So: what does my carrying example need to *have*, and what does it need to *lack*, to isolate the thing I'm after? Should I keep Mercury, or swap it for something from ordinary life or from lab/clinical practice where the fact was sitting there on the chart the whole time? Give me the swap if you think it's better, and say what it buys.

3. Two wrinkles I want to fit in somewhere and can't place. (i) Evidence that's old in the sense that everyone knew the fact for years, but whose *relevance* to the hypothesis was only noticed later — the bearing was new, not the fact. (ii) Evidence that's old for the person doing the reasoning but new to the community — so who is the agent whose updating is supposed to have gone wrong? Tell me which of these is a genuine variant of the problem, which is a red herring I should name and drop in one sentence, and where in the sequence each belongs.

4. The misconceptions I should expect, and exactly where in the sequence to head each one off. My guesses: "just ask what you would have thought if you hadn't known it" (the counterfactual-prior move — and I want to know why that move is worse than it sounds, not just that it's contested); "so Bayesianism can't handle being surprised"; "this is just the Duhem/auxiliary-hypothesis thing wearing a hat"; "it's only a problem if your priors are subjective"; "that's about discovery, not confirmation, so who cares." Fix my list if I've got the wrong ones or I'm missing the big one.

5. What I should openly refuse to get into at this level, and say out loud that I'm skipping.

6. One question for the last five minutes that tells me whether they've got the structure rather than the slogan. Ideally I'd hand them a pair: a case where the right verdict is "yes, the old evidence genuinely confirms now," and one that's superficially identical where the right verdict is "no, nothing should move," and make them say what the difference consists in.
