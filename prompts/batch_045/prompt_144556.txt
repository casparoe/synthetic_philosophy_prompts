I am a latecomer to philosophy, having spent thirty-five years as an experimental electrophysiologist — a discipline in which n was small, the noise was not a metaphor, and most of one's education in inference was picked up at the bench from people who were themselves guessing. I have been reading about the replication crisis, and I have run up against a summary of it that I half believe and half distrust. I would be grateful for an audit.

The summary comes from a popular book on why science goes wrong that was given to me last winter. I would rather not name it, since my question concerns the claims rather than the author. As closely as I can paraphrase it:

Null-hypothesis significance testing answers a question nobody asks. It gives the probability of the data assuming the null hypothesis, whereas what a scientist wants — and what, in practice, nearly every scientist takes herself to have obtained — is the probability that her hypothesis is true given the data. These are different quantities, and that single confusion is the engine of the irreproducibility crisis. Since a result below the 0.05 threshold tells one nothing whatever about whether an effect is real, and since an investigator may try any number of analyses and report only the one that succeeded, the published literature is largely a catalogue of flukes: a widely cited modelling exercise concluded that most published findings are false, and when psychologists eventually repeated a hundred-odd celebrated experiments, most of the effects shrank and far fewer than half reappeared. The remedy follows directly. Replace significance testing with Bayesian methods, which deliver the probability that a hypothesis is true and which, being governed by Bayes' rule, are unaffected by the stopping rules and the repeated peeking at data that inflate false positives under the frequentist framework. Add preregistration of hypotheses and analysis plans, which removes the investigator's freedom to choose after seeing the data, and a reported p-value or Bayes factor becomes an honest measure of the evidence. What remains — fabrication and fraud — is rare, is not a statistical matter, and is the only part of the problem that reform cannot touch.

My distrust is not that this is nonsense; it is that several of the moves look too easy, and that the conclusion seems stronger than the premises. But I cannot say where.

Please begin with a single sentence giving your overall verdict, then go through the passage claim by claim and tell me, for each: what a statistician or philosopher of science working on inference would grant, what they would want qualified, and what they would reject. I am especially interested in whether the book's practical conclusion — that a field which adopted Bayesian inference together with preregistration would largely solve its reproducibility problem — survives once the weaker claims are corrected, and in whether the confusions the book identifies are best described as defects of the frequentist method itself or as defects in how the method is used and taught.

One further thing. In my own work, the experiments that failed to repeat were usually not flukes: the effect was reproducibly there in one preparation, one batch of animals, one season, and absent in another, and the difference lay in the biology rather than in the arithmetic. I therefore want to distinguish three sources of irreproducibility — errors of inference, selective reporting and analysis, and the failure of an effect to transport from one preparation, population or setting to another — and to claim that the third is neither rare nor touched by anything in the frequentist-versus-Bayesian dispute. Is that a genuine third category, or only the book's flukes-plus-fraud under another description? And if it is genuine, does it bear on how much of the replication crisis we should expect any reform of statistical practice to remove?

I am comfortable with likelihood ratios, priors and power calculations, so please use them wherever they shorten the argument. I do not want references or a reading list — I can find those myself. Please keep the entire reply under seven hundred words.
