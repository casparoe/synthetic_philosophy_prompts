For several years I've followed an anonymous poster on the EA Forum who works in animal-welfare prioritisation and keeps a public document in which, most weeks, she gives a verdict on one moral-tradeoff hypothetical that readers send in. I've come to trust those verdicts more than my own instincts, largely because reading them has repeatedly exposed an assumption I didn't know I was making, but she has never written down the rule she is applying, and my attempts to reverse-engineer it keep producing two or three candidates that fit her track record about equally well. My giving circle has to allocate a modest pot of money this month, our three options happen to be arranged so that those candidates appear to disagree, and the three of us involved each favour a different option without being able to argue the others out of it. I have a small bet riding on what she would say if I sent the case in, so I want the prediction, not the truth.

Her recorded verdicts, as best I can summarise them. One: asked whether we should fund a programme that would bring about a large number of additional wild-animal lives, each net positive on balance but each containing a fair amount of ordinary wild suffering, she said no, and added that the net positivity of the extra lives counted for nothing on its own. Two: she has said that an instantly painless end to all sentient life would be an improvement, but always hedged, and she has explicitly rested the claim on empirical views about how much suffering the future contains together with her uncertainty about those views; she is firmly against anyone acting on it. Three: she has said more than once that with suffering held fixed, more happiness is better, and that a world with no suffering and a great deal of happiness beats an empty world, and she has denied being a negative utilitarian on exactly this ground while also saying she is much nearer the suffering-focused end of the spectrum than to classical utilitarianism. Four: when the intensities involved are comparable she aggregates straightforwardly, multiplying number of individuals by duration by intensity and discounting by her credence in sentience, then picks the larger product; she has said she sees no reason to care how suffering is distributed across individuals beyond that. Five: at the same time she has repeatedly argued that we should prioritise the worst forms of suffering, naming severe treatment-resistant pain and the ways animals actually die in slaughterhouses and in the wild. Every case in which she has said this, though, has been one where the milder-suffering alternative was something she had independent reasons to discount, whether the durability of mild well-being gains, the quality of the evidence, or the risk of flow-through effects. Six: on uncertainty about sentience she is an expected-value person, giving heavily discounted weights to shrimp and insects and, speculatively, to artificial systems, but showing no aversion to acting on a small probability and no tendency to treat uncertainty as a reason to wait.

Our case, with the usual escape hatches closed: the same money, the same quality of evidence, no flow-through effects on population size or on anything else, and, to take away her favourite move, take the sentience, the intensities and the durations as exactly as stated and every benefit as equally durable. Option X prevents chronic moderate pain, a four on the ten-point scale she uses, for the last year of life of about five million farmed fish each year, and does nothing else. Option Y prevents extreme suffering, a nine on the same scale, lasting about a month, for about two hundred thousand animals each year, and does nothing else. Option Z has no effect on suffering at all; it would raise the well-being of about a hundred million already-existing people by one point on the same scale for a year. I'm much less interested in being told which option is genuinely best than in the following: what rules fit her track record, including rules none of us has thought of; what each of them predicts here; which of her earlier verdicts are actually doing discriminating work and which are consistent with every candidate; what you would put your own money on and at what odds; what else she would be committed to if she chose each option; and which single further hypothetical you would send her, given that she will answer exactly one more.
