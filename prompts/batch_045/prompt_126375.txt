I write policy for a two-sided marketplace — the sort of job where one spends one's days deciding what may be listed, what may be priced, and what must not be traded at any price. To do that with rather more than instinct, I joined a small reading group that has spent the better part of a year on so-called repugnant markets: paid kidney donation, vote selling, commercial surrogacy, ticket touting and price gouging after a disaster. We have got nowhere, and I want a diagnosis of why, not a verdict on who is right.

Three positions, described as fairly as I can manage:

1. The consent camp. If the exchange is informed and voluntary, both parties expect to benefit, and no identifiable third party is made worse off, then prohibiting it is paternalism or moral squeamishness. On this view repugnance is a psychological fact about us rather than a moral reason: the same reaction once attached to lending money at interest and to insuring one's own life, and it is no argument now.

2. The corruption camp. Markets do not merely move goods around; they change what the good is and what it means to have it. Putting a price on a kidney, a vote or a pregnancy re-describes it as the kind of thing one may trade, and that re-description is a loss even where the particular transaction is wanted by both sides. Stronger versions add that such markets make the participants worse people, or that they erode the non-market norms that were doing the allocating.

3. The market-design camp. Repugnance is real, but it is a constraint on which mechanisms are feasible, not a verdict on the practice. A market nobody will use, or which provokes a backlash that destroys the institution it depends on, is simply a badly designed market. The task is to engineer around the constraint; moral argument beyond feasibility is mostly noise.

What frustrates me is this. On the empirical questions we are largely agreed. Nobody in the group believes that prohibition eliminates the trade. Nobody denies that the people who end up selling are mostly poor, and that what is disturbing in the standard case is that poverty is doing the work a gunman would do more honestly. We even agree that the dire predictions have not obviously come true in the one jurisdiction that has long permitted payment for kidneys. Everyone has heard everyone else's examples. Nobody has moved.

My hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis, so tell me if it is wrong — is that "is this market repugnant?" is not one question and that the group is not having one disagreement. I suspect at least four are stacked:

(a) a disagreement about the baseline: whether the relevant comparison is the seller's situation absent the market, or some threshold of decent options they are entitled to;
(b) a disagreement about whether the meaning or expressive content of a practice is a legitimate object of moral assessment at all, as opposed to its effects on welfare;
(c) something close to a verbal dispute, in which "coercion", "exploitation" and "harm" carry different intensions while the extensional agreements stay intact;
(d) a hidden empirical question — whether introducing a market actually erodes the non-market norm, and by how much — which both sides assert and neither side measures.

Two things I would like you to do with this.

First, test the hypothesis against the positions above. Which of (a)–(d) is doing the real work, and does one of them explain the others? In particular, run two probes through each position. Probe one: the vendor who does not need the money — a wealthy person who sells a kidney to fund a holiday, or sells a vote because registering is a nuisance. The coercion and exploitation arguments go quiet here; does the objection survive, and if it does, what exactly is it now an objection to? Probe two: the same sum moving in the same direction without a corresponding transfer of a right — a ban on buying kidneys combined with generous, unconditional compensation for donors' expenses, or paying people to stay at home on polling day. If the objection is to money touching the good, both should be objectionable; if it is to the transfer of control, only one is. I want to know whether each camp's answer to these probes is a considered application of its view or an ad hoc patch, and what the pattern of answers reveals about the structure of the disagreement.

Second, apply your diagnosis to a case my working group actually has to decide, and which the standard examples do not obviously settle: paying people for the right to train models on their face, voice and gait, structured as an ongoing per-use royalty rather than a one-off fee. Depending on how you tilt it, this looks like labour, like a body product, or like personal data, and I suspect that is precisely why it is hard. On your diagnosis, predict how the three camps will line up, and say whether it will deadlock in the same pattern or whether something about the case breaks the stalemate.

Please write in British English, roughly 1,200 words, addressed to someone with no formal philosophy training: define terms such as commodification, crowding out and expressive objection when you use them, and omit citations entirely — I will add those myself and would rather have the argument. What I am after is the structure of the disagreement, supported by what each side actually says, together with an honest account of what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a piece of evidence, a reframing of the question — or an argument that no progress should be expected and why.
