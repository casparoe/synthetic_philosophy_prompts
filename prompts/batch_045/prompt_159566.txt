After four decades as an experimental physicist, I have lately been reading philosophy of science in retirement, and I have become convinced that one debate in it has stalled badly, though I cannot yet say why. The debate concerns whether thought experiments in the natural sciences can yield genuine knowledge about nature, or whether they merely make explicit knowledge we already possessed.

One camp—which I shall call the amplificationists—holds that a well-constructed thought experiment can put us in cognitive contact with modal or nomic structure. On this view, by imagining a heavy body and a light body tied together and asked to fall, we can see that the composite cannot fall faster than the heavy body alone, and thereby learn something about free fall that was not itself a report of prior observation. The amplificationist also stresses that thought experiments sometimes appear to decide between theories that accommodated all the available data equally well, which they take to show that the imagination is doing more than unpacking old evidence.

The opposing camp—the deflationists—holds that a scientific thought experiment is nothing but an argument, usually narratively or diagrammatically presented. Its conclusion is already supported by background theory together with previously accepted empirical assumptions, so the sense of novelty is at most psychological, and any apparent power to break ties is really the power of a hidden auxiliary assumption in the reconstruction.

What frustrates me is that the two camps agree on the verdict in every canonical case I know. They agree that Galileo's tied-body reasoning undermines the Aristotelian claim that speed of fall increases with weight; they agree that Einstein's elevator supports treating gravitation and acceleration as locally indistinguishable; they agree that Maxwell's demon does not circumvent the second law, because the acquisition and erasure of information carries a physical cost. Yet they draw opposite lessons. The amplificationist says that pure thought has taught us something about nature. The deflationist replies that the conclusion was already implicit and that the thought experiment has only unpacked it.

When I follow the dialectic, it appears circular. Give the deflationist a case where the result feels surprising, and they produce a step-by-step reconstruction from background theory. The amplificationist objects that the reconstruction has quietly assumed the very modal or counterfactual judgment at issue, typically by selecting a model or an idealization that already encodes the outcome. The deflationist answers that this is simply how theory-mediated inference works and that no quasi-perceptual access to laws is needed. At that point the exchange stops.

My own attempt to break the loop has been to classify scientific thought experiments by what I call their input profile. The first class comprises data-preserving rearrangements: the scenario recombines familiar phenomena in a new way, as in Galileo's tied bodies. The second comprises idealizing suspensions: the scenario suspends or perfects a law-like regularity—frictionless planes, perfectly rigid rods, point masses—in order to isolate a dependency. The third comprises extrapolative parameter shifts: the scenario moves a well-confirmed parameter or initial condition into a range never observed, as when one imagines a universe in which the gravitational constant is marginally larger, or a Maxwellian demon equipped with a memory of strictly finite capacity.

My hypothesis is that the stalemate is an artifact of both sides having treated the first two classes as paradigmatic. Those cases really are relatively easy to model as arguments or as theory-mediated inferences, so the deflationist looks strong. But extrapolative parameter shifts seem different: they appear to license expectations about what would be observed under conditions no one has experienced, and it is much less clear either that the result is already contained in actual data or that we are perceiving an actual law. If the debate were reframed around extrapolative parameter shifts, I suspect it would turn out to be either a verbal dispute about the word knowledge or a substantive dispute about whether well-confirmed theories can carry inductive weight into untested parameter spaces.

I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is the long-running disagreement between amplificationists and deflationists best understood as verbal, a clash about how to use terms such as new knowledge or empirical? Is it substantive, a genuine disagreement about whether the mind has a priori or quasi-perceptual access to modal structure, or about whether counterfactual robustness can itself be evidence? Are the two sides perhaps answering different questions under one heading, one asking how scientists psychologically arrive at new beliefs and the other asking how those beliefs are justified? Or is a hidden formal or empirical question doing the work—about the semantics of counterfactuals, the logic of idealizations, or the conditions under which model extrapolation counts as confirmation?

Please ground the diagnosis in the actual dialectical moves each side makes, rather than in a survey of famous thought experiments, and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a sharper distinction among kinds of thought experiments, a formal criterion for when a deflationary reconstruction is question-begging, a reconstrual of thought experiments as model-based simulations, a proposal to change the question, or a reasoned argument that no resolution should be expected and why.
