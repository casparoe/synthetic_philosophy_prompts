I'm in a final-year seminar on explanation in the human sciences. We spent the first half of the term on the covering-law picture of historical explanation and on the familiar lines of resistance to it — that historical explanations are usually answers to contrastive why-questions rather than deductions from laws, that what looks like a law in history is either trivially true or false, and that a great deal of what historians actually do is make an outcome intelligible by exhibiting it as what a situated agent had reason to do. The second half has been on narrativist alternatives, on the thought that the shape of the story is doing explanatory work rather than merely packaging deductions. I'd like to write my final paper on a case rather than on the literature, and the case I keep returning to is Tolstoy's *War and Peace*, because it is a novel that seems to contain, in its own discursive chapters, an argument in exactly this area: Tolstoy accuses the historians of his day of explaining the 1812 campaigns by citing Napoleon's will and then insists that what looks like a decision is really the resultant of innumerable individually negligible human motions, which he thinks should be handled the way the infinitesimal calculus handles vanishingly small quantities.

Before I commit to it I want a reading that takes both sides seriously and tells me where I'm flatly wrong. I've been toying with a rough taxonomy of my own and would like it stress-tested against the book: call an explanation law-shaped when its force comes from subsuming the event under a pattern that would also cover other events; narrative-shaped when its force comes from making an outcome read as the intelligible continuation of a sequence, so that the reader's sense that it could not have gone otherwise is the whole mechanism; and aggregative when it works not by adding a law or a story but by changing the grain of description — swapping individuals, decisions and battles for smaller or coarser units, and redescribing the outcome at that new resolution. My suspicion is that Tolstoy's positive proposal is aggregative rather than narrativist, that his polemic against the historians is really a complaint about the grain at which they pitch their causal claims, and that the novel's own form — which is nothing if not a narrative, and a heavily shaped one — is in tension with the argument it contains. I also keep worrying about a test I half-invented: that a narrative explanation is trustworthy only if it would still look like an explanation read forward from the evidence rather than backward from the outcome, and that novels are structurally incapable of being read that way.

So, concretely: where should the paper actually linger? I have in mind the chapters at Borodino in which Pierre goes onto the field looking for the moment where something is decided and cannot locate it; the abandonment and burning of Moscow, and the way Russian and French accounts of whose act it was diverge; the council at Fili, where the novel withholds almost everything about how Kutuzov reaches his conclusion; and the argumentative chapters of the epilogue where Tolstoy intervenes in his own voice. Are there scenes I'm neglecting that carry more of the philosophical weight? And the part I care most about: does the novel contribute anything to the covering-law-versus-narrative dispute that the articles don't? My sense is that it dramatizes a cost of each option that the literature states abstractly — that law-shaped explanation buys generality by dropping out everything anyone involved cared about, and narrative-shaped explanation buys intelligibility at the price of a tidiness that no quantity of evidence can distinguish from hindsight — and I want to know whether that is a genuine contribution the book makes, or whether I am just projecting my seminar notes onto a famous novel. Please be blunt about where the reading fails.
