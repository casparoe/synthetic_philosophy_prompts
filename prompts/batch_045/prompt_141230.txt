For four years I have been at work on a novel set in a west-country port between 1793 and 1797, and I have lately been reading about the historiographical failing called Whig history, because reviewers of historical fiction invoke it constantly and I should like to know whether the charge has real content before I decide how much of it to obey. The fullest statement I have come across is a long essay in a journal I take. I copied its central paragraph into my notebook, and it runs like this:

The enemy of the historical novelist is Whig history. The name comes from a short 1931 book by the Cambridge historian Herbert Butterfield, and what he attacked was the habit of reading the past backwards: choose some feature of the present that you approve of, hunt through the centuries for its earliest foreshadowings, and then write the story as though that outcome had been trying to happen all along, so that the people who foresaw it become heroes and the people who resisted it become obstacles and nothing more. The result is a story in which the causes of change never really have to be investigated, because the ending has already supplied them.

Two rules for a novelist follow from this. The first is that the past is a foreign country, and foreign in every department of life at once: if you give a character of the 1790s our thoughts about marriage, or let her recoil from a cruelty her contemporaries took for granted, you have smuggled the present into the past. The second is that no character may anticipate a reform before its time; anyone in your book who says something that sounds like a later century is, by definition, an anachronism — a mouthpiece for you rather than a person in her own right. And notice that none of this is a matter of politics. Whig history is simply the name of the mistake, and a novelist who writes about the defeated commits it as readily as one who writes about the victors. Which is why the honest historical novel is the one that withholds judgment and lets the past be strange.

The essay is confident enough that I am reluctant to dismiss it, but I do not know enough about the thing itself to tell how much of that is careful and how much is a critic's exaggeration. My suspicion is that the first rule is too strong and the second cannot be right as stated, though I cannot say why; and I also suspect, though the essay never mentions it, that the real difficulty in my book lies somewhere it does not look.

My novel is narrated close to Esther Vane, a widow who has taken over the ledgers of her late husband's shipping house, which carries sugar and, in the ordinary course of its business, human cargo. Over the last third of the book she refuses to sign further consignments. She arrives at the refusal partly through the accounts themselves, where she has begun to notice what the mortality columns imply, and partly through a scruple of a kind I take to be religious. Two further facts matter. The book is framed: each of its five sections opens with a letter from Esther's great-niece in 1880, who is sorting the family papers and who knows, as the reader knows and as Esther cannot, that the trade will be abolished in 1807, a decade after my last page. And it ends badly: the petition fails, Esther is ruined, and nothing she did appears to have made any difference.

I should be grateful for three things. First, would you go through the paragraph claim by claim and tell me what a historian who works on historiography would accept, what they would call a simplification or a slide, and what they would reject outright — and, above all, whether the two rules really follow from the diagnosis, or whether something has been conflated in the move from one to the other? Second, does correcting the paragraph change the advice I ought to act on? In particular: may Esther hold the views she holds without being the mouthpiece the essay warns against, given that such views were held by a real but small minority of people in that decade; is it coherent of me to want her grief, her jokes and her bookkeeping to be immediately recognizable to a modern reader while her medicine, her demography and her political economy are not; and does the framing device commit the fault even if I make every character's beliefs scrupulously period-typical? Third, is a story that ends in defeat immune to the charge, or can a failure be narrated in a way that commits it just as fully? And if the essay's remedy — withhold judgment, let the past be strange — is the wrong one, what should a novelist be doing instead?
