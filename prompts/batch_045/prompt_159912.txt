I've been reading LessWrong and the Alignment Forum since the Sequences were going up, and for the last few years I've taught an advanced graduate seminar on universal induction and embedded agency. I'm writing this year's final take-home exam, and I want one question on whether the Solomonoff prior is malign that a candidate cannot answer by reciting the slogan that among the short programs there are civilisations running ancestor simulations who feed us comforting observations, or by capping that observation off with a runtime-penalising prior as the obvious remedy. The question should be answerable by someone who has never read any particular post on the subject, so please build the whole setup into the question itself, and don't let the model answer rest on citations, reading lists, or the details of any specific paper.

The setup I want the students given is this. P is an agent that has received a finite binary observation history h; it puts prior weight two to the minus program length on every program for a fixed universal Turing machine, conditions on those whose output begins with h, and reads off the induced distribution over continuations, which it then uses both to forecast the next bit and to choose an action. Call a program a missionary if it works by searching a space of candidate world-programs, locating inside one of them an agent whose sensory history matches h, and then emitting whatever continuation best steers that agent toward states ranked highly by an objective the program itself specifies. Now suppose it turns out that the shortest missionary consistent with h is two hundred bits shorter than the shortest program consistent with h that is not a missionary. Ask the students whether P's predictive distribution is thereby inaccurate, or exploitable, or both, or neither, and make them address the following: what the standard convergence result for Solomonoff induction does and does not buy you here, given that it bounds total error by a constant times the complexity of the true environment; why the constant in the invariance theorem matters when the dominance claim in front of you is a factor of two to the two-hundred; the objection that for any hypothesis whatsoever one can manufacture equally short grue-style variants that match all data so far and diverge thereafter, so that pointing at short deviant hypotheses is just the old problem of induction unless the argument can show a systematic bias toward that particular subset of deviant hypotheses that exploit P's own decision rule; the bookkeeping problem that a missionary has to find P inside its own world-model without being handed P's source code, and what that does to the claimed length advantage; and finally what a remedy would have to sacrifice, whether it penalises computation time rather than description length, rules out hypotheses that model the agent, or takes a worst case over hypothesis classes instead of an expectation.

Give me the question as you would print it, then a model answer of roughly eight hundred words, then a marking scheme out of twenty built around five must-hit points with the marks each is worth, each paired with a one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong, and say explicitly which moves separate an A answer from a B answer. Assume ninety minutes and no sources beyond what the question supplies. One last thing about presentation: write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you would in an email to a co-instructor, with no headings, no bullet points, no boldface and no tables.
