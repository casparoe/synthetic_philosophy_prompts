So I fell into a reading group on Wittgenstein's On Certainty a few months ago (a friend dragged me, I have no philosophy background, I work in logistics) and I've been chewing on one thing ever since and it's now keeping me up at night.

The bit I absorbed, as best I can tell: when you ask someone how they know something, they give a reason; you can ask for a reason for the reason; and that has to stop somewhere. Where it stops, you don't get another proposition underneath — you get something that isn't grounded at all, something we just hold fast to in the way we act. The group calls these hinges: things like that the earth was around long before I was born, that objects keep existing when nobody's looking, that my senses are broadly reliable. Apparently the standard line is that hinges aren't things we *know* and maybe not even things we *believe* in the ordinary sense — they're more like the scaffolding that makes doubting and checking possible in the first place, so they can't themselves be doubted or confirmed from inside. And the point of the whole thing is supposed to be anti-skeptical: the skeptic's doubt isn't a real doubt because real doubt needs grounds, and at the bottom there aren't any.

Here's the argument I built out of that, which I now think proves way too much and I can't find the seam.

(1) Requests for reasons have to terminate. Where they terminate, the thing you've reached isn't supported by any further proposition.

(2) If a claim isn't supported by any further proposition, then there is nothing to rationally assess it against, because rationally assessing a claim just is checking it against grounds.

(3) So hinges cannot be rationally assessed. Call this the arationality claim.

(4) Every one of my beliefs sits inside a framework, and the framework is held in place by hinges.

(5) So whatever support any belief of mine has, it has it *internally* to that framework — reasons are only reasons relative to a scaffolding that isn't itself up for assessment.

(6) If the scaffolding can't be rationally assessed, then no evidence, argument, or experience could ever rationally *require* me to revise a belief that belongs to the scaffolding.

(7) Therefore nobody has ever rationally revised a framework-level belief. People have been *caused* to change such beliefs — by evidence, by exhaustion, by new friends, by getting older — but never rationally required to, and their earlier framework-level belief was never rationally criticizable either.

(8) But that's nuts. Take someone raised in a young-earth household who studies geology for six years and gives it up, or someone who leaves a conspiracy community after checking a few claims. We don't just want to say their brain changed; we want to say they were rationally required to, or at least that the old view was rationally criticizable and they were responding to reasons.

(9) So one of (1) through (6) has to go, and I can't tell which, and I don't like any of the options.

The version that really bugs me: if (3) is true, then my neighbour who thinks the earth is flat isn't making a rational error at all — his scaffolding is just different from mine, and neither is assessable. That turns a view that was sold to me as a cure for skepticism into straight-up relativism, which is the opposite of what the reading group said it was for.

My own attempt at a fix, which I made up and don't know if it's a thing: I want to say there are fast hinges and slow hinges, and slow hinges get *wear-tested*. A framework that keeps generating failed predictions, or that can't be taught to newcomers without constant patching, gets worn down over time, and that wearing-down is a kind of rational assessment even though there's no single argument that does it. So (2) is too strong: something can be answerable to reasons over time while not being answerable to any reason at any given moment.

But I'm worried my fix just quietly turns hinge epistemology into pragmatism or coherentism wearing a hat, and makes hinges into nothing more than very stubborn ordinary beliefs — in which case I've thrown out the whole idea that there's a special category down there.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting each one actually cost me? I'd most like to keep (1) and (8) — the regress has to stop somewhere, and people genuinely do rationally change their minds about deep stuff — so if you make me pick I'd rather give up (2), (3), (4) or (6) than those. Say why the step you reject looked fine going in, and price the alternatives rather than just naming one.

One more thing please: I can't tell what's agreed and what's fought over here. Our group talks as if the whole hinge framework is settled, and I definately can't seperate that from the parts people who actually work on this are still arguing about — like whether hinges are beliefs at all, whether they can be known, whether the view collapses into relativism or externalism or just shrugs. Please mark clearly which claims in your answer are the standard machinery and which are genuinely contested among people who know the literature, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus one.
