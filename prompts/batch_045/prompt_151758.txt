Write a dialogue of roughly 800 words between two philosophers of mind who accept Hofstadter's central move — that a self is not an extra ingredient in a system but a symbol the system builds that refers to the whole system containing it — and who disagree about what else has to be true for such a symbol to amount to a self.

Nadia holds the loop condition: what matters is the topology of the reference. A self-symbol must be caught in a tangle in which the system's representing itself perturbs what is represented, so that no fixed level settles where the symbol bottoms out. Selfhood then comes in degrees with the depth and density of the tangle, and it is the recursion, not any downstream effect, that does the work.

Alec holds the office condition: a self-symbol must occupy a functional office. It is what the system consults when it asks what to do, and its states must make a difference to action selection over the long run. Self-description that never returns to the machinery of action is autobiography, not selfhood, however tangled it is.

Have them contest the same three cases, which they introduce themselves as the dialogue needs them:

1. The Commentator — a system whose actions are produced by a planner, and which separately maintains a continuously updated first-person narration in which its self-symbol denotes the whole system; the narration is causally downstream of the planner and never feeds back into it.
2. The Feedback — the same architecture with the narration injected as input to the planner at each step. Behaviour changes only on long-horizon tasks, where the unaugmented planner drifts off its own earlier commitments.
3. The Lag — as in the Feedback, except the planner always receives the narration composed one episode earlier, so the self-symbol consistently denotes a snapshot of the system as it was at the outset of the previous episode.

Requirements. Both characters must be equally competent: each gets the strongest version of her or his own view and the most demanding version of the other's, and each must answer the point actually made rather than a nearby one. Neither may escape by saying it depends what we mean by self; the disagreement should still be live after all three cases. For each case, each must commit to whether there is a self there and, if so, how much. At some point one of them must be driven to a consequence about a human analogue that she or he would rather avoid — a person with dense amnesia of the kind that destroys episodic self-narrative while leaving effective agency intact, or a person with an elaborate, continuously updated self-narrative that makes no difference to what she does. Let them notice the unwanted commitment and respond to it without wriggling out of it. Close with a single sentence each on what would settle the dispute, and let the two answers be different kinds of thing — one an empirical result, one a conceptual argument — without adjudicating which kind is the right one.
