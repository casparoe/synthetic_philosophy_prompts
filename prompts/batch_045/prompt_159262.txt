I edit a short humor feature that runs in the back pages of a philosophy-adjacent magazine — genre parody rather than stand-up, aimed at readers who took one metaphysics course, remember the puzzle about whether an all-powerful being can make a stone too heavy for it to lift, and remember roughly how the discussion went without remembering any names. I'd like a piece on the omnipotence paradox written as a government procurement document: a Request for Proposals, solicitation number and all, issued by an invented body — the Interfaith Board of Creational Standards, or whatever better name you come up with — seeking a contractor to supply and install an omnipotent being. Format it as numbered sections and clauses in the deadpan register of a real solicitation: synopsis, statement of work, mandatory pass/fail minimum qualifications, scored evaluation factors, a compensation clause, a questions-and-answers amendment in which anonymous bidders object and a contracting officer answers with increasingly baroque distinctions, and a notice of intent. Around 800 to 1,000 words.

The jokes should come out of the material rather than out of generic bureaucracy gags, so please build them from these. The scope of work has to define omnipotence in one sentence and then immediately begin issuing carve-outs, and the carve-outs should be the ones the discussion has actually reached for: the power to do anything logically possible, with contradictions reclassified as not being tasks at all; the claim that "everything" is not a determinate totality, so the specification has to be given second-order — anything anyone else can do, anything it is possible for anyone to do; and above all the split between tasks whose description makes no reference to the contractor (make a stone, part a sea, bring a universe into existence) and tasks that are essentially self-referential (make something you did not make, make a rule you cannot later repeal, surrender your own power, bring it about that you are not omnipotent, be surpassed, do wrong, forget something, be surprised). Have the Board visibly try to have it both ways: list the self-referential tasks as pass/fail minimums and then disqualify the only bidders who claim to meet them. Add a notice of intent to award to a single vendor on the grounds that if two beings both satisfied the standard, each would be unable to defeat the other and so neither would be omnipotent — and have the Board notice this and try to write around it. Require three past-performance references from previously created worlds while also asserting, in the evaluation section, that the Contractor can always do better than it in fact does, so that any record of what it has done understates what it can do and past performance becomes unrated; then, in the compensation clause, note that the successful bidder lacks nothing and therefore cannot be paid, leaving the contract value at zero or the consideration as worship and gratitude, which the Board's ethics officer flags. Include one bidder question about whether making a contradiction true counts as a capability or as an exclusion, with a contracting officer's answer that stays noncommittal in the way that is funny to a reader who knows both answers have been defended. Set the clause forbidding subcontracting — all work to be performed personally by the Contractor — next to the standard small-business subcontracting goal.

Two things to hold the piece together. Stay deadpan: no winking at the reader, no footnotes, no character saying outright that the specification is incoherent, and no explanation of the puzzle in the document's own voice — a reader should be able to tell what is being ironized from the document itself. The target is the practice of stipulative repair: every definition of omnipotence gets quietly renegotiated the moment a task breaks it, the renegotiations never settle, and the Board keeps scoring bidders against a standard it keeps rewriting. And please don't name real philosophers, cite anything, or hand the reader a solution; if you want a moment where the logic visibly runs out, give it to a bidder who is about to be disqualified for failing a requirement no one can state coherently.
