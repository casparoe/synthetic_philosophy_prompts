I'm in an upper-division seminar on language, power, and social categories, and we just spent a week on Hofstadter's "Person Paper" — the piece that grew out of his Scientific American column on default assumptions and became the chapter on default words and images in *Metamagical Themas*. As I read it, the device is a transposition: he runs a perfectly straight-faced campaign against the generic use of a racial default term, in a world where the unmarked word for a human being is the white/default one, the marked counterpart is the race-specific one, and the reformers want *person* everywhere — including inside the stock phrase that gives the piece its title. The target, of course, is the parallel campaign against generic *man*.

In section, the analogy underneath the satire — generic *man* is to *woman* as generic *white* is to *black* — got used to do real argumentative work rather than just to provoke. Our TA used it to close off discussion: the racial case is morally settled, the structure is identical, so the gender case inherits the same verdict at the same strength, which makes reform not a matter of politeness but of obligation, and puts anyone who resists in the position of the satire's comic defender of the default. I want the analogy audited rather than enlisted, and I'd rather have the audit than a verdict.

So: lay out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the unmarked term, the marked term, the markedness asymmetry (the way the marked word has to carry specificity, as in female or Black versions of professional titles), the proposed neutral replacement, the mechanism by which the default is supposed to do its damage, the demographic share of the marked group, the history behind it, and the remedy. Then separate the places the mapping breaks into ones that matter for the conclusion the TA wanted and ones that don't.

Three things I especially want addressed. First, which features of the racial case the analogy quietly imports into the gender case: a numerical asymmetry between default and marked group; a history of legally enforced separation and explicit derogation; and — the one I keep getting stuck on — the availability of a semantically clean substitute. Second, a wrinkle I raised and got nowhere with: the marked and unmarked groups in the gender case are not merely co-present but domestically and reproductively entangled, and the terms are acquired inside households that usually contain both. It isn't obvious that an unmarked default does the same kind of cognitive or social work when the marked category is roughly half of every speaker's daily population as when it names a segregated minority. Say whether that tells against the analogy or against the moral claim it is used to support. Third, whether the satire is arguing or borrowing: if the reader already finds racial defaults appalling, the parallel transfers the appalled reaction; but then nothing in the piece seems to stop a reader who thinks gendered generics are harmless from biting the bullet on both cases symmetrically. If that's right, say what follows about what the piece can actually establish.

And then tell me whether the strong conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole time.
