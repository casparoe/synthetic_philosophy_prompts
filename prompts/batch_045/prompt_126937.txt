I am a novelist, and the book I am currently revising is narrated in the first person by a man whose brain has been removed from his skull and kept alive in a vat, while his body walks about elsewhere under radio control — the situation Dennett builds in his essay "Where Am I?", though in my version the surgeons work in one city and the evacuated body is flown to another, and the link is eventually severed, leaving my narrator conscious and sensorily cut off in the first place while his body goes on blundering around in the second. Everything in the novel depends on the reader grasping, as he does, that he is the thing in the vat and not the thing in the hotel. A philosopher who read an early draft has sent me an argument which, she says without apparent discomfort, shows that no first-person narrator has ever referred to anything at all. I have spent two years inside this man's head and I cannot find where the thing goes wrong, so I am laying it out as I now understand it. (1) My narrator is whatever is the subject of the experiences he narrates. (2) What makes a physical system the subject of a given stream of experiences is entirely a matter of its causal-functional organisation — what feeds it, what it does with what it is fed, what it sends back out — together with whatever continuity of causal history is required for those experiences to be continuations of the ones the man had before the operation. (3) Now my own plot twist: the villain, wanting insurance, brings online a second, cell-for-cell duplicate of my narrator's brain in a vat in the room next door, fed an identical input stream, so that from that moment there are two systems with the same organisation and the same apparent past, each convinced that it is the man in the vat, and each from the inside unable to tell which of the two it is. (4) By (2), whatever makes the original the subject of the narrator's experiences equally makes the duplicate a subject of experiences that are, in every respect reachable from the inside, the same; and there is nothing left over — no intrinsic property that the one has and the other lacks, no relation in which the original stands to its own experiences that the duplicate does not stand to its own — in virtue of which the original rather than the duplicate could be singled out as the narrator. (5) So once the duplicate is online, there is no fact of the matter whether the narrator is the original or the copy. (6) But the coming online of the copy changes nothing about the original: it loses no property, gains none, and its experiences are not causally touched by what happens next door. (7) Hence even in a world with no duplicate — the ordinary case, mine and yours — nothing intrinsic to a person settles which subject he is; what does the work, if anything does, is the bare absence of a rival, which is a fact about how the rest of the universe happens to be arranged. (8) So my narrator's conviction that he is this one, and not merely the sole candidate, is unfounded: had he been the duplicate he would have been exactly as convinced, and for all he can tell he is.

I would rather keep (1), (3) and (6) than accept (8), and I would rather not give up (2) either, since without it I do not see what in the ordinary, unoperated case makes a brain the home of a person. So: which step fails, and why did it look sound when I first wrote it down? I want more than the name of a position. I have been turning over four different escapes and I cannot price any of them. One is to say that there is a non-qualitative fact of which thing this is — a primitive this-ness — that no amount of duplication reproduces, in which case I need to know what it would be for my narrator to be wrong about it and whether it can be anything a physical system could have. A second is to accept (5) and (7) and treat identity as constitutively a matter of there being no competitor, which I take it means that once my villain's duplicate is online my narrator is, strictly, nobody at all — a result that would end the book, and not in a way I can use. A third is to insist that (4) is confused because experiences are individuated by their subjects, so that the duplicate's qualitatively identical stream is simply a different stream and there is no role of being the subject of these very experiences for two candidates to compete over — in which case I need to know what the dispute between the two vats was ever about. A fourth is to bite down on (8) and say that first-person reference never did latch onto a further fact and that the narrator's sense of being someone in particular was always a useful fiction. Tell me which of these, if any, is the real diagnosis, what each of the others costs the person who takes it, and whether my duplication twist has smuggled in something Dennett's own case does not contain — that is, whether I have quietly written a book about fission when I meant to write one about location. Finally, whatever you conclude, give me a working model of what the first-person pronoun latches onto that a novelist could actually write two hundred pages in without cheating.
