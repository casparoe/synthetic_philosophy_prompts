I'm finishing a dissertation chapter on the ethics of influence — specifically on where the line between persuasion and manipulation should be drawn — and there is a pattern I keep running into that I can't get the available vocabulary to fit. I'd be grateful for help placing it. Three instances. (1) A colleague chairs a hiring committee; she is privately convinced on good evidence that one candidate is stronger than the other, and she also knows this committee reliably grows careless after the fourth file of the day, so she orders the agenda accordingly and, in each discussion, raises her objections in the sequence most likely to land given what has already been said out loud. Nothing she says is false, nothing she would retract under challenge, and the objections are ones the committee would grant on reflection; asked directly, she would cheerfully explain her method. (2) A friend who wants me to leave a job I complain about constantly waits for the end of a bad fortnight before laying out — truthfully, with evidence — the considerations against staying. (3) The case that started the chapter: an assistant app I have asked to help me keep resolutions I made myself, about meetings and about my phone after ten at night. It has a fine-grained model of which of my own stated reasons, in which order, at which hour, actually moves me; it never invents a consideration, never lies, and cites only reasons I have endorsed — but it selects among them by predicted causal punch rather than by how much they bear on the question, and I find that I experience this as being handled, even though the decisions it steers me into are ones I endorse afterward and would make again.

What I want is a carving, not a label, and I should say what I think it is not. It isn't a nudge in the usual sense, since reasons are being given and deliberation is engaged rather than bypassed, and nudges tend to be characterized precisely by working around the target's reasoning. It isn't the true-but-misleading case, since no false impression is aimed at and nothing is left out that the target would need. It isn't indifference to truth, since these speakers care about getting things right and would pay costs for accuracy. It isn't covertness or pressure or the wearing-down of repeated requests. And the account of manipulation I keep meeting in the literature — roughly, that manipulation is influence that travels by non-rational routes, by routes that would not survive idealizing the target's reasoning — seems to let all three cases off, because in each of them the influence runs straight through considerations the target accepts as reasons. My supervisor thinks the literature is right and that what I am calling being handled is just competence in argument plus self-knowledge about one's audience, and I don't yet know how to answer her.

My own hypothesis is that the morally loaded difference is between timing and ordering chosen to fit the target's capacity to appreciate the reasons — the teacher who waits until you have slept before raising the hard point — and timing and ordering chosen to fit the target's predictable vulnerabilities so that the reasons land harder than they deserve. Is that the right joint to cut at? If it is, does the phenomenon already have a name in ethics, rhetoric, or psychology? A friend in classics tells me the rhetorical tradition has categories both for timeliness and for the arrangement of arguments, which raises the possibility that this is a named rhetorical virtue and that my worry is really about whether such a virtue can be exercised viciously. If there is no established concept, please coin one, define it tightly enough to separate it from nudging, framing, one-sided advice, and ordinary rhetorical skill, and give me boundary cases: the speaker with mixed motives, the speaker who genuinely does not know which consideration is the strongest, the case where the target has delegated the influencing to the influencer in advance, and the case where the target's best epistemic condition just is their most suggestive moment. Please also tell me plainly whether you think these cases are genuinely manipulative or merely persuasion done well, and — since I'll be defending this in a workshop — state how confident you are in each thing you claim and single out the one claim you are least sure of.
