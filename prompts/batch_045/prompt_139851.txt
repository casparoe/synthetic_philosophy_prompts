Hi — I write novels (two out, a third due in the spring, badly behind on it) and I've gone and agreed to run a 90-minute session at a small writers' festival next month. About 12–15 adults in the room, mostly fiction, a couple of poets who wandered in, nobody with any philosophy. I get to pick the topic, and I'd rather use the slot to work out a problem in my own book than stand at the front teaching anything.

The book: two sisters, raised apart, identical in every way I'm able to put on the page — same history as far as anyone in the story can establish, same everything. Halfway through, one of them takes the other's place and nobody notices. My beta readers keep saying the same thing: if there's genuinely no difference between them, it can't matter which one is which, so why should anyone care who's still standing in the last chapter? And I have this stubborn feeling that it does matter — that there's a fact about whose body is walking around on the final page even though no sentence in the book settles it. I'm told this is basically the fight over the identity of indiscernibles, and the two-spheres counterexample of Black's, and Ive been poking around in it a bit. Someone described a move where two things count as told apart by a relation that runs both ways between them, so neither has a property the other lacks — I half get it and half don't, and I'd like the room to hit that too.

So: design me a 90-minute session that makes those writers feel the pull of both sides, and then feel the gap between them. Constraints:

- They're writers, so build it out of writing and reading aloud, not discussion questions. No lecture up front, and nobody hears the term identity of indiscernibles, or haecceity, or qualitative, until the last ten minutes if at all.
- There has to be a moment where the group has to act as though it matters which of two indistinguishable things is which — a forced choice, a bet, something with small real stakes (one text gets read out and one gets torn up, a chocolate, whatever) — and then can't say what the difference was supposed to be.
- There has to be a moment where they try to produce a difference themselves and discover that everything they come up with is either relational or collapses under a swap.
- Give me a timeline in minutes, a materials list I can carry in a bag, and the actual wording of anything I have to read out loud.
- The debrief should map what happened at each stage onto the parts of the argument, including why it's supposed to matter that in the sphere version there's nothing else in the universe (I think it matters, I don't know why), and the move from what anyone can detect to what's even possible.
- Be straight with me about what a room full of writers doing an exercise can't reproduce and I'll just have to say out loud. My own worry: the exercise can only make them unable to tell two things apart, whereas the philosophers seem to be claiming there's nothing there to tell. Can that gap be closed in the room, or do I name it and move on?
- And tell me what will predictably go wrong — the person who says the molecules are in different places, the poet who refuses to play, the ones who treat it as a puzzle with a right answer.

Last thing: don't tell me how to fix my sisters. Just tell me what the room's reaction would tell me about whether the thing I want is even available to me.
