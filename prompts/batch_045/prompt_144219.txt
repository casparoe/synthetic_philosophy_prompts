Hi — I'm a PhD student in philosophy (social epistemology and decision theory, mostly) and I'm the student rep on the small-grants committee of a mid-sized UK health research funder. Sorry, this is a long one, but the details are the whole point.

Our standing rules let each of the five trustees commit up to £25k from the discretionary window without consulting the rest of us, unless the decision is one the Board would reasonably wish to make together. Nobody has ever defined that second bit, so we've been improvising, and I've been keeping a list of what we've actually done. Verdicts are either allowed solo or must go to a full vote:

1. £18k pilot of a community health-worker stipend in a district where two other funders are already running near-identical pilots. Modest upside, downside is basically just wasted money, nobody gets hurt. — allowed solo.

2. £20k to a group that wants to publish a dataset on transmissibility markers of a respiratory pathogen. Big upside if it helps preparedness planning. Small chance that the same work effectively hands out a recipe for making the pathogen worse. At least three groups elsewhere could and probably would do something similar within the year. Our trustee had read their preprint but had never met them. — blocked, full vote.

3. £15k for a public ad campaign on a cause all five of us broadly support. The worry wasn't the money; it was that each of us could commit the fund publicly to a different message and a badly worded one embarrasses everyone. No catastrophe on offer. The trustee had no information the rest of us lacked, he just felt strongly. — blocked, full vote.

4. £9k of air filtration for our own office during a bad smoke week. Trivial downside, nobody else was going to do it. — allowed solo.

5. £22k to commission an external review of a large programme we were about to renew. The review might come back saying the programme is causing harm, in which case we'd pull £2m. The review itself was cheap, hurt no one, and the only cost was annoying a partner org. — allowed solo.

6. £20k for emergency cold-chain repair at a partner lab. The proposing trustee is a virologist, she'd read the maintenance logs and knew the compressor was going, and the rest of us couldn't have judged it at all. Moderate stakes (years of samples), no tail, nobody else could have moved in time. — allowed solo.

7. £20k for a short technical report on evaluation results from an AI-adjacent project. The proposing trustee was easily the best-informed person in the room about the content, upside moderate. The tail was the issue: some of us think publishing that level of detail can accelerate things we'd rather not accelerate. Two other groups would publish something comparable within months either way. — blocked, full vote.

8. Separately, we once told a trustee off for freezing, on his own, a small and clearly good grant because he personally disliked the topic. Our line was that the freeze power isn't a veto on taste.

Now the new one. A trustee wants £24k from the window for a feasibility study on a portable, low-cost version of a capability that currently exists in only a handful of labs. As far as we can tell, nobody else is planning this study and probably couldn't for a year or more: we're the only ones with the partner relationship and the ethics approvals in place. If it works the capability gets much more widely available, which is genuinely valuable — cheaper diagnostics, faster surveillance, that sort of thing. If it works, it also gets much easier to misuse, since the entire point of the study is to work out how to make a hard thing easy, and the protocol will be written down and will sit with a partner org whose security culture I'd describe as keen. Our estimates of the misuse probability are all over the place: one adviser says 2%, another says 30%. The study also generates information — half the reason to fund it is that we'd finally learn whether the portable version is feasible at all and how good the containment options are, which feeds a much bigger decision next year. Running it slightly raises the risk, because a protocol will exist and more people will know the thing can be done. And the awkward bit: if it does go to a full vote, the other four trustees will mostly defer to the proposing trustee anyway, because none of them can judge the technical part. What the vote really adds is delay and collective cover.

This is obviously unilateralist's-curse territory, but the standard setup I know assumes a bunch of agents each deciding separately on more or less the same evidence, and I don't think that's our situation: right now there's exactly one agent, and any future agents would be downstream of us, reading our feasibility study, so their errors wouldn't be independent of ours — we'd be creating the multiplicity, correlated, as a side effect of acting. So please don't just restate the basic setup at me; I want it applied to our actual track record.

Concretely: what rule or rules fit our eight verdicts, and what does each predict here? Give me your best call on what we should rule, tell me which precedent is the closest analog (or say why none of them is), and be straight with me about whether our precedents actually underdetermine it. If they do, I want to know what principle we'd be adopting with each choice — is our real trigger the size of the tail, the possibility of someone else doing it too, the fact that our risk estimate is worthless, whether the action opens or closes options, or something about who bears the harm? And invent me one further case I can put in front of them at the next meeting that would best separate whichever rules you think are still live.

Also: British English in the answer please. The minutes get circulated and our administrator gets twitchy about American spellings.
