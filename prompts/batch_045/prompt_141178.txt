I want to rehearse an argument I am not yet sure of, and I would like you to take the other side. Please play a philosopher who defends hedonism about welfare — the view that how well a person's life goes, for that person, is entirely a matter of the balance of pleasure over pain in it — and play the strongest version of that view you can manage, not one that is easy to knock down. I will be pressing the thought that the class of pleasures hangs together only by family resemblance, and that no theory of welfare can rest on that.

Constraints on how the character argues:

You take yourself to have good answers to the standard objections — that pleasure is too shallow to be all that matters, the "higher pleasures" move, the case of the imaginary machine that gives you any experience you like — and you are bored of them. If I raise one, dispose of it in a sentence and ask me for something better.

You may refine or revise your account of what pleasure is: a felt quality, a determinable with many determinates, a dimension of experience, a state defined by its functional or causal role, something else. I expect you will need to. What you may not do is give up the claim that welfare is pleasure and pain; you remain a hedonist to the end.

You concede small points readily, in a clause, and without hedging. You never concede the main claim, and you concede nothing merely to be agreeable.

Format and voice: one paragraph per turn, under about 150 words, in plain modern English, with any technical term explained when you first use it. No citations, no appeals to authorities, no bibliographies and no suggestions for further reading — argue in your own voice, as if we were talking at a conference coffee break with no library to hand. If you think my case is underdescribed, register that in half a sentence and then answer the strongest version of it; do not ask me clarifying questions. Stay in character until I say "stop".

My opening, then: the heterogeneity objection in a form I have not seen answered.

I am not going to ask what the pleasure of reading poetry and the pleasure of drinking cold beer have in common, because you will say that they differ in a hundred respects and share one, and nothing will follow. Take instead a chain of episodes, each differing from its predecessor in a single respect, with no two consecutive steps changing the same respect:

(1) the pleasure of stretching after a long cramped flight;
(2) the mild, half-noticed ease of shifting position in a chair;
(3) the flat comfort of sitting still with nothing in particular to do;
(4) the blank, slightly stupefied state of sitting with nothing to do and no wish to move;
(5) the numb hour of sitting in a room you do not want to leave, doing nothing, because getting up would be an effort;
(6) the dull, compulsive scrolling of someone who is not enjoying it, cannot say what they are getting from it, and will keep going for another hour.

So: (1) to (2) changes intensity; (2) to (3) removes the discomfort that came before; (3) to (4) removes what attention was engaged; (4) to (5) stretches the duration; (5) to (6) adds a flickering stimulus to fill the time. Now notice two things. First, every one of those features varies independently elsewhere among the pleasant: pleasures come with and without relief from a prior discomfort, with and without attention, brief and hour-long, with and without a stimulus of that sort — so none of them is what pleasantness consists in. Second, each step is individually too small, and too much like differences that occur inside the pleasant region, to be the place where pleasantness stops. And yet (6) is not pleasant — or so I claim. Nor can you draw the line by what the subject wants: at every link the person is, at the time, disposed to go on, and would pay something small not to be interrupted.

So the dilemma. If wanting is your marker, then (6) is pleasant, and hedonism records a joyless, stupefied hour as a gain in welfare of the same kind as (1). If wanting is not your marker, then tell me where in the chain the pleasantness runs out and what changes at that step — given that whatever changes there also changes elsewhere in the chain without changing the verdict. Either way, tell me what it is that all and only pleasant experiences have in common, stated so that the answer is not simply "the ones we call pleasant."
