I sit on the Autonomous Systems Review Committee at my hospital network — we're the body that has to sign off on or kill any clinical pilot where a system acts without a human commanding every step, and right now we're 4–4 and I have to circulate something before Thursday. The case: a supervised-autonomy suturing module for laparoscopic bowel surgery. It plans and executes a running stitch sequence for the anastomosis; the surgeon is formally in a "monitor and intervene" role and gets roughly two to three seconds of warning on the console before each stitch, with abort as the only available action. Three weeks ago, on case 14, it placed a stitch through a tissue plane the surgeon says he would never have picked. He didn't abort. The patient developed an anastomotic leak, needed a reoperation and eleven more days in hospital, and there's now a claim naming both the surgeon and us. The vendor's position is that this is "decision support," that the surgeon is the responsible decision-maker per their labelling and per the consent language our own legal team approved, and that their contract caps their exposure at the licence fee. The surgeon's position is that he couldn't evaluate the plan in two seconds, that the only act available to him was to abort the whole step, and that aborting whenever he felt unsure would mean never using the feature at all — so his practical options were rubber-stamp or refuse, and refusing isn't a real option mid-case. Risk management wants the pilot dead on the grounds that exposure is unbounded. One member argues the surgeon is plainly at fault. Another says plainly not, and that a no-fault compensation fund is the honest answer. Our patient-advocate member says the fund is just the hospital buying its way out of its own purchasing decision and that it launders the choice we made to buy speed over control.

Two things are making this harder than a normal adverse-event review and I want the memo to actually use them. First, our chair drew a distinction at the last meeting that I think is right and that nobody has applied yet: she said "responsibility gap" is really three different failures that get run together, and they don't have the same remedy. There's an attribution gap, where you genuinely can't locate whose conduct went wrong. There's what she called a control gap, where there's a perfectly identifiable nominal agent but their actual grip on the outcome is so thin that blaming them looks unfair even though the law will happily do it. And there's a third thing, where blame might be perfectly fair and still do no work — because the failure was a property of the assembled set-up (machine, interface, training, rostering, procurement incentives) rather than of a person, so censuring the person neither deters nor fixes anything, and the incident review ends up quietly distorted because everyone knows the visible human is the cheap legal target. She used the phrase "moral crumple zone" for that last pattern: a human left in the loop mainly so that blame has somewhere to land, which makes the pilot look accountable to administrators while actual safety learning gets worse. Second, the obvious technical fix isn't free: the vendor can ship a "confirm each stitch" mode that gives the surgeon real control back, but it adds about forty percent to that step's duration and our surgical lead says longer operative time in these cases carries its own morbidity, so restoring control plausibly costs patients something. There's also a middle option nobody has costed — have the module display its plan further ahead and require an affirmative confirm for the first stitch of each sequence only — and a governance option, where a named clinician-manager is accountable for the deployment decision (indications, case selection, training, when to escalate) rather than for each intraoperative moment.

So: draft the decision memo I'll circulate Thursday. It should end with one concrete recommendation we can vote on — pause, terminate, or continue in some specified mode — with conditions and a time frame or case-count trigger for re-review, and it should say which of the chair's three gaps this incident actually is, since the remedies come apart. It has to be something the researchers and the vendor could respond to rather than a restatement of our consent form, and it needs an honest account of the strongest argument on whichever side loses: if you tell me to keep the pilot, take the patient-advocate's point about institutional self-exculpation seriously; if you tell me to kill it, take seriously that the alternative is surgeons doing this step by hand with their own error rate, and that we may just be protecting the institution rather than the patient. Our lawyers also keep reminding us the module isn't a legal person, so "the system is responsible" can't be the bottom line, but I'd like the memo to be clear about whether that's a moral conclusion or just a fact about who can be sued.
