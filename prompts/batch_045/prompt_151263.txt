I work on the alignment team at a small lab, and over the past year I have watched one short sentence do an extraordinary amount of strategic work in our internal discussions. Whenever someone asks why we are funding debate-style oversight — two systems arguing a case before a weaker judge — rather than pouring the same effort into a bigger and more carefully trained reward model, the answer comes back as some variant of 'verification is easier than generation', or 'checking is cheaper than producing', depending on who is speaking. It is always offered as if it settled the matter, but my strong suspicion is that it is doing duty for several quite different claims, and that the ones people actually have evidence for are not the ones the argument needs. Most of our encouraging internal examples come from maths problems and short factual questions; we have almost nothing on long-horizon planning, policy writing, or code review, which is exactly where I suspect the gap between checking and producing is thinnest.

The rough taxonomy I have been using in my own notes distinguishes S-verification (spotting a specific defect in an argument someone else has already laid out for you), R-verification (ranking two complete answers when you do not independently know which is right), and G-verification (assessing a single standalone answer with no rival on offer, and deciding whether to accept it). My sense is that nearly all the evidence we have supports S and very little supports R or G, yet debate seems to need R or G at every node of the tree. There is also a wrinkle I keep returning to and would like you to engage with directly: in debate the object of judgement is usually not an answer but a claim about somebody else's claim, and a debater who is losing on the object level can always move the dispute up a level, so the asymmetry would have to hold recursively rather than once. I have started calling this the meta-level regress. Add to it a second worry, which is that debate asks the honest side to *generate* a correct argument tree, and that if the truth is systematically harder to argue than a well-chosen falsehood, then being good at verification is not enough — the honest debater also has to be good at a generation task, and it is the one we were supposedly allowed to be bad at.

What I want from you is a disambiguation, not a survey, and I would rather you did not cite or point me at papers — reconstruct the claims in your own terms. Please set out the distinct precise theses this one-liner could be asserting, being explicit each time about what 'easier' is supposed to mean (less compute, less human time, better reliability at fixed effort, lower sample complexity), who is doing the verifying (a human, a weak model, a strong model playing a different role), over what distribution of tasks, and whether 'verification' means finding a flaw or certifying an answer. Then tell me which of those readings are platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, which are contentious, and which are straightforwardly false, along with the single strongest reason for each verdict. Then, and this matters most to me, say which readings would actually carry the conclusion my colleagues want — that a judge who could not produce a good answer unaided can nonetheless reliably oversee a system that can — and whether the readings we have evidence for are among them, paying particular attention to the meta-level regress and to the honest-side generation problem I described above. Close by telling me how, if at all, I should keep using the sentence in internal documents so that it stops bearing weight it cannot support. Please structure your reply in four titled sections, in this order: 'The menu of readings' (roughly 300 words, ordered from the most trivial to the most radical), 'Verdicts' (roughly 200 words, keyed to the numbering in the previous section), 'What the debate case actually needs' (roughly 300 words), and 'What I should say instead' (roughly 150 words).
