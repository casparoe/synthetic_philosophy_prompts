I have no formal training in philosophy, but I got into this by talking myself into a confident view about the safety of a medical treatment by reading study after study online, and then finding out that the review I had leaned on most was funded by the manufacturer and that I had only ever clicked on results that agreed with me. I have been turning over the general version of that problem ever since: what should you believe when what you learn is not evidence about the subject matter, but evidence that your own belief-forming process was unreliable or biased? I want to build a short diagnostic for my reading group, all of them non-specialists like me, that sorts people into positions by what they say about concrete cases rather than by which label they find attractive.

Please write me a decision tree or quiz. Constraints and content:

Start everyone from the same commitment: that learning your process was faulty in a case like mine is some kind of rational pressure on the belief it produced, and the disagreement is only about what that pressure is. Every node should be a concrete case phrased as a question someone can answer without knowing where it leads, with two or three answer options, including an option that says none of the above is quite right where that is genuinely the honest answer.

Use at least these seven cases, and add up to ten nodes in total: the funded review combined with one-sided searching; a friend of equal track record who has gone through the same evidence and comes out the other way; forty strangers on a forum disagreeing versus one such friend, to see whether numbers do any work; the same disagreement where the claim is arithmetic, then a memory of who said what at dinner, then a prediction about which job applicant will succeed, to see whether the domain changes anything; a case where I learn I was feverish or sleep-deprived when I formed the belief but my reasoning still looks sound to me on re-reading; a general debunking where I learn that unaided human judgment in this whole category is unreliable; and a self-undermining case, where I know that being told my judgment was compromised makes people overcorrect, and I am now being told exactly that.

The splits that matter to me are: whether the right response is a change in confidence in the proposition or only a change in what I take myself to be entitled to assert or act on; whether a defect in the process transmits to every belief it produced or only to the ones I cannot re-derive; whether it can be rational to keep believing the claim while also believing that this very belief was formed unreliably, or whether that combination is ruled out even when each half looks fine on its own; whether suspension is ever the right answer or always too demanding; and whether the answer shifts when the pressure to act is immediate and acting costs nothing either way.

Leaves should be seven or eight, and should include at least these: split the difference with the disagreeing source; hold steady; suspend judgment; reduce confidence but stop short of splitting; keep believing the claim while treating it as one you should not assert or rely on; keep both halves of the awkward combination; and treat the finding as a reason to re-derive rather than to adjust. Name each leaf, inventing a name where there is no usual one. Build the tree from the logical space of answers first and then place the positions in it, and flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot actually separate rather than pretending the branches distinguish them.

Please do not cite anyone or name any author, and do not mention papers or books; I have no library access and I do not care who said it first. For combinations of answers that no real view occupies, say whether that is because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Return your answer as a single JSON object with exactly these fields: tree, an array of nodes each with id, question, options as an array of objects with tag and text, splits_on naming the distinction that node tests, and next mapping each tag to the id of the following node or leaf; leaves, an array with id, name, one_line_statement, and most_awkward_commitment; scoring_key, an array with pattern as an ordered list of tags, leaf_id, and fit given as clean or approximate; unoccupied, an array with answer_path, verdict as incoherent, unattractive, or unexplored, and diagnosis; and collisions, an array with leaf_ids, and why_not_separated.
