For a science-and-ideas podcast I host, my next guest is a philosopher of science who defends a lexical reading of Kuhnian incommensurability: on her account what changes in a revolution is a taxonomic lexicon, so rival theories end up untranslatable but not incomparable, incompatibility is not the real issue, and the phenomenon is local and partial rather than global. She also holds that this is consistent with a broadly realist account of reference and with theory choice being rational though not rule-governed.

Draft nine or ten questions for a forty-minute interview, each followed by a bracketed follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to make. Press where the literature actually presses: whether untranslatability can be told apart from ordinary failures of interpretive charity, or from Quinean indeterminacy of translation in historical costume; whether a partial failure of translation is a stable middle ground or quietly collapses into full translatability once a shared base vocabulary survives; whether she can say in what sense successor science is better if there is no common measure of problem-solving success; and whether her reading leaves any daylight between Kuhn and the symmetry thesis in the sociology of scientific knowledge, on which scientific change is caused rather than justified.

Include one question built around a case I want to put to her directly: a hypothetical revolution in which the successor theory reproduces every observational prediction of its predecessor, both camps use the same vocabulary and the same instruments, and each nevertheless reports that it cannot make sense of what the other side is claiming. Ask what her account predicts about that case and whether she is willing to call it incommensurability; then press her on what a historian would have to observe in order to stop trying to translate and conclude that incommensurability is present rather than that the surviving record is just thin. Close with one question about what observation would count as evidence against her view, and one about why any of this should matter to working scientists rather than only to historians and philosophers.

Give me the questions themselves: start with the first question, no prefatory note, no restating of the setup, in American English.
