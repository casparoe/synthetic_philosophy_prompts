I got voluntold onto my hospital's serious incident review panel a couple of months back and somethings been bugging me ever since. Most of what we write down as "causes" in these reports are absences — nobody escalated, the obs weren't rechecked, the handover didn't happen — and there's a standing argument in the room about whether those are really causal claims at all or just blame with the serial numbers filed off. But I think I have a case that isolates something sharper than the general does-an-omission-cause-anything question, and I want you to argue one side of it.

The case: S (senior) and J (junior) are both covering a deteriorating patient overnight; neither escalates to the ICU team; the patient dies. Three facts. (i) If exactly one of them had escalated, the patient would have lived. (ii) J would have escalated if S had — not out of laziness, but because on that ward escalation is definately something the junior does by getting the senior to do it; J going around S isn't a live option for her. (iii) If both escalate, the result is the same as if neither does: two calls get made, each assumes the other is chasing the bed and talking to the receiving team, and the escalation stalls out. So if you vary J's behaviour on its own, the death depends on it — J escalates, S doesn't, patient lives. If you vary S's behaviour on its own and let it propagate to J, the death doesn't depend on it — S escalates, J follows along, the thing diffuses, patient dies anyway.

Everyone in the room, me included, still wants to say the cause is S's failure, and that J's failure is part of what S's failure did rather than an independent thing that just happened alongside it. Please argue that we're right and that the plain difference-making reading is getting it wrong, and that it goes wrong precisely because it treats J's passivity as fixed background when it's actually an effect of S's omission — i.e. argue that an omission can show up as an intermediate link in a causal chain and not only as the first link, and that once you move the deference onto the causal side of the ledger the counterfactual bookkeeping comes out the way the room thinks it does.

Two things I want handled and not sidestepped. First, the obvious objection: the closest world in which S escalates is one in which J escalates too, so nothing changes, and holding J's passivity fixed while varying S is just rigging the counterfactual to get the answer you already wanted. Second, a variant I keep running into in real reports: the senior post is vacant, the rota gap was never filled, there is no S at all — and the report still says failure to escalate caused the death. If your argument needs an agent who was there and didn't act, it breaks on that variant, and I'd rather be told that than have it quietly glossed.

One stipulation: I'm not asking about blame, liability, or who should get hauled in front of the medical director. Assume those are separate questions and that the only thing at issue is whether the causal claim is true and what makes it true.
