I teach an undergraduate colloquium on non-human agency in history — the body of work that treats germs, droughts, and volcanoes as actors in the story rather than as the weather of it — and I want to use Roman Polanski's Chinatown (1974, from Robert Towne's screenplay) as a case study, because it seems to me to stage both the appeal and the danger of that move more sharply than anything in the readings. I'd be grateful for a close reading of the film along the following lines.

The tool I have been using in my own notes, and want pressure-tested, is a distinction between a non-human factor as scene-setter and as selector. A scene-setter fixes which options are live and what they cost without itself choosing among them; a selector determines which of the live options gets actualized, the way the arrival of a pathogen determines whether an epidemic happens. The drought in Chinatown presents itself as a selector and is repeatedly staged as one — the water hearings, the dry riverbed, the dying groves, the nighttime water releases. But on the reading I'm inclined to give, the plot reveals it to be a scene-setter at best, and in fact something worse than a scene-setter: a cover. Noah Cross manufactures a scarcity and stages a theft in order to acquire a valley; the natural event is not so much a cause of what happens as an instrument of a will that prefers not to appear as a will. I have been calling this laundering agency through the environment — the natural cause is made to carry the justificatory weight, so that an outcome which is somebody's project enters the record as nobody's fault. It has the same shape, I think, as episodes where a famine gets narrated as drought and the requisitioning is left out of the sentence that explains it.

Three things I want the reading to settle. First: does the film hold the scene-setter / selector distinction, or does it quietly collapse it? Is Cross's scheme meant to show that the environment is no agent at all, merely a vocabulary that power speaks — or does something irreducibly non-human survive the revelation, and if so what: the water itself, the land's actual need for it, the salt that betrays where a body was killed, the fact that the valley has to be watered regardless of whose name is on the deed?

Second: the film's fatalism. The closing line, in which Gittes is told to forget it because it's Chinatown, looks like a determinism — but is it an environmental one, or is it a determinism of jurisdiction and place, of what a private investigator can reach and what the law will touch? I think this matters more than it first appears: if the film's fatalism is about place rather than nature, then a viewer who takes the drought as the film's antagonist has committed precisely the error the film is about, and the film is best read as a critique of environmental determinism as ideology rather than as an instance of it. I want that claim argued for or against, not hedged.

Third: which two or three scenes would you build a session around, and what would you say about them? My shortlist is the night at the reservoir, the saltwater pond with the round spectacles in it, and the retirement home where it emerges that the valley has been bought in the names of people who cannot testify. I'd also like the honest version of the reading's limits: if pressing the film into a thesis about environmental determinism flattens it, say so, and say what the film knows that the argument does not.

Please write committedly. Give me a thesis about what the film does with non-human causes, use the scene-setter / selector distinction where it earns its keep and discard it plainly where it doesn't, and flag any point where your reading depends on a contested way of taking a scene.
