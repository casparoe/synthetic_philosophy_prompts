ok so my roommate got me into this at like 1am and I still cant find the exit. He's taking a psych elective and has been reading up on moral foundations theory — the Haidt-style thing where moral judgment runs off a handful of innate-ish modules that culture then tunes: care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, sanctity/degradation, plus the later liberty/oppression one. The finding he keeps repeating is that self-described liberals in the American sense score high on the first two and pretty low on the rest, while conservatives score moderately on all of them, and libertarians are mostly liberty plus fairness. He's built an argument out of it that he thinks shows the left is wrong about a whole family of issues, and my problem is that it seems to show enormously more than that, but I can't put my finger on the move. Here it is, roughly his words:

(1) A moral judgment is in epistemic trouble if the reason you hold it is that your foundation profile is what it is — i.e. if someone with a different profile, looking at the same act, would judge the other way, and you'd go on judging your way whatever the facts were.

(2) Your foundation profile is exactly what explains which moral judgments you make: give a person a strong sanctity module and suddenly the desecration of a flag or a grave is a big deal; take it away and the same act looks like nothing at all.

(3) So in practice, "you would judge differently if your profile were different" is the same thing as "you would hold this belief either way" — the truth is playing no role in fixing what you believe.

(4) So nobody on either side knows anything: left and right are equally unjustified, each one's confidence being an artifact of which modules got turned up.

(5) But that doesn't mean we should shrug. The right response to learning you're blind to a consideration is to defer to whoever registers more of the domain. Coverage principle: other things equal, a sensibility that responds to more foundations picks up more of the moral facts, and any consideration reachable only through a foundation you lack is one you are blind to. Conservatives respond to more foundations than liberals do. So where the two disagree, defer to the conservative judgment — not because it's justified (by (4) nothing is) but because coverage is the only ranking left once knowledge is off the table.

(6) And coverage has no stopping point. Imagine a people — call them the Vemm, I'm making them up — who have all six plus a strong "novelty is disgusting" module and a "duties to the unborn" module. On the coverage test they outrank everyone. Now imagine another group that adds "do what the man with the biggest stick says." They outrank the Vemm. The test just ranks whoever has the most and loudest gut reactions.

(7) Meanwhile (4) eats itself anyway: the psychologist's judgment that we should defer to coverage, and my judgment that tolerance is good, are also just outputs of a foundation profile. So no one is in a position to do the deferring either.

I think there's something doing two different jobs between (2) and (3), but I also think (5) is cheating somehow, and I can't say which. The bits I'd most like to keep: the thought that a moral sensibility can genuinely have a blindspot, so that some people really do miss something real (that feels right to me and I don't want to give it up just because he's using it badly), and the thought that the bare existence of cross-profile moral disagreement doesn't show that nobody is ever right. Which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what do you have to pay to reject it? If (5) is salvageable in some weaker form I'd like to know what it costs to keep it — and whether the Vemm are supposed to count as a reductio or whether my roommate can just eat them.
