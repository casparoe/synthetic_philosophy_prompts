I run a small reading group that came out of a local rationalist meetup — eight or nine people, most of us have worked through the SEP article on laws of nature, nobody is a specialist. We've now spent two sessions stuck at exactly the same place: everyone agrees that there is some distinction worth drawing between a genuine law and a generalization that merely happens to hold throughout the whole history of the universe, and we agree about nothing downstream of that. So instead of another evening of people defending labels, I want to hand out a decision tree that sorts people by what they say about cases, and only reveals the name of their position at the leaf. Think of it as a quiz where everyone has to taboo the labels until the end.

The root commitment should be thin: there is a real distinction between laws and merely accidental universal generalizations, so don't spend a leaf on the view that any true universal generalization is ipso facto a law. From there I want five or six branching questions, no more, ending in about ten leaves. Two constraints on the questions. First, each must be answerable by someone who has never heard of Humean supervenience or nomic necessitation — a concrete miniature world, or a concrete claim about what would have happened or could have happened, not a slogan. As a hard rule, no branch node may contain the words "govern", "supervene", "necessitate", "primitive", or "Humean": if you think one of those notions is doing the real work, find the case that forces it instead of the word. Second, each question must actually separate some pair of positions rather than restate one.

Here are the cases I want used. (1) The obvious one: a universal generalization that holds but that nobody wants to call a law. (2) The one-shot world: a universe whose entire history is short enough that the relevant generalization is instantiated exactly once — is there a law there, and if not, why does that feel like the wrong answer? (3) One I invented and haven't seen handled, which I think is the interesting one: a world whose total history is captured equally well, by any reasonable standard of simplicity and strength, by two different systems, where the two systems agree about every particular matter of fact that actually obtains but disagree about what would have happened in some circumstance that never arises. Is there a fact of the matter about which system is the law, and does anything ride on it? Call it the palimpsest world. (4) The decaying pattern: something that holds perfectly for an enormous stretch of history and then simply stops holding, with no accompanying change anyone can point to. Did the law change, was there never a law, or was there a time-indexed law all along? (5) A Goodman-style pair: two generalizations fitting all the data equally well, differing only in vocabulary — one stated in a familiar predicate, one in a predicate gerrymandered partly by reference to a time — where exactly one is the sort of thing we want to call a law. (6) The non-strict case: the generalizations of the special sciences, which hold only when nothing interferes, and the question of whether a theory of laws has to make room for them. Add your own cases where a split needs one, and if one of mine turns out not to discriminate between any two positions, say so and drop it.

Leaves I want reachable: a best-system-style account; a naive constant-conjunction account, if it survives the questions at all; laws as contingent relations among universals; laws as flowing from the intrinsic natures or causal powers of things; laws as brute, unanalysable facts about the world; a Platonic-style account on which laws are entities standing in a relation to the things that instantiate them; the view that the fundamental laws are strictly speaking false of the world and hold only inside artificially sheltered set-ups, with real regularities requiring something extra; an error theory or near-error-theory on which nothing in the world answers to "law" and the word does its real work inside models; and a quietist view on which "law" picks out whatever plays a certain role in scientific practice and the metaphysics is a pseudo-dispute. That's nine — add one or two if some cell is occupied by a real position I've missed. Attach names to leaves only where you're confident about them, and please skip citations, papers, and reading lists entirely: I can't check them from the meeting room and I don't want the handout to become a bibliography.

More important than the names: build the tree out of the logical space rather than out of the list of positions. Tell me how many answer-combinations your questions permit, which of those cells are actually occupied, and for each empty cell whether the corresponding view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored — I suspect at least one empty cell is a view somebody ought to hold, and I'd like to know what its most awkward commitment would be. Then flag the pairs of leaves your tree cannot separate and explain why the difference between them doesn't show up in any case you can construct; and tell me which single question does the most work and which one is redundant.

Last thing: end with a short note on where you're least confident — which leaf you worry you've described wrongly, which real position you may have folded into the wrong neighbour or left out, and what would make you reorganize the top of the tree. If you came to think the deepest split in this debate is not the one you used as your first branch, what would it be instead, and what case would convince you?
