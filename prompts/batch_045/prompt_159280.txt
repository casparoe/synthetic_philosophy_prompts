I started a new job about three months ago on the safety team at a self-driving car company, and somehow I got handed the spreadsheet where our ethics lead (Priya) logs her verdicts on "unavoidable collision" test scenarios. Now I have to write up the recommendation for next week's design review, my name goes on it, and legal reads it. I've been reading about the doctrine of double effect on my own but I definately can't get it to line up with her actual calls. Here's what's in the sheet:

- Divert the car from 5 pedestrians onto a trajectory that kills 1: permissible.
- Push a heavy person into the car's path so his body stops it: impermissible.
- Rotate the car so it heads at 1 instead of 5, where the 1's death does nothing to help — the car stops on its own: permissible.
- The only way to stop is to hit 1, whose body is what brings the car to rest: impermissible. Her note says he's functioning as part of the braking mechanism.
- Hospital backup power: reroute electricity to keep 5 patients alive, which kills 1 ventilator patient in another wing: impermissible. Note says "withdrawing support, not the same as the switch."
- Precision strike on a munitions plant, proportionate, civilian deaths foreseen: permissible.
- Same expected death count where the civilians dying is the point: impermissible.
- Sacrificing our own passenger to save 5 pedestrians: permissible, but flagged "reasons unclean."

The new case I have to write up: our car's brakes fail because of a firmware bug we shipped (not negligence exactly — it passed our tests). It's heading at five pedestrians. The only other trajectory mounts a sidewalk and kills one person at a bus shelter who is completely safe otherwise. She isn't what stops the car — the shelter frame is, she's just in the crush zone — and the numbers are proportionate. Two wrinkles: the threat exists only because of us, and our published safety policy says the car will never mount a sidewalk when pedestrians are present.

What would Priya say? I need one call, not a menu: give me the verdict, the rule you think she's actually running, which of her past verdicts that rule handles awkwardly, which precedent is the closest analogue, and how confident you are. If you think her likely verdict is the wrong thing to put in the policy, say that plainly too. Last thing: I can sneak exactly one extra scenario into the review — tell me which single case would tell the two candidate rules apart.
