I host a small epistemology podcast, and next week's guest is a philosopher who defends what she calls unrestricted evidentialism about higher-order evidence: higher-order evidence is simply more evidence, the sole rational response to it is to update by conditionalization on one's total evidence, and epistemic akrasia — believing that p while also believing that one's belief that p is not supported by the evidence — is therefore never rationally permissible.

Draft eight to ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. At least one question should press on each of the following:

- Granularity and order. Real higher-order evidence arrives incrementally and in driblets — a raised eyebrow, a half-remembered warning about the drug you were given, a statistical base rate learned later. Does her model require a continuous sequence of updates, and what should it say when the same total higher-order evidence, presented in a different order or in differently sized increments, would leave the agent with a different final credence?
- Shared sources. Consider a case where the higher-order and first-order evidence come from the same informant: one person tells you both that the meeting is at four and that your own memory of the time is unreliable. Does conditionalization then count the same source twice, and should that be described as a cost of the view or as a correct result?
- The level-connection. Any conditionalization model needs a mechanism by which a purely higher-order claim about your reliability moves your credence in p itself. Ask her to state the mechanism, and then whether it commits her to treating your own belief that p as evidence — and, if so, whether that is bootstrapping under another name.
- Regress and meta-akrasia. Can the same incoherence reappear one level up, as uncertainty about whether to trust your response to the higher-order evidence? Does the view have to posit a level of rational self-trust that is not itself evidence-responsive, and is that level stable?

Close with one question about what could count against the view, phrased so that she cannot fall back on dismissing the hard case as confused rather than counterexemplary.
