I am building a taxonomy of attempted transfers of Gödel's incompleteness theorems into domains outside mathematics, and I need the candidate applications sorted by how much genuine inferential work the theorems actually do in each case. I want a ranking, not a survey.

Start from the following transfer-schema. For an application outside mathematics to inherit the force of the theorems, something like all of these should obtain:

C1 (effective axiomatizability): the target system's rules and starting points are fixed and mechanically enumerable, so that there is a well-defined notion of what the system does and does not settle.

C2 (sufficient strength): the system can encode enough arithmetic, or otherwise simulate the diagonal construction, to produce self-referential sentences and to represent its own settling-relation.

C3 (coding of self-reference): the system can talk about its own rules, proofs, or outputs by something functionally equivalent to a numbering of its expressions, and can form a sentence that says of itself that the system does not settle it.

C4 (the right consistency assumption): the system is consistent in the relevant sense, and note that claims about the truth of the undecidable sentence need something stronger than bare consistency, roughly soundness for the relevant class of existential claims.

C5 (the Gödelian conclusion, and not a weaker one): the payoff is genuinely the in-principle one, namely a blind spot the system cannot close while remaining itself, rather than the much weaker observations that the system is vague, empirically incomplete, contested, or underdetermined by its evidence. In particular, the second theorem's conclusion is about what the system can prove from within about its own consistency, not about the impossibility of anyone having any reason to trust the system.

Now rank the eight applications below into four tiers. Tier A: the theorems do real work and the conclusion follows if the preconditions hold. Tier B: a disciplined analogy; most preconditions obtain and the stated conclusion is proportionate to them. Tier C: a suggestive metaphor that can still discipline thinking, but where at least one precondition clearly fails. Tier D: ornament; the conclusion would be entirely unaffected if the theorems were false, and they are present only to lend prestige.

For each item give: its tier; a rank ordering within the tier; a score from 0 to 100 for how much of the argument survives if the incompleteness theorems are replaced by a generic observation about vagueness or underdetermination, where 100 means nothing is lost and 0 means the argument collapses; one sentence identifying the precondition that fails or, for items in the top tiers, the precondition hardest to secure; and one sentence of justification.

The items:

(i) The anti-mechanism argument of the Lucas-Penrose kind: if the totality of what I can prove is captured by a consistent formal system whose soundness I can recognize, I can recognize the truth of that system's undecidable sentence, which it cannot settle, so no such system captures my mathematical capacity, so mechanism about the mind is false.

(ii) The argument from the second theorem to a limit on self-certification in machine learning: a deployed learning system cannot, from within its own training objective and by its own procedures, certify its own safety or alignment, because no sufficiently strong system proves a statement encoding its own reliability.

(iii) The claim that a final theory in physics is impossible: since any candidate unified theory would have to be rich enough to encode arithmetic and to state its own consequences, it will contain questions about itself it cannot settle, and cannot establish its own consistency, so physics can never be completed.

(iv) The Gödelian argument against self-certifying coherentism in epistemology: a body of beliefs that includes a claim about the reliability of its own belief-forming processes cannot establish that claim by reasoning internal to itself, so any coherentist theory of justification is structurally unable to vindicate itself.

(v) The constitutional self-amendment argument: a legal order whose amendment procedure can be exercised on that very procedure, and which can encode statements about what its own rules permit, must admit questions of legality, for instance whether a given amendment is itself lawful, that no court applying the code from within can settle without stepping outside the code.

(vi) The impossibility of a complete computable theory of rational play: for any formal decision theory, there is a decision problem featuring an opponent who possesses the decision theory's specification and can simulate it, in which the theory's recommendation about itself is not settled by the theory, so there can be no complete theory of rational choice for agents who are modellable by the agents they face.

(vii) The codified-ethics argument: any consistent moral code rich enough to represent its own prescriptions leaves some morally loaded case unsettled from within the code, and cannot establish from within that it never issues conflicting prescriptions, which is offered as support for moral particularism or for the irreducibility of judgement to rules.

(viii) The literary or postmodern use: since every sufficiently rich system contains sentences it cannot settle and cannot certify itself, no discourse or text has a determinate content, and every system of thought is shown to undermine itself.

Finish with two short paragraphs. In the first, identify the single item whose tier would change most if one precondition were repaired, specify the repair precisely, and say where the item would land. In the second, say which precondition in the schema you think is doing most of the discriminating work across the eight items, and why the schema as stated might be too demanding or too permissive in one respect.
