I'm putting together the function week for a grad seminar in philosophy of biology this spring, and I realised I don't actually know what I'm asking of a theory of biological function. My students do this thing where someone produces a case — a heart that has never pumped and never will, the very first heart token, the heart's thumping noise, a liver that does forty things — and everyone treats the case as decisive for or against an account, and I have no principled way to say how much weight it should carry. So before we read any more of the literature I want the spec instead. Write me the desiderata: what any account of what it is for a trait or part to have a function has to deliver, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of one camp. To keep it honest, fix the purpose: I want a function concept that can (a) underwrite a notion of malfunction or dysfunction that does real work for my colleague in philosophy of medicine, who needs to separate pathology from mere statistical atypicality, and (b) still carry the naturalistic load that theories of mental content lean on it for. If those two customers want different things from the concept, I want that said explicitly, with each desideratum tagged for which customer it comes from. And please route some of the requirements through cases I can use to break the usual rut, because the ones in the literature are over-rehearsed: a de novo designed protein dropped into a synthetic cell, whose only history is a lab selection protocol run by a robot; an enzyme produced by directed evolution where the selecting was done by an automated pipeline rather than by anything ecological; a trait in a digital organism in an artificial-life platform; a gene-drive construct engineered to crash a wild population, whose function seems to be something no organism ever benefited from; a transposable element that spread by selfish replication and is now doing regulatory work it was never selected for; and a holobiont or extended-phenotype case like a beaver dam or a termite mound, where it isn't clear whose history is supposed to matter. Distinguish also between what a theory must explain — why the trait is there, what it currently does, how a part can be broken — and what it must merely respect, like the distinction between having a function and having that function well, or between a function and a useful side effect.

The part I most want is the negative structure of the list: tell me which subset of the desiderata you think cannot be jointly satisfied, and say whether that's because the list is over-ambitious or because the concept is genuinely over-constrained (the origin-of-function problem plus the normativity requirement plus the explanatory-role requirement feel like they might not all fit together, but I want you to actually check rather than nod along). Tell me which single requirement excludes the most candidate theories, and which of the usual demands on a theory of function you think are prejudices dressed up as desiderata — the ones that just bake in an answer. A short scorecard at the end is fine: how the selected-effect/etiological account, the causal-role or systemic-capacity account, and some kind of pluralist or deflationary option do against your list, a few lines each. But keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases, since the criteria are where the thinking is and I can redo the scoring myself.

And be explicit about where you're unsure. Give me the desideratum you'd abandon first if I pushed back on it, tell me what would actually change your mind — a case, an argument, a result from biology — and flag the step in your reasoning you think is most likely to be wrong, rather than presenting the list as if it dropped out of the sky fully justified.
