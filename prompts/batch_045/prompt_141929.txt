I'm drafting a short position statement on material objects for a graduate workshop, and before I put it in print I'd like an audit of the commitments I've assembled. I don't know where the strain in the set is, so please don't treat any one of them as more negotiable than the others: take them all as things I currently believe, and tell me whether the package survives.

1. The unrestricted answer to the special composition question is false. My reasons are the familiar pair: I won't grant that there is an object composed of the tip of my nose and the top of the Eiffel Tower, and I take the problem of the many to count heavily against any view on which every near-candidate for being the table in this room is a real object. So: not universalism.

2. The never answer is false too. I want there to be tables, and not merely as a concession to ordinary talk — I don't think the paraphrase strategies that would let me say everything I want to say while denying that tables exist actually work.

3. So I want a restricted answer, and this is the one I've been defending: some things compose an object just in case their activity jointly constitutes a life, or they are integrated into a single function that some actual community of users confers on them. The first clause is meant to get me organisms; the second is meant to get me artifacts, which is where I most expect trouble.

4. Methodologically I want more than an extensionally correct generalization. I want the condition to be general, necessary, and stated in non-gerrymandered terms: a condition whose application turns on what some particular person happens to intend, want, or be attending to is not an answer to the question, it's a change of subject. And I don't want to fall back on a brute primitive. Treating it as an unexplained bedrock fact which pluralities compose strikes me as a research-stopper, and I'd rather revise almost anything else on this list than help myself to it.

5. I also hold that composition and existence are sharp. There are no vague objects; for any plurality it is determinate whether they compose something; and I don't want to solve the problem of the many by stipulating that one privileged candidate among the many near-candidates in the room is the table and that the others aren't.

6. I deny coincidence: I don't think there can be two material things made of exactly the same matter in exactly the same place at the same time. So the statue and the piece of clay it is made of are one and the same thing, if both are to be counted at all. But I also want to keep the ordinary pair of judgments that the clay would survive being rolled into a ball and that the statue would not.

7. On scattered objects: I deny that my nose and the moon compose anything, but I'm happy to say that there is a single object composed of my two shoes while they are in different cities, or of the two pieces of a bikini, or of a star and its planets.

What I want from you is an audit, not a survey of the literature. Go through the set pairwise and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like it — I'd rather you discriminate than flag everything. Order the genuine tensions by severity instead of listing them as equally bad. For each one, say exactly what I would have to give up or add in order to remove it, and what that would cost me elsewhere in the set; then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Two further things I'd like flagged: any commitment I haven't stated but that the position requires, and any case my two-clause criterion obviously mishandles. I'm worried in particular about a watch taken apart and left in a drawer, and about whether there was ever really a table here at all given how vague its boundary seems to be.
