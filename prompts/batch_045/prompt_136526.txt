My office-mate (a vision scientist, and annoyingly good at this) has a running habit of ruling on whether some timing oddity is Orwellian — you really had the experience and then the record of it got rewritten — or Stalinesque — the content was fixed before it was ever presented, so there was never an earlier version to rewrite. He also insists every case has a real answer and won't take "no fact of the matter" as an out. Verdicts I've got out of him so far: color phi (dot flashed at one spot, then another, seen as a single dot that moves and changes colour before the second flash has landed): Stalinesque. The cutaneous rabbit (taps at wrist and elbow, felt as taps running up the arm): Stalinesque. Flash-lag (a flashed object lined up with a moving one looks like it trails behind): Stalinesque. Chronostasis, the stopped-clock thing where the second hand seems to hang when your eyes land on it: Stalinesque. The change-blindness case where someone stops you for directions, a door passes between you, and a different person comes out the far side: Orwellian. And the one where people are told about a detail after the fact and then confidently "remember" it: Orwellian.

New case, and this is the one I need predicted. In a metacontrast set-up a shape is flashed and immediately followed by a masking ring (everyone in the lab agrees the masking is early, stimulus-driven, V1-ish, not something you can talk yourself out of). On each trial the subject does two things: a speeded forced-choice keypress inside about 200 ms — square or diamond — and then, a full second later, a report plus a second forced choice about what was on screen at the moment of the flash. The speeded press is well above chance and tracks the target. A second later they say they saw nothing, and the second forced choice is at chance. Asked about it afterwards they say the two answers don't conflict: they saw nothing, the keypress was a guess — even though it wasn't.

So: Orwellian or Stalinesque? I care much less about the verdict than about the rule he's running. The two I can think of that fit his track record equally well are something like 'Orwellian iff some response made at the time still discriminates the pre-revision content' and something like 'Orwellian iff the revision is done by a central, report-level, cognitively penetrable process rather than by early sensory processing' — and those two come apart right here. What other rules fit his verdicts just as well, which do you think he's actually on, and what single further case or experiment would seperate them?
