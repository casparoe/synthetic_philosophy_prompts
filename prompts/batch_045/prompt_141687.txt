I'd be grateful for your help stress-testing a position I've arrived at, and I'd rather have a verdict than a survey.

Some background, so you know what kind of interlocutor you have. I spent my working life in theoretical ecology — mostly on what keeps mutualisms from being invaded by cheaters — and I have been auditing philosophy seminars since I retired. I have no institutional access to journals and no training in the field, and I have found that I trust arguments far more than citations, so please reason from the structure of the cases rather than from what has been written about them: if there is a standard move I am missing, tell me what the move is, not who made it.

The topic is moral trade. The idea reached me under that name in a seminar a few years ago; I believe it originates in a paper by Toby Ord, though my notes do not say, and I may well be reconstructing it badly. As I understood it: two people who disagree about what is morally valuable can each do what the other takes to be valuable, and both can come out ahead by their own lights, much as two countries with different endowments both gain from trade.

My trouble began when I tried to write down the smallest model that exhibits the result. Two agents, each with one unit of a fungible resource — money — and each able to direct it to one of two causes, X and Y. Alice values X at 100 and Y at 10; Bob values X at 10 and Y at 100. Left to themselves, Alice funds X and Bob funds Y. Under the trade as I understood it, Alice funds Y and Bob funds X. But both arrangements send the same total resources to the same two causes, and if neither party has agent-relative reasons — if what we value is outcomes rather than who brings them about — then the two arrangements are the same outcome, and there is nothing to divide. The trade relabels the givers and changes nothing. (Unequal budgets don't help; that is just a difference in what a unit buys.)

So, my position, in two parts.

First: the popular presentation of moral trade generates no gains at all. The only engines I can find that produce real ones are these: the parties differ in what a unit of their resource buys, and in a crossed way, so that each is relatively better at producing what the other wants — which is ordinary comparative advantage and has nothing specifically to do with moral disagreement; or one party's untraded action would be positively bad by the other's lights, so that the trade is really a payment to desist; or the values are not separable across the two actions in the first place. In my old field the first is niche partitioning and the second is interference competition. Neither is a moral phenomenon.

Second — and this is the claim I actually want tested — if the engine is comparative advantage or the threat of mutual harm, then the interesting question about moral trade is not the one that seems to dominate discussion. It is not whether I may act on a moral view I reject; it is an informational one. The gains depend on each party knowing what the other values and what the other can produce, and both are private, hard to verify, and cheap to misrepresent. I half-remember a classical impossibility result in mechanism design to the effect that no voluntary, budget-balancing arrangement between two parties with private values can be relied on to realise all the gains available — I would rather not hang anything on my memory of who proved it, but if the result is what I take it to be, the shortfall is structural rather than a defect of existing institutions. And here the analogy with my old subject stops being decorative: where each partner's contribution is invisible to the other, cheaters invade. The population-level outcome is that the currency of professed moral concern is debased by the practice of trading in it — people do best by adopting views that are cheap for them to satisfy and expensive for others to buy them out of. I have taken to calling this the debasement problem, and I think it belongs at the centre of the topic rather than in a footnote about enforcement.

What would count against it? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to commit to a ranking rather than lay out a menu for me to choose from. In particular:

Is there any source of gains that survives when the resource is fungible, the values are separable and outcome-directed, and the parties' productive capacities are identical? If there is, my first claim is false, and I would like the cleanest version of the case.

Is the informational obstacle genuinely structural, or is there something about moral disagreement, as opposed to ordinary commerce, that shrinks it? The argument I can make on the other side is that a moral view is not a private taste: it is expressed across a whole life, and so is far more legible than a reservation price. I would like your estimate of how much weight that bears.

Have I insulated the second claim? I notice I can rescue it by redescribing whatever arrangement you propose as a degenerate case of trade, and I cannot tell whether that is an honest strength or a way of making the claim unfalsifiable. Tell me plainly which it is, and whether, for a claim of this kind, unfalsifiability would be fatal or merely embarrassing.

Finally: which of these defeaters should someone in my position concede, and which would I be tempted to explain away but should not? For what it is worth, I would find one clean counterexample — a trade with real gains under the austere conditions of my first claim — more persuasive than any argument about how I have framed things, and I would distrust any reply that works only by accusing me of asking a badly posed question. But tell me if that instinct is itself a mistake, and say so flatly where you think I am simply wrong.
