I am a journalist working on a piece about how large philanthropies decide what to fund, and I keep meeting the same argument in different mouths. The version I heard last week, from an economist who sits on the investment committee of a billion-dollar foundation, ran like this: allocating charitable money is allocating capital. A cause area is an asset class; importance is the size of the payoff; tractability is the conversion rate from money to that payoff; neglectedness is mispricing. Donor attention flows to whatever is fashionable, so crowded causes are overbought and unfashionable ones are cheap per unit of good. The foundation's job is therefore arbitrage: find the mispriced asset, size the bet, and rotate out once the gap closes. He drew the operational conclusion as well — never fund what other people are already eager to fund, since that merely substitutes for money that would have arrived anyway, and hold a small number of concentrated positions rather than spreading across causes. A second source, a historian of philanthropy, says the analogy is carrying the whole load: markets supply a price, philanthropy supplies none, so mispricing is a metaphor with nothing behind it.

I want an audit rather than a verdict on either man. Give me the mapping explicitly — what plays the role of price, of the arbitrageur, of the bid-ask spread, of liquidity, of the mechanism that closes the gap, of risk — and then where the mapping fails, and which failures actually bear on his conclusion. Three points I raised and got waved away should be dealt with directly. First, in a market the return the arbitrageur pockets is what sends him looking; in philanthropy the person who spots the gap is the same person who must fill it and who captures nothing. Does the absence of that feedback loop break the claim that gaps get closed, or is it incidental? Second, cheap assets are sometimes cheap because of hidden liabilities: neglectedness may signal intractability, unmeasurability, or a payoff that is an artifact of the model doing the estimating. Is there an adverse-selection version of this, and does the importance-tractability-neglectedness framework have any way to tell a bargain from a lemon — that is, is neglectedness a fact about the world or a bet against other funders' judgment? Third, a newer wrinkle: several funders now publish continuously updated cost-per-unit-of-good figures for each cause area, explicitly to make neglectedness legible the way a price is. Suppose the analogy's defenders get what they want and every cause carries a live public number. Does that make the market picture truer, or does the act of publishing a number that people then allocate against manufacture the crowding the number was meant to reveal? And if the number is the price, what fills the role of market error — who or what is wrong when the number is wrong?

Close by proposing a better analogy for what a large funder is doing, if there is one — field triage, portfolio choice under deep uncertainty, exploration versus exploitation, something else — and say plainly how much of his concentrated-betting conclusion survives the audit. Do not hedge.
