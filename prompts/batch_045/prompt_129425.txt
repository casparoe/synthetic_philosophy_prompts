I'm a senior, and I've been asked to speak for the students at the retirement dinner for Ms. Halvorsen — Ruth Halvorsen — who taught Print and Lettering here for thirty-eight years and ran the shop behind the gym, the one with the letterpress, the two presses that only work if you know how to swear at them, and the drawers of wooden type she let us touch exactly once a term. The dinner is in three weeks. The school is renaming the shop for her, and a new sign has already gone up outside it. I have about four minutes. Please write the speech.

The difficulty isn't that I don't know what to say about her. It's that she spent three years teaching us a particular account of what makes a thing the thing it is, and nearly everything a retirement speech normally does is something that account rules out.

Her view, as I can reconstruct it: there is no stroke you can point to and say, that is what makes an a an a. You can remove the bowl, flatten the tail, break the curve, and it is still the a of that alphabet. Two identical a's, she said, means one of them is dead — the letter is not any of its instances and is not a list of features they share. What makes a shape the a is the job it has in a whole system: an alphabet is not twenty-six things, it's one thing with twenty-six parts, and a mark pulled out of its alphabet isn't determinately a letter at all. She used to draw a bowl with a stem on the right and ask us whether it was an a or an o, and when we argued she'd say it was whichever one the rest of the alphabet left room for. And her own lettering was instantly recognizable, but not because of any shape: a hand, she said, is a system of decisions, not a set of curves. When I once traced one of her a's as exactly as I could and showed it to her, she looked at the page and said that was the only one on it that wasn't an a.

She also thought, and said out loud once when a colleague asked whether someone who'd changed completely after a long illness was still the same person, that this was the same question as the question about letters — and she answered it by asking what the rest of us were.

Three things follow that I can't get around. I can't give a list of her qualities; she called that a spec sheet, and I think she'd hear a eulogy made of adjectives as the claim that a person is a bag of traits, which is exactly what she denied about letters. I can't fall back on you had to be there, or some things can't be put into words, or she had a gift you either have or don't: whatever makes a letter itself was, for her, perfectly sayable, just not sayable as a feature list. And I can't say that she lives on in the new sign. The sign's letterforms were drawn by Mara Danes, class of 2013, now at a studio in the city, who scanned one of Ruth's hallway signs and redrew the alphabet from it; she's proud of it, she'll be in the front row with Ruth's husband and her sister, and she has said more than once that she traced Ruth's actual a. Ruth's only comment, when I asked, was that it's a good a. Under Ruth's own view I don't think that a is Ruth's a, whatever Mara means, and I don't think Ruth thought it was either — but Mara was her student, Ruth was glad she did it, and the speech has to be true about what survived and what didn't without turning into a fight with someone sitting three feet away.

The worst version of the problem is this: if a letter is nothing but its place in a system, and the system moves on, then the honest thing to say sounds like nothing of her persists, which is not a thing you can say to a room of sixty people who loved her.

What I have to work with: the gift. Everyone in the shop this year drew as many a's as they could in one sitting, and we mounted about three hundred of them on one sheet, no two alike, none of them hers, all of them unmistakably the same letter. That sheet will be on an easel next to me.

So: about four minutes, for a room that is her husband, her sister (who has never thought about letterforms in her life), teachers from art and English, the head of school, current students, and six or seven former students who are now working designers and will notice the instant I betray what she actually thought. Let skeleton, counter and terminal come up once or twice in a way her sister can follow, and let the technical people feel that the view is intact. It should land as love and not as a lecture, and it should end somewhere the whole room can stand without saying anything she would have called false.

After the speech, please add a short note — a few sentences — on the two or three places where her view made the speech hardest: what you wanted to say, what you couldn't, and what you said instead.
