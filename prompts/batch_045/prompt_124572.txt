A philanthropic funder has asked me to scope a single-team, three-year research program on whether machine learning reopens the classical socialist calculation debate, and I need an agenda rather than a survey. The motivating observation is that most resource allocation in advanced economies now happens inside hierarchies — firms and platforms that plan internally, set prices administratively, and increasingly delegate both to learned models — while the classical objection from Mises and Hayek was never that planning is computationally heavy but that no central body can access the dispersed, local, partly tacit knowledge that prices transmit, and Lange's reply was that a planning board could simulate a market by adjusting administered prices until excess demand disappeared. The specific hypothesis to be tested: replace Lange's board with a large predictive system trained on the economy's own transactional and logistical data, which additionally runs counterfactual simulations of the downstream effects of any price vector it considers. Give me the four or five questions such a program should try to answer, in priority order, where priority means: the questions whose answers would most change whether a funder should treat machine-mediated market socialism as a live hypothesis rather than as settled history. For each, say what makes it upstream of the others, whether it is tractable for philosophers and economic theorists working without large-scale ML infrastructure, and what developments over the coming years — inside-firm planning, algorithmic pricing, simulation-based policy tools — would move it up or down the list. Also say which of the obvious questions are overrated or beside the point, and be explicit about how you are separating the knowledge-communication objection from the incentive-and-motivation objection, since which of those the program engages should determine its design.
