I'm writing something on supertasks and I need a Thomson's lamp case built to order, not the usual one.

What I want: a machine that gets switched infinitely many times before a limit time (t = 1, say) and that is determinately lit at t = 1 — determinately in the sense that the setup itself forces it, not in the sense that the switch, or whatever drives the switch, ends up in some final position that just answers the question for you. The claim I'm after is that the standard "the story simply doesn't say what state the lamp is in" verdict is an artifact of tracking only a two-valued state variable, and that it evaporates once the machine has some continuous quantity — charge, temperature, position, whatever — that actually does have a limit. So a case that gets its answer by stipulation, or by making the supertask impossible, is useless to me.

Specs:

- the on/off sequence at finite stages has to have no limit. I want the underdetermination complaint beaten on its own ground, not routed around by defining a limit into existence.
- the lamp is lit at t = 1 and stays lit for some nonzero stretch afterwards, so it isn't an "at the instant" trick.
- the mechanism should be describable in one paragraph using ordinary lab or shop kit. No demons, no timers needing unbounded precision — and if you use something Zeno-ish (a bouncing ball, a discharge curve) to generate the shrinking intervals, say why that doesnt just smuggle back in the precision you were avoiding.
- ideally the actuator itself has no determinate position at t = 1. If your design needs it to settle somewhere, flag that and say what the answer depends on instead.

Then show me it works: why the discrete sequence has no limit, which continuous quantity does converge, and how its limit forces the lamp on. Name the single feature doing the work, show that deleting it collapses the case back into plain old underdetermination, and give the best attack available to someone who still thinks the story doesn't determine an answer — plus whether that attack actually lands.

Finish with one last line and nothing after it: \answer{ON}. Or \answer{INDETERMINATE} if you think the specs can't be jointly met, in which case say in the body which one you'd have to drop.
