I take the minutes at the grants committee of a small foundation, and I don't have a vote, so mostly I watch. We have to commit about three million dollars by the end of the month, and last week our two analysts — Marcus and Lena — spent the entire session arguing about how to decide under moral uncertainty rather than about the grants themselves. They had more or less agreed on which moral views are worth taking seriously and on rough credences: 0.5 on an impartial welfarist view that counts animals heavily but discounts the far future (call it T1), 0.3 on a near-term prioritarian and person-affecting view that also carries a deontic side-constraint against funding campaigns that knowingly impose severe costs on identifiable present communities without their consent (T2), and 0.2 on a longtermist view on which shifting the trajectory of institutions is worth vastly more than anything that happens this decade (T3). They had also scored four proposals in each view's own units, which are deliberately not comparable across rows — order T1, T2, T3: the bednet-style grant (100, 1000, 1), the farmed-animal welfare campaign (400, 300, 3), the institutional-resilience grant (10, 20, 500), and a reckless ballot measure on the same subject that our own polling says would probably backfire (−1000, 0, 0). Nobody on the committee wants to fund the ballot measure; it stayed in the spreadsheet only because it had already been drafted.

Here is roughly how the argument went, and I genuinely cannot tell who won. (1) Marcus: Weight each view's verdict by our credence in it, pick the proposal with the highest expected choiceworthiness — 0.5(100) + 0.3(1000) + 0.2(1) ≈ 350 for the bednets against 291 for the animal campaign and 111 for the institutional grant — so fund the bednets, and stop treating moral disagreement as a special case; this is just expected utility with an unusual kind of uncertainty. (2) Lena: That sum adds numbers denominated in three different currencies, so it isn't a computation but a choice of exchange rates dressed up as arithmetic — any rescaling of a single row is permissible and the rescaling decides the answer: renormalize each view so its best option scores 1 and its worst scores 0, and the animal campaign wins at about 0.59 against 0.42 for the bednets and 0.20 for the institutional grant. (3) Marcus: Then let's use that normalization — it is invariant under any positive rescaling and shift of a view's scale, which is the most you could demand if only within-view comparisons are meaningful, and it also cures the fanaticism problem, since capping each view's spread means no view can swamp the others merely because its units run to astronomical numbers. (4) Lena: But it isn't invariant to the option set, and that is fatal — put the ballot measure back on the table, a proposal that lands last under every rule we have tried and that nobody would fund, and every renormalized score moves: the bednets come back and win at about 0.69 with the animal campaign at 0.59, while the raw sum is untouched at all (the ballot measure scores −500 and the bednets still lead), so your convention makes the answer depend on which hopeless proposal somebody bothered to write up. (5) Marcus: Then don't do intertheoretic comparison at all — give each view a share of the budget equal to its credence and let each spend its share on its own favorite: half to the animal campaign, thirty percent to the bednets, twenty percent to the institutional grant. That uses only within-view rankings, so it is immune to rescaling, immune to the ballot-measure problem, and cannot be hijacked by a view with larger numbers. (6) Lena: Two problems. Splitting the budget isn't unit-free either — it is a competing normalization that assumes a 20 percent credence is properly expressed as 20 percent of the money, so a view on which the stakes are a thousand times greater gets no more than a view on which they are trivial. And worse, both your procedures run over value scores, but T2 isn't a maximizing view: its 300 for the animal campaign is an estimate of welfare produced, but T2 also forbids funding that campaign, because it knowingly imposes severe costs on identifiable farming communities who never consented, and the tables quietly drop that veto and convert a constraint into a tradeable quantity. (7) Marcus: Then take the constraint seriously as a constraint — drop any proposal that some view we credit forbids, and only then aggregate over the survivors; once the animal campaign is out, every rule we have discussed, with or without the ballot measure, picks the bednets (80 percent of the budget under the split version), which is what the raw sum said in the first place, so perhaps all the machinery was decorative.

Could you referee this move by move? Please give me exactly eight numbered items, each no more than two sentences long, with no preamble and no closing summary: items 1 through 7 should each rate one move on a scale from plainly mistaken, through more wrong than right, half right, and more right than wrong, to plainly right, and say in one sentence what the move gets right and precisely where it slips; item 8 should say what both of them are missing, or where a committee that has to decide by the end of the month should put its remaining effort if neither line of argument settles the question.
