I am teaching the causation-by-omission session of an upper-level metaphysics seminar, and I would like to build the session around Ursula K. Le Guin's short story "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" (1973) rather than around the stock examples — the gardener who does not water the plant, the driver who does not brake, the physician who withholds a dose. The story is almost always taught as a parable about utilitarianism or scapegoating, and I want to set that reading aside and ask a narrower question: do the citizens of Omelas cause the suffering of the child by omitting to intervene, and if they do not, what exactly is the relation between them and it?

The case has structural features the stock examples lack, and I want each of them handled rather than smoothed over. First, the redundancy: the child's misery does not depend on any individual citizen's non-interference, only on the pattern; yet an individual who did intervene would end the misery, at the cost of the city. So the counterfactual structure is not the simple one — there are two candidate outcomes, and the collective and individual interventions come apart in their effects. Say what a counterfactual or interventionist treatment would have to look like to return a verdict here: which variables, which contrasts, and whether the view then counts the citizens as causes or counts only the city, or the practice, or nothing. Second, the process or physical-connection view, which denies that absences can be causes at all, and the two-concept views that distinguish dependence from production: where does each land, and does the story's insistence that the child's misery is a positive condition of the city's felicity — a stipulation the narrator never mechanizes — do any work for either? I take the terms to forbid the citizens even from speaking kindly to the child; flag it if I have that wrong.

I also want you to take up a distinction I have been toying with and tell me whether it is metaphysics or only moral rhetoric. Call it ratifying omission: an omission that is public, known to all, sustained by an institution, repeated after deliberation, and expressive of acceptance — as opposed to the private, perhaps unwitting failures that dominate the literature. My suspicion is that the story dramatizes exactly this, and that it thereby suggests causation by omission is a social or structural notion that an individualist metaphysics built on single agents and single omissions cannot represent. If that suspicion is wrong — if what the story really gives us is complicity, authorization, or endorsement, and no causal claim at all — then say so, and say why the difference matters.

Finally, three things that I think decide whether the session works. The ones who walk away perform an act, not an omission, and it is the one response that changes nothing for the child: does the story concede that causal notions cannot do the moral work here, or is departure better described as a further omission, an omission to stay and contest? The citizens are fully informed, whereas most real-world structural harm is done by agents who are not: does informedness belong in the metaphysics of omission — as part of what makes an action the default, say — or only in the ethics, and if it belongs in the metaphysics, are our theory-friendly cases misleading us about the hard ones? And the child itself is depicted as too damaged to resist or even to complain: what does the framework do with an omission on the part of someone who is barely an agent?

What I want back is a reading I can hand to students: a verdict on whether the citizens cause the child's suffering, the two or three moments in the story I should point them to, and one claim about what the story adds to the debate that the argumentative literature does not supply. Please also be explicit about where the story resists the framework, and where pressing it into the framework would distort it — I would rather end the session with those limits on the board than with a tidy result.
