i teach a 14-person upper-level metaethics seminar, 80 minutes, and we're about halfway through a unit on expressivism. they've read the standard material so you don't need to explain the view to me, but please don't build the activity around a lecture either — i want them to run into the problem before anyone names it. no "Frege–Geach" from me until the last ten minutes, and ideally they shouldn't even hear the word "embedding" before the debrief.

what i want is a runnable in-class game — cards, markers, roles, a payoff scheme in points or chips (no real money) — that makes them feel why the unasserted occurrences of a normative sentence are where the boo/hurray story starts to smoke.

three things they should walk out seeing:
1. the attitude story is fine when the sentence is asserted and gets weird the moment it sits inside an if-then, a negation, or a question;
2. whatever they put in the embedded slot has to be the same thing for two speakers with opposite attitudes — otherwise validity turns into a fact about who happens to be talking;
3. the obvious repair (add a third, neutral state and treat negation as a flip into it) quietly breaks some classically valid forms, and they should be able to say which ones and why.

specific mechanics i want built in:

- Three markers only: DISAPPROVE, ENDORSE, TOLERATE. Tolerance is defined by the rules as the negation-marker — wherever a card says "it is not the case that [__]" they must place TOLERATE on the bracketed bit. don't flag this as a trap.
- An invariance rule: a validity verdict only counts if it survives swapping in a player holding the opposite markers. if two players who disagree about the situation would give different verdicts on the same argument form, the form counts as invalid and that pair loses points. this is what should push them off "the embedded bit just expresses whatever i happen to feel."
- A "torn" card: in each pair one player is dealt a situation toward which they're explicitly told they have no settled attitude — genuinely torn, not indifferent. they still have to place a marker. whatever they place should come back as a problem later (tolerance and disapproval turn out to be contraries, not contradictories).
- One round that's nothing but contraposition pairs: "if X is wrong then Y is wrong" next to "if Y is not wrong then X is not wrong." have them assign the state expressed by each and then say whether those two assignments are the same state. my prediction is they'll insist the two sentences obviously say the same thing and then not be able to make that come out with three flat markers. if you think that prediction is wrong, say so and tell me what will actually happen.
- One nested monster, something like "it's not the case that, if the council knocks down the playground, then the council does nothing wrong" — negation scoping over a conditional that itself embeds a normative sentence. they should have to build the state out of three levels, see whether their rule even has a defined output there, and then check whether the argument from that sentence to the asserted conclusion "the council does something wrong" comes out valid on their own rule.

deliverables: the materials (index cards and a printer, nothing fancier), the actual argument list — 8 to 10 forms written out in the marker language, not in English prose — a minute-by-minute timeline for 80 minutes, and a 10–15 minute debrief that maps specific moments onto specific parts of the problem: which move in the game is the embedding requirement, which is the neutrality/content-identity-across-disagreeing-speakers bit, which is the negation worry.

tell me what to do when the group "solves" it by voting the arguments valid and reverse-engineering a rule afterwards, because they definately will. and give me an honest note on what the game can't do: i assume it can't reproduce the real expressivist replies that posit structured higher-order attitudes, since my marker vocabulary is deliberatley flat — so tell me where i have to supply that in words, and how to do it without sounding like i'm letting the view off the hook.

finally, what will predictably go wrong the first time i run it. the two failure modes i'm most worried about: someone smuggling in "wrong = has the property of wrongness" in disguise, and the whole session turning into an argument about the word "wrong" instead of about the attitudes. give me a concrete intervention for each.
