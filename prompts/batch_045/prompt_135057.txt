Trying to work out what I actually think about whether you can pick up aesthetic knowledge second-hand — from someone telling you a thing is beautiful or moving or badly made. Going case by case on gut feel, then I want the principle, if there is one.

1. Priya and I have agreed about basically every novel we've both read. She says a new one is beautifully written. I read it, I agree with her. I know it's beautifully written. Obviously fine.

2. Same Priya, same confidence, different novel, I haven't read it. I'd bet money on it, I'd recommend it to people, I'd put it on a syllabus. But if someone asked me do you *know* it's beautifully written, I want to say no — I'd say Priya says so and she's never once been wrong about this. That feels different from 1 even though my actual confidence is about the same in both.

3. A critic I trust says a sequel is a lazy retread, phoned in, embarrassing. I never see it. I'm totally comfortable saying I know it's bad.

4. Same critic, same paper, same level of confidence, says a different film is a triumph. I'm *not* comfortable saying I know it's great until I've seen it myself. I've tryed to talk myself out of the asymmetry between 3 and 4 and I can't, and it bugs me, because the epistemic situation looks identical.

5. My brother says the flat he viewed stank of cat piss. Assuming he's honest, I know it stank. That's also a you-had-to-be-there property, so it's not just about experience-dependence in general.

6. My wife says durian tastes like onion custard. I've never eaten it. I feel like I learned something real, and also like I didn't — I still don't know what onion custard tastes like *to her*. Genuinely torn.

7. A musicologist whose taste I don't especially trust says a piece is structurally masterful and explains what the counterpoint is doing. I think I can know *that* from her telling me. Then she says, so it's a great piece, and I'm back to not knowing. The descriptive half seems to transmit and the verdict doesn't.

8. Everyone says a painting is a masterpiece — hundreds of people whose judgment I've got independent reason to respect. I stand in front of it for twenty minutes and see mud. I'm inclined to say the problem is me. If it had been one friend saying that, I'd have shrugged and said not my thing. So numbers seem to matter, which smells like inconsistency on my part.

9. The one I keep going back and forth on: I can't tell two red wines apart, anosmic to that whole class of thing. A friend whose palate I trust says a particular wine is extraordinary. I taste it and get nothing at all. Do I know the wine is extraordinary? I lean yes, but that sits badly with 2, and I can't say why it should be different.

10. Separately: a friend who knows my taste says you'll love this film. Different kind of claim, isn't it — about me, not about the film. I don't know where it slots in.

My tentative principle is the boring one — you only know an aesthetic property by encountering the thing yourself — and you can see it's already sprouting exceptions: except negative verdicts (3), except properties I can't access anyway (9), except the descriptive sub-claims (7), except when enough people say so (8). That's a list wearing a principle's clothes.

So: give me a principle, or two or three candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it cold to a new case, and say which of my cases motivates each clause. Tell me which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and which one you think I should give up. Give me its verdict on a couple of cases I haven't listed — I'd especially like the moral parallel, since I feel like I *do* pick up moral knowledge by deferring to someone I trust, and also like I'm being a bit of a coward when I do, and I can't tell whether your account should treat that the same as the aesthetic cases or not.

Last thing: tell me where your own answer is most likely to be wrong. Which case, or which argument, would actually move you to a different principle — not 'reasonable people disagree' but the specific thing that would make you switch.
