The position I want stress-tested is my own, and it has two parts.

The first part is the familiar-sounding one: all-things-considered betterness over outcomes — lives, total states of a person's life, distributions of welfare across a population — is sometimes genuinely intransitive. Not merely apparently intransitive, not intransitive because our judgments are unreliable; the relation itself can cycle. I arrive at this by way of the usual continuum or small-improvement arguments, and I hold it partly because it looks like the most economical way to block the Repugnant Conclusion without giving up on the idea that adding worthwhile lives is in itself good.

The second part is where I think I depart from the standard versions, and it is the part I most want probed. Call it Contrast-Sensitive Weighting (CSW). When we compare two outcomes, the weight that each dimension of comparison receives is not fixed in advance but depends on how the pair is configured: specifically, on the size of the difference on that dimension relative to the differences on the other dimensions in play in that particular comparison. So a dimension can loom large in the comparison of A with B, drop out of significance in the comparison of B with C, and reappear in the comparison of C with A. Since no single weighting is held fixed across a chain, there is no single ordering for the chain to be drawn from, and cycles are exactly what one should expect. I like this version because it makes the intransitivity a consequence of a structural feature of comparison rather than a brute refusal to accept the transitivity axiom, and because it predicts intransitivity only under specifiable conditions: many relevant dimensions, wide differences between the items on different dimensions, and near-threshold margins on at least one link.

Among the candidates, please address at least the following, but do not let them be the whole of your list:

One. The redescriptive collapse. A defender of incomparability or parity can redescribe every cycle as a case in which at least one link is not really a betterness link at all, but a merely apparent one: not better, not worse, not equal. Is my contrast-sensitivity story genuinely distinguishable from a completeness-failure plus imprecision view, or does it collapse into it? What would settle the question, given that both views predict the same pattern of hesitant, locally confident judgments?

Two. Vagueness and epistemic deflation. The continuum arguments work by chaining comparisons each of which is individually plausible, typically near a threshold. Is the intransitivity verdict an artifact of chaining borderline judgments? What would it take to show that the links are determinate rather than borderline — and can that be shown from the armchair at all?

Three. The calibration disanalogy. In domains where we have independently validated, transitive-by-construction measures — health-state indices of the quality-adjusted-life-year sort, skill ratings in competitive games — pairwise human comparative judgments do display intransitivities, and we uniformly treat those as noise and the measure as the truth. What exactly is my disanalogy between those domains and evaluative betterness, and is it principled or ad hoc? Note that I cannot answer 'there is no measure in the moral case' without saying why the absence of a measure is evidence of intransitivity rather than evidence of our ignorance.

Four. Sequential improvement. If each step in a chain is an improvement, it is natural to say the whole sequence is an improvement; but on my view a sequence of improvements can return you to where you began, or leave you worse. Try to turn this into an actual argument against me — for instance by adding a replication or doubling principle, so that many copies of the starting point can be compared with many copies of the endpoint, and see whether you can derive a violation of dominance or a flat contradiction. If the construction fails, say precisely where and why it fails.

Five. Loss of the monadic. CSW appears to leave no room for how good an outcome is, full stop, only for how it fares against this comparandum. It is also unclear what becomes of 'the best of the options', or of maximizing as a decision procedure, if betterness can cycle over a finite menu. How much of this can I absorb as a cost, and at what point does absorbing it turn my view into a different view — a pure comparativism on which the pairwise relation is fundamental and monadic value is an illusion, or a buck-passing view on which betterness is explained by facts about what it is correct to choose or to prefer?

Six. The social choice analogy, which cuts both ways. Majority preference can cycle even when every voter's ordering is transitive, and the standard impossibility results show how hard it is to aggregate orderings while preserving transitivity. I have used this to argue that a relation assembled out of respect-relative betterness need not be transitive. But if I take the analogy seriously, I should also expect that there is frequently no best option, and I inherit whatever is troubling about that. Worse: if respect-relative betterness is itself transitive and only its aggregation fails, it looks as though my view is not really about betterness but about some second-order relation, and the interesting claim has moved.

Seven. Psychological confounders. Reference-point and direction-of-comparison effects, asymmetric similarity, loss aversion: all of these independently predict intransitive patterns in pairwise verdicts. What would distinguish my claim that the relation itself is intransitive from the claim that my intuitions about it are contaminated? Be honest about how much of my evidence is introspective.

Eight. The insulation worry, which I take most seriously of all. Because CSW predicts intransitivity only where dimensions are many, aspect-spread is wide, and margins are small — that is, exactly where verdicts are hardest to trust — have I qualified the view into something unfalsifiable? If so, is that a defect for a claim of this kind, given that it is a conceptual and normative claim rather than an empirical one? Tell me what unfalsifiability is worth here, and whether a view like mine should be expected to risk anything.
