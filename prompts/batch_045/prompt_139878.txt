I work as a backend/infrastructure engineer and read about this stuff on weekends, so I have no formal philosophy background and may be misusing terms. I'd like an audit of a cluster of views I've ended up holding about uncertainty. They feel consistent to me, but someone I trust says the first two can't both be right.

(1) I think ambiguity aversion is a bug rather than a legitimate attitude. In the classic setup — one urn with a known 50/50 red-black composition, another whose composition is completely unknown, one draw each — I think preferring to bet on red from the known urn is an error, and that the only way to make that preference come out is to break the principle that your choice shouldn't hinge on how you'd have felt in outcomes that don't occur. I'm also willing to bite a related bullet: I would refuse to pay even a token amount to have the unknown urn's composition replaced by a public coin flip, since that doesn't change anything about the draw I'm facing.

(2) At the same time, at work I refuse to provision against a single guessed distribution. I size for the worst case within the set of traffic models I can't rule out, and I regard a reliability argument that only holds under one assumed prior as no argument at all. I don't think of this as a pragmatic concession to limited time or compute; I think it's what rationality demands when the model class itself is unknown.

(3) Here's a case where I can't tell whether my own rule applies. Suppose the unknown urn was filled by a technician told only "some reds, some blacks," who normally fills close to 50/50 but, when rushed, just dumps in whatever is in the crate, which is 90% red — and whether they were rushed is unobservable to me. Here I find it obviously reasonable to prefer the known urn, and my explanation is that there's a known generating mechanism rather than brute ignorance. But I can't articulate why the existence of a mechanism should matter if (1) is correct.

(4) On the replication crisis in experimental work: I think it's mostly a selection and reporting problem, and that preregistration plus tighter thresholds fix more of it than switching to Bayesian methods would.

(5) Relatedly, I run Bayesian-style A/B tests at work using a weak prior chosen for its smoothing behavior, and I also require that the same pipeline have valid long-run frequentist error guarantees. I've been told these are in conflict; I think they aren't, but I'd like to know if I'm kidding myself.

Which of these genuinely conflict, and which only look like they do? Please rank the real conflicts by severity, say for each what I'd have to give up or weaken, and be blunt about whether my distinctions in (3) and (5) are doing real work or are rationalizations. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and what it would cost me elsewhere.
