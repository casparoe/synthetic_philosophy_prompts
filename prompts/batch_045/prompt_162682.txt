**The question (Question 3 of 4, 20 points).**

On Monday there is a lump of clay on a studio shelf; call it Clay. By Friday a sculptor has shaped that same clay, adding and removing nothing, into a statue; call it David. On Friday, David and Clay occupy exactly the same region of space and are composed of exactly the same matter. Intuitively, though, they differ: Clay existed on Monday and David did not; and Clay could survive being rolled into a ball, whereas David could not survive being squashed into a ball (even though the resulting ball would still be Clay).

Are David and Clay one object or two? Your answer must:

(a) set out the argument from Leibniz's Law in the form given on the handout — if x = y, then x and y have all the same properties — and identify which premise an opponent would most plausibly reject;
(b) address the Difference-Maker Principle (DMP), introduced in Week 6: necessarily, if x and y are coincident material objects, then some non-modal, non-haecceitistic property that x has and y lacks explains why they differ in their persistence conditions;
(c) say briefly how a defender of temporal parts would handle the case, and whether that treatment costs more or less than the constitution view.

Answers were told that a strong paper engages the best version of the view it rejects, not the weakest.

**The student's answer.**

1. The Leibniz's Law argument. Suppose, for reductio, that David = Clay. Clay can survive being squashed into a ball; David cannot. So Clay has a property — being able to survive squashing — that David lacks. By Leibniz's Law, if David were Clay, they would share all their properties. They don't, so David is not Clay. The temporal point gives the same result independently: Clay existed on Monday, David did not, and existence on Monday is a property too.

Which premise could an opponent reject? Not the modal one. It is simply obvious that the lump could survive squashing while the statue could not; anyone who denies this has confused the claim "Clay can survive being squashed" with the quite different claim "Clay can survive being squashed *and still be a statue*." So the identity theorist cannot reject the modal premise. Her only remaining options are to reject Leibniz's Law itself, or to retreat to the idea that identity is relative to a sortal — which is just a denial of Leibniz's Law wearing a technical costume, since it lets one thing have and lack the same property depending on how we describe it. Neither move is available in a course that takes Leibniz's Law as a constraint. Hence David and Clay are two objects, not one.

2. The positive view. I hold that David is constituted by Clay but not identical with it. Constitution is not identity, and it is not a mereological relation either: David is not part of Clay, and Clay is not part of David. It is the relation something bears to the matter that makes it up when the two "share" a place. Accepting this means accepting coincidence: there are at least two material objects in exactly the same place at the same time on Friday. That is not mysterious. Coincidence is only puzzling if you assume in advance that spatial occupation is exclusive, and nothing in the argument above requires that assumption.

3. The Difference-Maker Principle. DMP sounds demanding but is easily met. The differentia is *being statue-shaped*. David has that property essentially — a thing that ceased to be statue-shaped would cease to be David — whereas Clay has it only accidentally, since Clay could lose it and go on existing. The property itself is a perfectly ordinary, non-modal shape property; what is modal is only the manner in which each object has it. So DMP is satisfied: the objects differ in how a non-modal property is had by them, and that difference explains why they have different persistence conditions. (If one prefers a different differentia, take the historical property *having been shaped by a sculptor*: David has that property essentially, Clay only accidentally. The argument is the same.) The objection that coincident objects must be "indistinguishable in principle" therefore fails.

4. Temporal parts. The four-dimensionalist will say that David and Clay are two distinct sums of temporal parts. But that is only the coincidence view in four-dimensional vocabulary, so it inherits every problem the constitution view has and buys nothing. I set it aside.
