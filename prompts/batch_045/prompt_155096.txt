I am in the first year of my doctorate, and in five weeks I am presenting a paper in my supervisor's research seminar on whether the criteria of authenticity can do the work historical-Jesus scholars ask of them. My supervisor — I will call her Professor Alderton — has spent her career defending a small but firm core: that Jesus was a Galilean Jew, baptized by John, who announced an imminent divine kingdom in eschatological terms, and whose ethical teaching took the form of radical, unnegotiable demand. She is not a skeptic about the criteria, but she is severe about how they are used, and she has a long track record of individual verdicts. She grades in two steps only: yes or no.

The verdicts below come from her published work, her seminar comments, and conversations over the past year. I record only what she has said, not why.

1. The baptism of Jesus by John: yes.
2. The absolute prohibition of divorce, in the version without the exception clauses that appear in one of the gospels: yes.
3. The command to love one's enemies and to pray for those who persecute you: yes.
4. The saying in which the kingdom is said not to come with observable signs but to be already present in or among the hearers: no. A variant of the same idea appears in a fourth-century sayings gospel, which did not change her mind.
5. The apocalyptic saying in which the Son of Man is depicted as coming on the clouds with power and glory: yes.
6. The promise that a particular disciple is the rock on which the community will be built: no.
7. The saying in which Jesus claims an exclusive and mutual knowledge between himself as Son and God as Father, in language with no close parallel in Jewish literature of the period: no.
8. The use of the Aramaic word for father as the address in prayer: yes.
9. The cry of abandonment from the cross, in the wording of the opening of a psalm: no.
10. A saying preserved only in the fourth-century sayings gospel, pronouncing a blessing on a lion that becomes human: no.
11. The saying about rendering to the emperor what is his and to God what is God's: yes.

The case I want to put to her is not a discovery; I have constructed it for the seminar because its features pull in different directions, and I would like to know where she will land before I put it in front of her. Suppose that an unprovenanced parchment leaf, written in a Syriac dialect and assigned on palaeographical grounds to the middle of the fourth century, preserves a saying of Jesus that also appears, in Greek, in a passage of the Gospel of Mark, and — in a form differing only in word order — in the sayings source shared by Matthew and Luke. The saying, in the form I have drafted it: the kingdom is not a thing that is coming but a yoke already laid on the shoulders of those who seek, and whoever seeks will find it in the doing of the least of these commandments. As stipulated, it has two independent early streams behind it, with the leaf as a late third witness; it has no close parallel in Second Temple Jewish literature for the combination it expresses; and it would have been awkward for the predominantly Gentile church of the late first century, since it appears to assume that the distinctions of the Torah remain permanently binding on Israel. Nothing in it looks generated out of Scripture, and it is hard to name a later group whose interests it serves, though the Torah-observant Jewish-Christian communities are the obvious candidate — an explanation she has used elsewhere, but sparingly.

I would be grateful for an answer in five parts.

First: state, as explicitly and as generally as you can, the two or three rules that fit her eleven verdicts jointly. I am interested in rules general enough to be applied to a case she has not seen, not in a summary of each verdict. If more than one rule fits, I want to know which verdicts each of them handles more or less comfortably.

Second: what does each rule predict about the hypothetical leaf, and why?

Third: your best guess at her answer, with the reasons for preferring the rule that yields it over the others, and with the closest analog among verdicts 1 through 11 identified — including, where it matters, the respects in which that analog is not really parallel.

Fourth: if the rules that survive your first part still agree on this case, or if the case does not separate them cleanly, name the single further case you would have me draft — a sayings-form, attestation-profile, and content — that would force her rule into the open.

Fifth: in what form should I put the case to her in the seminar? If I announce the candidate rules myself, she will simply adopt whichever formulation sounds most defensible, and I will have learned nothing about what she is actually doing. I would like a way of framing the question — the wording, or the order in which I present the features — such that her answer reveals the rule even though she has not been asked to state it.
