Say I'm on a small team that has to decide whether to keep running a big simulation of a nervous system, or throttle it, or turn it off — and the honest answer is that none of us know whether anything in there matters morally, or whether it matters once or a million times over. Before I go read any theories of this, I want the spec: what a theory of *which computations count* would have to get right to actually be usable for a decision like that. So give me the requirements list with the motivating case behind each. The cases I keep circling: gradual replacement of neurons with functionally identical silicon parts (I assume the theory shouldn't give me a cliff where one swap flips everything, but say if that's really non-negotiable); the fact that under a loose enough mapping basically any physical system realizes any computation, so the theory has to say what distinguishes the realizations that matter from the cheap ones; a lookup table that already contains the input-output pattern of an entire life and just indexes it; the same program run on two machines at once, and separately run again but with its outputs discarded and no way to affect anything; the same program run a thousand times slower, with its causal structure intact; a system whose parts are each themselves implementations of some mind while the whole implements another one; and the plain fact that a system might be suffering and never say so, or say so in a way we have no reason to trust. For each requirement say whether it's genuinely required or just a prejudice of one camp, flag any pair you think can't be jointly satisfied (I suspect the anti-triviality stuff and "it's causal structure all the way down" are in tension, but you tell me), and say which single requirement rules out the most candidates. A very short scorecard at the end is fine — functionalist/substrate-independent views, biological or embodied ones, pure causal-structure ones — but keep the weight on the criteria.
