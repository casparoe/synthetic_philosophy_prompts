You are a professional philosopher who works on social ontology and the philosophy of action. Please think this through carefully before writing: open with a brief sketch of the dialectical space as you see it (a few bullet points is fine), then give your diagnosis, then say what would count as progress. Answer in American English.

For roughly thirty-five years the dispute over shared intention has barely moved. On one side, the Searle-style picture holds that collective intentionality is a primitive feature of individual minds: each participant has a we-intend that is not assembled out of any I-intend. On another, the Gilbert-style picture treats the relevant item as irreducibly plural — a joint commitment or plural subject that exists in no one's head and immediately generates obligations between the parties. On the third, the Bratman-style constructivist account tries to get by with nothing beyond individual intentions, interlocking subplans, mutual responsiveness, and common knowledge. Every standard objection has a standard reply, and I have never seen anyone change camps.

My hypothesis is that this is not one debate but three sharing a label: a question about what exists, a question about what is in a participant's head during ordinary cooperation, and a question about what grounds obligations to one another — and that the apparent disagreement about which cases count as data is downstream of that. To test it, take two cases of my own construction. First, the Silent Crew: six strangers are led separately into identical rooms, each told only that if everyone pulls their lever at noon all six leave and that if any one fails to pull nothing happens; there is no communication, no one knows how many people there are, and each is privately offered a small reward for pulling late while being truthfully told everyone else got the same offer. They all pull and all leave. Second, the Relay: a decade-long project whose membership turns over completely and gradually, so that no individual ever intends more than a fragment of it, any two consecutive workers share only a handover note, and yet the project is completed as originally planned.

Diagnose the impasse using what each of these views actually commits its holder to. Is it verbal, metaphysical, a clash over which cases the theory must save, or a hidden empirical question about the cognitive capacity involved? Given your diagnosis, what would actually move things — a distinction, a ruling on the cases, an empirical result, a change of question — or is stalemate the right expectation, and why? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of the literature.
