I'm in a little reading group that's slowly working through contractualist ethics — the reasonable-rejectability version, where wrongness is a matter of whether an act would be ruled out by principles that nobody could reasonably reject. Last week we got onto the aggregation problem, and someone in the group wheeled out the old slogan that everyone counts for one and none for more than one, and used it as a knockdown: if contractualism won't let a million tiny harms outweigh one person's much bigger harm, then contractualism is basically saying the million don't matter, or at least matter less per person, which is elitist nonsense. I pushed back a bit but honestly I don't think I was very clear, and I want to reply properly in the group chat this week.

The thing is, I don't think that slogan is anywhere near as obvious as it sounds, and I suspect it's doing several different jobs at once. Here's what I've been mulling: there's a difference between (a) the claim that each affected person gets to be a complainant at all — nobody's interests get silently dropped from the comparison, (b) the claim that each person's complaint gets equal weight in some sense, and (c) the claim that complaints should be summed, so that a big enough pile of small ones beats a single large one. Those feel separable to me but I can't quite say how or why. I also had a half-baked idea that numbers might enter not by multiplying a complaint but by changing which principle it would be reasonable to reject — e.g. maybe the fact that a rule would burden a million people is itself a reason to reject it, even if no individual burden is worse than the alternative rule's burden on the one. Or maybe numbers only work lexically, as a tie-breaker once complaints are in the same ballpark of strength. I made up terms for these in my notes — something like headcount-monotonic versus strength-monotonic versus lexically-monotonic — and I'd like to know whether any of them is even coherent.

There's also a wrinkle in the example we were arguing about that I think makes it worse: imagine the million people each get a headache so mild that none of them would bother to take a pill or complain about it even if asked, while the one person loses a limb. And then imagine a variant where the million headaches are just as mild but each person would be annoyed as hell about being ignored, and a variant where the million never find out they were affected at all. My sense is the slogan's verdict is supposed to be the same in all three, and that's exactly what makes me suspicious of it. So: can you lay out the precise theses that slogan could be expressing, from the trivial to the strong, and for each say whether it's something almost everyone already accepts, something substantive and defensible, something a contractualist in particular should reject, or something just false? And crucially: which reading would my group-mate's argument actually need in order to go through, and is that reading one of the plausible ones? A menu with verdicts is what I'm after — I want to bring it to the chat, not to be told which side wins.
