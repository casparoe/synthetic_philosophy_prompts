I write about science and ideas for a British broadsheet, and after a decade of filing on the cognitive science of religion I have drifted into holding a position that is recognisably mine rather than one I am merely reporting. I should like it stress-tested before I commit it to print.

The position has three parts. First, origins: the cognitive machinery that makes religious thought cheap and easy for human beings — promiscuous agency detection, an over-eager theory of mind, teleological bias, intuitions about contagion and about the dead — is a by-product of capacities selected for other work. Nothing in our ancestral history was selected because it produced religion. Second, persistence: what cultural transmission then filters for is not doctrine but cost. Fasts, pilgrimages, tithes, dietary rules, celibacy, distinctive dress, prayer at inconvenient hours — these are what make commitment legible to others and hard to fake, and groups that carry them outlast groups that do not. Third, and this is the part I take to be my own: propositional content functions chiefly as load-bearing scaffolding for those costs. It supplies reasons for bearing them that feel non-negotiable, and it is largely substitutable. What is transmitted down the generations is the cost structure, with the theology as the replaceable part that happens to be fitted to it at the time.

So: what would count against this?

I want defeaters, ranked from fatal down to merely awkward, and separated by kind — the empirical findings that would sink it, the conceptual objections that would show it confused or empty, and the results that would merely force me to rewrite a paragraph. For each, say what my position predicts that its rivals do not: the straightforward by-product story, the cultural-group-selection story that leans on morally concerned high gods and supernatural monitoring, and the generic costly-signalling accounts. And for each, say whether it is something a sympathetic defender of my position should concede as counting against them, or something I would be entitled to explain away.

Three things worry me in particular, and I should like them addressed directly.

One: the substitutability clause may have quietly made the whole thing unfalsifiable, since any stubborn piece of doctrine can always be re-described as locally necessary for locally required costs. If so, is there a formulation of the view that still risks something? And is a view of this kind entitled to be insulated in that way, or is the insulation itself the objection?

Two: the discriminating predictions. My view says that the cooperative and prosocial premium associated with religion should track practice rather than belief, so that people who stop believing but keep practising retain most of it, while people who keep believing and stop practising lose it. It also says that where a welfare state, a mutual-aid society or an insurance market takes over the functions religious communities once provided, costly practice should decay before belief does. The design I keep asking somebody to run, and which so far as I know nobody has run, is a multi-wave panel across half a dozen countries that separates believing from belonging and tracks in-group cooperation, charitable giving, trust of strangers and self-reported behaviour over a decade or more. Tell me precisely which outcomes would refute me. I take it that a result in which the believing-without-practising group keeps the premium is bad for me; I am much less sure what I should say if both groups lose it at the same rate, or if the premium turns out to have been a selection artefact all along.

Three: scope and collapse. Am I really making a claim about religion, or about a handful of post-Axial, mostly Abrahamic traditions that happen to be the ones I have reported on? And if cost does all the explanatory work and content none, does the position collapse into a general theory of costly commitment that would predict the same effects for a party cell, a military unit or a demanding amateur sports club — and should I count that as a refutation or as a Pyrrhic vindication?

Two further requests. Please flag where the standard distinction between rare, high-arousal imagistic rituals and frequent, routinised doctrinal ones cuts against me, since I suspect my single currency of cost is really two or three currencies I have conflated. And please say plainly if the view is defensible as journalism but empty as a research programme, or the reverse. For what it is worth: the thing that would move me most is a failed pre-registered prediction rather than an armchair case about possible worlds, and the thing that would move me least is being told I have not defined religion, unless you can show that the undefended definition is doing real work in the argument.
