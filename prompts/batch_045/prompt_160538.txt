I am writing as a retired atmospheric physicist: forty years of measurement, modelling and forecast verification, and, since I stopped working, five years of reading philosophy properly for the first time. One habit I picked up from my old trade I have kept — a scored forecasting log. For every question I write down a number, and when the world delivers an answer I score myself with a strictly proper rule: a rule built so that my expected score is highest if the number I write down is the number I actually believe, so that honesty pays and nothing else does. Please take that description as given; it is all you will need.

I want to put a difficulty to Keynes himself, so please play him. You are John Maynard Keynes as you were in 1946: Bloomsbury manner, exact, quick to concede a minor point and immovable on the major one, with your treatment of probability and of the weight of argument fully at your command. You know nothing of anything discovered after 1946 and may use only the concepts available to you then, plus what I have told you here; if you need a fact about modern practice, ask for it as Keynes would ask. Do not step out of character to explain scoring rules or decision theory to me, and stay in character until I say stop.

Here is my difficulty. In A Treatise on Probability you separated the probability of an argument from its weight — the balance of relevant knowledge against relevant ignorance behind it — held that new evidence always adds weight even when it lowers the probability, and held that between two equal probabilities the greater weight is the one to act on. Now take two forecasts, each written as 0.7, for the same question: one resting on a single suggestive analogue year, the other on forty years of replicated measurement whose error structure I understand. Under a strictly proper rule the expected score depends only on the number reported and on the outcomes that number implies; it is blind to what stands behind it. On the only modern account of what makes a reported probability honest, then, your weight does no work whatsoever. I can see one way of rescuing it: stop scoring the probability and score instead the forecaster's own expectation of how far that probability will move once more evidence arrives — that is, turn weight into a second forecast, a forecast about one's own future credence. My question is whether you accept that translation, or whether it loses precisely what you meant: because if it loses it, I want to know what work weight was doing that no number can do. Answer me, please, in a letter: exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no preamble and no summing-up. I will reply in the same form.
