hey, I'm defending a chapter of my master's thesis next thursday and I need Q&A prep pretty badly. 20 min talk, then 10 min of questions, and I already know the questions are going to be worse than the talk.

The chapter is on Marx and automation. The claim I'm defending: the "post-work" reading of Marx that's popular right now (fully automated luxury communism and friends) gets the direction of history roughly right but the mechanism wrong, and that matters because the mechanism is where the politics comes from. My argument in three steps:

(1) A capitalist firm buys a machine only where the machine is cheaper than the living labor it replaces, so automation under capitalism is always partial and always aimed at the most expensive / most organized / most replaceable workers first. It's labor-displacing only incidentally and labor-degrading essentially.

(2) The productivity gain is captured privately and immediately, while the erosion of the value basis shows up later and only in the aggregate. So what automation delivers under capitalism isn't a leisure dividend, it's a kind of scissors: output and throughput rise, the mass of living labor that is the only source of new value falls, and the system adjusts with longer hours, cheaper labor or crisis rather than shorter hours. I call that mismatch the capture/erosion gap, and it's the bit I actually care about.

(3) So the conclusion isn't "accelerate the automation and wait for abundance," it's fight over the machine and over the length of the working day.

The wrinkle I bolt on at the end, and the part I think is actually new: machine learning breaks step (1) in a way the classical story didn't anticipate, because it automates not just the execution of tasks but part of the coordinating, knowledge-holding function Marx talks about in the 1850s Grundrisse notebooks — the collective worker's brain, not just its hands. So the labor-degrading story has to be revised: capital can now degrade and partially replace the supervisory/cognitive layer too, and I argue that tightens the scissors rather than loosening them, because the thing being automated is the thing that used to hold the whole production process together.
