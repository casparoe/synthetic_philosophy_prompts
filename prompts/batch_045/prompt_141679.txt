I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy training beyond a couple of podcasts, and I keep running into the same shape of situation in completely unrelated settings: a standards-committee fight at work, a funding dispute on an open-source project I help maintain, and a school-board fight in the town where I grew up. In each case there was an outcome that both sides preferred to the status quo, that both sides privately admitted was the best available result, and that would nevertheless have collapsed immediately if anyone had said out loud why it was good. What each side needed in order to sign on was not the substance it won but the ability to go back to its own people and credibly say "we lost something real here." The open-source case is the one I know best. Half the maintainers thought taking money from a surveillance-software company made them complicit; the other half thought refusing the money was self-indulgent. The arrangement we ended up with took the money, routed it into an untagged general infrastructure fund, kept the company's name off every page and release note, and let the objecting maintainers register their objection in writing in the repo. Nobody's first choice; everyone preferred it to the alternatives. But two things about it were strange. The objectors could only accept it while describing it as a defeat — when the other side posted a blog entry saying the objectors had come around and agreed the funding was fine, the objectors were forced to escalate again, even though nothing about the actual arrangement had changed. And the funding side had the mirror-image problem: they needed the money to have cost them something. So the deal depended on both sides leaving unchallenged a story both sides knew was false, and anyone who pointed out "you both actually won" was treated as an enemy by everyone.

What makes it more than ordinary grumbling, I think, is where the gain came from. Neither side gave up much of what it actually cared about: the objectors cared about their own project's endorsement, not about whether that company's money existed somewhere in the world, and the funders cared about the money, not about the logo. The trade was possible because each side's operative concern was narrower than the principle it stated, and the principle was the thing its own constituency was holding it to. That is why the real justification can't be published: publishing it narrows the principle in public, and the constituency that was enforcing the wide version then punishes the person who accepted the deal. A friend who took one econ class said it sounded like "a Pareto improvement that dissolves when you point at it," which is cute but doesn't sound like a term of art, and it isn't quite right either, because the improvement isn't merely unsayable to the other side — each side can say it to the other side in private and it still has to stay out of public.

So my question, and I would much rather have a careful carving than a label to drop in my next meeting: is there an established concept for this, and if so what exactly are its edges? I can't tell which ingredient is doing the work — the requirement that the appearance of loss be credible to a home audience, the fact that making the true justification public destroys the arrangement, the fact that what is being traded is not a policy but which side is seen to have been overruled, or the mismatch between a stated principle and a narrower operative concern. Which of these could I remove and still have the same phenomenon? Does it survive if both sides are fully honest with their own constituents, or if the two constituencies overlap and read each other's blogs, or if one side genuinely does prefer the middle option, or if the concessions are made by representatives whose constituents will never check? And if there is no established concept, I'd like a name plus a definition tight enough that a careful person would classify a new case the way I would, along with the nearest misses it has to be distinguished from — because "compromise," "hypocrisy," and "saving face" each seem to catch part of it and explain away the rest.
