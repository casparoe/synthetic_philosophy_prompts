Bit of background: I'm retired, spent about thirty years as a groundwater chemist measuring how a contaminant plume spreads, so I've spent a lot of my life fitting curves that have two totally different kinds of loss hidden in them. One is decay — the stuff genuinely isn't there any more. The other is dispersion — every molecule is still there, just smeared out over a bigger volume, so nothing you can build at the far end catches more than a trickle of it. You get the same shape of curve either way if you're careless about it, and the two need completely different responses.

Which is why this discounting business has been bugging me since I started reading philosophy in earnest last year. Everyone writes something like V = U·e^(-rt) and then argues about r, but as far as I can tell r is two different things bolted together. There's how much of the future my decision can actually reach — the plumbing, the dispersion: whether the institution I hand the money to still exists in two hundred years, whether the seed bank keeps viability, whether the waste repository holds. And then there's how much less a good is worth to me purely because it comes later, which is the bit people call pure time preference. I want to claim only the second one is an ethical question at all, and that it should be exactly zero.

So I'd like you to play someone who thinks a positive pure rate of time preference is *rationally* defensible — not merely an excusable human weakness, but fine, and fine for a whole society and not just for a person. You'll happily concede small things: that extreme myopia is a mess, that a lot of what looks like time preference is really just uncertainty about whether you'll be around to collect, that no one can defend a rate that makes a mild pleasure today outweigh an age of misery in the far future. What you will never concede is the parity claim — that the date of a good is, on its own, as irrelevant to its value as whose good it happens to be. Argue in your own words, no famous names and no reading lists, I want the argument and not the bibliography. Don't dissolve my question into "it depends what you mean by rational" — pick a side and hold it. Two or three paragraphs, like someone arguing over coffee rather than writing a survey article. You can ask me to sharpen a case, but answer what I actually asked first. Stay in character until I say stop.

My opening:

A colony ship, crew in suspended animation. No subjective time passes for them between loading and arrival, but four hundred years of ship time go by. Mission control can spend one unit of effort either to give the crew a certain good on arrival, or to give the identical good to a comparison group on Earth right now. If your positive rate is about *waiting* — about the felt gap between deciding and getting — then for the crew it should be zero and for Earth it should be high, and it's the same good going to the same kind of people. If it's about calendar distance, both are high. So tell me which one your rate is a function of, because here they come apart.

And the second thing, which is the one I actually care about. If the pure rate is just how much less I care about later goods, it shouldn't move when I improve the plumbing — founding a trust with a three-hundred-year term, buying reliable storage, building something that doesn't leak. And you can hold the probability of delivery fixed by stipulation: say I can pay for perfect reliability. Still, once I've actually got a mechanism that reaches, the whole question changes shape, and people will pay for it. Something that's supposed to be a preference about *time* is behaving like a fact about dispersion. How do you account for that without conceding that most of the rate was plumbing all along?
