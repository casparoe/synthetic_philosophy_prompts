I'm a second-year PhD student in cosmology, and at last week's group meeting I made the mistake of raising a worry I haven't been able to shake. Our group compares two different ways of counting observers in an eternally inflating spacetime — two "measures," as we call them. Both fit every observation we currently have, but they disagree by many orders of magnitude about what we should expect to see in the next round of data. My question was whether the choice between them is the kind of thing we could ever settle by evidence, or whether we are quietly making a decision about what counts as a typical observer and then congratulating ourselves when the data come out the way that decision predicted. My supervisor answered with a line I have now heard three separate times in this department, and which gets trotted out whenever someone raises anything remotely conceptual: "Physics doesn't need philosophy's permission slip, and philosophy never hands back anything you can put in an error bar." (A postdoc in the group prefers the older joke about ornithology and birds; I take it he means the same thing, but I'm not sure he does.)

I don't want a literature survey, a reading list, or citations — I can't look anything up, and I've already heard the famous broadsides from the physics side. What I want is a disambiguation I can bring back to the group. Please lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses that slogan could be expressing, ordered roughly from the platitudinous to the wildly overcommitted, and for each one tell me: what exactly it asserts (distinguishing, where it matters, normative authority over what we investigate from authority over what we should believe, philosophy as actually practised from philosophy as such, individual physicists from physics as an institution, and "can't be measured" as in not-measurable-now versus not-measurable-in-principle); whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, contentious, or straightforwardly false; whether any of it is self-undermining in the way a demarcation criterion tends to be; and — most important for me — whether that reading would actually entitle my supervisor to set my worry aside. I have a rough hypothesis of my own that I'd like you to test rather than flatter: I suspect the slogan is trading on a slide from the perfectly reasonable claim that physicists need no one else's leave to chase a research programme, to the much stronger claim that no conceptual or methodological reasoning outside physics can bear on whether our evidence supports our conclusions, and that only the second claim would do the dismissive work it's being used for here. Is that the right place to cut, and is the stronger claim defensible? Finally: treat the measure case as a stress test for the second half of the slogan specifically. Here the choice in question is not itself measurable with the data we have, yet it visibly changes what we predict we will measure — so it seems to be neither measurable nor irrelevant to measurement. Say which of your readings that combination causes trouble for, and which of them shrug it off.
