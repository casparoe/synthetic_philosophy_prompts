You are a working political speechwriter who also holds a doctorate in political philosophy — someone who has spent years putting lines in the mouths of elected officials and who is unusually alert to the gap between a sentence that moves a room and a sentence that is true. Think the assignment through carefully before you draft: begin with a few paragraphs of working notes in which you work out what an honest version of this speech can and cannot say, and where the honest version departs from the one a campaign consultant would hand me. Then give me the speech itself. Write in American English throughout, in a register that sounds spoken rather than written.

The occasion. Tomorrow is election day on a statewide ballot measure that would take redistricting away from the legislature and give it to an independent commission. The last public poll had our side at 50.4 to 49.6. Expected turnout is roughly 1.1 million. I am the field director for the yes campaign, and tonight I am giving the closing ten-minute speech at our final volunteer rally: a high school gym, about three hundred people. Two-thirds of them have been phone-banking or canvassing for weeks; maybe sixty walked in tonight for the first time. My old political theory professor is standing at the back near the doors. She taught me the paradox of voting in my first week of college, she has come to three of our rallies, and she will be listening specifically for whether I tell three hundred people something I know to be false in order to get them to knock on doors tomorrow.

Here is what I have worked out, and what the speech has to live inside.

The expected effect of any single vote on the outcome is, by our consulting statistician's estimate, on the order of one in a thousand at best, and she thinks that is generous. So I cannot tell the room that any given person's vote will be the difference. The standard version of that line — the one about how this election will be decided by a handful of votes, and one of them might be yours — is off limits. Our own text bank tested that framing last month against a plainer one, and turnout among low-propensity voters came in a few tenths of a point lower: it seemed to make people feel like spectators at a coin toss rather than participants. Relatedly, I cannot tell any one of them that we need them. We need a few hundred of them; any particular person in that gym could be replaced by a stranger and the campaign would not notice. And I cannot appeal to anybody being thanked, seen, or credited for voting, because the ballot is secret and no one will ever know how anyone in that room voted. I also do not want the move where the franchise is described as something won for us at great cost by earlier generations. My grandmother used it constantly. It does not answer the objection; it changes the subject.

What makes this interesting is that I do not think the paradox settles the question, and I would rather the speech say why than pretend otherwise. Two things the statistician has convinced me of. First: that one-in-a-thousand figure is an ex ante probability per person, but in the possible worlds where the margin really does come out at zero or one, an enormous number of people are each individually decisive — everyone who voted on one side or the other is such that, had they stayed home, the result would have gone the other way. So hundreds of thousands of us may turn out to have mattered, while none of us can know beforehand which of us they are, or know afterwards either, since nobody ever observes what would have happened. I would like the speech to do something with the gap between what can honestly be said to you in advance and what may turn out to be true of you. Second: the campaign's own decision to spend money on getting out the vote is justified in a way that no individual's decision to vote obviously is. We shift the expected margin by a few thousand votes, which is a real effect, and it is an effect that belongs to the campaign and not to any voter inside it. I am standing on one side of that gap asking people who are standing on the other. Do not paper over it; I would rather you point straight at it.

One last thing about the audience. Almost everyone in that gym is going to vote no matter what I say tonight. What I actually need from them is that they spend all day tomorrow calling and driving low-propensity voters, and that when a stranger answers the phone and asks, skeptically and not stupidly, why anyone should bother when it is only one vote, they have a reply that is neither a lie nor a shrug. So write the speech for the room and, through the room, for that phone call: give them something they can repeat in about fifteen seconds to a tired person who did not ask to be called.

Constraints on the finished text: ten minutes, around 1,100 words; no bullet points or headings inside the speech proper; do not name the professor or attribute any view or finding to a real author, study, or publication, since the people in the room would not recognize the names anyway. Deliver your working notes first, then the speech.
