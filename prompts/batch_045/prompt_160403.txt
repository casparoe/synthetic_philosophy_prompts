Do this for five claims separately:

1. Scale: if nothing goes badly wrong, the future contains vastly more of what's valuable than has existed so far, so most of what is morally at stake in any decision we make now sits in the very distant future rather than the near term.
2. Waste: letting that potential go unrealized — extinction, permanent civilizational collapse, some other irreversible catastrophe — is a loss orders of magnitude larger than everything bad that has happened up to now.
3. Leverage: we happen to live at a point where the long-run trajectory is unusually plastic, present action has unusually high leverage, and that window is temporary.
4. Priority: from 1–3, the best use of marginal effort and money is normally to improve the very long-run future, and concretely to reduce existential risk, rather than to relieve present suffering.
5. Robustness and asymmetry: the priority claim doesn't stand or fall with total utilitarianism or with any particular population axiology, because across a wide range of such views the magnitudes swamp the disagreements; and it's apparently much easier to wreck the long-run future than to steer it well, so the case for risk-reduction is stronger than the case for ambitious trajectory change — and both depend on how much difference our actions really make.
