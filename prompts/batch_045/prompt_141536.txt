I sit on the standing revision committee for a national adaptation of an international diagnostic manual, and I am tired of watching our categories move under us. We meet three times a year; we are eight clinicians, one epidemiologist, one medical sociologist and two service-user representatives; each of us can spare about a day of preparation per item; and every change has to be defended in a published two-page rationale, because our codes drive billing, school accommodations, insurance and disability benefits, and both industry and advocacy groups will attack anything that looks like a guess. The pattern we keep hitting is that a category changes the people it classifies: prevalence climbs after a label becomes a usable social identity, patients arrive already fluent in the criteria, clinics build referral pathways and billing around a code, and within a decade the presentation we wrote criteria for is no longer the one we see. We have been handling this ad hoc, and I want it turned into a procedure.

Here is the crude vocabulary we have fallen into, which you should feel free to discard if it does no work: we distinguish loops that run through identity and self-narrative, through our own diagnostic instruments (a checklist that teaches patients which symptoms to endorse), through institutions (referral routes, accommodations, form design), and through markets (pharmaceutical marketing, self-help publishing, social-media communities); and we distinguish loops that tighten a presentation toward a stereotype from loops that splinter it into variants. We also suspect our baselines are contaminated before we even start, because draft criteria circulate and advocacy communities reorganise around a proposed category years before adoption, so that pre-adoption data already reflects a category that does not officially exist. Hacking's ideas about looping effects and interactive human kinds are obviously the theory here, but we cannot cite philosophy in a committee rationale; the procedure has to carry the theory implicitly, in a form a working clinician with no background in it can run in half a day using administrative data, published literature and clinical experience, and with explicit instructions for what to do when the number we want does not exist.

What I want is that procedure: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each given our constraints, the point at which it stops with a no, and the point at which it hands the decision over as a matter of values rather than facts. It must tell a loop apart from its lookalikes — genuine change in incidence, improved ascertainment, changes to the criteria themselves, diagnostic substitution, and compensation or litigation incentives — since those call for different responses and we have confused them before. It should say what we are allowed to do about a looping category: freeze the criteria, prefer symptom-descriptive over identity-shaped labels, narrow to a stabilised core phenotype, split, retire, or keep a code for access while excluding it from surveillance counts and research case definitions, and it should say which of those is a real remedy and which only hides the problem. It should specify what monitoring or sunset clause attaches to whatever we decide. And it should be honest about where it misfires: rare categories, categories anchored to a biomarker, categories adopted under time pressure. Then run it on three of our live items: adult attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder, where reported prevalence and prescribing have risen steeply and self-referral is now largely mediated by social media; paediatric bipolar disorder, which expanded in some countries and not others, in a way that looks institutionally and commercially driven; and a proposed new category for extreme avoidance of everyday demands, which a well-organised parent and patient community is pushing hard for and which several of us, myself included, want to adopt. I will be the one defending the third decision, so if the procedure returns an answer we will not like, give it anyway, and tell me what evidence within two cycles would change the verdict and where my argument is weakest.
