I have been reading about the post-war tribunals and have talked myself into an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept. Set out in steps:

(1) A verdict is an act of punishment rather than an act of revenge only if both of these hold: (a) the defendant is convicted of something that was already criminal, under a rule that bound him, at the time he acted; and (b) the court would have applied that same rule to people on its own side.

(2) The charges doing the real work at Nuremberg and Tokyo were new — though not in the sense people usually mean. Killing civilians was already murder under German and Japanese law. What was new was that the charges were international; that the Charter severed liability from local legality, defining crimes against humanity to cover acts "whether or not in violation of the domestic law of the country where perpetrated"; and that the answers a domestic court would have heard — superior orders, act of state, the defendant's official position — were withdrawn in advance, the first two expressly, and treated at most as grounds for mitigation. Call this what you like: the defendants lost, after the event, the defences they would have had when they acted.

(3) The tribunals were one-sided by construction. Only the defeated were in the dock; the area bombing of cities and the atomic bombings were never charged; the Japanese head of state was not indicted; and tu quoque was ruled out as a defence before the trials began.

(4) So both limbs of (1) fail, and the verdicts were revenge wearing a legal costume.

(5) Here is why I think the argument must be rotten: limb (b) would disqualify every criminal court that has ever sat. No state that punishes killing refrains from killing; no tribunal is drawn from both sides of a conflict; and if a court must be prepared to indict its own side before it has jurisdiction, then the alternative to victors' justice is not neutral justice but no trials at all — impunity for any aggressor who is never wholly defeated. An argument that ends by showing that nobody may ever punish anybody has proved too much.

(6) But when I drop (b), I lose my grip on (a). Both are fairness conditions; if the partiality of the tribunal does not defeat its verdicts, why should the novelty of the charges? And if novelty is fatal, then the later codifications look like an attempt to launder 1945 and 1946 after the event — including the exception written into the post-war European human rights instrument for acts that were already criminal under principles generally recognised among nations.

I want to keep (2) and (3), which seem to me to be facts, and I want to keep the judgement that the trials were worth holding, so I seem bound to give up something in (1). The mistake may equally lie in the move from (2) and (3) to (4), or in what "punishment" is doing in (1): I cannot locate it. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me?
