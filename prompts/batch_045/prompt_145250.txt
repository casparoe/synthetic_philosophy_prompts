so I go to this interdisciplinary reading group thing — half physics grad students, half theology and philosophy people — and we completely derailed last week on "God does not play dice with the universe." It came up because one of the physics guys used it to shut down a theology student who was arguing that quantum indeterminacy is the gap where free will lives. His move was basically: Einstein settled this, there's no genuine randomness, the universe is deterministic, end of story. The theology student then came back with: if Einstein is dragging God into a physics argument then he clearly believed in a personal creator who arranged things, so it's a religious claim and doesn't belong in a physics discussion. I'm pretty sure both of them are mangling it but I can't say exactly how.

The bit that's been nagging at me: I looked it up afterwards and the original seems to be from a letter to Max Born, and the wording is closer to "The theory delivers much but it hardly brings us closer to the Old One's secret. In any event, I am convinced that He is not playing dice." No capital-G God, no "with the universe," at least not in the version I found. So the popular one-liner has had extra stuff bolted onto it. Does the bolted-on stuff smuggle in a claim that isn't in the letter?

Can you separate out the different precise claims that sentence could be standing for? The cuts I came up with on the walk home were: probabilities in the formalism vs. chanciness in the world itself (I said Einstein only cared about the first, someone in the group said no, he cared about both); dice as ignorance about hidden variables, like in statistical mechanics, vs. brute objective chance vs. mere unpredictability that's compatible with determinism; a completeness claim about quantum mechanics vs. a metaphysical claim about nature vs. just a methodological rule about what kind of theory to keep looking for; and then whatever work "God" is doing, given that Einstein apparently used the word in that Spinoza-flavoured way where it more or less means the lawful order of nature rather than a person. I don't know which of those are the real distinctions and which are me inventing things.

For each reading I'd want a verdict: platitude, substantive and defensible, or actually false / settled the other way. And then the part I care about: which reading does the physics guy need for his free will argument, and does that reading survive Bell's theorem and the Bell test experiments? People keep telling me "Einstein lost that one," but someone else said de Broglie-Bohm is a perfectly deterministic hidden-variable theory that nothing ruled out, so I genuinely cant tell whether the no-dice claim is dead or only dead if you also want locality. And which reading does the theology student need — is "God" doing any real work in the sentence, or does her reading collapse it into something like "nature is lawful"?

Last thing: even granting determinism, the physics guy then jumped to "so theres no free will," and the theology student jumped to "so quantum randomness gives us free will," and neither leap looks right to me — randomness isn't agency, it's just noise. Someone also mentioned that Einstein himself denied free will and liked to repeat some line he took from Schopenhauer about being able to do what you want but not being able to want what you want, which I'm probably half remembering wrong. Where does that leave the slogan? Is it doing any work at all in an argument about free will, or is it purely a quantum foundations remark that people drag into free will debates because it has the word "God" and sounds like it's about freedom?

Give me the readings as some kind of structured list I can read out at the start of the next session, with what each of them would need to make their argument go, and whether it does.
