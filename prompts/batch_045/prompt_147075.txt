Q3 (20 marks, one short paragraph each).
(a) State the lottery paradox and the preface paradox.
(b) Say whether they have the same structure — one paradox in two costumes, or two different problems — and why.
(c) Apply your answer to this third case. A ten-author handbook's general editor, who has read only her own chapter, writes in the preface that everything in the volume is true, while also knowing that handbooks of that length essentially always contain at least one error. Is she in a preface case, a lottery case (400 claims, 400 tickets), or neither?

Student's answer:
(a) Lottery: a thousand tickets, one winner. For each ticket there is overwhelming evidence that it will lose, so you are justified in believing of each that it loses; but you also know that some ticket will win. So you have a thousand justified beliefs that cannot all be true, and you know that. Preface: the author has good evidence for every claim in her book, but her track record gives her strong evidence that at least one claim is false. Same shape: many individually justified beliefs plus a justified belief that they are not all true.
(b) Not the same structure, though. The preface is a deductive closure problem: closure takes you from justification for each claim to justification for the conjunction, and the conjunction clashes with something else you justifiedly believe. The lottery is not a closure problem at all — nothing deductive is doing any work, it is a problem about probabilistic coherence, since each individual probability is high while the probability of the conjunction is zero. The evidence differs too: lottery evidence is statistical, preface evidence is inductive, a track record about your own fallibility. On the knowledge side, the sensitivity theorist and the safety theorist both have to say you do not know that your ticket will lose: if it were false that the ticket loses you would still believe it loses, so the belief is neither safe nor sensitive. That is why friends of those views retreat to justified belief in the lottery case.
(c) The editor is in a lottery case, not a preface case. Her reason for expecting an error is a base rate about handbooks, not a record of her own past mistakes, so her evidence is statistical evidence spread over 400 claims, like tickets. The consequence is that she, unlike the single author, is under no pressure to withdraw any particular commitment: lottery cases show only that a body of justified beliefs can be collectively inconsistent, not that any one of them is defective, and statistical evidence never tells you which one anyway.
