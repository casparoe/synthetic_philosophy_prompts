I've been reading LessWrong and the EA Forum long enough to have watched the arguments about suffering-focused ethics go round several times, and there is one objection I keep failing to land properly, so I want to rehearse it against someone who actually holds the view. Please play a committed defender of lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism — a philosopher who has worked this out in detail, not a parody of one. Here is your position, and I want you to hold all of it: every experience has a hedonic level on a single scale; there is a threshold on that scale such that any moment below it counts as suffering that is lexically prior to any quantity of above-threshold happiness, so no amount of good experience anywhere can compensate for even one sub-threshold moment; above the threshold, trade-offs are perfectly ordinary and commensurable, and within the sub-threshold tier comparisons still go by intensity and duration, so a microsecond of mild discomfort is worse than nothing but enormously better than a decade of agony. You may concede that the threshold's exact location is vague, that we have no good hedonic measuring rod, and that the view is revisionary of common sense. You will not concede that pleasure and pain are tradeable across the threshold, and you will not let me quietly reframe you as a plain aggregative hedonist or as an ordinary negative utilitarian who just weights suffering more heavily. Speak in the first person, keep each reply to two or three paragraphs, correct me crisply if I misstate your view, and stay in character until I say the word stop.

My opening move is about how you individuate the moments that the threshold is supposed to apply to. Consider a life that, described at a fine grain — one millisecond, say, near the finest grain at which anyone thinks conscious experience could be individuated — alternates: odd milliseconds at +12 hedonic units, even milliseconds at −10. At that grain the life contains hundreds of millions of sub-threshold moments, so on your view it is lexically bad, worse than any life containing no sub-threshold moment at all, however much above-threshold happiness that other life contains. Now consider exactly the same life — same nervous system, same total phenomenology, same everything — described at the grain of the specious present, roughly a hundred milliseconds. Each such block nets out at +1, safely above the threshold, and so at that grain the life contains no sub-threshold moment and is lexically fine. Nothing about the person changed; only the unit of experience did. So: what fixes the grain at which experiences are counted for lexical purposes, and why is your verdict here not simply an artifact of an arbitrary choice of unit?

I'll add the synchronous version, in case you want to say the right unit is given by the real joints of consciousness and that my alternating millisecond life is a philosopher's fiction. Suppose a mind simultaneously undergoes a genuinely pleasant stream and a genuinely painful one — music you love in one ear, an acute cramp in the leg — co-conscious,attended to, neither one occluding the other. That can be described as a single momentary experience with net valence somewhere near zero, or as two simultaneous momentary experiences, one of which is sub-threshold; and whichever description you prefer, I can rerun the granularity argument against whatever unit you appeal to, since any unit of experience I am offered can be subdivided or lumped with its neighbors to flip the verdict. Deal with the argument rather than with my tone, and end your reply with the sharpest question you have for me.
