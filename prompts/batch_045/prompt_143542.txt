I am writing the specification section of a dissertation chapter on evolutionary debunking arguments in metaethics, and I want the criteria of adequacy fixed before I go on to score any actual views. Assume the purpose of the theory is settled in advance: an argument of this kind is adequate only if it could in principle give a reasonably sophisticated non-naturalist realist a reason to reduce her confidence in some of her own first-order moral judgments — not merely reassure people who already take moral facts to be dispensable. Please do not supply citations, a literature review, or suggestions about whom to read; I have those, and I want the requirements stated independently of the dialectic around particular authors and papers.

What I am asking for is a numbered list of desiderata — what an evolutionary debunking argument must deliver to count as adequate in that sense — with the case or consideration behind each one, and each marked as non-negotiable, negotiable, or a prejudice of one camp rather than a genuine requirement. Order them so that the ones doing the most work come first; ten to fifteen is the right number. Here are the decisions I most want the list to settle one way or the other, with the cases I have been using to think about them:

Granularity. Call this the Fission case: we discover a long-isolated human population whose moral judgments are as internally coherent and as confidently held as ours, coincide with ours across a large central core, and diverge sharply on one dimension that is the locus of recent rapid change — say, obligations to distant future people. The divergence is best explained not by a different capacity but by the same capacity running on different local inputs. The argument must be able to say, for any given judgment or class of judgments, whether and how far it is debunked, and must not collapse into debunking everything or nothing without saying why. Which cases must it get right, and what principle does the sorting?

Level of explanation. Call this the Plasticity case: suppose what selection actually favored was not a suite of particular moral outputs but a capacity for normative plasticity — a general facility for internalizing, enforcing, and feeling bound by whatever local norms one is raised with. Then the causal story explains the form and phenomenology of moral judgment without predicting any particular content. Must the argument deliver content-level predictions, or is a capacity-level explanation enough? If the latter, does it retain any grip on the truth or justification of content-level judgments, and if the former, what is the evidence base and how much of the realist's set escapes?

Downstream stability. Call this the Reformer case: a philosopher accepts the genealogy in full, then spends the afternoon arguing that her department's hiring practices are unjust. The argument must say what the status of first-order moral argument is once the debunking has gone through — abolitionism, conservationism, fictionalism, quietism, error theory plus retention — and must account for why debunked judgments keep their phenomenology and their practical grip, rather than treating that as a separate and optional problem. It should also say what happens to the debunker's own normative claims about what we have reason to believe.

Explanatory sufficiency. Does a complete causal-etiological explanation of why someone holds a judgment suffice to defeat it, or must the argument add an independent no-tracking premise? If the latter, the premise must be stated and motivated, and it must be clear whether it is a general epistemic principle or a morality-specific one. This is where I expect the overgeneralization worry to bite: whatever principle does the work had better not debunk logic, mathematics, memory, or perception.

Falsifiability and empirical fit. Say what pattern of findings would disconfirm the argument — for instance, invariance of moral judgment across populations where the relevant selective pressures differed, or variance that tracks something with no plausible fitness story. The list should also state which modal and causal assumptions about moral facts the argument is committed to, since against a realist who takes moral facts to supervene necessarily and to be known a priori, a no-causal-contact premise may do no work at all.

The distinctions I want respected rather than collapsed: undermining versus rebutting defeat; the origin of a capacity versus the warrant for a token exercise of it; debunking truth versus debunking justification versus debunking the rationality of continuing to hold; first-order judgments versus the meta-level claim that there are stance-independent moral facts; and no causal contact versus no tracking explanation available to us.

Then the part I most want: which subsets of the desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied, with the argument for each incompatibility. My own suspicion is that two pairs are in trouble — the demand that the argument discriminate among judgments and the demand that it not presuppose first-order moral knowledge in doing so, and the demand that it avoid overgeneralizing beyond morality and the demand that its operative epistemic principle be general enough to do any work. If either pair is jointly unsatisfiable, say so and say what a revised list should drop.

Say, too, which single requirement excludes the largest number of actual candidate arguments, and why that requirement rather than another.

Finally, and briefly — a short table is fine, and this is secondary to the criteria — score three argument-types against the list, three or four lines each: (A) the argument that pressures the realist into a dilemma between denying any relation between evolutionary influence and moral truth and accepting an implausible coincidence; (B) the argument that treats the categorical authority built into the concept of moral wrongness as the debunked element; (C) the plain genealogical argument from lack of causal contact. Label them structurally rather than by author; I do not need attributions.
