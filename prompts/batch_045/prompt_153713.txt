I'm doing a PhD chapter on Nozick and animals, and my supervisor is herself a card-carrying Nozickian — she thinks the bit about animals in Anarchy, State and Utopia gets something roughly right: persons are side-constrained, animals get something closer to a utilitarian treatment, and the creature with the greater capacity for a meaningful life can be favoured, but only when its superiority isn't merely marginal. I've been keeping a not of her verdicts across supervisions because I want to know where she'll land before I spring my new case on her next week.

Her record so far:

- One normal adult human vs one normal adult chimp, both painlessly dead if not rescued, save one: the human, and she isn't remotely apologetic about it.
- One human vs ten chimps: she refuses to aggregate. Says it has to be settled pairwise or not at all.
- One chimp vs one pig: the chimp. So inside the animal category she ranks by capacity, not by cuteness or by whom we happen to like.
- Chimp painlessly killed to give a human a liver or a cornea: fine.
- Chimp painlessly killed to spare a human a fortnight of mild eczema: not fine.
- Chimp painlessly killed to save a human's index finger: fine, apparently, though she went quiet for a while first.
- One human killed to save five humans: absolutely not.
- I once asked about a human with advanced irreversible dementia — no self over time, no projects, no future-directed agency, but still pleasures and pains and people who love her — against a healthy chimp. She changed the subject.

The case I'm bringing next week is an uplifted chimpanzee: gene-edited before birth and then given some neural graft in adolescence, so that she now has everything the Nozickian clause seems to care about — a self over time, long-term projects, a narrative sense of her own life, language, the lot. Call her U. So: U vs a normal adult human, one for one, painless; and U vs the demented human above. And two variants if they matter to your answer: U only got the graft last year, so 39 of her 40 years were ordinary chimp years; and the mirror image, where the human lost the capacities she once had while the chimp never had them until the graft.

What I want is: predict her verdicts, but more usefully, set out the two or three rules that fit that track record about equally well — some species-or-kind-norm reading, some individual-capacity reading, some biographical or historical reading, whatever else you can construct — show exactly where they come apart on U, and tell me which single further case I should actually put to her to force her hand. Also say which of her existing verdicts is the least stable, the one I could push on without her noticing where it takes her. I know the literature, so no reading lists or citations, I just want the argumentative map. And please write it in British English (colour, favour, -ise endings) — I'm lifting chunks into the thesis and she is wierdly irritable about American spellings.
