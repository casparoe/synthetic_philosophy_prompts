I'm applying for a one-year junior research fellowship and need to turn a half-baked idea into a two-page proposal by the end of the month. Context: I've been hanging around the rationalist blog scene since the Overcoming Bias days, I run a small reading group that's currently working through the superrationality chapters of Metamagical Themas, and I got into an argument at a meetup in London last weekend that I haven't been able to let go of.

Here's the thing. Everyone uses Hofstadter's Platonia setup as the clean intuition pump for superrationality: a bunch of people each get a letter saying a large prize goes to you if and only if you're the only one to claim it, and the superrational move is to randomise rather than grab, each person entering with probability 1/N, which gives the group a (1 - 1/N)^(N-1) chance of someone getting paid, i.e. about 37% however big N is, versus the near-certain zero you get if everyone reasons like a causal decision theorist. My gripe is that this is the measure-zero case: the whole argument turns on the other N-1 replies being perfectly correlated with yours, because you're all running the same computation on the same problem. Every case anyone actually cares about — turnout, strike participation, open-source contribution, not dumping your junk in the common — is somewhere in between, and I can't find anywhere that says what you're supposed to do there. The functional decision theory people are crystal clear when your decision and the other person's are literally the same computation, and completely silent on what happens when they're only mostly the same, which is the only situation that ever occurs. So I want to set up a parameterised family of games: with probability ρ each other player's move is determined by the same deterministic policy as yours, and with probability 1-ρ they're independent (or, second version, they're correlated with yours via a common cause that screens off your own decision — I suspect the 'logical' and 'common cause' stories come apart as soon as ρ < 1, in a way that the usual discussions of Newcomb with an imperfect predictor obscure by running the two together). Then I want to know whether the superrational policy is continuous in ρ as ρ goes to 1, or whether there's some threshold below which cooperation stops being worth it at all and the whole Hofstadter argument turns out to have no practical force. I'd also like a second knob for what I'm calling the lure: instead of the winner-takes-all Platonia payoff, a fixed pot that gets diluted by the number of entrants, split either evenly among entrants or as a lump to one of them — my guess is that in the diluted case the symmetric superrational optimum isn't attained at all, only approached as the entry probability goes to zero, in which case superrationality doesn't give a determinate answer to the very question it was invented to answer, and that would be the interesting result.

What I need from you is the proposal document itself, in a form I can hand to a philosophy panel: the question stated tightly enough that it has a determinate answer, why the existing positions (causal, evidential, and functional decision theory, plus the standard game-theoretic move of just positing a correlating device) all leave it open — bearing in mind they're all answers about the endpoints — the method and why it's the right one rather than the obvious alternatives (I don't want to run another psychology study on whether people cooperate, and I don't want to invent a new decision theory from axioms, that's a decade's work; I think the right shape is a sensitivity analysis of the existing theories across a parameterised family, with a month-by-month plan), what a positive result would look like as a deliverable, and honestly what would count as the project failing — e.g. if it turns out everything is boringly continuous and the answer is just interpolate linearly between the two endpoints, or if it collapses back into ordinary correlated equilibrium theory with nothing new said.
