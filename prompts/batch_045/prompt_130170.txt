Hi — I'm a journalist, I mostly cover science and ideas, and I've fallen down a rabbit hole on the "you can have a true belief, arrived at impeccably, and still not know" literature, and I was hoping you could tell me whether the thing I think I've noticed is actually a thing. A philosopher friend I was complaining to about it said "oh, that's just Gettier," and maybe it is, but the more I read the less that answer satisfies me, because what interests me isn't the cases themselves, it's a pattern in how they behave.

So here's the pattern, with three cases I keep coming back to. (1) A guy drives through farm country where the locals have put up dozens of extremely convincing fake barn fronts. He glances at a field, sees the one real barn, thinks "barn." His eyesight is fine, the light is fine, the object in front of him really is a barn. Verdict: no knowledge. Here the accident seems to live in the surroundings — it's a fact about the neighbourhood, not about him. (2) I'm in a lobby, I see someone across the room who looks exactly like my colleague, and I text my wife "she's here." It's her twin; but she is here, in a meeting two floors up, which I had no way of knowing. Verdict, apparently: also no knowledge. Here nothing is rigged at all, no decoys anywhere; the accident is that what makes my sentence true is a different thing from what I was looking at. (3) A man has a device in his head, installed without his knowledge, that feeds him perfectly accurate temperature readings. It's 70 degrees, he says 70 degrees, the device has never been wrong, there are no fakes, no false step in his reasoning, no coincidence about the object. Every diagnosis I've run into seems to imply he knows — his belief lines up with the world better than mine does — and yet the intuition people report is no knowledge. Here what's wrong seems to be that he's lucky to be so well equipped, not that anything came apart on this occasion.

The thing I want a name for is the pattern across these: every time someone proposes a condition meant to rule the accident out, the next counterexample moves the accident to a place that condition isn't policing — out of the reasoning, into the object, into the surroundings, into the subject's own equipment — and there seems to be no level where you can pin it down. Does that have a name? And is it even true, or is the appearance of inexhaustability an artifact of how the cases get told? I definately don't just want a label, though — the label is the boring part. What I want is the carving: how many genuinely different kinds of accident there are here (are my three the right three, is one of them really two, is the third one even the same animal?), what the difference between them actually consists in, whether "luck" is the right genus at all, and where the edges are — for instance, is the lottery case, where the odds are overwhelming and you still lose, one of these or something else? Please don't send me a reading list or a pile of citations; I can chase references up myself and this isn't an academic piece. What I do need is for you to flag your confidence part by part: which claims are the standard move in the field, which are you reasoning to on the spot, and what would change your mind — what sort of case or argument would make you say the taxonomy collapses, or that the "no level to pin it down" claim is false, and where is your reasoning most likely to be going wrong? And if you end up coining a term for it, tell me what its boundary cases are and what it would rule out.
