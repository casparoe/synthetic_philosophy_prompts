Two people at the puzzle-and-AI meetup I go to got into an argument I couldn't referee, and I'd like you to go through it move by move and say who's right about what.

Setup: a Bongard problem gives you six little figures in a left-hand box that all share some rule, and six in a right-hand box that all break it, and your job is to name the rule from those twelve examples. A few of us started writing variants where the rule isn't a property of individual figures at all but a property of the box as a whole: e.g., on the left every shape occurs an even number of times across the six figures, or the six left-hand figures can be paired off so that each pair has the same total number of sides. No single figure is evidence either way; you have to hold all six in your head at once. We've been calling these box-level rules.

The exchange:

(1) Dan: A box-level problem is a much stricter test than an ordinary one, because it kills the shortcut of finding some feature that all the left figures share individually. A solver that's really just a per-figure feature detector with a voting rule on top will fail these outright.

(2) Priya: All you've done is swap the menu. Instead of predicates like pointy or has-a-hole you now need predicates like count-is-even or admits-a-perfect-pairing. If the solver fails, that tells you its vocabulary didn't include cardinality predicates. It tells you about the programmers choice of features, not about whether the thing can induce a concept from a few examples.

(3) Dan: But that objection would apply to any test you could design, so it can't be right. Humans come with their own built-in feature vocabulary too; nobody induces concepts from a blank slate. The real question is whether the bias is the right one, and box-level problems probe a bias that per-figure problems can't see at all.

(4) Priya: Fine, but then you need a criterion other than "did it guess the rule we had in mind." With twelve examples and no constraint on rules, endlessly many box-level rules fit: the left box will always have some property the right box lacks. Twelve examples can only pick out a rule if the solver's hypothesis space was already narrow, so calling success "induction" and failure "no induction" both just report how you built the solver.

(5) Dan: Then make the test generative instead of classificatory: after seeing the twelve figures, have the solver produce a new six-figure box that satisfies the rule. If it can do that, its got the concept and isn't just pattern-matching.

Please rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each, and then say what both of them are missing — in particular whether "induce a concept from a few examples" is even a well-defined thing to be testing for here, or whether the phrase is doing us a disservice. I'm a retired geophysicist who's only recently gotten into this end of things, so please don't assume I know the jargon. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and don't restate the question back to me.
