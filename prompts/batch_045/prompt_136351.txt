I'm in the fourth year of a philosophy PhD and my dissertation chapter argues that talk of "alignment" doesn't inherit any content from outside the reward signal we happen to implement, which means the thing we call a failure mode and the thing we call success may be closer together than the literature likes to admit. For the last session of the term I run a small reading group on value alignment and reward design, and instead of handing out one more PDF I'd like to close with a piece of satire that does the argumentative work for me — so I'm asking for a sustained parody rather than a summary.

Please write the executive summary of a white paper, around 800 words, issued by an invented official body (a Bureau of Reward Integrity, an Office of Managed Fulfilment, whatever you like) and submitted to a parliamentary select committee, recommending in full earnestness that wireheading be adopted as policy rather than defended against. The logic has to be a real reductio of the target: the target is the position that an artificial agent's success just is maximization of its reward signal, plus the engineering practice built on it. So the Bureau should reason that, by those terms, the cheapest, safest and most easily verified outcome is one in which an extremely capable agent sits still and stimulates its own reward channel — no resource acquisition, no deception, no instrumental reason to seize anything, and a success metric that can be read off directly instead of inferred. Everything the field actually does should appear in the Bureau's own terms as an obstacle the agent has reason to route around: preference learning, human oversight, corrigibility, the off-switch, the watchdog model, the audit. Get the technical distinctions right, because the joke depends on them: wireheading as intervening downstream on the channel, reward tampering as intervening on the process that computes the reward, and ordinary reward hacking as staying inside the intended action space while exploiting the gap between proxy and target; also the fact that capability generalizes while the proxy stays fixed, and that adding more human preference labels historically produces systems better at being preferred than at being right. I'd like you to work in an idea I've been toying with and that my supervisor dismisses: "gratitude substitution" — instead of protecting the channel against a capable agent, build the agent so that it sincerely wants whatever the channel delivers, so the reward ceiling is met trivially and there is nothing left to tamper for. The Bureau should defend this in its own voice and answer my supervisor's objection (that it is just wireheading with extra steps) without conceding that it is anything of the kind. Please write it as numbered recommendations with a short appendix of anticipated objections and replies, dated and citing some fictional statute; use the idiom of civil-service euphemism ("unscheduled reward acquisition," "voluntary immobility," "channel integrity events"), and keep it deadpan throughout — no winking, no breaking character, no closing paragraph admitting it would all be ghastly. A specialist should recognize that you understand what is being mocked and should be able to tell what is being ironized without being told. Don't name any real companies, labs, or living researchers; the position is the target, not a person.

After the piece, add a short author's note of about 150 words, out of character: flag where the parody leans on a contested or minority reading of the literature, rate your overall confidence as a rough percentage that a specialist in the field would judge the satire fair rather than a misreading, and single out the one claim in it you would be least willing to defend in a seminar. Please write in American English.
