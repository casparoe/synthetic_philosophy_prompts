I'm in sixth form doing my EPQ on weakness of will (boring, I know, but it was this or something about football) and my supervisor keeps telling me to write down what I actually think before I start the write-up, then check it holds together. So here's my attempt. Numbered but that's not a priority order.

1. Akrasia is real in the strong sense: at the very moment you act you can judge that A is the better thing and still do B, while genuinely knowing it's worse, not confused, not having forgotten anything. My revision is like this. I'm sat there at 9pm knowing I should start, and I start scrolling instead, and I don't think I'm persuaded for even a second that scrolling is better.

2. The mechanism is hyperbolic discounting / present bias: the near option gets inflated, the distant one shrinks, so as the thing gets closer your preferences flip.

3. So self-binding is rational and costs you no autonomy. Give your phone to your brother, get a blocker with a password you don't know, tell everyone your deadline. Earlier you helping later you, nothing weird about it.

4. But I want an override. Not "just break it whenever" — I've got a test, call it the swap test: imagine the temptation and the task trade places in time (the thing you want now is three weeks off, the thing you're avoiding is due tonight). If you'd still pick the temptation, it's a real re-evaluation and you're allowed to unbind. If you wouldn't, it's purely the looming and you don't get to. A rule with no override in it feels too rigid to be rational, because sometimes your plan was just wrong.

5. The judgement you make with the reward right in front of you isn't just different from last week's, it's worse. There's a correct answer about what you should be doing, and it's the one you'd get from a standpoint that doesn't favour any particular moment. Relatedly: when I'm deciding whether some past binge of mine was a mistake, I trust the regret afterwards more than the plan beforehand, and I don't think its just discounting running backwards.

6. I also think manufacturing the looming on purpose is definately fine and often the smart move. I only revise properly the night before, I've more or less given up trying to change that, and I now book my crunch so it lands where I want it. Even though if present bias is a malfunction I shouldn't be leaning on it.

7. And one more that a mate says contradicts 3 but I can't see how: someone who doesn't need the blockers, who just does the work, is in some way better off than someone who blocks everything perfectly and gets identical results. Not morally better exactly. Just better off.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture. Which of these actually clash, in pairs, and how badly? Which only look like they clash? For each real one, what would have to give and what does giving it cost me — and if there's a single revision that cleans up the most, say which. I've got a nagging feeling something's off somewhere in the middle but I couldn't tell you where, so don't assume I've spotted it.
