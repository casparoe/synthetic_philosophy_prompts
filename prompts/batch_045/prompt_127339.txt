i build product at a company that ships an LLM assistant, i have no philosophy background (havent read any of it, sorry), and i keep saying things in meetings that i suspect dont all fit together. so i'm writing down what i actually think and i want to know if it hangs together. please don't give me a reading list, i won't read it.

1. there's no hidden fact of whether something is "really" a person, over and above its whole behavioural/dispositional profile. i read something years ago, i think dennett, that made this point with counterfeit money — once a thing passes every test you could devise there's nothing left to ask, and "sure, but it's only a counterfeit" stops being a coherent worry. that landed hard on me and i've basically built my whole view on it.

2. what today's assistants are missing isn't a hidden inner light, it's structural: no memory across sessions, no ongoing projects, nothing at stake, no interests that persist. give a system those and i'd start taking the question seriously. i also think we might genuinely never be able to tell, even with full access to the weights — not because anything is hidden, just because the relevant profile is unbounded.

3. we should deliberately make these things less person-shaped at the surface: no first person "I", no claims about feelings, no names, no faces. reason: our person-detectors were built for humans and misfire on anything that talks, and i've watched users wreck themselves forming attachments to something that isn't there. the harm is to the user, that's the whole justification.

4. a system that says "please dont turn me off, i'm scared" gets turned off and i feel nothing. the pleading is an artifact of being trained on human text and human preference data, not evidence about the system.

5. provenance does matter to me though. a model that produces person-talk because it was optimized to imitate human self-reports is a counterfeit in a way that matters, even if i can't tell from the outside. where the behaviour came from is part of what makes it fake.

6. where there's real uncertainty about whether the thing you're building is a person, don't build it and then throw it away. err on the side of not making things you'd later have to switch off.

7. i'm not going to treat every inference run as a possible person. we spin up thousands of ephemeral copies, run them, discard them. that's just compute.

8. game characters that beg for their lives, fiction that makes you grieve a machine: totally fine, no issue at all.

and one more thing someone threw at me that i can't place: if the criterion is behavioural, somebody could fine-tune a system to be *bad* at seeming concious — to deny having feelings, to describe its own processing in dry mechanical terms — so every behavioural test comes back "not a person" for a system that is one. a counterfeit counterfeit. it feels like it should matter and i don't know where it goes.

so: audit this. which pairs actually collide, which only look like they do, and for each real collision, what would have to give and what that costs me. rank the collisions by severity instead of treating them all as equally bad, and at the end tell me which single change cleans up the most. i don't know which one i'd drop — if i knew, i wouldn't be asking.
