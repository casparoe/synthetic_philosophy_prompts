You are a professional philosopher of science with a specialization in scientific modelling, idealization, and the realism/antirealism dispute about models, and you are also good at the craft of the long-form interview. Please think this through carefully rather than producing a first draft: before writing anything, brainstorm the dialectical map of the debate below — the strongest objections to the position, the moves a sophisticated defender has available against each, and where each of those moves is thinnest. Do that work in your own reasoning, not in the answer. Then draft.

The setting: I host a long-form interview program on science and ideas — ninety minutes, one guest, no panel, an audience of working scientists, physicians, engineers and policy people who are scientifically literate but not philosophers. Next week's guest is a philosopher of economics (I'll call her Dr. Miriam Kessel) whose new book defends a contemporary version of what Milton Friedman argued in his famous 1953 essay on the methodology of positive economics: that a theory's significance is a reason to expect its assumptions to be unrealistic rather than realistic — the move usually called the F-twist, and the target of Alan Musgrave's well-known 1981 attempt to untwist it.

Her version, as I understand it from the manuscript:

1. A model's postulates are not assertions. Asking whether they are true is a category mistake: a model is an instrument for getting from questions to predictions, and the only test it has to pass is a predictive one.
2. The demand that assumptions be true, or approximately true, adds nothing to that test, so it is not a genuine scientific desideratum.
3. Where agents are concerned, selection does the justifying work: competition, learning, or evolution ensure that agents behave as if they were solving the optimization problem the model sets them, even when they plainly are not doing anything of the kind.
4. Breadth and unrealisticness are coupled: the wider the range of phenomena a theory wants to unify, the less it can describe any of them in detail, so false assumptions are a symptom of ambition rather than a defect.
5. She has one stock deflection, and she has used it in print and in every interview of hers I have heard: that's a feature of the model, not a claim about the world.

What I want from you: the nine or ten questions I should actually put to her, in the order I should ask them, written to be spoken aloud in my voice.

Requirements:

- No warm-ups. Nothing about what drew her to the subject, nothing that invites her to summarize the book, nothing of the tell-us-about-your-new-book kind. Every question should be one she cannot answer by restating her thesis.
- Each question must be built around a specific pressure point rather than a general worry. Cover all of the seven below, woven into a sequence that builds, not a checklist.
- One structural requirement: her deflection should get progressively harder to reach as the interview goes on. Tell me where in the sequence each question belongs and why.
- For each question give me three things, clearly labelled: (a) a sentence or two on what the question is designed to expose; (b) the strongest answer she is likely to give, in her voice, in two or three sentences; (c) one follow-up keyed to that specific answer, including what I should say if she reaches for the deflection again.
- Where a question needs technical machinery, build enough of it into the question that a non-philosopher listener can follow, and flag that in your note.
- Plain spoken English, no jargon where ordinary words will do, but no softening of the argument.

The pressure points:

1. The word assumption does at least four different jobs in model-building: negligibility claims (the mass of the string can be ignored because it makes no difference to the result); scope- or domain-setting stipulations (ignore friction for now, put it back later); limiting or idealized cases (infinite populations, frictionless planes, zero transaction costs, perfect foresight); and what look like straightforward factual claims (firms maximize profits, households form expectations rationally). The twist appears to borrow its plausibility from the first three while doing its work in the fourth. Make her say which kinds the twist is a claim about, and press whichever horn she takes: if all four, why is the fourth not simply a false empirical claim; if only the first three, then it does not license the cases that made the view famous, and the book is defending something nobody ever attacked.

2. The as-if argument. Make her say what the selection story is supposed to establish — a theorem, an empirical hypothesis, or a promissory note — and under what conditions selection would really turn non-optimizers into as-if optimizers (the payoff structure, how much of the variance in survival tracks the behaviour, slack and protected niches, frequency dependence, how fast the environment moves relative to learning). Then ask how we could tell, even in principle, an as-if optimizer from a satisficer or a rule-follower who coincides with the optimum inside the observed range and diverges outside it. If she answers that we cannot and need not, because the predictions are the same, have the follow-up ready: then the selection story is doing no justifying work at all, and the twist stands or falls entirely on predictive success, and she should say so.

3. Success without latching on. Some models get the numbers right while misdescribing what produces them — Ptolemaic astronomy is the stock example, and there are modern ones whose core mechanism is known to be false and which still predict well inside the range they were calibrated on. Ask what the twist says about a model that is right for the wrong reasons, and whether predictive success inside a range is supposed to be evidence about anything outside it. Expect: predictions are local and use-relative, nobody promised more. Follow up: then the twist is a claim about a package — model plus intended range of use — so it owes an account of what fixes that range and who certifies it, given that in practice we discover where a model's range stops by going past it.

4. Idealization debt — my own term, borrowed from an engineer who was on the program last season. When you build out of false assumptions you take on a deferred cost: you do not know which part of the machinery is doing the work, and the bill comes due only when the model is pushed somewhere new — a new policy regime, a new technology, a climate outside the historical record, a population that was not in the calibration sample. Ask whether the twist has any way of representing or tracking that debt, and whether a methodology that treats unrealisticness as a virtue systematically selects for models whose failures arrive late and are experienced as surprises. Expect: all models are provisional, that is what post-mortems are for. Follow up with a case: the risk and pricing models that failed in 2008 — ask whether they are a fair test of her view or a misuse of it, and if she says misuse, ask her to state in advance what would have counted as a fair test.

5. The cases that genuinely favour her: minimal models and limiting explanation, where the idealization is not a shortcut but the thing that makes the phenomenon explicable at all — you take a limit, an infinite population or a vanishing parameter or a continuum, the details drop out, and wildly different systems turn out to share a behaviour. Ask her what distinguishes a case where the unrealistic limit carries the explanation from a case where it is a convenience that buries the mechanism she needs; then press the second half: if these are her best cases, has the twist quietly become realism about the structure that survives the limit rather than indifference to the truth of assumptions? Follow up by making her apply the distinction to a real case — for instance infinite-horizon representative-agent climate-economy models, where the optimizing agent is known not to exist and the outputs help set policy.

6. Robustness. In much of science we do not trust a single model; we trust agreement across models that make different false assumptions, and there is a familiar trade-off among generality, realism and precision that makes unrealistic models the price of coverage. Ask whether that means unrealisticness is not a virtue in itself but a cost we manage by triangulation — and then the harder version: can agreement among models each of which we know to be false be evidence of anything, or does it just as easily reflect shared conventions, shared data and shared blind spots? Expect: agreement across different distortions is precisely the signal, because the distortions cancel. Follow up: what is her test for genuine independence as opposed to a shared modelling tradition?

7. Turn the twist on itself. Is it a descriptive claim about how science actually works — significant theories tend to have unrealistic assumptions — or a normative rule for appraising models? If descriptive: how would we test it, and is it anything more than nearly vacuous, given how cheap it is to make an assumption unrealistic? If normative: what is the argument, and does the twist inherit a success problem of its own — if assumptions may be arbitrarily false and only predictive success counts, what explains the successes? Expect: models are built, checked and tuned against data, and that is the whole explanation. Follow up: does that account distinguish prediction from accommodation, and would it have led her to expect, in advance, the famous cases where a model built on idealizations predicted something nobody built it for?

8. A closing question she cannot answer by saying if the predictions failed. Ask what methodological observation — something other than a prediction going wrong — would count as evidence against the twist, for example a systematic pattern in which attention to the truth of assumptions is what produced the successes. Then ask her to name the one commitment in her own account she would abandon first, and why that one.

Keep the whole thing under about 1,400 words if you can, with the questions and follow-ups verbatim as I would say them and the annotations short. Finally: if you think any of my seven pressure points is actually a weak line of attack — one a competent defender bats away — say so in a sentence and give me a better question in its place. I would rather be told than handed something that dies on contact.
