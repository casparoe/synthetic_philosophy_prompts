So I'm giving the toast at my wife's uncle Ray's retirement party this Saturday: forty-three years as a letter carrier, basically the same walking route the whole time, and now he's done. Union hall, seventy-odd people, I've got maybe four minutes, and I've written four drafts and deleted all four. I'm a forklift driver, not a reader, but I got wierdly into this one thing over the winter. There's a story that philosophy people pass around about a digger wasp — Sphex, I think? — that stings a cricket, drags it to her burrow, leaves it on the doorstep, goes inside to check the nest, comes back out and pulls it in. If somebody moves the cricket a few inches while she's in there, she comes out, hauls it back to the doorstep, and goes in to check the nest again, and you can apparently make her do that over and over. Sphexishness is what somebody called it — Hofstadter, maybe. My wife's cousin Devi is a psych postdoc who works on habits and she muttered that the story has been embellished and that the big number everyone repeats is folklore; I have no idea if she's right, and I don't want to be making claims about wasps at a retirement party anyway.

The problem is that everyone keeps describing Ray as a creature of habit and meaning it nicely, and the phrase has started landing wrong in my ear. His routines were real — same coffee stop at 9:15, same order of streets, same everything, he'd have done it in his sleep. The thing I keep circling is a test I invented: don't watch whether a routine runs smoothly, watch what happens when somebody interrupts it. Something that has to go back to step one and start the whole sequence over is one kind of thing; something that stops, handles the interruption, and picks up in the middle is another. I think Ray was the second kind, and the evidence is that he let the route interrupt him constantly — he knew who'd been in the hospital, he'd sit on somebody's porch for twenty minutes in the rain, and he could always tell you why he did the streets in that order. But here's where I'm stuck, and it's the part I actually want help with: my wife's brother Andy is the exact opposite, new city and new job and new personality every three years, never repeats himself once, and he strikes me as more mechanical than Ray rather than less, because the one thing he never examines is whatever tells him it's time to change. Flexible in the joints, rigid in the spine. Is there anything to that, or am I just rationalising because I like Ray and find Andy exhausting?

Either way, what I need is a four-minute toast built around whatever's true in that distinction, and that doesn't quietly betray it the way toasts normally do. The room is mixed in a way that's making this hard: half of it is carriers from his branch who'll hear any mention of insects as an insult and who also know perfectly well he was stubborn as hell, the other half is family including his sister Marlene, who takes her faith very seriously and will hear anything like "we're just animals running programs" as an attack on the soul even though that's not what I mean, and Devi will be sitting right there and will notice if I say something false about how habits work. So nothing like "he never once went through the motions" — I'm pretty sure he'd be the first to admit some days were pure autopilot, and I can't know it anyway — no "he was his own man" filler, and nothing that implies the route was running him rather than the other way round. I also definately don't want it to turn into a lecture; it should land as affection. Can you write it? And if my interruption test or the thing about Andy is confused, say so in a couple of sentences and then write the version that survives the correction — plus give me the one sentence to build the whole thing around, because that's the bit I keep failing to find, and I'm driving up Friday night after my shift and would like to stop thinking about it.
