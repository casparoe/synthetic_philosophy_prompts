I am writing the final paper for a first-year course my old physics department has asked me to teach — Measurement, Infinity and Motion — twenty students, a term of calculus and mechanics behind them, no philosophy at all. I came to philosophy late, after a career in experimental physics, the last twenty years of which were spent arguing about thresholds: when a signal counts as detected rather than swallowed by noise. That preoccupation has shaped what I want to test, and I would be grateful for your help with one question.

I want an essay question on Zeno's paradoxes that cannot be answered by writing down that the sum of a half, a quarter, an eighth and so on is one. The convergent-series reply is true and settles something; I want students to have to say exactly what it settles and what it leaves untouched.

Four things I would like built into the question.

First, the Dichotomy in the form I have taken to handing out, which I call the Backward Dichotomy: before reaching the end you must reach the halfway point, before that the quarter point, before that the eighth, and so on without end — so the series of required sub-traversals has no first member. I want students to confront the fact that the forward and backward orderings of the same division are not the same problem, and that convergence addresses the one more directly than the other.

Second, the assumption I have been calling the task-decomposition assumption: that traversing an interval consists in the successive completion of the traversals of its sub-intervals. Please make students say, for each argument, whether it needs this assumption, and whether the standard modern replies grant it or quietly reject it. My suspicion is that the real work in these debates is done by accepting or denying this, and not by the mathematics.

Third, the Grain of Millet — the single falling grain makes no sound, the bushel does — together with my own reading of it: that it is not an argument about the infinite at all, but about threshold phenomena, and that it survives untouched in a world with no infinite divisibility whatsoever, a world of discrete grains and a detector with a finite sensitivity. On that reading, nothing in the theory of infinite series or of infinite sets has any bearing on it. I am far from certain this is right. Please test it, and if you think it is wrong, say so plainly.

Fourth, the Stadium — three rows of equal blocks, two moving past each other and past a stationary row — where I want the question to ask against what sort of space-time it has any force: a continuous one, or one built from indivisible minima. I was surprised to hear in a seminar that this argument may cut against atomism rather than against continuity, and I would like students to have to work out whether all of these arguments really share a target.

Please give me: the question as it would appear on the paper, self-contained, stating each of the arguments in its own words so that no one needs the historical sources and nothing rests on how Zeno is read (ninety minutes, closed book); a model answer of about eight hundred words pitched at what a strong first-year with calculus could actually produce in that time; and a marking scheme out of twenty, built around five must-hit points, each with its mark and a line on how candidates typically get it wrong. Include in the scheme a note on what an answer that does nothing but sum the series should receive.

One further request, which matters to me as a scientist rather than a philosopher: in the model answer, mark each substantive claim as settled mathematics, as mainstream but philosophically contestable, or as genuinely contested among people who work on these questions. In the marking scheme, say for each contested point what a student who takes the opposed view should be awarded, so that I do not end up penalising dissent and calling it error. If a student concludes that there is no single solution to be had here, I would rather that counted as insight than as evasion.
