Our reading group has drifted out of set theory and into philosophy of mathematics this term, and the rule we've adopted is that everyone has to write down where they stand before anyone is allowed to argue. So here is mine, written out flat, in the order I thought of it rather than in any order of importance. I'm a working mathematician (analysis and combinatorics, not logic), so I know the mathematics but I'm shaky on the philosophical vocabulary, and I'd rather be told my set of views is broken than be told who to go and read.

1. Mathematical sentences have determinate truth values, fixed by how things are and not by us, our conventions, or what we can prove. I'd give up a lot before I said any mathematical question is settled by a decision. In particular I want to say the continuum hypothesis has a truth value and we simply don't know it.
2. The reason I'm not a nominalist is the indispensability argument: our best science quantifies over mathematical objects, and if I believe in electrons on those grounds I should believe in numbers on the same grounds. This is the only argument for platonism I actually feel.
3. Epistemically, I don't think mathematical beliefs get confirmed by experience. We get the basic stuff by something like rational insight or intuition into the abstract realm, and proof and deduction do the rest. But I want nothing spooky here: whatever this capacity is, it's a natural cognitive capacity of human beings, not a pipeline to another world.
4. Arithmetic and higher set theory stand or fall together. I don't see a principled halfway house where you're a realist about the natural numbers and then go quiet once the cumulative hierarchy gets going.
5. Mathematics isn't answerable to physics. A new axiom — a large cardinal axiom, a forcing axiom, whatever — can be justified by its coherence and fruitfulness inside mathematics, with no help from physics and no help from anything empirical.
6. It doesn't matter which objects the numbers are. Von Neumann ordinals, Zermelo ordinals, some other reduction nobody's thought of: only the structure matters. So if you ask me whether 3 is a member of 5 I want to say "that isn't a real question", and I've never understood why people treat the identification problem as a crisis.
7. Case verdict. If someone actually pulled off a complete nominalistic rewrite of our best physics — empirically as good as ours, no quantification over abstracta anywhere — I'd take that to remove the main argument for platonism, and I'd stop asserting that numbers exist.

Two scenarios I keep testing myself against, which may or may not be doing any real work:

- Light Physics: that's 7 above.
- Heavy Physics: the reverse. Future physics turns out to need to quantify over measurable cardinals to state its laws, and it's confirmed to ludicrous precision. My instinct is that this should do nothing whatsoever to whether measurable cardinals exist. But by 2 it looks like it should do a lot, and the only thing I can find to say against it is 5, which looks like it flatly contradicts 2.

What I want from you is an audit of the seven, not a survey of the field. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, ranked by severity rather than all flagged as equally bad, with what has to give in each case and what giving it would cost me. Which pairs only look like tensions and dissolve once you're careful — I suspect the continuum-hypothesis-versus-the-independence-results worry is one of these (Cohen's forcing constructions keep making me think I've lost 1, and then I think I haven't), and I half suspect 6 against 1 might be another, but I genuinely can't tell which way either goes. And then tell me which single revision would clean up the most of the mess at once. Please write in British English.
