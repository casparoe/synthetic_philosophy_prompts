I've rather landed myself in it. My brother-in-law keeps a few hives and over Christmas he asked why the workers don't just lay their own eggs, I tried to explain and made a complete hash of it, my sister said the colony is basically one big animal anyway, and we ended up shouting at each other in the kitchen. Now I've stupidly agreed to run a 90-minute session at the county beekeepers' AGM in March. Roughly 20 people, mixed ages — a handful of teenagers doing their proficiency badges, a couple of retired maths teachers, and plenty who'd start checking their watches if I put anything that looks like algebra on a slide. It needs to survive only 12 turning up or 30 turning up, and I've got one facilitator (me), maybe a helper, a hall with tables, paper counters and cheap sweets, and no projector. What I want is for them to come away feeling why it's still a live question whether selection acts at the level of genes, of individual organisms, or of colonies — and to have built that problem themselves before anybody names it. So: no lecture up front, and please design it so I physically can't say the words group selection or kin selection until the debrief.

The game itself. I want a multi-round thing where the two levels pull in opposite directions: inside every group, the selfish type out-reproduces the cooperator, but groups with more cooperators produce more offspring overall, so that across the whole room cooperators can end up increasing even though they lose in every single group. I'd like an assortment dial I can turn between rounds — one round where new groups are founded from a single parent group, another where I scramble everybody together — so the room can watch the sign of the outcome flip without me telling them what's coming. And I want a built-in wrinkle about currency: get half the tables scoring their group's success by average output per individual and the other half by total output, or by whether the group clears some survival threshold, because I suspect the same set of groups gets ranked differently depending on which of those you count as the group-level trait, and I want them to find that argument rather than hear it. If there's a clean way to put a third level underneath the individual — something that boosts its own transmission whilst dragging down the individual or the colony carrying it, so people see the levels question doesn't stop at the organism — I'd love that too, though it's the bit I'm least sure can be done with counters and sweets. Optional nice-to-have: some neutral inherited marker they can track, so they can see that what goes up in frequency needn't be the thing that looks well designed.

Then the debrief, which is where I'm most likely to muck it up. Map the specific moments onto the specific bits of the idea: which round shows the within-group/between-group split, which shows why the amount of variation sitting between groups is doing all the work, which shows the currency problem, and how to handle the moment somebody definately says that in a real hive all the workers are sisters so relatedness is baked in from the start — I'd rather that arrived as their own insight than as me conceding it under pressure. Tell me what will predictably go wrong: my guess is the scramble round reads as me rigging the game, the teenagers run the counting and the older members switch off or the reverse, and everybody leaves thinking the lesson was just that it depends on the numbers. And be honest about what the game cannot do — I take it the competing ways of carving up the same population are arithmetically equivalent, so no amount of playing settles which one is the real explanation, and that bit I'll have to say out loud. Tell me how to say it without deflating the whole session.
