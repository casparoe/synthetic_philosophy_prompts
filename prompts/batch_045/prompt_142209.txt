I'm an accountant who fell into this rabbit hole after a friend lent me some of Penrose's books and I started reading around online, and I've ended up somewhere close to the opposite of where I started: I now think the Lucas–Penrose argument is basically empty, and that whatever makes it feel powerful is coming from one hidden assumption that has nothing to do with Gödel. The way I'd put it is this — the argument only gets off the ground if I can know what my own program is, and know that I'm sound. Because the Gödel sentence for a system is, as far as I can tell, equivalent to that system's consistency statement, so "seeing that my Gödel sentence is true" just *is* knowing I'm consistent. Once you say it out loud it seems obvious that I can't do that, and the mechanist's reply — you're a machine, you can't read off your own source code, and your confident reports about your own reliability are just more outputs — looks completely comfortable. I also don't think incompleteness is doing any special work here. I feel like I could run the same "I can always step outside any system you name" routine with the halting problem, or with the fact that no machine computes it's own halting set, and get exactly the same spooky feeling with no Gödel anywhere.

The thing that nearly shook me was a move my friend Priya came up with, designed to get the argument going without any self-knowledge: you don't need to know *which* machine you are, you just say of every machine on the list, "whichever one of these I am, that one's Gödel sentence is true" — and since the programs can be enumerated, the anti-mechanist seems to win without ever identifying himself. But I've been turning it over and I think it collapses. What you can actually assert without knowing which one you are is only the conditional "if machine i is consistent then G_i is true," and if I am machine k, then machine k can assert that very conditional about *itself* too, since that's more or less the easy half of the incompleteness theorem. So the universal quantifier buys nothing, and we're back to needing to drop the consistency antecedent — back to self-knowledge. That made me more confident, which makes me suspicious of myself.

So: what would count against the position I've landed on? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I especially want the ones that someone who already agrees with me should concede, not just the stuff Lucas and Penrose fans say. Some things I'm actually worried about: (1) is it even right that the argument reduces to self-knowledge, or does the self-referential bit do something a bare limitative result can't — I keep hearing the charitable reconstruction is a disjunction, either mechanism is false or I can't know my own program, and that Gödel is what makes that disjunction rigorous, which if true means my "nothing special about incompleteness" line is definately too strong; (2) my position seems to need that I can't trust my own reasoning even a little, which sits badly with the fact that I think I've followed the proof of the theorem itself, and I can't tell whether that's a real cost or just self-reference doing it's usual tricks; (3) the version where you Gödelize a candidate system and the mechanist upgrades to a bigger one feels like a regress, and I want to say the regress is exactly where the self-knowledge assumption gets smuggled in, but I can't say why; (4) Penrose's later stuff seems to be about understanding rather than about what can be proved, and I'm not sure my critique touches it at all. Also tell me honestly whether my position has hardened into something unfalsifiable — if my answer to any anti-mechanist argument is going to be "you'd need self-knowledge for that," is that a thesis or just a way of never being wrong? The thing that would move me most, I think, is something showing that self-knowledge isn't an extra assumption the anti-mechanist has to sneak in, but something the mechanist is already committed to.
