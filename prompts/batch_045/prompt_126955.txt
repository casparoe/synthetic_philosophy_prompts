I'm putting together the final for my final-year seminar on intention and practical rationality, and I want one question on Kavka's toxin puzzle that my students can't answer by writing down that you cannot intend to do what you believe you will not do and then stopping. They've read the original paper and a good chunk of the secondary literature, so they all know the standard setup and the usual menu of moves — the thought that the reward itself gives you a reason to drink, the thought that the intention once formed generates its own reason, the comparison with Newcomb's problem, the stuff about whether intending requires belief about your own future action. I want the question to force them to say which of those moves is actually doing the work and what it commits them to, and to do it on a case they haven't seen before, so I've been building a variant. The setup I'm playing with: at noon today a billionaire offers you a million dollars if at midnight tonight you intend to drink a vial at noon tomorrow; the vial will make you violently ill for a day with no lasting effects and no other consequences; the money is wired at 12:01am whether or not you drink, and drinking has no further payoff. On top of that, two complications. (a) The vial you are handed at noon tomorrow is drawn at random from a batch of ten, one of which is a harmless placebo; you can't tell which one you have and won't find out unless you drink, so tonight you're confident but not certain that you won't drink. (b) At 11am tomorrow, long after the money is wired, a second billionaire offers you five thousand dollars in cash if you hand the vial back unopened, and you know tonight that this is going to happen. I also want them to handle two shorter things: whether it matters that for a hundred dollars you could pay someone to hypnotise you into genuinely intending to drink, and whether the puzzle is at bottom a claim about the psychology of intention, a claim about reasons, or both, and whether it generalises to states that aren't intentions at all — say, a prize for genuinely wanting something, or for being the sort of person who would do the thing. The move I especially want to filter out is the student who reaches for evidential decision theory or the Newcomb comparison and thinks that dissolves it; I'd like it to be obvious on the face of the question that the issue is whether the relevant state is available to you at all, not which act to pick once it is.

Level: final-year undergrads and MA students, in-class exam, 90 minutes, 20 marks. Give me exactly five numbered parts, no preamble and no sign-off, and keep parts 3 to 5 terse — a sentence or two per bullet.

1. The question as it would appear on the paper, 400–500 words, with the case spelled out cleanly and the sub-questions in whatever order you think works best.
2. A model answer of 800–900 words — the sort of thing that would actually get a first-class mark, not a sketch of one.
3. The marking scheme: five must-hit points with marks adding to 20, plus one line each on what seperates an A answer from a B answer on that point.
4. Five specific mistakes or lazy moves that should cost marks, with one line each on why students fall into them.
5. Exactly five short bullets telling me which claims in your model answer are safe or near-orthodox and which are genuinely contested among people who work on this. I havent got any stake in the model answer sounding more settled than it is: I'd rather know where the live disagreements are, so I don't build a marking scheme that penalises a student for taking a reasonable dissenting line.
