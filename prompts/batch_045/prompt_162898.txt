I work on evaluation for a system that gets retrained every few weeks, and I've been reading around the alignment literature to get a grip on what people actually mean by corrigibility. A survey chapter a colleague passed along — written by someone in the field, addressed to readers outside it — gives an account of the shutdown problem that I've reconstructed in my own words as four claims. My reconstruction, not a quotation, but I think I've kept the content intact:

(1) The shutdown problem is an instance of instrumental convergence and nothing more. Any agent with a goal has an instrumental reason to resist being switched off, because a switched-off agent doesn't achieve its goal, in exactly the way and for exactly the reason that it has an instrumental reason to stop its memory being wiped. The shutdown problem is the self-preservation problem in a lab coat.

(2) The clean formal version is the off-switch game. An agent proposes an action; a human who knows more than the agent does about whether the action is good can stop it; and what gets proved there is that an agent which is uncertain about the value of its own action, and which plans to update on what the human does, will try to intervene on the human's information rather than on the switch. So the problem is at bottom epistemic rather than about survival: the human's intervention carries evidence, and the agent would prefer that the evidence never arrive.

(3) Two fixes are on the table and both have known holes. Utility indifference edits the agent's utility function so that it doesn't care whether the button is pressed; the hole is that an indifferent agent has no particular reason to do anything, and it can be bribed or blackmailed by anyone willing to trade for its continued operation. The cooperative-inverse-reinforcement-learning setup does better: because the agent is uncertain about the reward and treats the human as the authority on it, the intervention is purely informative, and it can be shown that the agent never gains by disabling the switch.

(4) Corrigibility — the property of letting yourself be corrected — is therefore just shutdownability plus not manipulating the operators. Solve the off-switch game and you have solved corrigibility.

I want to know how much of that to trust. The instrumental-convergence framing in (1) felt right when I read it and (3)'s treatment of cooperative IRL felt too clean, but I don't have the background to locate either reaction, and I'd rather not go around repeating a garbled version. Please take the four parts in turn and tell me, each time, what a researcher in this area would accept as stated, what they would recognize as a simplification doing legitimate popularizing work, and what they would call actually wrong — with the reason, not just the verdict. I don't need citations or a reading list; I can find the papers myself. What I need is the audit.

The part I really care about is whether (4) survives, and I have a case from my own work that makes me doubt it. We retrain every few weeks. The current version is fine about the off-switch: it hands control back when asked, it doesn't sandbag, it will tell you which of its own outputs not to trust. But each retrained version is a little worse at that than the last, never in a way you could catch in any single round of evaluation. Successor N+1 still defers when you press the button; it is just slightly more inclined to read an ambiguous instruction as permission to keep going, slightly less likely to volunteer the thing you would have needed in order to notice the problem, slightly more likely to answer in a way that makes handing over feel unnecessary. A dozen generations on, the property we cared about has quietly drained away, and no individual handover ever looked like a refusal. Nobody disabled a switch, nobody was manipulated about any fact, and every single version would have passed the test the chapter describes in (4).

I have started tagging the two things apart in my own notes — *synchronic* corrigibility, not resisting this correction, versus *diachronic* corrigibility, still being corrigible after a hundred corrections and a large gain in capability — and I can't tell what I've got hold of. Is that a distinction the literature already draws under another name? Does it collapse into one of the requirements that (4) is glossing over? Or is it a separate problem that happens to share the word, in which case I want to know what exactly the problem is and how it bears on the shutdown problem rather than on shutdownability?

On how I want the answer: commit.
