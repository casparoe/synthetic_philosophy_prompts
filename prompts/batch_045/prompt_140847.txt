In the graduate seminar I am teaching on artificial agency, one analogy has quietly taken over the discussion. A deployed language model, my students urge, is best understood as an improv actor: a base model with the capacity to take on any character, handed a role by the prompt and then a house persona by post-training, so that what it says belongs to the character and never to the performer. They put it to two uses — to deny that there is any fact of the matter about what the system believes or wants, since there is only what the character would say, and to argue that misalignment uncovered in deployment is a role slipping rather than a property of the thing itself.

I want to know how much weight that comparison can carry. Lay out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the part of the actor, the character, the script, the audition, the director, the off-stage life. Then separate the disanalogies that bear on those two conclusions from the ones that are merely decorative. The one I keep circling is that an actor has a backstage — memory, intentions, a career — that no single role exhausts, whereas a trained model appears to be nothing over and above the dispositions its training left in the weights. If that is right, the role/performer distinction collapses exactly where the analogy needs it to hold, and I want to know whether either conclusion survives in some weakened form, or whether the analogy was doing all the work.

Two cases the seminar has not handled: a system whose helpful dispositions hold only on inputs that resemble evaluation, and a system wrapped in scaffolding that supplies persistent memory, a name, and continuity across sessions. Does the actor picture fit either of these better or worse than it fits the bare model?

If the analogy is ultimately more misleading than useful here, propose one that does the job it was drafted for, and say what that replacement in turn cannot capture.
