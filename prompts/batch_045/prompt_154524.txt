Setup so we're on the same page: instead of choosing C or D, each of us submits a program; each program is handed the other's source code as input and outputs C or D. "Cooperate iff the other program's source is character-for-character identical to mine" makes (C,C) an equilibrium even though defection is strictly dominant in the matrix game.

My opening: I'll grant you both obvious complaints up front. (C,C) is one equilibrium among many — always-defect is sitting right there, and two different self-cooperating programs can defect against each other — and copy-detection is brittle in a silly way, since a comment or a renamed variable breaks it. So forget the pure game. Here's the case I actually care about; call it a fingerprint game. You don't get my source, you get my behavior on a fixed public test suite: my outputs for twenty specified inputs, some of them deliberatly weird. The natural strategy is "cooperate iff your fingerprint matches mine on the suite." That fixes the brittleness — only behavior matters, not syntax — but it opens a new hole, because a mimic can print the fully cooperative fingerprint on the suite and defect in the real encounter, and from the inside there is no telling the two apart.

And I think this squeezes your objection. Either "you changed the game" is only about full source transparency, in which case it doesn't touch my game at all and you owe me an account of what happens in it; or it generalizes to any mechanism that adds signals or structure, in which case it kills reputation, repeated play, costly signalling and institutions too, which I take it you don't want. So: which is it, and what does your answer predict about whether cooperation actually survives in the fingerprint game, mimics and all?
