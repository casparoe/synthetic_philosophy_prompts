hey — hoping you can help me with something ive been going in circles on for a while.

theres a pattern I keep noticing in how autonomous-ish systems get built, certified and investigated, and I want to know what it actually assumes. Roughly: the system is designed (and often legally required) to keep a human in the loop — a fallback-ready operator who's supposed to be able to take over in a couple seconds, whose attention gets monitored, and who is designated in advance as the responsible party (the manual, the terms of use and the statute all say you must keep your hands on the wheel / supervise the output / review before you send). Then when something goes badly wrong, the investigation concludes the human messed up: failed to monitor, over-trusted the thing, got complacent. Meanwhile the same report will note automation surprise, mode confusion, skill fade, and the fact that sustained monitoring of a system that's right almost all the time is basicly impossible for a person to do — and the manufacturer mostly just faces product-liability exposure, while no individual gets criminally blamed. So the human gets blamed for not doing something the design made them unable to do, and nobody quite says that out loud.

I've started thinking of this as the deployment needing a blame-shaped slot: the architecture has to contain some node whose job description includes absorbing blame, and a lot of design and regulatory effort goes into making sure that node exists and can be described as having had a fair shot. Two things make me think its about the slot and not about actual responsibility. First, the system is required to log and explain itself, and those logs and explanations are basically what let anyone say the human "had situational awareness" — so the epistemic side of blame gets manufactured by the system too, not just the control side. Second, there's a proposal floating around that deployments should have to file a pre-deployment responsibility map with the regulator, naming in advance, for each foreseeable kind of harm, who answers and in what currency (compensation, explanation, sanction, redesign, apology). That sounds more honest, but it might just be the same slot-filling moved earlier in the process.

So: what theory of responsibility does this whole pattern presuppose? Does it strictly presuppose that responsibility attaches to a designated role rather than tracking anyone's actual control, or could a control-based view explain the difference some other way? Is the slot doing work a control-based view should want — putting an incentive on the node that's cheapest to move, giving victims someone to confront, keeping a deterrent pointed somewhere — or is it just the need for a human-shaped target? And is the pattern coherent, given that the same legal order normally insists blame requires control and has excuses for people who couldn't have done otherwise? (I think philosophers call those the control and epistemic conditions on blame, but tell me if I've got the terms wrong.)

Then the part I care about most: suppose you swap in a view where responsibility for these harms is genuinely spread over the whole socio-technical setup and there just is no individual blame-taker — only answerability and duties to repair and redesign. What, concretely, would have to change in how these systems get designed, certified and investigated? And would the responsibility-map idea actually presuppose something different, or does it secretly need the same role-based story — I mean, can blame even be allocated in advance by stipulation, given that it's supposed to be retrospective and sensitive to what actually happened?

Please don't give me a survey of the literature or any citations, I can't check them anyway. I want the implicit theory reconstructed, I want to know which parts of the practice strictly presuppose it versus which parts would survive fine under several rival theories, and where its genuinely underdetermined just say its underdetermined.
