You're a professional philosopher who specializes in philosophy of mind and philosophy of science, and who also knows the interpretability literature on neural networks properly. Please think carefully about this one, and do some rough brainstorming about where the hard cases are before you commit to anything.

Here's my situation. I'm a philosophy professor, but I spend two days a month as philosopher-in-residence at a mid-sized AI lab, and my actual job there is to read internal interpretability reports and either sign off on or strike sentences of the form "the model represents X." It comes up four or five times a month, I get about two hours per claim, and the people writing the reports are ML engineers with no philosophy background. I keep swinging between a Dennett-flavoured "this is just a useful stance were taking" and a full-blooded realism on which some direction in activation space literally is a representation, and its not clear my gut is worth anything here.

So give me a decision procedure my engineers can run, not an essay:

- the questions in the order to ask them, and what counts as a sufficient answer to each (including what to do when the number simply isn't available)
- where the procedure just stops with "don't say represents," and where it hands the call to a person because it's a values question dressed as a factual one
- a short set of tests they can run in a couple of hours: steering, patching or scrubbing, probing, behaviour under distribution shift, whatever actually earns its place

The cases that keep breaking my gut, and that the procedure has to handle:

1. Dictionary degeneracy. Fit a sparse autoencoder to the residual stream and you can often get a different dictionary — different sparsity penalty, different seed — that reconstructs just as well but has no feature corresponding to X. So which decomposition is "the" one, and why should I defer to it?
2. Elicitation-dependence. We went looking for X because we happened to have labelled data for X, and lo, a probe finds X.
3. A direction that steers beautifully on our eval distribution and turns into something else, or into nothing, off it.

Then run it on three claims: (a) a "sycophancy direction" that, added into the residual stream, makes the model flatter the user; (b) a "golden retriever feature" that lights up on pictures and text about golden retrievers — I suspect this one comes back no, and I want you to tell me straight if it does; (c) a "deception" feature we'd have to assert in a model card going to a regulator. One of these should come out in a way I don't like.

Finally, say plainly what the procedure can't do. I assume it can't settle whether the model understands anything, or whether realism about representations is true in the first place.
