I'm writing a reading note for a graduate seminar on Dewey's experimentalism — the idea, as I'm reading him, that democracy is not merely one form of government among others but the political shape that inquiry takes when it is conducted in common — and I keep failing at the same sub-task. Intuitively, "experimental" cannot simply mean "tried out and then adjusted," because plenty of thoroughly undemocratic arrangements do that; but whenever I try to say what else is required, I end up either with something so demanding that nothing real qualifies, or with something so thin that a marketing A/B test qualifies. My supervisor has spent years handing down off-the-cuff verdicts on examples, and I have been writing them down. She also has a visible allergy to the phrase "evidence-based policy," which I take to be a clue, though I cannot say to what. Her rulings so far: a school district in which teachers and parents spent a year arguing about what the problem even was, two reading programs were then assigned to matched schools, results were published, and the district went on to reopen the question of what reading well consists in — experimental, without hesitation. A statewide initiative imposing a supermajority requirement for tax increases, passed after a well-funded campaign, containing no review provision and defended by its authors as a permanent correction to a known bias in legislatures — not experimental, on the grounds that this is a doctrine with a ballot attached. A city zoning ordinance preceded by eight months of genuine town halls and then never revisited, the council treating the town halls as having settled the matter — not experimental. A health department that randomized two appointment-reminder messages, measured attendance, published a working paper, rolled out the winner nationally and then declared the question closed for a decade, with no patient or staff involvement in framing the question and the attendance target never itself questioned — not experimental. And a housing authority that meets quarterly with a tenant union to review how a rent-relief rule is working, proceeding on residents' testimony that is minuted and published rather than on any formal measurement, revising the rule repeatedly and at one point redefining what would count as its working — experimental, and more warmly than the school district.

The case I actually care about is a live proposal in our city, and I need to predict her reaction before I present it. A public employment agency wants to replace its fixed schedule of caseworker contact with an adaptive allocation system: a learning algorithm continuously re-randomizes claimants across different intensities of support and reallocates effort toward whichever variant is currently producing the highest rate of employment at six months, updating weekly. Everything is logged, and a public dashboard reports the current allocation and its measured effects. Claimants are not told they are in an allocation and no claimant body has been consulted; the six-month employment target is written into the enabling statute, and the agency says it has no authority to reconsider it, though the statute sunsets in five years with a mandatory legislative review. A claimants' advocacy group has been given access to the dashboard and publishes its own readings of it, but has no formal role. Her verdicts seem to me jointly consistent with several different rules, and they come apart here: one that requires only that consequences be systematically observed, fed back into the practice, and made public, with no point at which inquiry is declared finished; one that additionally requires that the people affected be parties to fixing the problem and reading the results; and one on which the test is whether the end is itself held revisable rather than given, so that optimizing means toward a fixed end would not count.

What I would like is a reconstruction rather than your own view of Dewey. Set out the candidate rules that fit her track record equally well, say what each of them predicts about the employment-agency proposal, and give me your best guess at her verdict with your reasons for preferring that reading — tell me which of her past verdicts is doing the most work in your reconstruction, and tell me honestly if the track record is better explained by her having been inconsistent than by any single rule, and if so which verdict is the outlier. Then name the one further case you would put to her to force the surviving readings apart, and what she would have to say about it to confirm each. If her precedents genuinely leave the new case open, say which principle she would be committing herself to with each answer; I would much rather she commit to something explicitly than let the word "experimental" go on doing its work by sounding vaguely complimentary.
