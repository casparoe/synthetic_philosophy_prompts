I am a senior at a large public high school, and together with a faculty advisor I run our peer mediation program. We handle three or four conferences a week — roommate conflicts, group-project breakdowns, accusations of copying each other's work, disputes about whether something posted in a group chat counted as a threat — and each conference is capped at about twenty-five minutes. Both parties and the advisor sign whatever comes out of the room, and the dean of students reads it. My mediators are sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds with no formal training in anything.

What we do not have is any way to tell, in the room, whether we are looking at a disagreement that more talking will dissolve, one that no amount of talking will dissolve, or one where the honest thing is to stop and hand the matter to someone with actual authority. Right now we improvise, and our fallback is splitting the difference, which I have come to think is almost always the wrong move and is mostly just a way of ending the session.

I recently came across Aumann's agreement theorem and would like to build our intake procedure on it. As I understand the result: if two parties start from a shared prior, and each has come to know the other's posterior after each has seen their own evidence, and each knows that the other knows, and so on without end, then their posteriors must already be identical — so a disagreement of that kind, fully laid out on both sides, is not available to them. My use for this is diagnostic rather than mathematical. If a disagreement is not dissolving, then one of the assumptions behind that result has failed, and I want a procedure that finds out which one, in order, in about ten minutes, without asking either party for a number they cannot produce.

Please write that procedure for my mediators: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than of fact. It has to work under these constraints:

- Our parties cannot give probabilities. Give me questions that function with plain-language confidence, or with more-likely-than-not, and say what the mediator writes down when nobody will commit to anything sharper than that.
- One party sometimes holds evidence they are not free to disclose — a student who saw something but was told about it in confidence. Say what the procedure does when the posterior can be shared but the evidence behind it cannot.
- Sometimes the two parties do not share a prior, because their experience genuinely differs: one student has been in three fights, the other in none, and they are estimating the same shove.
- Sometimes what they disagree about is not really a proposition with a probability at all but a question of value, or a question about what to do rather than about what is the case.
- There is a clock. I have been told that the convergence result depends on the exchange running long enough for the posteriors to become common knowledge between the two, and that with a small fixed number of rounds it can fail even for otherwise faultless reasoners. Tell me whether my procedure should treat that as the normal case, and what exactly it should instruct a mediator to do when the bell goes and the parties are still apart.
- There is the delicate diagnosis: the case where the procedure concludes that these two should already agree and do not, which means someone is not updating or is arguing for the record rather than for the truth. I want the actual script for saying that to a seventeen-year-old to their face, and I want to know what the mediator is required to record.
- Please do not send me to the literature. I cannot get at journal articles, and my mediators will not read them; everything has to be usable from the procedure itself.

Then run the procedure on three cases from our docket.

1. Two lab partners. One says the other copied numbers from a shared spreadsheet into their own report. Both have looked at the same spreadsheet and the same edit history. They disagree about what probably happened.
2. A roommate dispute. One says the other's late-night calls are loud enough to hear through the wall; the other says they are not. Underneath that, they disagree about whether quiet hours are a matter of courtesy or a matter of rule.
3. The one I expect not to like. Two students, one of whom is a friend of mine, disagree about whether a message sent in a group chat was a serious threat. Both have read the same message and both know the same things about the person who sent it, and they disagree about how likely it was meant. I want to keep mediating, because I think one of them is wrong and that talking will show it. If the honest output of your procedure is that the priors here genuinely differ and that I should stop and escalate, I would rather it say so plainly than spare my feelings.

Finally, tell me where this procedure will misfire, which of its steps are judgment calls dressed up as steps, and what it cannot do at all.
