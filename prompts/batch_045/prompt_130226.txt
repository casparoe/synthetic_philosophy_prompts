I work on the research staff of a philanthropic foundation that spends roughly $25M a year on global health and poverty programs, and my job is to write the evidence memo that precedes each quarterly allocation decision. Eight to ten times a year the same question arrives in a different shape: should we decide now on what we already believe, or buy more information first — commission a pilot, pay for a longer-run follow-up, fund a replication in a new country, or delay a scale-up by a year so that a measurement can land before we commit? The decisions themselves range from $300k to about $10M; the studies we might commission range from $80k to $1.5M. The final call is made by a five-person investment committee voting by majority, three of whose members are not comfortable with anything more technical than a percentage. I get half a day with one analyst to produce the memo, and the memo has to survive being read out loud in the meeting.

I have read the standard expositions of Good's theorem. My understanding is that for an agent who maximises expected utility and updates by Bayes, the expected value of information that arrives before the decision and is free to act on is never negative, and that it is strictly positive only where the information could actually change what the agent does. If I have misstated it, correct me before building on it, because everything below rests on it.

What I do not have is a procedure. The formula is not my problem; my problem is that our decision environment violates the theorem's assumptions in at least five ways I can name, and I cannot tell which of those violations should reshape the procedure and which are noise. Specifically: the decider is a committee with heterogeneous priors voting by majority, and the committee that reads a study is frequently not the one that commissioned it; we almost never manage to pre-commit in writing to the decision rule that will be applied to the result; our findings are usually published, other funders read them, and our money is perhaps 4% of the relevant pool, so a result can help or hurt us through what it makes other people do; the committee does not maximise expected value but applies thresholds — it will not fund anything with more than roughly a 10% posterior probability of net harm, and it prefers a crisp ranking to a maximised one, which means variance-increasing information can be actively bad for us even when it is free; and the thing a study measures is only a noisy proxy for the thing we care about, because of external validity, implementation drift, and spillovers, so informativeness rather than sample size is usually what drives the answer. There is also the fact that delay has identifiable costs falling on identifiable people who would otherwise have been served, and how much to discount those people is a values question I do not want smuggled in as a parameter.

What I want from you is a written procedure my team can actually run, not a tutorial. It should be an ordered set of questions with a stated sufficient answer to each; it should say at which steps we can stop early and with what verdict; it should say where the procedure returns don't buy the information — because no result could change the action, because the prior already settles it, because the result cannot arrive inside the decision window, or because we cannot commit to acting on it; and it should mark clearly, as such, the places where judgment genuinely cannot be eliminated and the question must be handed to a person, naming which person: the analyst, me, or the board. I need it to work when we have no credible numbers, which is most of the time, so it should say what to do with rough elicitations and one-sided bounds rather than silently substituting a guess for a distribution. It also has to be presentable to a non-technical trustee in a paragraph, and I want it to be hard for a staff member who already wants the study to game.

Please apply it in full to three cases, using the numbers I give.

One. A $6M scale-up over three years into a region where we have not operated. Our prior on the effect, expressed in units of our cost-effectiveness bar where 1.0 is our marginal dollar elsewhere, is roughly lognormal with median 1.5 and an 80% interval of 0.7 to 3.2. A pilot would cost $250k and take 14 months, and would give an approximately unbiased estimate of the local effect with a standard error near 0.45 in bar units, but we think its correlation with the eventual scale-up effect is only about 0.6.

Two. A $400k follow-up measurement of long-run outcomes on a program we have funded for five years. The renewal vote is in 6 months and the results would arrive in 20; the program is one of two candidates to anchor a $12M country strategy that we will set in 18 months. The measurement is therefore late for the decision it was proposed for and early for a larger one, and in the interim the result would be published and read by other funders.

Three. Our flagship program: $10M over three years, about 40% of the portfolio, four staff attached to it, and a proposed $1.2M three-year rigorous evaluation. The board has never exited a program of that size, and a null result would in practice be read as a mandate to implement better. I expect the honest verdict here to be one I would rather not hear. Give it anyway, flag it as the unwelcome one, and defend it.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do. I assume it cannot rescue a bad prior, cannot assign value to hypotheses we have not enumerated, cannot settle whether we are buying a decision input or producing a public good for the field, and will manufacture false precision if I let it. Tell me which of those are fatal and which I can live with, and what else belongs on the list.
