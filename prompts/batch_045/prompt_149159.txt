i've been arguing with a friend about the extended mind thesis — the Clark-and-Chalmers Otto-with-a-notebook case — and he's built an argument I think definately proves too much, but I can't locate the broken step, and two of my own cases seem to help him rather than me. here it is, cleaned up a bit:

(1) Parity: if some structure outside the skull does the same job, in the same way, as an internal state we'd happily count as a belief, and the agent stands in the relation to it that everyone recites — reliably available, endorsed without re-checking, easy to access, and endorsed at least once before — then it is a belief of the agent's.
(2) The job in question is functional: a belief is a state that combines with goals to steer action and gets revised in response to evidence. Nothing in that job description mentions skulls or introspective access; being in the brain is just one way of getting the job done.
(3) So whether the notebook entry is a belief, or merely something Otto thinks with, is a question about the job, not the stuff.
(4) And the job is spelled out in causal-explanatory terms: a state is a belief if it's the state that best explains and predicts the agent's behaviour in the way beliefs do.
(5) Now look at the built environment. The layout of the supermarket near me reliably steers my route, I trust it without checking, it was endorsed by experience, and it predicts where I walk better than any sentence I could produce about where the milk is. Same for the worn patch in the sidewalk, my phone's icon arrangement, the fact that my mug is always to the left of the keyboard.
(6) So all of those are beliefs of mine. My mind contains the supermarket floorplan.
(7) And worse: that sidewalk patch steers everyone's route, and it's sitting there on days nobody walks it. So either one token state is a belief in dozens of minds at once, or there are beliefs belonging to nobody, or the thesis has no determinate content.
(8) And any apparent counterexample can always be redescribed as the resource not having been properly coupled, so the thesis can't be tested — it's either empty or false.
(9) So drop parity or drop the functional job description. Either way the thesis is dead.

Two cases of mine that I thought were counterexamples but now think might not be:

(a) The compressed notebook. Otto doesn't store addresses; he stores a grid of numbers plus a substitution rule he keeps in his head. Nothing in the notebook carries the content on its own — you need notebook plus decoding to get an address. But we're happy saying people believe things they can't currently state, that memory is stored in something like compressed form, and that a state you can't read off without decoding is still your belief. So content seems to depend on the process, not just the stuff — which looks a lot like the move he makes at (5).
(b) The sealed field notes. Otto writes observations into a notebook, seals it, mails it to a vault he has no key to, and never reads it again; if he ever got access he'd treat it as authoritative without checking. The internal analogue is a memory you can't recall but that still biases what you do. Does the sealed notebook hold beliefs? If yes, the access-and-endorsement conditions look decorative and parity does no work. If no, those conditions are doing all the work and parity is just window dressing on a skull-bound criterion — which is my friend's conclusion anyway.

My best guess is that the slide is between (4) and (5): causal coupling isn't constitution, and (4) illicitly lets anything that causally explains behaviour count as one of my states. But (4) also just sounds like functionalism stated badly, so I'm not confident, and (a) makes me think the coupling/constitution line is harder to draw than I'd assumed.

So: which step do you actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd rather keep (1) and (2) than give them up — I want Otto to be a genuine case and I don't want my mind to include the supermarket — so tell me what I'm paying for that combination, whether one of (a) or (b) is a red herring (and which way it cuts if it isn't), and what each of the usual moves — reject (4), tighten the job description at (3), or bite the bullet at (6) and (7) — has to give up.
