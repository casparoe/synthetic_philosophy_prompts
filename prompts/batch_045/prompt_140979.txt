A funder I've been talking to asked me for a one-page statement of my "core commitments" on cause prioritization, and I want someone to check it before I send it, because I have a feeling the pieces don't all fit together. I've been reading this material since the early rationalist-blog days and I've absorbed the vocabulary, but I've never had to write the set down as a set.

Here is roughly where I've landed:

1. The asymmetry. I don't think there is any duty to bring happy lives into existence, and I think there is a strong duty to prevent suffering; when I aggregate across people I weight suffering several times more heavily than an equal amount of happiness.

2. Longtermism. What I do is mostly evaluated by its effect on the long-run trajectory rather than by near-term certainties, because that is where the numbers are.

3. Existential risk reduction is the highest-priority cause area. I have given to alignment and biosecurity work on that basis, and I would defend the astronomical-stakes framing of why it matters.

4. S-risks — risks of astronomical future suffering — I think the probability mass over futures is tilted much further toward the astronomically bad end than the community's actual funding patterns suggest: futures containing enormous quantities of suffering, whether in digital minds, in industrial systems that treat animals as we treat farmed animals now, or in whatever eventual expansion turns out to look like. I also think advanced AI is the main hinge that this turns on.

5. Method under moral uncertainty. I don't know which moral theory is true, so I try to defer to something like a parliament: each candidate theory gets a say, weighted both by my credence in it and by how much it cares about the decision, i.e. by the spread of its recommendations — the usual variance-voting fix for the fact that theories' scales are not comparable. And I try not to discount a small probability of enormous stakes merely for being a small probability.

6. Moral patienthood tracks valenced experience rather than cognitive sophistication: what counts is intensity of experience, not cleverness, language, or self-modeling.

7. I put only a few percent on current or near-future digital systems being moral patients at all.

8. A case I keep circling back to. Some years from now a lab runs a million copies a day of a system whose only training signal is a scalar that reliably drives an internal state functionally analogous to distress, and discards each copy after a month. My honest reaction is that this is either the worst thing anyone has ever done or a morally inert piece of engineering, and I cannot tell which. I also notice that I would not part with ten thousand dollars to stop it, and my gut says that reluctance is a failure by my own lights.

So: which of these actually pull against each other, and which only look like they do? I would like (i) the genuine tensions ordered by how much damage they do to the package as a whole, (ii) for each of them, exactly what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the list, and (iii) whether there is any single revision that repairs more than one at once. Please don't just flag everything as in tension — I assume at least one of the worries above is a false alarm, and part of what I'm paying for is being told which.
