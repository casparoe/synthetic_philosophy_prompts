hey — I've got a works-in-progress thing on Thursday and I've slowly realised that what I call my view on luck and blame might just be a list of things I find plausible rather than a view. Diss chapter is on resultant luck, and the profile is roughly this:

(1) Some control condition. Differences in blameworthiness have to track something that's within the agent's control; roughly, you can't be *more* to blame purely because of how things turned out, if how things turned out wasn't up to you.

(2) But I accept resultant luck. The drunk driver who hits someone is more blameworthy than the otherwise identical drunk driver who makes it home, and I don't think that's just me caving to our practices — I think it's correct, and I think guilt, apology and what the agent owes afterwards should track it too.

(3) Constitutive and circumstantial luck erode. It counts against blameworthiness that being the kind of person who'd drive drunk is itself mostly a product of how you were raised, your temperament, your genes. Doesn't take responsibility to zero, but it erodes it. Same for the person who's never been tempted — I don't think their clean record shows more virtue than someone who resisted.

(4) Quality of will. What blame is *about* — what makes it the attitude it is rather than just a bad feeling aimed at someone — is what the action reveals about the agent's attitudes and practical reasoning. That's the core of blameworthiness for me.

(5) Tracing. Control can be diachronic: if your not-noticing is itself the upshot of an earlier thing you chose not to do, you're blameworthy for the result even though you had no control at the moment of action. I use this constantly for negligence.

(6) Method. I think the way to settle these disputes is to ask what our actual blame practices presuppose — what we're *doing* when we blame — rather than to armchair out what a rational agent would demand of another. I'm suspicious of revisionary accounts that tell us the practice is just mistaken.

Here's the case I keep using in the chapter and can't get to behave. Avery is very drunk, speeding, knows exactly what she's doing and doesn't care. Blake has had one drink over the limit, is annoyed with herself for driving at all, and is going slowly and carefully. Avery's speeding is what makes the pedestrian flinch backwards onto the sidewalk, so Avery kills nobody. Blake is the one who's there when a child runs out between parked cars, and Blake kills the child — and let's stipulate the death would have happened in exactly the same way if she'd been stone cold sober, so her impairment is causally idle. I want all of these: Avery is more blameworthy than Blake; Blake is more blameworthy than Blake in the world where the child doesn't run out; and Avery is not less blameworthy than a hypothetical Avery who killed someone. All three feel right to me and I can't see what single principle gives me all three. (Also, if it helps: I want to say the driver who kills someone who was already dying of something unrelated is somewhat less blameworthy than the one who kills a healthy person, which I know sounds unhinged.)

So: go through (1)–(6) plus those verdicts and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like it — I specifically want the merely-apparent ones separated out, because I keep getting told (1) and (2) are flatly inconsistent and I don't think that's quite the objection. Rank the real tensions by how bad they are, say for each what would have to give and what giving it would cost me elsewhere in the set, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Please don't just hand me "distinguish blameworthiness from blame" or "distinguish desert from sanction" — say what the distinction has to do in my case verdicts and what I lose by making it.
