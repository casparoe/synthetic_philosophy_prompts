I am writing a paper on whether Harsanyi's aggregation theorem really gives anyone a reason to be a utilitarian, and I keep going in circles with a friend in the economics department about how the theorem should be read. He says the result has two readings that are, in the end, one principle in different words. On the first reading, the one usually credited to Harsanyi, we rank social options as an impartial observer behind a veil of ignorance would rank them: someone who has an equal chance of turning out to be any member of the population and who evaluates a policy by its expected utility under a single probability distribution over states of the world. On the second reading, we rank options by how good they are for a member drawn at random from the population, where that member judges the options by her own lights, that is, with her own utility function and her own subjective probabilities. This second reading is the one you would get institutionally from sortition, from handing the choice to a randomly drawn citizen or assembly, and it is what my friend calls average expected welfare. In the baseline case of a fixed population and a single shared probability distribution the two readings return the same number, because the average of expected utilities is the expected value of the average. My supervisor thinks the whole debate is terminological, and I do not know whether he is right. So: is the agreement in the baseline case evidence that these are one principle rather than two, or is it an artifact of assumptions that the baseline quietly builds in? Where precisely do the readings come apart, and which of them, if either, is Harsanyi's own?

Here is my tentative map of where the difference might live; please check it, correct it, and tell me which of these points is doing real work. First, the observer's probabilities. If the veil operates with one shared distribution while the random-member reading uses each person's own beliefs, then under widespread disagreement about probabilities it can happen that every single person prefers policy A to policy B ex ante, each by her own lights, while the impartial observer prefers B. The observer then violates what looks like a Pareto condition, and the unanimity in favour of A begins to look spurious. Second, the observer's attitude to risk behind the veil. It seems to me that a great deal rides on whether she is risk-neutral in the currency of individual well-being: a risk-neutral observer gets something recognisably utilitarian, an observer who applies a concave transformation to each person's well-being before summing gets something prioritarian, and an extremely risk-averse observer gets something close to maximin, close to the use Rawls makes of the original position. If that is right, the theorem settles much less than its defenders claim, and the distance between Harsanyi and Rawls is not about the veil at all but about risk-attitude behind it. Third, variable population. If the observer's chance of being a given person is one over the number of people who actually exist, the reading appears to yield average utilitarianism; if instead the veil ranges over possible people in such a way that the chance of being any given possible person does not shrink as the population grows, it appears to yield total utilitarianism together with the repugnant conclusion. Is that a coherent way of stating the difference, and is the choice between those priors itself a moral assumption dressed up as probability theory? Finally, a worry that bears on both readings: individual von Neumann-Morgenstern utility functions are fixed only up to positive affine transformation, so weights and zero points can be traded off against one another, and it is unclear to me how much interpersonal comparison the theorem actually purchases.
