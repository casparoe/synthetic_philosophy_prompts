I'm a backend engineer, about twelve years in, mostly distributed systems, and I have basically no philosophy background — one logic class in undergrad that I hated. So please write for someone who thinks in terms of code review and unit tests, not in terms of possible worlds.

I got nerd-sniped a while ago by an AI alignment proposal I ran into through a blog summary (I genuinely don't remember who wrote the paper and I don't want a reading list — I can't get at papers from where I'm working anyway). The proposal: instead of trying to have a weak human or weak model directly judge whether a much stronger model's answer is right, you have two strong models argue against each other in front of the weak judge, and the judge just picks whoever argued better. Both debaters are rewarded for winning the argument, not for being correct, and the hope is that in equilibrium truth has a structural advantage, because a lie has to survive being poked at and a true claim has an easier time.

The standard objection people raise is what they call obfuscated arguments: the dishonest debater produces something that's wrong in a way that takes about as much work to find and explain as it would have taken the judge to just check the original claim, so the whole scheme buys you nothing.

Here's where my half-baked thinking starts. I think people run together two different failure modes and the difference matters a lot:

(1) Needle-in-haystack obfuscation. The flaw is completely understandable the moment someone points at it, but finding it means searching a huge space. Canonical example from my world: a 3000-line diff where one line quietly flips a signed comparison, or moves a lock by two statements. My instinct is that debate genuinely helps here, because the honest debater can do the expensive search and then the *pointing* is cheap, and pointing is all the judge has to verify.

(2) Competence-gap obfuscation. The flaw gets pointed at, and the judge still can't tell whether the pointing is right, because adjudicating it needs knowledge the judge doesn't have. Canonical example: this kernel has a data race, and here is the happens-before chain — good luck, judge. You can't fix this with more search, because the search already succeeded.

Then my second half-baked idea: you can sometimes convert (2) into (1) by demanding a witness that compiles into something checkable. "There's a bug in there somewhere" is unverifiable. "Run this input and it segfaults" is verifiable by, you know, running it. So maybe a lot of what looks like obfuscation is really just a claim that hasn't been compiled into a checkable artifact yet — and debate should work great in domains where claims have cheap witnesses and terribly in domains where they don't.

But here's the thing I actually can't settle and want the essay on. Does that move actually *scale*? Suppose you try to climb out of the competence gap with a ratchet: adjudicate a bunch of debates with your current weak judges, train the next generation of judges on the outcomes, repeat, so each generation can referee slightly harder debates than the last. My worry is that at every rung you're training on labels produced by a judge that was unreliable in precisely the cases that matter — the ones where the liar was good enough to win — so the ratchet amplifies the judge's failure modes faster than its competence. And if you patch that by running a debate about whether the previous judge got it right, you get a regress, and the regress burns judge attention and context, which is a real budget and not a free move.

One more gut feeling you can take or leave: the setup seems to structurally favor whichever debater says "there's a flaw here" over whichever one says "no there isn't," and I can't tell if that's a bug, a feature, or me misunderstanding the equilibrium.

So: write me an essay, roughly 1200 words, casual-ish but not sloppy, that weighs both sides and lands somewhere. Specifically I want you to (a) tell me whether the needle-in-haystack vs. competence-gap split is a real distinction or whether I've just described the same problem twice; (b) take a position on whether the checkable-witness move actually rescues debate in the competence-gap case, or whether it's circular because producing and checking the witness is itself the hard part in exactly the domains we care about; (c) take a position on the ratchet — climbs, slips, or something in between — and defend it against the best version of the other answer. Don't end on "more research is needed" or on a both-sides shrug; commit. If you think one of my framings is confused, say so and say why, but still answer.

End the essay with a final line that is exactly one of these three, with nothing after it:

Verdict: SCALES
Verdict: PLATEAUS
Verdict: DEGENERATES

where SCALES means the ratchet climbs arbitrarily far past the original judge's competence (obfuscation is an engineering problem with engineering fixes), PLATEAUS means it buys something real but asymptotes somewhere near the frontier of what the judges can already check, and DEGENERATES means that once debaters are strong enough that obfuscation is cheap to produce, rewarding them for winning makes the outcome actively anti-correlated with the truth, so debate is worse than just asking.
