I sit on the committee of a small family foundation that is deciding whether to move a slice of its grant budget into work on risks to humanity's long-term future, and my wife and I are expecting our first child in the spring, so I have been reading around the doomsday argument rather more obsessively than is good for me. The version I keep encountering — in a popular book one of the trustees pressed on me, and again in the discussion that followed — is carried almost entirely by an analogy. There are two urns, one holding a hundred consecutively numbered balls and one holding a thousand times as many; an urn is selected by the toss of a fair coin; you reach in blind and draw ball number seven; that, and nothing more, is said to be overwhelming reason to think you drew from the small urn. Applied to us the mapping is meant to run like this: we are not the people reaching into the urn but the balls; our birth rank, on the ordinary estimates something of the order of a hundred thousand million, is the number on the ball; the two urns are a near-term end to the human story and a long one; and so the total number of humans who will ever live is probably only a small multiple of the number who have lived already. My fellow trustee took this to show that the risk registers must be underestimating how close we are to the end, that the money should move, and that anyone who hesitates is simply refusing to update on a base rate.

Before I take that to the rest of the committee I would like the analogy audited rather than asserted, because the two things I most want to know are things the urn seems to settle by stipulation. The first is whether the set-up has already answered the question at issue. In the urn case it is fixed in advance that an urn was picked by a coin toss and that one ball was then drawn at random from a completed collection. In our case one could model the very same situation the other way round: a machine holds a ticket for every possible person, draws one ticket at random, and builds whichever world contains the person drawn — bigger worlds hold more tickets, so they are more likely to be built. I have a strong suspicion that in that re-description the inference vanishes, which would mean the original urn was not neutral scenery but a choice of sampling assumption, and that the analogy is doing its work by concealing a decision rather than by exposing one. My second worry is whether the analogy can say what the balls are. In the urn case the collection is given; in ours, whether it is all humans, all beings capable of entertaining this thought, or all of those plus whatever minds we might later build, changes the answer by orders of magnitude, and the last option seems to predict doom however well things go for us on Earth — which strikes me as a reason to think the collection has to be fixed by something outside the analogy itself. A related thought I cannot get straight: we are drawing whilst the urn is still being filled, and the balls are in a position to affect how many more get made.

I am not asking for references or further reading — I have no library access at the moment and I want your own reconstruction rather than a survey of what has been said, so please argue it out in your own terms and mark where you are uncertain. Please structure the response as follows: The mapping (about 150 words), setting out the correspondence between urn and world as a table; What the analogy gets right (about 200 words); Where it breaks (about 400 words), covering at least the sampling assumption, the reference class, the fixed total, and the fact that we are inside the collection rather than outside it; My re-descriptions (about 300 words), on whether either of my two alternative set-ups is a fairer model of our situation and what follows if it is; Does the conclusion survive (about 300 words), addressed specifically to whether a grant committee should shift money on this basis; and A better analogy (about 150 words), if one is to be had.
