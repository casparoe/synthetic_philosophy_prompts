I'm a senior at a fairly ordinary public high school, and I run a small philosophy club that meets on Fridays — usually eight or ten people, about half of whom are also in the psychology elective. Our next session is on the color-phi phenomenon and the argument that there may be no fact of the matter about *when* a conscious experience happens, and instead of just summarizing a paper I want to hand out a one-page flowchart that people can walk themselves through. As I understand the setup: you flash a red dot at one position, then a green dot at a second position a fraction of a second later, with the timing arranged so that people report a single dot travelling from the first position to the second and turning from red to green somewhere in the middle — that is, they report the change at a time before the green dot has actually been shown. The obvious way to divide things is into two stories. On one, the subject really was conscious of some earlier content and the memory of it was subsequently rewritten, the way a ministry of truth edits the past. On the other, the brain held things back, finished its processing, and only then produced a conscious experience already containing the final content, the way a show trial is scripted before the audience ever sees it. The move I keep running into is the claim that no experiment could ever tell these two apart, because any test would have to know when the experience occurred, and that is exactly what is in dispute. I don't want the handout to just assert that; I want it to show where in the logical space that conclusion sits.

So what I'd like is a decision tree built up from the logical space first and populated with the names of positions only afterward. Start everyone from something the whole club already accepts — say, that the subjects' reports are evidence about something and that the display timings are as I described — and lead them to a leaf by questions they can answer without having heard any of the labels, where each question is a concrete case or a crisp claim rather than something like "do you think memory gets revised?". The leaves I want included are: the view that there is a determinate fact of the matter and the original content was the conscious one; the view that there is a determinate fact and the reported content was the conscious one; the view that the two stories are equivalent descriptions and the question of when it became conscious is ill-posed; the no-central-theater alternative that is supposed to be what you are left with once you give that up; a quietist view on which there is a fact but it is in principle undetectable and the sensible response is to stop asking; and what I have been calling the matter-of-degree view, on which the two stories are not really a clean dichotomy because there is no sharp temporal boundary to the present moment. Where your questions allow combinations of answers that nobody actually defends, keep them as leaves and say in a line why the slot is empty — incoherent, wildly unattractive, or merely unexplored. And please flag any two leaves your questions cannot really pry apart instead of inventing a distinction to separate them.

Two things I especially want built into the diagnostic questions rather than deferred to the discussion. First, a mismatch case: suppose some immediate, automatic, non-verbal indicator — a speeded eye movement, a pupil response, a button pressed under time pressure — tracks the first content, while the subject's later verbal report tracks the second, so the two measures disagree about what was experienced. The club will split over whether that is evidence of revision or merely evidence about two different systems, and I want that disagreement to be a branch of the tree rather than a footnote. Second, a case a friend and I made up and could not settle: same two flashes, except that the subject has a button which, if pressed within about two hundred milliseconds of the first flash, cancels the second flash entirely — and on the trials where they press it in time, they still describe seeing the dot move and turn green. To me that seems to break the version of the second story on which the brain waits for the second flash and then writes the story, since the story contains something that never arrived; but maybe it only breaks a crude version of it, or maybe it breaks nothing at all. Put the case wherever in the tree it actually does work, and if the honest answer is that it fails to discriminate anything, say so and explain why. Keep the wording at a level a bright fifteen-year-old with no philosophy background can follow, gloss any term of art the first time it appears, and give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment — that is the part that tends to make people in the club change their minds.
