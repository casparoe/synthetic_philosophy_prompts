I'm at the prospectus-ish stage of a thesis on wether genuine moral dilemmas are possible — not the loose "that was a hard call" sense but the strong sense where whatever you do you're doomed either way — and before I let myself drown in the literature I want the spec rather than the survey. So: write me the desiderata that any adequate account of moral conflict has to meet. Which cases it has to get right (the set-pieces like Sartre's pupil or Sophie's choice, but also the boring ones — two promises that clash, a promise versus a small rescue, a triage call), which distinctions it must not collapse (conflict versus incomparability versus mere ignorance about where the balance of reasons lies; "must do A and must do B" versus "wrong either way" versus "no right answer here"; first-personal residue versus third-party blame), and which of the things people demand are genuinely data and which are just one camp's reflexes — everyone leans on agglomeration, for instance, and I suspect that's deontic logic flexing rather than a moral requirement. For each requirement give me the case or consideration that earns it a place on the list, and mark it non-negotiable or a preference of a particular tradition.

Two wrinkles from my seminar that I want the list to actually rule on instead of politely stepping around. First, buyout cases: suppose I promised my sister a lift to the airport, and there's a stranger on the roadside who needs getting to a hospital, and suppose the promise we made has an explicit escape clause — I can be released from it by paying her 200 euros. Is that still a dilemma at all, or just a choice with a price tag on it, and does your answer change if I can't afford the 200, or if paying it would break a third commitment? Second, self-inflicted and repeatable ones: does it matter that I knowingly walked into the clash, or that a nurse faces the same allocation conflict every single shift and stops feeling anything about it — one friend says culpable origin only makes me blameworthy for being there, not out of the dilemma, another says it collapses into ordinary avoidable wrongdoing. If some subset of the desiderata can't all be satisfied together, say so and say which gives — that's the outcome I most want, since it would be a result rather than a checklist. And tell me which single requirement knocks out the most otherwise-popular views; you can give consequentialism, Ross-style pluralism, a Kantian line and a residue/particularist line one sentence each if you score them at all, but don't spend the answer there.

Give it to me as a numbered list of exactly seven items, one or two sentences apiece, no intro and no wrapping-up paragraph at the end — fold the tensions and the "which requirement excludes the most" bit into the seven rather than tacking them on.
