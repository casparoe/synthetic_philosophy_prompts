I'm a PhD student in environmental policy, and I'd like help reconstructing the implicit theory behind a practice I'm writing about, because the more closely I look at it the less consistent it appears to be.

The practice is point-of-sale carbon offsetting. My university's travel policy now requires every flight to be offset: the booking tool tells me my share of the flight's emissions, computed by taking the aircraft's fuel burn and dividing it across seats or passenger-kilometres, sometimes adjusted for load factor and cargo; it lets me pay a per-tonne fee; and it then reports that my trip is carbon-neutral. The credits are denominated in tonnes and treated as fully fungible, so that a tonne of avoided deforestation somewhere cancels a tonne of jet fuel at altitude, and the cancellation is treated as complete, so that after paying I am described as having made no contribution at all. On the credit side, however, the accounting is much stricter: a project is credited only for tonnes that are additional, that is, for a counterfactual difference the project itself made.

Three things puzzle me. First, the debit side looks like average allocation rather than counterfactual accounting: the tonnes attributed to me are not the difference my flying made. On the same aircraft, the passenger whose booking tipped the airline into adding a second daily rotation and the passenger who took a seat that would otherwise have flown empty are charged the same amount, even though their causal contributions differ by orders of magnitude, and in the ordinary case both are close to zero. Second, my department both buys offsets and imposes a hard cap on flights per person per year, and I cannot see what view makes that combination sensible: if the offsets really neutralize my contribution the cap should be redundant, and if my individual flights make no difference to anything the cap should be pointless as well. Third, the institution sells the offset to me, the passenger, and treats my responsibility as discharged by the payment, while saying nothing about the airline, the fuel supplier, or my continuing participation in a practice whose harm is entirely collective.

So what I want is the implicit theory. Does this practice presuppose that an individual's contribution to a collective harm is a difference-making causal contribution, measurable in counterfactual tonnes, so that responsibility can be zeroed out by producing an equal and opposite difference elsewhere? Or is it also compatible with views on which my contribution is a matter of complicity, or of failing to do my share under a fair division of a collective duty, views on which the payment might express something but could not cancel anything? Is the asymmetry between strict counterfactual accounting on the credit side and averaging on the debit side a defect, or can some single theory license both?

I also want to know what the practice strictly presupposes as opposed to what it merely fits well with. Does neutralized-at-checkout require that harms be additive and roughly linear in tonnes, so that a tonne here and a tonne there are interchangeable units, or is that only a bookkeeping convenience that could be dropped while keeping the practice? If aggregate harm is instead threshold-shaped, or if marginal damage rises with cumulative emissions, does the notion of a neutralized individual contribution survive at all? And if we replaced the implicit view with, respectively, a strict difference-making view, a no-difference view of the inefficacy-objection sort, and a fair-share view, what would have to change in the policy: would offsets be revealed as incoherent, or merely relabeled as contributions to a fund whose point is not cancellation? Finally, is the practice underdetermined, in the sense that several of these theories would all recommend buying the offsets while disagreeing entirely about why, which would mean my department's policy cannot be treated as evidence for any one of them?
