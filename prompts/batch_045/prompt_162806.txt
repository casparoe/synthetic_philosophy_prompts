I spent a little over forty years in experimental biology, most of it on membrane transport, and I have been reading philosophy in earnest since I retired. One habit I carried over from the bench is an intolerance of disputes that never reach the point where you write down, in advance, what result would count against you. So what I would like from you is this.

Produce the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration on Parfit's hitchhiker case and the thought attached to it that it can be rational to make oneself irrational. Set the case out in your own words as you go; I want the argument, not a summary of a text. The bare story is this. A driver will carry a stranded hitchhiker to town only if she promises to pay him a substantial sum on arrival. Once they arrive, paying is pure cost to her and he cannot compel her. She therefore needs something at the roadside that will make him willing to open the door. Both camps grant that a bare resolution to pay does not do it, and both grant that she would do well to be a grateful and reliable person, or to be fitted with some mechanism that makes her pay. They disagree about what is carrying the weight.

Camp A, the belief-first or rational-irrationalist camp: the load-bearing state is genuinely doxastic. She must actually believe, or assign high enough credence to, the proposition that she will pay, and instrumental rationality can permit or require her to bring that state about and sustain it, even though she knows she installed it by non-evidential means and even though her evidence does not support it. On this view the evidential norms governing belief have whatever authority they have in virtue of their instrumental value, and they can be overridden.

Camp B, the evidentialist or policy-first camp: the norms on belief are not negotiable in that way. Whatever such manipulation installs is either not a belief or not a rational one, and either way it is not what should be recommended. The work must be done by desires, intentions, policies, character, reputation, or external pre-commitment. Beliefs may follow along behind, but they are not what the driver is relying on.

Characterise the positions rather than their adherents. I want no names, papers, or citations: I am reading your answer on a machine with no library behind it, and I would rather have the arguments standing on their own feet. If you think one of the two camps as I have described it is not a view anyone could actually hold, say so and reformulate it into the nearest view someone could hold, rather than knocking down the version I gave you.

Format the document as follows.

1. A numbered list of agreed claims, the claims both camps could sign. These should be substantive enough to bite rather than instances of the observation that the case is hard: claims about what the driver is responding to, about what a mere intention or a mere desire achieves, about what a device that destroyed her responsiveness to reasons altogether would and would not achieve, and about what any state must satisfy in order to solve the driver's problem.

2. A disagreements table, one row per crux, with columns for the crux; Camp A's own formulation of its position; Camp B's own formulation of its position, each in wording that camp would accept as a statement of its own view; the case, argument, or consideration both camps agree bears on the crux; Camp A's prediction about how that bears on it; and Camp B's prediction. Predictions should be stated so that they could in principle be checked: give a direction of effect, a rough magnitude, or a threshold at which the camp would concede, rather than a hedge.

The cruxes I expect to see treated, though you should add, drop, or substitute as you judge right: whether the state that moves the driver must be a belief as opposed to an intention, policy, or settled disposition; whether her knowing that the state was self-installed undermines it; whether instrumental considerations can override evidential norms or whether those norms are constitutive of belief itself; whether rationality here is assessed over the whole episode including the manipulation, or only at a time-slice; and the broadly empirical question of how reliably such states can be installed, how long they last, and how far they contaminate her other beliefs.

For the bearing column, please use at least the four variants below, and record what each camp predicts of each.

(a) The meter. The driver carries an instrument that reads, with a known reliability, the hitchhiker's present confidence that she will pay, and is blind to her desires, intentions, and policies. Does Camp A's mechanism still work? Does Camp B's?

(b) The bond. Before the ride she may lodge a sum with a third party, forfeited if she fails to pay. Both camps should agree this solves the driver's problem. What does its success show about the cruxes above?

(c) The ledger. She hitchhikes repeatedly in a district where every completed transaction and every default is publicly recorded. What happens on the early rides, on the late rides, and on what each camp should treat as the last ride?

(d) The leaky installation. Whatever technique installs the confidence that she will pay also raises her confidence, by some margin, in unrelated false propositions that she will act on. At what rate and magnitude of leakage does each camp withdraw its recommendation?

3. A short section on which parts of the dispute are merely verbal, that is, which parts a stipulation about the words belief, rational, and irrational would dissolve, and which parts survive every rewording you can devise.

4. A confidence note. For each numbered agreed claim and each row of the table, state how confident you are that both camps would genuinely sign it, and give your overall confidence in the substantive claims of the document as a whole. Then single out the one claim in what you have written that you are least sure of, and say what would settle it.
