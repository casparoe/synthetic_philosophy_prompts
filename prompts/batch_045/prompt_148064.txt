Me and two people from my old LessWrong meetup got into it in the group chat a few weeks back about the doomsday argument, and I've been chewing on it since, partly because I actually moved money over it — I shifted maybe 15% of my giving from longtermist stuff to near-term stuff — and now I'm not sure the argument can bear that kind of weight. Here's the version I ended up defending, with the parts I think are load-bearing written out explicitly instead of waved at. (1) Anthropic sampling: I should reason as if I'd been drawn at random from all the humans who will ever exist — conditional on the total N, my birth rank is uniform over the N slots. I've been leaving future uploads and AIs out of the class, which I realize is itself a choice. (2) I don't know my exact rank, but on the usual back-of-envelope there've been something like 10^11 humans so far and ~8×10^9 of us alive now, so my rank is order 10^11. (3) Likelihoods: if N is only a few times 10^11, a rank that low is completely ordinary; if N is 10^15 or 10^18, the chance of landing that early is r/N, so somewhere between 10^-4 and 10^-7. (4) Prior: I've got no special reason to think the total is one order of magnitude rather than another, so I spread my credence evenly over the log of N across, say, 10^11 to 10^20 — that's supposed to be the humble part, the bit where I assume as little as possible. (5) Multiply and normalize: for any N above my rank the posterior goes like (1/N)·(r/N), so it falls off as 1/N², and something like 90% of the posterior mass lands below ten times the number of people who've already lived. So: expect human headcount to stop growing more or less around now. (6) Here's the part that made my friend say the whole thing is broken: the ratio in (3) is a fixed factor of four to seven orders of magnitude that doesn't care what else you know. If you think you have strong evidence that we can build settlements that last a billion years, that evidence has to move your prior by more than those four to seven orders of magnitude just to break even, and you can rerun the same shape of argument on narrower classes — people who've ever lived in a democracy, people who've ever heard of the doomsday argument — and get a fresh "this class is about to stop growing" verdict each time, which is a lot of conclusions to get out of one piece of a priori reasoning about where you sit in line. (7) And the upshot is just wild: on (5) I'm supposed to expect humanity, or at least human-scale reproduction, to fizzle within centuries to a few thousand years, while simultaneously being able to look at the same world and see how a big future would get built. Somebody in the thread said something about the self-indication assumption cancelling the whole effect exactly, which I never followed and honestly don't trust.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you? I'd rather keep (1) and the bare Bayesian bookkeeping in (5) than anything else, since those feel like they're just probability theory applied to a self-locating question, so if there's a cost to pay I'd rather pay it somewhere else — but I can't tell which of the other steps is doing the illegitimate work, and I want to know what I'm buying with the 15%. One more thing that bugs me and that I can't place: the argument as I've written it bounds headcount, not calendar time, so a future that lasts a billion years at a trickle of births fits (5) perfectly well, and I genuinely can't tell whether that makes the argument weaker, or just weirder than people admit.
