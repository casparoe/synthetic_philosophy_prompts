I'm teaching an upper-level seminar this term (phil of language / language and power cross-list) and I need to write one essay question for the final on Hofstadter's Person Paper satire—the one where the narrator defends 'white' as the generic word for humans and 'whe'/'ble' as the third-person pronouns, and the negrists want 'person' instead. I want a question that students can't answer just by parroting 'it maps sexism onto racism: defending generic 'man' is like defending generic 'white', so generic 'man' is sexist the way the fictional language is racist.' That's the easy summary and I want to fail anyone who stops there.

Quick recap so we're on the same page (no outside reading, no citations, just from the piece itself): the narrator says there is great beauty to a phrase such as "All whites are created equal." He claims that in most contexts it is self-evident when "white" is being used in an inclusive sense, in which case it subsumes members of the darker race just as much as fairskins. He also says that, used inclusively, the word "white" has no connotations whatsoever of race. About compounds like chairwhite, mailwhite, saleswhite, and freshwhite, he says the suffix "white" is usually pronounced like "wit" in terms like saleswhite, freshwhite, penwhiteship, and first basewhite, and he adds: "It's just a simple and useful component in building race-neutral words." The fictional third-person pronouns are "whe" for whites and "ble" for blacks, and one of the jokes is that a crazy reform is supposed to be abandoning the racial distinction and using he for male whites (and blacks) and she for female whites (and blacks). In actual English the analogues are man, mankind, chairman, mailman, and generic he versus the proposed person replacements, with women playing the role of blacks and sexism the role of racism.

The complication I want the question to force is one I've been calling the bleached-default vs transparent-generic distinction. A bleached default is a compound or affix-like use where the group-marking morpheme has, apparently, lost its group-marking force: the "white" in "chairwhite" is, on the narrator's own telling, just a useful component, pronounced "wit", not a claim that the chair is white or that whites are the norm. A transparent generic is a live, semantically compositional use where the term really does present one subgroup as the unmarked norm for the whole kind: the inclusive "white" in the "All whites are created equal." example, or the generic "whe", is, or at least seems, transparently about making whiteness the default human. The same distinction can be run on actual English: is "chairman" a bleached compound, or is it still a transparent default? Is "mankind" bleached? Is generic "he" a bleached pronoun or a live default assumption? What would count as evidence either way?

So please write me three things.

1. The essay question itself, aimed at upper-level undergrads, something they could answer in about 45 minutes (roughly 700 words). It should ask whether (and for which uses) the satire's moral indictment of the fictional "white" language transfers to actual English man/he language, and it should explicitly make them use the bleached-default vs transparent-generic distinction. It should not be answerable by reciting the analogy, and it must not let them off the hook with the two-sense/ambiguity defense (that man just has a generic sense) unless they say what the satire implies about the parallel claim that inclusive "white" has no connotations whatsoever of race.

2. A model answer of about 600 words that a strong student would give. It should name the crux: e.g., that the satire's force depends on treating the narrator's inclusive-meaning claims as false in the fiction (readers are supposed to hear "All whites are created equal." as making whiteness the default), so if a real use of man is genuinely bleached or genuinely ambiguous in the same way, the analogy weakens; but the satire can still be a powerful debunking of transparent generics and of the rhetorical move of insisting that inclusive man has no sex connotations. Don't just sit on the fence: give the best positive account of how far the indictment transfers.

3. A marking scheme out of 20, built around five must-hit points worth 4 marks each, with a one-line note on the classic mistake for each point. The five points should be something like: correctly reconstructing the satire's target and the direction of the analogy; stating the bleached-default vs transparent-generic distinction and applying it to at least one fictional and one actual example; handling the just a simple and useful component bleaching defense from the text; handling the ambiguity/two-sense reply and the narrator's inclusive-sense claim; and giving a reasoned verdict on which actual uses (compound vs live generic) are convicted and which, if any, escape, plus what would count as evidence. Make the A/B separation turn on whether they actually apply the distinction instead of just asserting it, and on whether they see that the satire's own text gives the bleaching reply in the narrator's voice.

No bibliographies, no reading lists, no 'as so-and-so argues', nothing that needs a library or the web. Everything should be doable from the satire plus the distinction I gave you. Casual style is fine for the question—address the students directly if you want—but the model answer should be tight. I'd rather have a slightly weird, picky question that seperates the people who did the reading from the people who memorized the slogan than a broad one that lets everyone write 'Hofstadter shows that language embeds default assumptions' and sit down.
