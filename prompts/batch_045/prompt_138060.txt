hey — I'm doing methods review for a psych journal and I keep having the same fight with a co-author. Papers come in where subjects use some resource while doing a task — a notebook, a phone, a checklist, an autocomplete, another person — and the write-up says "participants remembered / decided / reasoned", and my co-author says no, the resource did that bit and the participant read it off. I dont want to keep settling this one paper at a time, so I want the spec.

Write me the desiderata for a criterion of cognitive extension in the extended-mind sense. Fix the purpose for me: the criterion's job is to say, for a given subject + task + resource, whether an explanation of the performance should count the resource as part of the cognitive system that did it or as part of that system's surroundings. So heavy metaphysical "constitution" talk only earns its keep if it cashes out in some difference to how we describe, model, or intervene on the performance — if it cant, say so and drop it. I do NOT want a new theory of my own; I want the list of things any theory has to deliver to be usable for that job.

For each requirement give me the case or consideration behind it — why anyone should accept it, not just that people do — and mark whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp. Here are cases I already think a criterion has to sort; add your own where mine arent load-bearing:

- the famous one: someone with memory trouble who always carries a notebook, writes things in it, and uses what he reads there without checking — vs. someone who hears about an exhibition, remembers it herself, and goes; vs. someone who has to look the address up in a guidebook he has never seen before
- a blind person's cane, and seperately a pair of glasses: if the criterion lets both in, is that a cost or a feature, and what does it do to the criterion's purpose
- my phone's contact list: I can recall maybe two numbers, I'd be lost without it, and I often dont know whether a number it shows me is right or whether the entry is the current one. This is the case that breaks things for me.
- a generative writing assistant that is maybe 85% right on the kind of thing I use it for, that I've learned to correct for, whose output I cant predict in advance but recognise as soon as I see it, and that I sometimes argue with and overrule
- a couple's shared calendar where one partner's recall depends on what the other one typed in
- an abacus user's abacus, and the same person's abacus twenty years later when he can no longer say what his hands are doing but still gets the right answer
- a notebook its owner has stopped trusting but still cant stop consulting; and the same kind of notebook for someone who only started using it last week and still checks it against memory every time
- a whiteboard across the room that I know is there but cant currently read from where I'm sitting

Stuff I explicitly want handled rather than assumed:

1. Whether "extended" is all-or-nothing or admits degrees — and if it admits degrees, what the criterion says in the middle cases and what a reviewer is supposed to write down there. I suspect most of my fights with my co-author are secretly about this and nobody says so.
2. Whether the subject gets a vote. Does first-person authority ("that's not me, that's the app talking") settle the question, get weighed, or get overruled by the criterion? Pick one, justify it, and give the case that shows what each option costs you.
3. Both boundary conditions, as one requirement each if you can: what stops the criterion from swallowing everything (cognition depends on oxygen, on language, on culture, on the layout of the room — at some point "extended" stops being a claim) and what stops it from being so strict that nothing external ever counts and the whole literature was pointless.
4. A temporal requirement. The papers I review are about learning and about skill decay, so I need the criterion to say something about a resource that is in the middle of being integrated into someone's performance, or in the middle of being abandoned. A criterion that only scores snapshots is not much use to me.
5. Something about error and disagreement: what has to happen when the subject and the resource conflict, who wins, and why the answer to that is evidence about whether they form one system. I think this does more work than the trust condition people keep listing, but argue me out of it if I'm wrong.

Then two more things. First: which two or three of your desiderata probably cant be jointly satisfied, with the case that shows the strain — and be explicit that this is a result about the list, not a complaint about any particular theory. Second: which single requirement knocks out the most candidate theories, since that's the one I should press people on in review.

Sections, in this order:

- "What the criterion is for" — about 100 words, and open by saying plainly what my stated purpose does and does not entitle me to demand
- "The desiderata" — the bulk, around 900 words, one short block per requirement with its motivating case and its negotiable/non-negotiable marking; a table at the end of the section (requirement / case / status) is fine if it makes it readable
- "Where the list fights itself" — about 250 words
- "What to give up on" — about 150 words, i.e. which requirements I should stop expecting any theory to meet

Pass/fail per requirement in a table is fine, and dont explain the four views to me.
