Hi — I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so go easy on the jargon (but please don't dumb it down, I'm happy to do a bit of work). I've got a passage I keep going round in circles on and I'd love someone to talk me through it.

My book is about a sound archivist, Marta, who is trying to reconstruct a lost opera from a crate of damaged wax cylinders and a partial score. That's the setup, and the bit I actually need help with is this — from Wittgenstein's *Tractatus*, written during the First World War and published in 1921 (I'm reading it in the old Ogden/Ramsey translation):

4.0141 In the fact that there is a general rule by which the musician is able to read the symphony out of the score, and that there is a rule by which one could reconstruct the symphony from the line on a gramophone record and from this again—by means of the first rule—construct the score, herein lies the internal similarity between these things which at first sight seem to be entirely different. And the rule is the law of projection which projects the symphony into the language of the musical score. It is the rule of translation of this language into the language of the gramophone record.

Four things I can't settle:

1. What on earth is a "law of projection" here? Is he just saying: for the score to be a picture of the symphony at all, there has to be some rule linking them — and leaving it at that? Or is he making a stronger claim about where that rule lives (in the notation? in logic? in the musician's head?).

2. Marta's narrator insists there's an asymmetry: score → performance is *following* a rule, while groove → score is *guessing*, invention, not obedience. But 4.0141 seems to run the other way too, getting the score back out of the groove "by means of the first rule". Is the passage quietly denying the asymmetry my narrator cares about, or is that my misreading?

3. The bit I most want to use. Marta's dead mentor has a home-made doctrine I've invented, which she calls the shortest groove: of all the rules compatible with everything that's been played and recorded so far, the one you're really following is the most compact one. That's my characters' answer to the Kripkenstein worry — you know, the one where the sceptic says no fact about you settles which rule you meant, and you can fit infinitely many rules to your past behaviour. So: is "pick the shortest one" a genuine answer to that sceptic, or is it the sort of move that's already been dealt with? I half-remember something about the word "simplest" needing its own justification, which would just restart the problem a level up — is that right, or am I confusing myself?

4. And the bigger thing: would the 1921 Wittgenstein even have felt the force of that sceptical puzzle, given that he's working with this picture of an internal, logical scaffolding shared by score, sound and groove?
