I am a high school senior, and over the past year I have gotten into the habit of noticing how the word "true" behaves in arguments — in debate club, in class discussions, and in group chats. It started when a friend of my brother's, who is in graduate school for philosophy, mentioned that a lot of philosophers hold that saying "that's true" adds nothing to simply repeating the sentence it is about: that "it is true that the meeting is on Friday" and "the meeting is on Friday" say the same thing, and the word "true" is just a device for referring back to, or signing on to, a sentence. I have been testing that idea informally, and most of the time it seems right. But I keep running into a use of "true" where it seems to be doing real work that repeating the sentence would not do, and I would like to know what, if anything, this is called.

Here are the cases. In a debate round last month my partner said, "It's true that the dress code applies to every student, but that's irrelevant to whether it's a good policy." When I replace the first clause with the bare sentence — "The dress code applies to every student, but that's irrelevant to whether it's a good policy" — the result is grammatical, but the feel changes: the bare version sounds like asserting something and then immediately undercutting it, while the "it's true" version sounds like handing the claim over untouched and then refusing to go where it leads. A second case: a classmate said of a history textbook, "Everything in that chapter is true," without being able to produce a single sentence from it; here "true" seems to let you take a stand on claims you could not state, and on indefinitely many of them at once. A third: someone in a group chat typed "It's true, I guess" after losing an argument, everyone treated the argument as over, and yet what he conceded was not really the point that had been in dispute.

What I think is going on, put as carefully as I can: "true" lets a speaker do something with a claim other than assert it — grant it, generalize over it, or set it to one side — while presenting the claim as coming from nowhere in particular rather than from the speaker's own endorsement. It is impersonal in a way that nearby phrasings are not. "I agree that the dress code applies to everyone, but..." makes the concession about me; "It's true that the dress code applies to everyone, but..." makes it about the claim, and lets me grant it without owning it — a kind of no-fault concession. The device also seems to have a boundary I cannot explain: "It's true that she has a headache, but that's no excuse" sounds fine, while "It's true that I have a headache, but that's no excuse" sounds off, as if the move requires the claim not to be one I am the authority on.

So my questions. Does this family of uses have an established name in philosophy of language or linguistics — the hand-over use, the generalizing use, the concede-then-object use — or are these several unrelated things that I am wrongly lumping together? I know there are views on which "true" is a device for endorsing or generalizing over sentences, and views on which it does something else entirely; I want to know which of them, if any, predicts both the impersonal feel and the first-person oddity, and what each of them gets wrong about my three cases. If nothing established fits, I would like a term and a definition with the boundary cases stated — what counts as an instance, what does not, and how it differs from ordinary hedging and from simply saying "I concede that." And one last thing: does any of this threaten the idea I started from, that "true" adds nothing to the sentence, or is the extra work it does here the kind of work that idea was never trying to rule out?
