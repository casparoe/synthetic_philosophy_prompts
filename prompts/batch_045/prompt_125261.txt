For six weeks a graduate reading group I lead has been going round the same three moves in the debate over the principle of alternative possibilities, and I want a diagnosis of why it has stalled rather than a verdict on the principle itself.

The dialectic as we have reconstructed it. Frankfurt's original argument: a counterfactual intervener wants the agent to A, prefers not to show its hand, monitors for a prior sign that she is about to do otherwise, and would force A if that sign appeared; the sign never appears, she As on her own, the intervention never occurs — and the shared judgment is that she is responsible for A-ing, so responsibility cannot require the ability to do otherwise. Three replies then recur. First, the flicker reply: some alternative always survives (deciding a moment later, an involuntary twitch, the prior sign itself), so the case never really eliminates alternatives; the real question becomes whether the survivors are robust enough to carry the weight the principle puts on them. Second, the dilemma: either a flicker remains, or the case purchases the absence of alternatives by positing a deterministic tie between sign and action, which assumes what the incompatibilist denies. Third, the retreat: incompatibilists grant the case but relocate the requirement, insisting that responsibility needs the agent to be the ultimate source of her action rather than to have had alternatives — arguably a different thesis trading under the old name.

The case I added, and which broke the group in half. Call it the unwatched case: there is no intervener, no monitor, and no predictor. The guarantee that the agent will A comes entirely from her own deliberative dispositions — every step of her reasoning is one she was disposed to take, she has no disposition to revise, and nothing outside her makes it so. Compatibilists in the group treat it as an obvious Frankfurt-style case and keep their responsibility intuition; incompatibilists say it is determinism in disguise and therefore inadmissible. That split is my evidence for a hypothesis.

My hypothesis, offered as one: the stalemate is not about alternative possibilities at all. It is that the two sides count different hypothetical cases as data, because 'could have done otherwise' is implicitly relativized — to the circumstances held fixed, including whether the intervener's presence is part of the background, and to a contrast class of alternatives — and they disagree about which relativization matters for responsibility, which is itself a normative disagreement about what holding responsible is for. If that is wrong, I want the better diagnosis and the reason.

Give me exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. Item 1: your verdict on what kind of disagreement this is. Item 2: the single distinction or hidden question doing the work. Item 3: the strongest evidence for it in what each side actually says, including how it explains the split over my unwatched case. Item 4: the strongest reason to reject my hypothesis. Item 5: what would count as progress given your diagnosis, or why I should expect none. Do not survey the literature, do not adjudicate the principle, and do not tell me the question is simply hard.
