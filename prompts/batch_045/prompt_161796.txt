I'm an undergraduate taking two courses this term that seem to be about the same thing and never mention each other. In a philosophy of mind seminar we are working through the exclusion argument: mental properties supervene on physical properties; every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause; effects are not, as a rule, systematically overdetermined by distinct sufficient causes; so mental properties, if distinct from physical ones, are causally inert with respect to physical effects such as behaviour. In a methods course I am learning causal inference entirely in the vocabulary of structural causal models: directed acyclic graphs, structural equations with independent error terms, d-separation and the Markov condition, do-interventions and identifiability, mediation and path-specific effects, potential outcomes, and faithfulness. I can read an applied mediation paper in that vocabulary, but I do not yet know where it stops being able to say things.

I would like you to translate the exclusion argument into that vocabulary as faithfully as you can, and then give me a residue report. Do not give me a bibliography, citations, or a reading list, and do not attribute premises to named authors or papers — describe the positions instead. Where a premise belongs to a specific school, say which kind of theorist holds it rather than who.

Here is my own attempt at the encoding, which I want you to correct or replace rather than humour:

Supervenience: a mental variable M is a deterministic structural equation M = f(P) with no error term of its own, so M sits downstream of the physical variable P and adds no information about the world beyond P.

Causal closure: the later physical-and-behavioural variable Y has all of its parents among physical variables, and there is a directed path from P to Y.

The exclusion step: once P is fixed — do(P = p) — variation in M produces no variation in Y. Equivalently, M is d-separated from Y by P, so M has no directed path to Y that is disjoint from P's path, and M is not a mediator of the P-to-Y effect. On this reading the conclusion is not "M causes Y a little" but "the arrow M → Y is absent; M is a leaf."

The no-overdetermination premise: the graph has exactly one directed path that is sufficient for Y, not two, and the second route through M is excluded by a parsimony or faithfulness-like constraint rather than by anything in the joint distribution.

Please take that apart and rebuild it properly. Then the residue report, in three labelled parts: what carries over without loss; what carries over only after distortion — and for each distorted item, say precisely which assumption of the target framework does the distorting, so I can tell whether the translation is honest or is smuggling in the framework's metaphysics and calling it translation; and what cannot be expressed in the structural causal model vocabulary at all.

Four things in particular I want pressed, because I suspect each of them is where the translation fails:

1. Vacuity of the screening-off premise. If M = f(P), then M conditional on P is constant, so M is independent of Y given P for every possible distribution over these variables. That means the independence the argument relies on cannot distinguish "M is an inert leaf" from "M is a bona fide mediator whose effect is perfectly screened off by its own cause." Since no observational or interventional data could separate those two graphs, the exclusion step looks like it is not being made in the language of causal discovery at all. Is that right, and if so what kind of claim is it instead?

2. The manipulability problem. In my methods course causation is defined only for variables that can be intervened on independently: a cause is what you can change by surgery while holding the rest of the system fixed. But if M is a deterministic function of P, there is no do(M = m) that leaves P untouched, so on the interventionist definition the question "does M cause Y?" may not be answerable negatively as much as ill-posed. If that is right, the argument's negative conclusion may be an artefact of forcing a non-manipulable variable into a formalism built for manipulable ones, and a defender could say the framework simply lacks the resources to pose the question. Alternatively, the lesson may run the other way: the interventionist definition was designed to strip out the very notion of "what actually produces the effect" that the argument needs. I want you to adjudicate that, not just list it.

3. Variable constitutions and levels. My course treats the choice of variables as exogenous: you are handed a variable set, and different variable sets give different, mutually incompatible graphs over the same system. The reply to the exclusion argument that says physical causes are usually too fine-grained to be the causes of behaviour, while the mental property is at the right grain, looks to me like exactly this: a claim about which variable space to use rather than a claim about arrows within a fixed space. Does taking that seriously defuse the exclusion argument, restate it as a problem about model selection, or leave it untouched? I especially want to know whether "grain" can be represented as a relation between two structural causal models at all, or whether it is one of the things the formalism cannot say.

4. Faithfulness and the status of the no-overdetermination premise. Faithfulness says the graph encodes all and only the conditional independencies in the distribution. The exclusion argument seems to want something in that neighbourhood but stronger — a ban on redundant sufficient causes that is not read off the data and does not follow from the Markov condition. Is there any constraint in the framework that plays that role, or is the premise an outside import? And if it is an outside import, does that make the argument weaker than it looks, or does it show that the framework is deliberately agnostic about something the metaphysicians care about?

Finally, the verdict I actually want: is the residue best read as evidence that the exclusion argument was trading on a notion of causation — production, efficacy, "doing the work" — that interventionist causal models deliberately replaced with something thinner, or as evidence that the interventionist notion is too thin to underwrite any metaphysical claim about what causes what? If there is a third reading, give it. And please flag explicitly anywhere you have to stipulate something my methods course would not sanction, since I need to be able to tell which parts of your reconstruction I could defend in front of the causal inference instructor and which parts only in front of the philosopher.
