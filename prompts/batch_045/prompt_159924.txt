I've been reading about the epistemology of markets on my own, and I keep running into the same move: asset prices are like betting odds, the market is like a prediction market, each trader puts money behind a view, the price that clears is the market's implied probability, and because money talks the aggregate ends up knowing more than any participant. I notice I've been leaning on this myself to wave off the no-trade results. My reasoning was that if everyone started from the same priors and saw the same order flow there would be nothing to trade, but in real life people arrive with different private information, so trade happens and prices get accurate. Someone pointed out that the standard no-trade argument is aimed precisely at that case: it says informationally motivated trade shouldn't occur at all, even with genuinely different private information, once it's common knowledge that everyone is rational and nobody is trading for liquidity or to hedge an existing exposure. So I'd like an audit of the analogy before I keep using it. Give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the event, the settlement, the odds, the bookmaker, the bettor's edge, the house take. Then the places it breaks. Three I have in mind: in a sportsbook the result is settled by an outside referee and isn't moved by the betting, whereas in a market the thing priced can be changed by the price itself; a bettor can have no stake in the outcome beyond the bet, while a holder usually has exposure she is hedging; and a book posts odds, while a market price is just a momentary clearing outcome. Tell me which of these actually matter for the use I'm making and which are decorative, whether the analogy is quietly importing a common-prior or risk-neutrality assumption I haven't earned, and whether my conclusion survives: that heavy volume plus dispersed information is compatible with, and even evidence for, prices tracking the truth. If the betting analogy can't carry that, give me a better one.
