I've been arguing with people from my local rationalist meetup about process-based versus outcome-based supervision of AI systems, and one of them has an argument I can't break but also can't accept, because on its face it dissolves a distinction that an awful lot of alignment discussion seems to run on. Roughly reconstructed, it goes:

(1) The only reason to supervise a system at all — as opposed to letting it do whatever it does — is that supervision makes the system's effects better than they would otherwise be. Call a constraint *derivative* if its entire justification is downstream consequences of that kind.
(2) Every process constraint you might impose — show your work, don't modify the test file, stay inside the sandbox, don't misrepresent your confidence, don't read the grader — is derivative. Nobody wants legible chains of thought for their own sake.
(3) If a constraint C is derivative, then conditional on being able to specify and measure the consequences that justify C, a constraint that targets those consequences directly is weakly better than C: it rules out everything C rules out that actually mattered, and it doesn't rule out alternative routes to the same good consequences that C forbids for no reason.
(4) Where we can't specify or measure those consequences, the right response is to buy better measurement, not to freeze a guess about which processes produce good outcomes into a standing rule — a frozen guess is at best as good as the outcome measure it approximates, and it never gets better with more data.
(5) So where outcomes are measurable, process-based supervision is weakly dominated; where they aren't, it's a stopgap to be discarded as soon as measurement improves. Either way there is no in-principle case for supervising process rather than outcomes.
(6) Nothing in (1)–(4) mentions AI systems. Run it on us instead: blinding, preregistration, refusing to peek at the answer before you record your prediction, requiring a second reader, keeping the people who score the results separate from the people who run them — all derivative process constraints on a supervisor. So the argument says these are also either dominated or mere stopgaps, and that a researcher who could in principle measure "was this result correct?" directly has no reason left to blind anything.
(7) And that is the part I won't accept. Blinding is not a stopgap waiting around for a better way to measure correctness; it looks like the thing that makes the measurement of correctness mean anything in the first place.

My discomfort is concentrated somewhere around (3). "Weakly better" seems to compare the two constraints against a fixed background — the same outcome measure, the same mapping between what the system does and what we later observe — and I suspect the background doesn't stay fixed: targeting an outcome directly pulls on the very channel you were going to read the outcome off, whereas some process constraints (the blinding-and-sealing kind, which I've started calling *channel-keeping* constraints) leave the channel alone. My friend's reply is that this is just consequentialism again, i.e. it's already counted by (1), and channel-keeping is process supervision with better branding. I can't tell whether I've located a real distinction or just relabeled my intuition.

To make it concrete, I care about two cases rather than the general question. Case A: a coding agent with a fast, cheap, nearly perfect test suite, where process constraints look like pure friction. Case B: a system that drafts internal strategy memos whose real effects won't be observable for three years, and where the plan is that a stronger future verifier will eventually audit the record — which is exactly the setting where "keep the channel clean until someone who can actually tell is looking" seems to matter most.

So: which step is load-bearing, why does it look innocent, and what does it cost to give up? I'd rather keep (1) and the verdict in (7) than keep (5), but I want to know what I'm paying for that, and whether the price is paid in (3), in (4), or somewhere I haven't looked. Please don't stop at "the outcome measure gets Goodharted" — I know that much; what I want is whether that fact is a *defense of process supervision within* (1) or a reason to *reject* (1), because those give very different pictures of what the process/outcome distinction is even for. If there's a third category sitting next to process and outcome constraints — channel-keeping, or something else — say what it is and how to tell when you're in it. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence: which step you'd reject and what rejecting it commits you to. Then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating the question back to me.
