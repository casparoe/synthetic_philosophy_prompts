I'm a retired field ecologist — thirty-odd years of small-mammal population dynamics in farmed landscapes, mostly voles and wood mice, more livetrapping than I care to remember — and I've lately got into philosophy the way a lot of retired scientists do: badly, slowly, and with a lot of arguing with my daughter. She does animal advocacy and reads the effective altruism stuff; I read it too and then go back to my old traplines in my head. We have one argument we can't get past, and I'd like you to take a side with numbers attached.

The proposition: for an ordinary consumer in a rich country, replacing the beef in their diet with chicken reduces the total amount of morally relevant suffering they cause, once the wild animals affected by the land used to grow the animals' feed are counted on the same ledger as the farmed animals themselves.

Please don't go looking anything up — I'd much rather you mark a figure as a guess than invent a citation, since neither of us can check it. So treat the following as stipulations I made up as placeholders, not data: a kilogram of chicken protein takes roughly a third as much cropland as a kilogram of beef protein; cereal cropland carries about three times the small-mammal biomass per hectare that pasture does; a wild mouse lives about a year and most of those years end in something I would not want; and consumers are only partly efficacious, so that a person's dietary change shifts production by maybe half of what the naive per-kilogram arithmetic implies, because prices move and someone else eats a bit more.

Here's the bit I keep coming back to and that my daughter waves away: the hectares that actually enter or leave cultivation at the margin are not average hectares. They're the marginal ones — the poor, stony, badly-drained fields that are first abandoned and last ploughed — and in my experience their carrying capacity for the animals we'd be counting is well below the cropland average. If that's right, then multiplying hectares by an average-per-hectare animal count systematically overstates the wild-animal cost of the extra feed, and the beef-to-chicken switch looks better than the headline calculation suggests.

Give me your credence in the proposition, decomposed. I want the sub-questions you think it turns on — whether a mouse (or a beetle, or a soil mite) has valenced experience at all and where you'd put the threshold; whether a wild mouse-year is net negative rather than net positive; whether the moral weight sits in the number of deaths or in the number of animal-years of whatever quality, given that a lot of wild suffering may be concentrated into short terminal events; what the appropriate counterfactual for spared land is, given that it may just support a different population rather than none; the marginal-hectare point above; whether sufferings across species are commensurable enough for the sum to be well-defined at all; and whether an individual consumer is causally connected to any of this in the relevant way. Number each, one line of justification, then combine them, then tell me which single term your final number is most sensitive to and what kind of study — describe the design, don't name a paper — would pin it down.

For reference I'm at about 0.55 and she's at about 0.9. Tell me which of those sub-questions the two of us most likely disagree about, because I suspect we aren't actually arguing about the same thing.

One last thing: separate clearly, as you go, what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present a contested position as the consensus just because it's the one you find most attractive. Flag the places where you're extrapolating from thin grounds or where you think the question as I've put it is ill-posed — and if you think the total just doesn't exist as a quantity, say so, give a number for the nearest well-posed cousin of my question, and say what I'd have to assume to make the original one defensible.
