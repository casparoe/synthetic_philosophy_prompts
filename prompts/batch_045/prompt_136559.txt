I write short fiction on weekends; my day job is backend engineering, and I have no philosophy training, so please don't assume I know the technical vocabulary for what I'm after.

The idea: a port city runs an office called the Register. Any citizen may submit one precisely specified statement about a future event — "the harbor fleet clears the bar at dawn on the fourteenth" — and the next morning the Register returns a single stamp: TRUE, FALSE, or OPEN. In two centuries it has never been wrong, and no stamp has ever been revised. Nobody knows how it works, and the city is divided over whether the Register discerns the future in advance or merely records the matter at the instant it stops being open.

I want the story to stay with one person — a harbor pilot — who receives a stamp concerning something she is herself in a position to decide, and who then has to act. The part I can't get right is her temptation to treat the stamp as a *reason*: to submit a statement about her own future action and let the answer tell her what to do. I want the reader to feel both that this is obviously incoherent and that it is nearly impossible to shake, and I want the story to end without settling whether the stamp was a discovery or a cause.

Two constraints. First, no version of the "it came true precisely because she tried to avert it" punchline — that twist closes the thing I want left open. Second, no narrator and no character who states the puzzle in general terms; the tension should land entirely in what she does and in what the people around her say afterward. Around a thousand words.
