For most of my working life I looked after a set of long-term grassland plots at a field station — the same few hundred square metres, mown and limed and scored on the same schedule for something over thirty years, with a low grassed bank running between plots 7 and 8 whose purpose appeared in none of the notebooks I inherited and which nobody still alive on the staff could explain to me. Every few years a new postdoc would arrive, look at the plan, and propose re-randomising the whole layout properly on a computer; and I would hear myself saying that we should leave that bank alone because somebody put it there for a reason, and then feel slightly fraudulent, because I could not produce the reason and I had spent a career teaching students not to accept that style of adaptive story without a mechanism and a test. Since retiring I have been going to a philosophy reading group at the adult education centre in town, and we have just finished a short term on conservatism and reform — Burke, and the familiar Chesterton maxim about not being allowed to remove a fence until you can say why it was put up. I have been asked to close the final session with a short poem, and I would much rather do that than stand up and summarise.

What I would like is a sestina. I looked the form up: six stanzas of six lines, unrhymed, in which the same six end-words return in the rotating order — the last end-word of one stanza opening the next, then the first, then the second-to-last, then the second, and so on — closed by a three-line envoi in which all six come back. Let the end-words be hedge, use, dead, drift, gate and why, and let the rotation do the work: an arrangement that keeps coming back in an order you didn't expect, never quite the arrangement you meant to be inspecting, so that by the envoi the reader has the sense that they have walked the same boundary six times without ever seeing it whole.

I don't want a poem that just says tradition is wise, and I don't want the young postdoc turned into a straw man either — in my experience they were usually right about the statistics while the old technician was usually right about the water. The distinctions I keep coming back to in my notes are these, and I would like them to be legible in the poem without being listed: a purpose still being served; an original purpose that has lapsed while the arrangement has quietly been taken over by a second purpose nobody chose, the way a feature built for one thing gets co-opted for another; an arrangement that never had a purpose at all but is a leftover of how something else was built — I think architects have a term for the tapering triangles left above the arches when you set a dome on them; an arrangement that had a purpose plainly enough, but one that served whoever paid for it against the people now living inside it; and, the one I find hardest to shake, the fact that the people who took such things down and came to grief are not the people who wrote the notebooks, so that the surviving sample is skewed toward the arrangements nobody ever dared test. Someone in the group who actually knows Burke should not wince at the philosophy, and someone who has never heard of either writer should still come away with a poem. Keep the hedge an actual hedge, keep the water and the plots in it, aim for something that can be read aloud in a bit over two minutes, and don't let the last stanza announce the moral: let the envoi put the six words back in whatever order you think honest, and stop there.
