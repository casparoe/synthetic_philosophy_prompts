Hey — I'm putting together the handout for a small reading group I run on population ethics (a few grad students, a couple of people from the bioethics side, one guy who just shows up to argue) and I'd rather have a script than another page of bullet points. Could you write me a dialogue between two philosophers who *both* accept the core of the non-identity problem — roughly, that if a choice affects which person comes to exist, and the person who ends up existing has a life worth living, it's hard to say that person was harmed or wronged — but who disagree sharply about how far that goes?

The case I want them to fight over is one I made up, so there's nothing to look up: at a fertility clinic there are two viable embryos, A and B, and exactly one will be transferred; the other will be discarded. Embryo A carries a mutation that will give the resulting child a mild but lifelong impairment — a life clearly worth living, but maybe 15 percent less good than it would otherwise be. Embryo B doesn't carry it. The technician loading the catheter can choose either embryo, or, if she prefers, hand the decision to a wheel: a fair coin-flip device, or a weighted one that's 80 percent likely to select B. Call the first character a "candidate comparativist": she thinks the non-identity problem only gets its bite when the *number* of people is at stake, and that in fixed-number cases we have genuine person-affecting reasons to select the candidate who would have the better life — that B is, in some sense, someone who can be benefited by being picked. Call the second a "staunch non-identitarian": he thinks identity is doing all the work and the number of people is a red herring — whoever is born has a life worth living, nobody is worse off than they'd otherwise have been, and the pull toward picking B either dissolves on reflection or has to be recast impersonally (total or average welfare) or as a claim about the technician's attitudes and procreative character rather than about anyone's claims.

I want real back-and-forth, not two monologues, and neither of them should be a strawman — they've clearly heard each other's views before. Some things I'd like to see them chew on: the "identity laundering" worry, i.e. if the reason to pick B is that B would fare better, why should it matter whether we interpose a randomizing device, given that the same person gets born either way and the probabilities of who gets born are identical? Her reply to that (maybe distinguishing reasons to *select* from reasons that an *outcome* obtain, or arguing the laundering move generalizes to ordinary cases in a silly way) and his comeback. Then push on whether the candidate framework survives once the number isn't fixed — say the technician could instead transfer both, or split one embryo to make a third — and whether anything she says about fixed-number cases quietly commits her to creating extra people, or anything he says commits him to indifference in cases where we'd normally think there's an obvious duty. Around 900–1200 words, mostly dialogue, and I'd like it to end with each of them having landed at least one hit and at least one of them conceding something specific rather than just agreeing to disagree. No need to name-drop real papers or construct a bibliography — keep it self-contained and just argue the thing out.
