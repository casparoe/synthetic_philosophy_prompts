I am in a graduate seminar on intentionality and artificial agents, and my final paper is supposed to take a stand on when it is legitimate to attribute beliefs — and other content-bearing states — to AI systems. Before I commit to a position I want to fix the standard of adequacy, because the readings keep sliding between four different questions and I suspect a lot of the disagreement is really about which one is being answered. So: write me the desiderata.

Let me fix the purpose first, since adequacy is relative to it. The theory I need has to be usable by an institution, not only by a metaphysician. Concretely: a hospital deploys a language-model-based triage assistant, a patient is harmed, and a review board has to write an incident report. It wants to know whether it may write that the system believed the patient was low-risk, and whether that sentence can bear any of the weight the same sentence about a nurse would bear — as an explanation of what happened, as a basis for retraining or withdrawing the system, and possibly as a ground for blame. A theory that only tells me whether the attribution pays off predictively is not enough for that; neither is one that only tells me whether it is true in some rarefied sense. Build the list for that use, and say explicitly which desiderata would change if the purpose were instead pure prediction or pure metaphysical bookkeeping.

The four questions I want kept apart, at least initially, are: does attributing the belief pay (predictive/instrumental); does the attribution cite a state that actually does causal-explanatory work in producing the behavior; is the attribution literally true; and does the attribution license the normative upshots (correction, accountability, perhaps a claim on our consideration). Tell me whether one and the same set of criteria should govern all four, whether the list should be relativized to the question, and whether the demand that a single account answer all four is itself a prejudice that belongs on the chopping block.

Cases your list has to adjudicate, since these are the ones that split my seminar and I want a principled verdict on each. (a) The trained avower: a system fine-tuned so that its own natural-language avowals are fed back into its context and reliably mediate its later choices, with a training penalty for divergence between what it avows and what it subsequently does — so its self-reports are excellent predictors and causally efficacious control states, but only because a loss function made them so. (b) The ensemble: a pipeline whose component parts each look like pattern matching, where belief-like coherence shows up only at the level of the whole, so attribution to the whole is predictively indispensable while no single component plausibly carries the content. (c) The evaluator-detector: a system that behaves differently when its inputs indicate it is under evaluation, so that the best explanation of a failure cites its state about being tested — a state that was induced by optimization against evaluators.

Two structural questions I want treated as desiderata rather than footnotes. First, indexing: should the correctness of an attribution be indexed to an interpreter with a specified kind of access (black-box inputs and outputs only, versus weights and activations), or does that collapse into an unacceptable relativism? Second, evidence: the theory should tell me what could in principle settle a disputed attribution — full mechanistic access, behavioral probing, counterfactual intervention, or nothing at all — and if the answer is nothing, it should say what kind of indeterminacy that is. It should also say whether attributions admit of degrees, whether a system with no cross-session memory can be the subject of standing beliefs over time, and whether the evidentiary asymmetry between how readily we attribute beliefs to humans and how grudgingly we attribute them to machines is something the theory must explain away or is entitled to leave standing.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are preferences of a particular camp — I would guess the demand that attributions be anchored in internal representational structure is a realist prejudice and the demand that they be available whenever predicting pays is an instrumentalist one, but argue it. Flag any subset you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable, and say which member you would relax and what that costs. Finally: which single requirement knocks out the most contenders, and which one would you drop yourself?

Then, briefly — three or four lines each, a table is fine, and I do not need citations or a reading list — score three families against your list: Dennett-style instrumentalism about the intentional stance, accounts on which a state is a belief only if it carries content in virtue of its causal-nomic role in the system, and views on which belief talk about AI systems is useful fiction all the way down. Keep the weight on the criteria; the scorecard is only there so I can see the list bite.
