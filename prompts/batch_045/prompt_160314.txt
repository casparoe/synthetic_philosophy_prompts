I am finishing a novel about a real woman who, in 1913, publicly denounced her brother in a letter to a newspaper, and who left diaries for every year of her adult life except the three immediately preceding that letter. I intend to write those three years.

My working rule, which I call the Doctrine of Borrowed Interiority, has two limbs. Where the archive is silent I may supply inner life, but only inner life that renders the one surviving public act intelligible; and I must supply it through a fictional confidant — a cousin who never existed — so that everything I attribute to her is staged as something she said aloud to someone rather than as the content of her mind. My friend, also a novelist, holds what she calls the Sealed Room rule: a gap in the record is not missing evidence but evidence of a self that withheld, and the dramatist owes the dead the staging of that withholding rather than its violation. She adds that the cousin buys me nothing, since a confession overheard by an imaginary cousin is still a confession I wrote, and a reader who believes it believes something about a woman who lived.

Write the dialogue between us at her kitchen table on the night she finished my draft. We are equally competent and equally stubborn, and neither of us may be a fool. Require of the exchange the following. Each of us must say what exactly is owed, to whom — the woman herself, her living descendants, the reader, the historical record — and why the novelist's debt is or is not a species of the biographer's. Both of us must answer the asymmetry objection: we flatter the dead as readily as we traduce them, so if unflattering invention is a theft, why is flattering invention not one too? Both of us must settle whether a borrowing can be repaid: I claim it can, by giving her a coherent motivation the archive denies her, and she must meet that claim rather than repeat that the loan was never requested, since I will reply that repaying an unrequested loan is only a second unauthorized gift. Each of us must concede one thing the other can actually use, and every speech must answer what the other has just said rather than a version of it neither of us holds. Close by sharpening the disagreement about the single scene I care most about — the night before she wrote the letter — into a decision about what I may put on the page, and name the one line I may not write.
