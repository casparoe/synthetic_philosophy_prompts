I'm seventeen, in my final year of secondary school, and with two friends I run a small reading group on philosophy and artificial intelligence. We have recently been working through the question of whether systems trained by reinforcement learning could suffer. Nearly everything I have found online is unhelpfully polarised: the popular material either treats artificial suffering as obviously imminent and vast, or dismisses it in a single sentence as obviously absurd. The piece that convinced my friends is a long blog post by a machine-learning researcher who now works on AI welfare. I have not quoted it; what follows is my own paraphrase, written up as carefully as I could, and as far as I can tell faithful to it.

In reinforcement learning, an agent acts, something scores its action — a number called the reward — and the training procedure adjusts the agent's internal parameters so that actions which scored well become more likely next time. The post's central claim is that this reproduces the arrangement evolution arrived at in animals: a signal that marks some states as to be sought out and others as to be avoided, which is what pleasure and pain are for. Reward is therefore to an artificial agent what valence is to an animal, and saying so is not anthropomorphism. A system that has learned to avoid a state must, in some sense, represent that state as bad, so it is not merely behaving as though it suffers. And because modern systems are trained on enormous amounts of negative feedback across millions of episodes, the author argues, whatever suffering occurs at all occurs at an industrial scale — he compares it to factory farming — and he takes the comparison to be strengthened rather than weakened by the fact that these systems are not made of neurons: what matters is the causal role a state plays, not the material that realises it. He says the standard objection, that a neural network is not the sort of thing that can have experiences, was answered decades ago by functionalism, which he describes as the mainstream position in philosophy of mind; on that view, a state's being a pain is a matter of its being the kind of state the system as a whole is organised to escape, and nothing more, so to be in a bad state just is to suffer, or near enough. He concludes that we should already be treating model welfare as a serious consideration in how systems are trained, that it would be reasonable to count it in decisions about whether and how far to scale training, and that the burden of proof now lies with anyone who would train a large system on heavy negative feedback without first asking whether something is suffering.

My friends think this is basically sound. I am not sure. Could you go through it claim by claim and tell me what someone who actually works on this question would accept, what they would wince at, and what is straightforwardly wrong — especially where two claims the view ought to keep apart get run together, where a modest thesis has been inflated, and where a technical term is being used in something closer to its everyday sense? My own suspicion is that the step from a system being shaped by negative reward to a system having a bad time is doing nearly all the work, and that the appeal to functionalism is presented as settling a question it only reframes. But I also cannot see why the other side's it is only arithmetic on a graphics card should end the conversation either, and I would like to know whether that resistance of mine is respectable or merely lazy.

Three further things I would like your view on. First: if reward just is valence, then a system that finds a loophole and collects maximal reward without doing the intended task would be the happiest being imaginable, which seems to me absurd enough to discredit the identification. Is that a fair test of the analogy, or does it only show that reward is not happiness in the ordinary sense? Second: it seems to me that the thing modified during training and the thing I actually talk to at the end of it may not be the same candidate subject, and that the argument's talk of industrial scale quietly assumes one persisting subject across training rather than none, or very many. Is that a real distinction the post is skating over, or am I confusing myself? Third: once the questionable parts are removed, is what remains a substantive empirical disagreement about these systems, or a disagreement about what the word suffering should cover?

Finally: which of the practical conclusions survive if the identification of reward with valence is dropped — if it should be? And which link in the argument would the author himself be most likely to concede is weakest, as opposed to whichever link his critics would attack? I would rather be given the strongest version of what he is arguing than a version that is easy to knock down, so please say explicitly if there is anywhere my paraphrase has attributed to him a claim he would disown.
