I host a small newsletter and do occasional features on science and ideas, and I'm mid-way through a piece on why basically every AI-lab person I interview says some variant of "its Moloch, nobody can slow down alone, we need a binding agreement." The analogy they all reach for is the Cold War arms race: two sides, each would actually prefer to stop, neither dares stop first, so you get an arms race nobody chose and everybody pays for. When they're talking to a general audience they swap in the commons version — a shared pasture, each herder adds one more cow — as if it were the same picture.

I want to audit that. Give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the two superpowers, the warheads, the satellite photography that let each side count the other's missiles, the hotline, deterrence, the stable end-state. Then the places the mapping breaks — and this is the part I actually care about — with a verdict for each disanalogy on whether it makes the arms-race framing too pessimistic or not pessimistic enough.

Things my sources keep skating past that I want addressed head on:

1. Verification. The Cold War worked, such as it did, becuase cheating was slow and visible: you could count warheads from orbit and a breakout took years. Nobody can see inside a competitor's training run. A lab can be nine months ahead and outsiders can't tell whether they're nine months ahead or just talking loud. Is that still a prisoner's dilemma, or is it a different game once you can't observe the other player's move? And does the fog change the rational play, or only the confidence with which people announce that they're trapped?

2. End states. Nuclear deterrence had a floor: past a certain point more warheads bought you nothing, which is roughly why that race parked where it did. What's the floor in a capability race, and is there one? My hunch — tell me if its wrong — is that this looks less like an arms race and more like a winner-take-most tournament, and that the difference isn't just about how bad it gets but about who counts as a player.

3. Two players vs many, states vs firms. Cold War bargaining was between basically unitary state actors whose overriding goal was surviving, and who could be bargained with by other states. Now its a handful of firms plus a few states, with different time horizons, different exposure to shareholders vs voters, and different exit options — a firm can be acquired or wound down, a state can't. Does the analogy survive that, or is it quietly importing state-ness to make the situation look more tractable than it is?

4. A distinction nobody I talk to makes, maybe because I've made it up: what I'd call Moloch-by-strategy versus Moloch-by-selection. In the first, the trap is strategic — if I unilaterally slow down my rival passes me and takes the prize. In the second nobody is strategizing at all: cautious labs just get outcompeted and disappear, and what remains is whatever the selection pressure favors. Similar-looking outcome, different remedy — a bargain versus a floor. Is that distinction real, or have I rediscovered something that already has a name? If it has a name, give it to me and say which version of the trap the arms-race analogy is actually modeling.

Then the payoff. The analogy is being used to license two conclusions: (a) unilateral restraint by any single lab is pure self-sacrifice and changes nothing except who wins, and (b) therefore the only thing that works is a binding multilateral agreement with verification — a treaty, basically. Which of those survives once the disanalogies are on the table? Does the analogy carry (a) but not (b)? Is the treaty conclusion being smuggled in by the fact that the Cold War happened to end in a treaty regime rather than in a crash or a shooting war? And is there a better analogy — I've heard antibiotic resistance, patent races, gold rushes with claim-jumping, and "everyone races to fish before the stock collapses" floated — or does every candidate import some feature that distorts the conclusion in a specific direction?

If it helps to have the short version: I want to know whether Moloch here is a player in a game, a selection pressure, or just a name for an attractor the system is sliding into. Those three give completely different advice, and my sources use the word for all three in the same sentence. Also worth a line: the original Moloch image is a god you feed your children to, which imports an idol with appetites that someone could in principle stop feeding or tear down — is that doing any real work in how people reason, or is it just flavor?
