I maintain, with a team of six, the clinical rule base for a network of eleven hospitals: roughly four thousand rules written in a controlled natural language, which drive automated checks in the electronic record at the moment of ordering and also serve as the reference text nurses and pharmacists actually read on the ward. We meet weekly in change control; every substantive change needs a compliance officer's signature and a rotating clinical lead's, and my team gets about a thirty-minute slot per reported inconsistency, so whatever we do has to be runnable by a new hire with no training in logic and has to terminate in a status tag, an action, a named owner, and a review date.

The reason I'm writing is that I had a long argument last night with a friend who works in formal ontology. Her position was that every contradiction in our base is a bug — sloppy modelling, stale copies, hidden parameters, imprecise predicates — and that paraconsistent logic is a solution to a problem nobody has outside the semantic paradoxes and a few corners of law and mathematics. I pushed back, because some of our conflicts look structural to me: two authorities of equal standing with no hierarchy between them, two specialty bodies whose guidelines genuinely disagree, an emergency-override meta-rule that conflicts with the rule about how rules get suspended, and policies that encode rival values rather than rival factual beliefs. But I couldn't tell her what the paraconsistent apparatus actually buys us beyond what "flag it and escalate" already does, and I don't want to borrow a metaphysical thesis — that some contradictions are really true — to dignify what may just be unwillingness to clean up my own data. So: write me a triage procedure, as a flowchart in words, for one reported inconsistency between two rules, with the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a resolution, where it stops with something like "both in force, named owner, review date," and where it hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values. Ground it in what this literature genuinely supports: why explosion is the thing to be afraid of, the difference between a glut and a gap, the difference between an inconsistency in our representation and one anyone would have to call real, the claim that rationality can require holding both while you look for a resolution, and the gap between a logic that tolerates contradiction and a thesis about what is true. Say plainly at which of our cases the theory is doing real work and at which it is a redescription of ordinary engineering caution. Two specific things I need: a containment test, because our rules inherit from one another and a single glutty pair shouldn't silently invalidate every downstream check — I've been using an informal dependency-set idea plus a relevance test along the lines of "would this consequence still be drawn if the disputed rule were withdrawn," and I want you to adopt it, repair it, or replace it with something better; and a verdict on a proposal of mine, which is that every "both" tag should carry a thirty-day expiry so the base doesn't accrete gluts forever. I suspect that's just the assumption that all inconsistency is temporary, smuggled back in, but I'd like it argued rather than conceded. Please distinguish at least conflicts that dissolve once a hidden parameter, time index, or sense of a vague predicate is made explicit; conflicts between sources of equal authority with no tie-breaker; conflicts involving a rule's application to itself or to the override mechanism; and conflicts where the disagreement is not factual at all. Mark explicitly the points at which judgment cannot be eliminated, even in principle.

Apply the finished procedure to three live cases. First: two specialty societies of equal standing give incompatible red-cell transfusion thresholds for the same post-operative cardiac population — 7.5 g/dL versus 9.5 g/dL — and the evidence does not settle it; my team's instinct is to pick one and delete the other, and I want to know whether the procedure forbids that. Second: our emergency rule says the incident commander's order overrides every other rule in the base, while our governance rule says no rule may be suspended except by a two-thirds committee vote; these collide exactly when the collision matters. Third: our policy determines death by neurological criteria and stops organ-support, while our accommodation policy permits continued ventilation for a family who holds that the patient is alive as long as the heart beats, and I don't think any amount of procedure will settle which of those the institution is committed to. At least one of the three should come out somewhere my team will not want to go. Finally, give me a five-line glossary of the status tags for new hires, each with a note on what not to do with it, and a short closing section on what the procedure cannot do: I take it it cannot tell me which authority to trust, cannot see conflicts nobody reported, and cannot aggregate two hundred pairwise decisions into any global verdict on whether our institution's commitments are jointly satisfiable — but tell me what else is missing, and in particular where the procedure's willingness to hold both stops being a claim about our rule base and starts being a claim about the world.
