In a reading group on Anscombe's protest against Oxford giving Truman an honorary degree, one member keeps shutting down every discussion with the same line: *the deliberate killing of the innocent is never permissible*, and that is supposedly all there is to say about Hiroshima. I think it's doing about four jobs at once and I want the menu, not one of them explained.

Separate the precise claims that sentence could be making — about who counts as innocent (non-combatant? morally blameless? not currently attacking anyone? not liable for some reason other than fault?), about what deliberate covers (intended as an end, as a means, merely foreseen, accepted as a side effect, risked because the upside is huge), about whether it's a rule about acts or about what sort of person one turns into and what a community can afterwards avow, and about whether it's absolute or just a very heavy presumption. For each, say whether it's a platitude, a substantive thesis someone could deny, or something that quietly begs the question, and whether it actually gets him his conclusion about Truman or only looks like it does.

Two cases where I got lost: a night strike on a munitions plant whose workers are all asleep and so not attacking anyone, though the plant is what keeps the war running; and a crew that aims at the plant, gets blown off course by wind, and kills a schoolfull of children — same deaths, no intention of them. Plus a case I made up: Truman is credibly told the bombs will end the war, but the same report says a demonstration detonation over an empty bay would probably work too, and he picks the city because if the demonstration failed he'd be blamed for the extra months of fighting. Is that deliberating about the deaths, or only about his career, and does it matter which?

Last thing: someone said the whole argument rests on a forecast, and since the forecast was uncertain, Anscombe should've been uncertain too. I think that's a bad argument but can't say why — what would the good version of that worry look like, and does it cut against her or against Truman?
