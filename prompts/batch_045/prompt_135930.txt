I work on the payments platform at a mid-sized fintech, and I should say at the outset that I have no philosophy background — one ethics module at university, long forgotten — so I would rather the argument be carried by concrete examples than by terminology. Here is what prompted the request. Six weeks ago we shipped an AI-assisted rewrite of an overnight reconciliation job. The code was clean, the review comments were answered, and the test suite, which had been generated alongside the code, was green. Three weeks later the finance team noticed a slow drift: under a particular combination of a retried request and a currency conversion, some entries were being matched against the wrong side of the ledger, and the behaviour had been there since the day we shipped. When asked, nobody on the team could say why the new version had been written the way it had. Two of my colleagues, Nadia and Femi, have been arguing about what exactly went wrong ever since, and they have asked our engineering guild to let them present it as a written dialogue rather than as two competing proposals.

Nadia's view, as I understand it, is that nothing structurally new happened. Engineers have always relied on things they do not fully understand: compilers, query planners, managed cloud services, libraries maintained by strangers, answers copied from a forum, senior colleagues who simply tell you the answer. What matters on her account is whether the whole arrangement is reliable and whether people are well calibrated about when to trust it — not whether any individual could re-derive the result. She thinks the demand that you be able to reconstruct a derivation before relying on it mistakes a particular feeling for an epistemic good, and that because attention is finite it is better spent on specifications, invariants and end-to-end checks than on re-deriving code that already works. Femi's view is that AI assistance has a failure mode we have not had before, and he has coined two terms for parts of it. The first is comprehension debt: a deferred cost that, unlike ordinary technical debt, leaves no trace in the codebase and falls due precisely when you need to change something, because nobody left can say what the current behaviour was there for. The second he calls the interlock problem: the system is tuned to be plausible to someone like you, and you were trained on much the same material, so its mistakes cluster in exactly the places where your own checking is weakest — which means your review and its output are not the two independent checks that the usual reliability arithmetic assumes. He also insists on separating three abilities we normally run together: being able to re-derive a result, being able to localise and repair a fault in it, and being able to tell a correct output from a subtly wrong one. His claim is that heavy assistance erodes the third of these first and fastest while leaving the subjective sense of competence untouched, so that the person least able to notice the problem is also the most confident.

I would like you to write the dialogue they would present: set it at the guild meeting, with the reconciliation incident in front of them and the handbook rule under discussion. Please make them equally competent and equally stubborn — no strawmen, no easy concessions — and please have them actually answer each other rather than deliver parallel monologues. In particular I would like the argument to work through four points I cannot settle myself. First, whether behavioural checks really settle the matter, given that in our case the same system wrote the code, the tests and the pull request description, so that a green suite may amount to little more than the system agreeing with itself. Second, whether Nadia is right that comprehension can simply be distributed — across the team, across our monitoring, across whoever trained the model — in the way it already is across a query planner and the person who wrote it, or whether there is a point at which distributed comprehension is no longer available to anyone and the arrangement becomes one that nobody is in a position to vouch for. Third, whether the damage, if there is any, is to our reliability or instead to something more like our ownership of the work, so that one could in principle have perfectly safe systems built and maintained by people who understand less and less of them. Fourth, whether it matters that the cases where assistance is most valuable — unfamiliar domains, ambiguous requirements, novel design — are also the cases in which our external checks are weakest. Each of them should give ground on at least one point and neither should win outright; if you can, let the exchange arrive at the single crux on which they genuinely disagree rather than closing in agreement. Aim for roughly nine hundred to twelve hundred words, keep it dramatised rather than essayistic, and if a philosophical term of art is genuinely unavoidable, have one of the characters define it in passing in ordinary language.
