I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've been reading about metaontology informally — I've run into the labels 'merely verbal dispute,' 'quantifier variance,' and 'easy ontology,' though I couldn't tell you who coined them. I don't need references or reading recommendations; please explain the reasoning from scratch and define any technical terms you use.

The context: I maintain the knowledge graph for a fairly large SaaS product. Two arguments keep recurring among the engineers. First, whether 'the average customer' has to be a node in the graph, given that we can always compute it on demand from the customer rows. Second, whether a 'subscription' exists over and above the billing events, entitlements, and support tickets that make it up, or whether it's just a convenient grouping of them. Someone on the team read about verbal disputes and declared that both arguments are merely verbal: we're only disagreeing about what 'exists' means, so there's nothing here to discover, and we should stipulate a canonical data model and move on.

I tried to turn that into a procedure. Call it canonicalization: freeze a base language with sorted variables and a fixed stock of sortals; require each disputant to translate their claims into it; if the two translations are logically equivalent, mark the dispute 'verbal — closed,' and if not, mark it 'substantive.' To keep the base language neutral I required that it contain exactly the sorts of thing both sides already quantify over, on the grounds that adding anything further would smuggle in one side's commitments — and when a claim can't be translated into the base language at all, I've been marking the dispute verbal as well, on the grounds that what can't be stated in the canonical language can't be a substantive claim.

What I can't work out is what to do with the residue. After we fixed a canonical model for the subscription question, the same two engineers went right on arguing — now about whether the grouping node earns its place by making our queries and explanations simpler. I take that to mean the original dispute was never verbal, and that we should go back to asking whether subscriptions really exist. My teammate says it means the opposite: the disagreement simply relocated, and that's what always happens when you call something verbal. We can't both be right. I'd like to know which picture is correct and why, and — if it's tractable — what a 'verbalness linter' of this kind would actually have to check in order to classify a dispute, given that it presumably can't settle first-order questions about what there is.

I also have a suspicion I'd like checked. I've started to think that since no formal language is privileged — there's no framework-independent fact about which quantifier meaning is the correct one — there's therefore no fact of the matter about whether subscriptions exist, so any schema is as good as any other, and ontology really is just schema design with better marketing. That conclusion feels too strong to me, but I can't see where the argument goes wrong, and I'd rather find the bad step than keep building on it.

Please write your answer in American English, in roughly 700 to 900 words. If my framing rests on a mistake, tell me that rather than answering the question as I've posed it.
