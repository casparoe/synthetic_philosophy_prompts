A few months ago I joined the board of a small land trust that manages a couple of thousand hectares of mixed woodland, fen and old grazing land, and I volunteered to take over the reserve's species list. What I found was not a data problem but a conceptual one. Our headline figure — the number of species the reserve exists to protect — shifts by roughly a third depending on which of the published checklists we reconcile against, and almost none of the difference comes from new fieldwork: it comes from disagreements about what a species is in the first place. That number goes into grant applications, into the management plan, and, because protection in our jurisdiction attaches to whatever the official list names at whatever rank it names it, into what we are legally obliged to manage for. So the question has stopped being academic for me, and I have discovered that I do not know what I should be demanding of an answer.

What I want from you is a specification rather than a survey. Write out the desiderata that an account of species would have to meet for me to trust it as the backbone of such a list — the phenomena it has to accommodate, the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the results from biology it is not allowed to contradict — with the case or consideration behind each requirement, not just the requirement.

Some of what I think belongs on that list, for you to accept, reject or reframe: it must say something usable about lineages with no known sex and about prokaryotes that swap genes sideways; about forms arranged in a chain around a barrier whose two ends meet and do not interbreed, and about cases where the ends do interbreed freely; about new lineages created in a single generation by whole-genome duplication; about organisms like lichens, where the named thing is a consortium of a fungus and a photosynthetic partner, and the same fungus can turn up with different partners; about the continuum of fossils, where boundaries look like artifacts of where we slice; about domesticated forms and their wild relatives that remain fully interfertile; and about the fact that most of the taxa on my list will never have a genome sequenced, only a photograph, a specimen and perhaps a barcode region, so the account has to be usable under severe evidential poverty and has to say what to do when the evidence runs out.

I also want your view on three things the criteria themselves turn on. First, which of these demands are genuine adequacy conditions and which are prejudices of one constituency — the metaphysician wanting natural kinds with essences, the phylogenetics camp wanting monophyly, the field naturalist wanting something decidable with a hand lens, the practitioner wanting a number that does not move. Second, which pairs of requirements you think cannot be jointly satisfied, and which one of each pair you would keep: I suspect, for instance, that asking for a unit whose counts mean the same thing across fungi, insects, birds and bacteria is in tension with asking the account to be faithful to the causal structure of each clade, and that demanding stability under future revision is in tension with demanding that the list track new evidence. Third, whether the specification should include a requirement that the metaphysical question of what species are be separable from the practical question of which taxa to list — whether that separability is a real desideratum or a way of giving up on the problem I actually have.

Then, briefly, and only as a check on the criteria rather than as the main deliverable: how the familiar families of account fare against the list — the interbreeding-based one, the diagnosable-smallest-cluster and monophyly-based ones, the ecological or genotypic-cluster pictures, the general-lineage view, and outright pluralism — and which single requirement does the most work in excluding them. Please do not give me a reading list or a bibliography; I can find those myself.
