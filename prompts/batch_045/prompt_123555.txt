I write and maintain the software that a group of fertility clinics uses to rank embryos before transfer. I have no philosophy training, and I have run into something in the way my own field and the legal system talk about what looks like the same situation, and I cannot reconcile the two. I would like you to reconstruct the view each is committed to and tell me whether they are the same view.

What the clinic does. A patient's embryos are biopsied, genotyped, and scored; the software returns, per embryo, estimated lifetime risks for a list of conditions plus a composite score. The screen the counselor reads from shows, for the embryo under discussion, its own risk alongside the best risk in the cohort: it will say that this embryo's risk of developing a given condition by sixty is 14 percent against a cohort best of 9 percent, and the counselor will describe transferring a different embryo as lowering your child's risk by five points. Where every embryo in a cohort carries a serious dominant condition, the standing protocol is to transfer none of them: the cycle is abandoned or donor gametes are offered, and the consent paperwork describes this as avoiding the birth of a child who would suffer avoidably. Note what the software cannot represent: choosing a lower-risk embryo does not improve any embryo's score, and the cohort being ranked exists at all only because the cycle was run. A second item in the same guidance makes it stranger: when a patient asks whether to delay a cycle by a month for a non-medical reason, the recorded answer is that the delay disadvantages no child, since a different egg will be released and a numerically different child would result.

What the courts do, as best I can tell from reading around. When a clinic or laboratory misses a genetic problem and a child is born with a disability, the parents can sue for the extra costs of raising that child. In most of the United States the child cannot sue on her own behalf at all, on the reasoning that she has not been harmed, because the alternative was never being born. A few states let the child sue, but at least one restricts recovery to objectively provable expenses rather than anything for what it is like to live with the condition; a 1980 California decision that opened the door there said roughly that the plaintiff both exists and suffers, and that we need not be troubled by the fact that without the negligence she might never have existed at all. Other jurisdictions go further in the opposite direction: the German constitutional court held such claims unconstitutional on the ground that they imply one life is less worth living than another, while German courts go on awarding parents the cost of the child's upkeep.

The thing I want unpacked. In the clinic we talk as though there is a fact of the matter about which possible child would be better off, as though selecting among candidates is a gain for someone, and as though a cohort with no good option is a reason to start over. In court the same kind of choice is treated as one that benefits and harms nobody, and even where money changes hands the measure of damages is carefully arranged so that no one has to price the difference between a disabled life and no life at all. That looks like two incompatible accounts of what it takes to make someone worse off, or of who the comparison is between, operating on opposite sides of the same hospital.

So: what exactly does each practice presuppose? Which parts of the clinic's language and protocol are strictly required by the view it is committed to, and which are decoration that a different theory would generate anyway? Is the pair genuinely inconsistent, or can a single coherent view explain both why we rank embryos and why the resulting child has no complaint? Tell me what would concretely have to change in each setting if the presupposed view were swapped for its rival, in both directions: if the clinic took seriously the claim that nobody is benefited by being the one selected, and if tort law took seriously the claim that bringing the worse-off possible person into existence is a harm to her. And please be explicit about where the practice is underdetermined. If several theories license exactly the policies I have described, I would rather be told that than handed one tidy reconstruction. If this tangle has a standard name, give me the name and define it rather than assuming I have heard of it. Argue from the cases as I have given them; I do not want references or reading suggestions, and please flag which of my questions have no settled answer.
