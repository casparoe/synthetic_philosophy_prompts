Here is the background as I've pieced it together. Nozick's tracking account says, roughly, that a subject knows that p when p is true, she believes p, she would not believe p if p were false (sensitivity), and she would believe p if p were true (adherence), with these counterfactuals relativized to the method by which the belief is held. It is notorious that the account refuses closure: you know you have hands; having hands entails that you are not a brain in a vat; competently inferring the latter still doesn't get you knowledge of it, because in the nearest worlds where you are envatted your method delivers the belief that you have hands just the same. Two friends of mine — Colm, a retired logician who defends the anti-closure verdict, and Renata, a philosopher of science who thinks closure is a datum any theory must respect — argued it out in my kitchen and left me with no idea who had the better of it:

1. Renata: Closure — if you know p, you know p entails q, and you competently infer q, then you know q — is not an optional extra bolted onto a theory of knowledge; it is what makes knowledge the sort of thing you can extend by reasoning, and it is why deduction is a way of finding things out at all. Tracking contradicts it. But when a theory contradicts something that obvious, the obvious thing is not what should give way; the theory should. The envatment result is a reductio of tracking, not a discovery about knowledge.

2. Colm: Two things. First, the anti-closure verdict isn't bolted on; it falls out of a mechanism that earns its keep elsewhere. Because the counterfactuals are relativized to a method, tracking gets the intuitive verdict in ordinary cases: in fake-barn country you don't know the thing is a barn, because in the nearest worlds where it isn't one you'd believe it was all the same; and a visitor at the zoo who sees a zebra and has never considered the possibility of a cleverly painted mule knows it's a zebra but doesn't know it isn't a painted mule. That is closure failing in a completely unremarkable setting, so closure failure as such can't be absurd. Second, the envatment case has the same shape as the zoo case: the entailment holds, the subject can draw it, and the belief in the conclusion is insensitive. If the pattern is acceptable in the zoo, you don't get to call it absurd in the vat. Indeed this is the whole point of the theory: it explains why skeptical arguments feel valid step by step while their conclusions remain unbelievable.

3. Renata: I grant the mechanism is doing work, but I don't think it delivers your verdict, and I think the theory has a worse problem. On the first point: take the method by which the envatment-conclusion would be reached, and notice that everything flips with how finely you describe it. Describe it as inferring q from the deliverances of perception, and q comes out sensitive — in the nearest not-q worlds perception doesn't deliver p, so the inference never runs. Describe it as inferring q from believing p, and q comes out insensitive, because in the nearest not-q worlds p is false but the subject still believes p, her experience being unchanged, and so still infers q. Nothing in the theory settles which description is correct, so the account's headline anti-closure verdict rests on an unargued choice about the grain of methods. On the second point: for any necessarily true proposition there are no not-p worlds, so sensitivity is satisfied for free, and tracking will count a belief in a necessary truth as knowledge however it was arrived at. Consider my invented case of a retired actuary who comes to believe a true and necessarily true claim in number theory because a dead relative recited it to her in a dream. Tracking apparently credits her with knowledge. A theory that cannot tell that case apart from a proof has troubles that dwarf the question of closure.

4. Colm: On grain, I'll concede it's a genuine difficulty, but it isn't specific to tracking — reliabilism faces the same generality problem about how to individuate the relevant process, so it can't be the objection that sinks this theory in particular. And there is a natural answer available: the method has to include the subject's actual premise-beliefs and inferential dispositions, i.e. you hold fixed everything she would still be doing in the counterfactual situation. That reading is what makes the envatment case and the zoo case come out the same way, which is the result we both want, whereas your coarser reading would also make the painted-mule proposition known and would turn the fake-barn case into a case of knowledge. On necessary truths, I concede the cost too, but I think it's separable: treat tracking as an account of knowledge of contingent matters of fact, and add a supplementary condition for necessary truths — say, that the belief be held through a method that is reliable across the range of propositions it gets applied to, so that the dream doesn't count and the proof does. Losing the mathematics to a special case is not a reductio; it preserves the verdicts in the territory the theory was built for.

5. Renata: The tu quoque about grain actually cuts my way. A reliabilist has a principled way to fix the grain — pick the description under which the process has a well-defined success rate, since that's what the theory's central notion quantifies over — whereas a counterfactual can flip from true to false under a redescription that changes nothing statistically, and tracking theory has no comparable constraint to offer. So grain is worse for tracking than for its rivals, not equally bad. And the restriction you propose is worse than you admit: mathematical and logical knowledge is the paradigm case, not a corner, and once you bolt on a reliabilist supplement for the necessary half of the territory you have a two-track theory in which the anti-closure verdict is delivered by the contingent half alone — which means tracking was never explaining why the skeptical argument feels compelling; it was relabelling it. Finally, on your ordinary cases: in the zoo case my willingness to say the visitor doesn't know it isn't a painted mule is explained by her not having ruled out a live alternative, not by closure failing. The entailment from hands to not-envatted is a priori and its auxiliary is certain. Those are structurally different situations, and your theory's inability to mark the difference is exactly the defect.

6. Colm: I think your last point is the crux, and I also think it asks for something no single-condition theory can deliver. Ruling out a live alternative, being a priori, having a certain auxiliary: these are all ways of saying that the subject's epistemic position is stronger in one case than the other, and tracking registers position only through counterfactuals. If you want closure to hold exactly when the subject's position is good enough, you need a threshold notion — how strong the position must be, what counts as relevant, what is at stake — and then whether closure holds is settled by something other than the conditions we started from, and our disagreement is no longer about tracking at all.
