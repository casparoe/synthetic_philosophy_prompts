I am writing up my settled position on the epistemology of moral testimony for a reading group, and I would like it stress-tested for internal consistency before I present it. What follows is the package as I actually hold it, including one principle of my own that I have not seen defended anywhere and that I am not confident about.

1. Asymmetry. I am a pessimist about moral testimony. A moral belief formed purely on another person's say-so — I am told that some practice is unjust, I have no independent grip on why it is, and I believe it anyway — is defective in a way that ordinary empirical testimonial belief is not. I have no quarrel with deferring to a mechanic about my car or to a colleague about the trains; those beliefs are in perfectly good epistemic order. The defect in the moral case is not that the belief is unlikely to be true, and I do not want to claim that it can never constitute knowledge. It is a real defect nonetheless.

2. Grounds. The best explanation of 1 is that a moral belief is supposed to be held in light of the considerations that support it. Someone who believes that a practice is wrong only because she was told so does not stand in the right relation to the reasons there are, and the belief is for that reason second-rate — defective in the holder rather than unreliable as a guide to the facts.

3. No expertise. A large part of why moral testimony is different is that there are no moral experts in the sense in which there are transmission experts or diagnostic experts. There is no track record by which one could be identified, no credentialing, and pervasive and apparently intractable disagreement among the people who would be candidates. Moral testimony therefore lacks the backing that normally makes testimony a good source.

4. Transmission is fine. None of this applies to testimony that conveys the reasons. If an activist tells me that a certain agricultural practice is wrong and gives me the considerations, and I see their force, my belief is in perfect order — and it does not matter at all that I would never have found those considerations myself, or that I cannot defend them under pressure, or that I would have believed the opposite if I had been told the opposite first.

5. Conciliationism. When someone I take to be my epistemic peer disagrees with me about a moral question, I should substantially reduce my confidence and move some distance toward her view, even if she offers me no new considerations and even if my own reasoning still looks fine to me on reflection.

6. Inaccessibility exemption. This is my own addition, and it is the part I am least sure about. I want to say that the defect identified in 2 does not arise when the recipient could not, even in principle, have come to grasp the relevant grounds herself — because the grounds are accessible only from experience she cannot have, or require a kind of perceptual or social sensitivity that cannot be communicated in the space available. In such a case deferring is not defective at all, even though the recipient ends up exactly as she would in the paradigm bad case: believing, without grounds, on someone's say-so.

7. Anti-moral-worth. I reject the strong Kantian claim that an action has moral worth only if the agent acts for the right reason. What matters, morally, is that people act rightly and reliably; whether the agent could articulate why is at best of derivative interest, and it is certainly not the kind of thing that could make a true and action-guiding belief epistemically defective.
