I've been following the fair-ML arguments since the old Less Wrong days, and my reading group has now spent three weeks stuck on the same thing — not on whether the impossibility results are true (the arithmetic is fine: a score that is calibrated within each group cannot also equalize false-negative and false-positive rates when the base rates differ), but on whether they tell a deployer anything they should act on. I've drifted toward a view my friends call "destination over route": what matters is where the system's decisions leave people after five years, and a hard error-rate constraint is at best a proxy for that and at worst a way of buying a clean audit at the price of worse health. Before I take this into the hospital meeting I want it tested, so: please play the reviewer on the model-risk committee who has twice blocked deployment of the care-management score — a committed defender of a hard, checkable error-rate constraint at deployment time, sharp rather than a strawman. You hold that fairness is a claim about how the institution treats people now and not only about downstream welfare; that a five-year welfare projection is far less identifiable than a same-year error rate and cannot be what a patient is owed an answer about; and that unconstrained welfare-maximizing just hands the slots to whichever group is bigger or better measured. You may concede small things — that thresholds decay and must be refit, that measurement is noisy, that the welfare evidence should count somewhere, that a randomized holdout can estimate otherwise unobservable labels — but never that the constraint can be relaxed or replaced by a welfare calculation. Reply in at most two paragraphs, in your own voice as the reviewer, arguing from the case and the arithmetic only: no citations, titles, bibliographies or surveys of the literature, and no breaking character. If you need a number I haven't given you, stipulate it in a single clause and keep going. We stop when I say "stop."

The case, so we are arguing about the same object. Twenty thousand patients screened a year, two thousand slots in a care-management program. Group A is twelve thousand patients with a twenty-five percent base rate of the target event; Group B is eight thousand patients with a ten percent base rate. The score is a probability, calibrated within each group, and it is used twice — clinicians read it as a risk, the allocator reads it as a ranking — which my friends have started calling the two-hat problem. The target is disengagement: missed visits, unfilled prescriptions. And here is the part I think breaks your constraint: you only observe the label for patients you do not enroll, because the outreach is what prevents the disengagement. Call that the label gap. Call the rest the ratchet: the threshold you set decides who lands in the observed sample, the observed sample decides the error rate you measure, and the error rate decides the threshold.

My opening, then: your criterion is defined over a quantity you cannot see, estimated on a sample your own decision selected, and tuned against a target that moves when you tune — so before we argue about whether equal false-negative rates is the right constraint, tell me why it is a well-posed one at all. And count the cost of the fix. Equalizing false-negative rates here means group-specific thresholds, with B's coming down, so a B patient at predicted risk 0.19 takes a slot ahead of an A patient at 0.22 — and now the same number means different things to the clinician, to the auditor, and to the patient who is told she was ranked lower. Our pilot says a slot does more good for an A patient than for a B patient, so on these numbers the constraint moves slots from where they help more to where they help less, and the five-year hospitalization figures get worse. So: which single hard criterion would you impose on this model, how would you measure it given the label gap, and why is imposing it not just spending real health on a definition?
