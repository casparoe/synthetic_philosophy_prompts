I'm a PhD student in philosophy — my dissertation is on the epistemology of memory, and I've recently been reading into the debate about whether qualia are a coherent posit. A well-reviewed trade book on consciousness that I read over the summer summarises Dennett's argument in "Quining Qualia" more or less as follows. I no longer have the book to hand, so I'm reconstructing the passage from my notes, but I've tried to preserve its structure and its emphases:

"Almost everyone in the mind–body debate, dualists and materialists alike, has accepted that experiences have qualia: properties that are ineffable, intrinsic, private, and directly present to the consciousness of the person whose experiences they are, and that determine what it is like to have them. Dennett's target is this package, and his strategy is to show that it is not merely false but empty — to 'quine' it, in the sense he borrows from Quine, that is, to deny a place in one's ontology to something everyone else takes to be obvious. His central case is that of Chase and Sanborn, the two chief coffee tasters of a large coffee company, whose gustatory wiring is surgically exchanged while they sleep. The exchange leaves every capacity to taste, discriminate, rank, and describe coffee exactly as it was, and leaves both men's preferences and verdicts quite unchanged; nothing in their behaviour or in how the coffee seems to them alters by so much as a shade. So there is no test, even in principle, that could reveal that anything had happened to them, and therefore no fact of the matter as to whether their experiences have been inverted. Since a genuine property must make some difference somewhere, the moral generalises: there are no qualia. What exists is the whole network of discriminations, preferences, and verbal dispositions; the conviction that behind all of it lies something inner and ineffable is simply a mistake."

Several things here make me uneasy, but I can't locate the problem precisely, and I'd rather have a claim-by-claim audit than a general verdict.

First, the inference. As the book renders it, the argument runs: no possible test could reveal the difference, so there is no difference, so there are no such properties. That reads like a blunt verificationism of a kind most philosophers would reject on sight, and I find it hard to believe that a paper argued over for decades rests on it. My inchoate sense is that Dennett's claim is meant to be about the emptiness or incoherence of a theoretical posit rather than about the limits of our evidence — but I can't say what that distinction amounts to here, or what the positive case for emptiness would have to be.

Second, the set-up. The book has the swap leave every verdict, preference, and capacity untouched, so that nothing observable changes. I have a nagging memory that in Dennett's own telling the two men's verdicts and preferences trade places — each ends up responding the way the other used to — and that the puzzle is supposed to be that a change shows up in their reports while there is no saying where, or in what, it lies. If that is right, the book has quietly converted a case in which the difference is visible but unlocatable into one in which it is invisible, and I cannot tell whether that helps Dennett or hurts him.

Third, the conclusion. The closing claim — that what remains is a network of discriminations, preferences, and verbal dispositions, and that the conviction that something inner lies behind them is an error — sounds to me like eliminativism or behaviourism about conscious experience as such, which I take Dennett explicitly not to be. But I don't know how much of that is the journalist's flourish and how much is in the text.

The best reconstruction I can manage on my own is a dilemma: either the supposed intrinsic property has some relational or dispositional consequence, in which case it is not the purely intrinsic property it was advertised as, or it has none, in which case there is nothing to the posit. I don't trust it, because it seems too cheap to have generated the literature it did.

My reason for caring is a variant I've been worrying at, which comes out of my own work. Suppose only Chase is rewired, and suppose that, unlike Chase in the story, he has kept detailed tasting notes for twenty years — not merely his verdicts but his descriptions of how each sample struck him. After the operation his rankings are exactly what his notes would have predicted, but he reports an unmistakable mismatch: the coffee now strikes him the way he remembers the bad coffee striking him, and he insists that something about how things seem has changed even though none of his judgments about the coffee have. My supervisor says this is no objection at all, on the grounds that memory traces are just more internal states which the operation could have swapped along with everything else, and that a remembered way-things-seemed is no less of a posit than a present one.

So, concretely: which claims in the passage would a sympathetic reader of the paper accept, which are defensible simplifications for a trade audience, and which would Dennett reject; does the closing eliminativist-sounding claim follow from anything the argument actually establishes; how should the argument be stated so that it does not depend on whether the swap is silent or symmetric; is my dilemma reconstruction on the right lines, and if not, where does it misdescribe the dialectic; and finally, is my memory variant a genuine counterexample, or is my supervisor right that it is already absorbed — and on the corrected reading of the argument, what would have to be the case for there to be a fact of the matter about inversion at all?
