Practical question that turned into a philosphy one, sorry.

I'm rewriting my psychiatric advance directive — the form where you say now what should happen if you get sectioned during a manic episode. During my last episode I was maybe 99% sure I was completely fine and didn't need meds, and it genuinely felt like clear thinking at the time. Today I'd put maybe 15% on "I was fine." My psychiatrist wants a clause that commits me to a 14-day admission regardless of what I'm saying when they see me; my solicitor is fine with that.

But a philosopher friend says van Fraassen's Reflection principle means I'm rationally required to line my current credence up with my expected future credence — so if I know future-me is going to sit at 99% on "I'm fine", I should already be near 99% now, and pre-committing against my own future judgment is a kind of diachronic incoherence that a bookie could exploit with a Dutch book. That can't be right, surely: the whole point is that the 99% is manufactured by the episode.

So: what are the most important implications of the Reflection principle for how I should actually write this thing? Concretely — is it a live constraint on someone in my position, or only on an ideal agent who is certain she won't become irrational, won't forget things, and won't get pushed around later? Where exactly does the argument break, on the Dutch book version and on the conditionalization version? And does it say anything about who the directive should defer to: me during the episode, my psychiatrist, or some weighted mix of us? Is there a sensible reading on which the thing I should be deferring to is more like "me after three weeks on medication" than "me on day two"?

Two wrinkles I actually care about. One: my insurer knocks a chunk off my premium if I sign a declaration of recovery, so future-me will have a financial reason to be confident — can that motive reach backwards through Reflection and change what I'm required to believe today, which is definately not what I want the directive to do? Two: would it be smarter to trigger the clause on my confidence staying above 90% for 72 hours rather than on its level at any single moment?

Not after medical or legal advice, obviously — just the shape of the argument and what the principle really commits me to.
