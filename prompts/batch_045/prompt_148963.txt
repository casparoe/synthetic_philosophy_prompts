I'm putting together the final for my upper-level moral psychology seminar (20 marks, 45-minute in-class essay, 1,000 word cap) and I need one question on self-deception that can't be answered by reciting the paradoxes and the standard menu of responses. Every year half the class writes the same essay: here's the static paradox, here's the dynamic one, here's the partitioning move, here's the deflationary alternative that drops the intention and the dual belief, and look, no paradox. They get Bs and I can't tell whether they understand anything.

So I want a question built around a case I've started using in lecture, which I'm calling "standard-shopping," because it seems to me to dodge the whole framing of the debate. The case: Ana is going up for promotion, and the written criteria require "sustained national visibility." Reading the file the first time she takes that to mean invited talks at other universities, a national award, grant panels — and on that reading she knows she doesn't clear the bar. Over the next few weeks she comes to think that "visibility" really just means that people in the field have heard of your work, and on that reading she clears it easily. Nothing else about her view of her record changes: she doesn't come to believe she's given more talks than she has, or that she won an award. Six months later she's on the committee for a junior colleague, reads the same sentence in the same document, applies the original stricter reading, and votes no. There's no intention anywhere to end up with a false belief, no blackout, no partitioned-off compartment, and no first-order factual belief that contradicts another.

The thing I want the question to force is this: standard-shopping suggests the literature has been looking for self-deception in the wrong place — in a belief, or in a contradictory pair of them — when the real unit might be a policy for counting considerations as evidence. But I'm not sure that's right, and I'm not sure it isn't just the deflationary view wearing a new hat. So the question should make students say precisely which condition of each familiar account the case does and doesn't satisfy, and then take a side on whether that's a problem for the account or a problem for the case. It should also demand a contrast condition: what would have to be different for Ana to count as self-deceived rather than (i) sincerely disagreeing with her department about what the word means, (ii) merely reasoning self-servingly, or (iii) plain akrasia — and their condition has to survive the fact that Ana reverts to the strict reading the moment her own promotion is settled.

Please give me:

1. The question, worded the way you'd actually paste it into an exam paper. Multi-part is fine if the parts do real work; feel free to rework my case or rename my term, and tell me briefly if you'd change it and why.

2. A model answer of about 700-800 words. Names don't matter much (I tell them that on day one), so use structural labels — the traditional/interpersonal model, the partitioning version of it, the deflationary alternative — rather than leaning on who said what.

3. A marking scheme out of 20: the four or five must-hit moves, what each is worth, and one line per move on how a student typically gets it wrong (I've found the usual failure is describing a view instead of testing it against the case, but you may have better guesses).

4. A short note on which single move in your model answer a strong student could reject outright and still deserve full marks — I hand the markers an "acceptable variations" list and I want it to be accurate.

Last thing: at the very end, tell me how confident you are in the central claim of the model answer, and pick out the one claim in it you're least sure about. That one goes straight on the acceptable-variations list too.
