I am a high school senior, and I would be grateful for your help with a dispute I have somehow ended up in the middle of.

Last term our honor council heard the case of a classmate who submitted a history essay that he had drafted with the help of an AI writing assistant. His defence rested almost entirely on one sentence, which I have since heard repeated in three unrelated places: "the mind isn't just in the head." He used it to argue that there is no principled line between consulting your own memory, consulting your notes, and consulting a chatbot on your phone, because in all three cases something outside your skull is doing part of the work, and so a charge of cheating has no line to stand on.

What I would like from you is a careful disambiguation of that sentence. Please set out, as a menu rather than as a single favoured reading, the distinct precise claims it could be used to express, ordered from the most modest to the most radical: that the environment causally shapes how we think; that external props make internal tasks easier; that external items can in some cases partly constitute a mental state, so that what you believe depends on what is in your notebook; that there is no interesting boundary at all between a thinker and her tools. For each, tell me whether it is a platitude that even someone sceptical of the extended mind would happily grant, whether it is substantive and defensible, or whether it is false or close to incoherent.

Three cases matter to me here, and I have not seen them discussed anywhere, so please test your readings against these rather than against the usual examples. First: the assistant is a subscription service. The company can quietly change how it responds, or withdraw it, and it sometimes produces confident fabrications. Does the reliability that such accounts demand belong to how the thing in fact behaves, or to whether the user could tell? Second: suppose this particular assistant has been trained on my classmate's own past essays and notes, so that its outputs are largely a statistical distillation of his earlier thinking, while the model itself belongs to a company. Does that make the resulting cognition more his, or less? Third: five students share one account, and only one of them curates and edits the material it draws on, so the others find their apparent recollections shifting without noticing. Whose mind is extended there, if anyone's?

Then, and this is what I actually need: which readings would do the work my classmate needs, and are those the defensible ones or only the ones nobody could accept? My chemistry teacher uses the same sentence to tell us not to bother memorising anything, which seems to me to require a different reading again, so please say which.

Please write in continuous prose, as you would in an email: no headings, no bullet points, no numbering, no bold or italicised terms, no tables, and no bibliography, just paragraphs, somewhere between six hundred and nine hundred words.
