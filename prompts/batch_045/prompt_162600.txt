You are advising a legislative commission that has a single window — one bill, drafted within about eighteen months, with spending capped at a fixed share of annual revenue — to design a reparations program for an injustice whose direct victims are all long dead: a state-run system of forced labour and land dispossession abolished three generations ago. The commission must settle three things: who qualifies as a recipient, how much each recipient receives, and who pays.

The philosophers on its advisory panel have raised four worries, and the chair wants to know which of them genuinely need answering before the bill can be drafted and defended in public:

(a) The baseline problem. The size of the debt depends on a counterfactual history about which there may be no fact of the matter: what the victims' descendants would now have had, had the system never existed, given that it also shaped migration, marriage, and technology in the region.

(b) The existence problem. Many of the people who would end up on the recipient list would not exist at all had the system not existed; their ancestors were brought to, or held in, the territory by the very institution being complained of. So arguably the system did not leave these particular people worse off than they would otherwise have been.

(c) The overlap problem. The tax base that funds the payments will contain, in unknown proportions, descendants of the victims, descendants of those who administered the system, and recent arrivals connected to neither — and some of the victims' descendants are now among the wealthiest people in the country.

(d) The currency problem. What was taken — land, kinship networks, political standing, recognised standing in the community — is not obviously the sort of thing cash converts into, and the public ceremony of payment may be doing expressive work that a larger or smaller cheque actively undercuts.

Rank these by how much they should shape the commission's decisions over the next eighteen months, and say which, if any, are philosophical noise that a legislator can safely ignore. I want the ordering defended rather than listed: for each, what concretely turns on it inside the three drafting choices; how much settling it would change what the other worries come to; and what new information — demographic data, archival findings, constitutional constraints — would move it up or down the list. If you think some fifth question matters more than all of these, name it and place it in the ordering.

Answer in at most 600 words.
