I convene a small reading group — two political theorists, an economist, a graduate student in history, and me (I work in public policy) — that has been meeting for about a year on questions about the long-term future. For our last four sessions we have been completely stuck on one question: whether people alive now live at a hinge of history, in the sense that the expected difference our actions make to the very long-run future is far larger than it would be for a comparable action taken at a randomly chosen point in the past or future. The stuckness is strange, because nobody in the group is hostile to the framing. Both sides grant that the future could be enormous and that some periods could matter much more than others; they disagree about whether this is one of them, and nothing either side says seems to land with the other. Nadia, the skeptic, has three moves. First, a base-rate point: nearly every generation has believed it stood at a unique turning point — 1914, 1945, the various millenarian movements — and nearly all of them were wrong, and the thing they all said was that their new technology made them different, so self-sampling should deflate any claim to specialness. Second, a patience point: even granting that the future is huge, the case for acting now rather than later is a further argument, since future generations will be richer, better informed and more numerous; if influence can be stored in institutions, capital, norms and knowledge, then the rational policy might be to bank resources and let better-positioned later people act, so hinge-ness does not by itself yield an argument for spending now. Third, a measurability point: the relevant quantities are model outputs dominated by deep uncertainty about how far effects propagate, and reasonable people can move the estimate by orders of magnitude, so the debate is not tracking anything. Stefan, who defends the hinge claim, answers each: the base-rate point conflates the true claim that people are prone to think themselves special with the unsupported claim that no period is special, and his own case rests on arguable structural features rather than self-congratulation — an unprecedented concentration of destructive and transformative power, earliness in a trajectory whose eventual scale may dwarf everything so far, and institutional memory far shorter than the speed of technological change. Against patience he argues that banking influence presupposes both that influence is storable and that the vault survives: if there is a substantial chance the trajectory is derailed or locked in badly in the near term, resources saved for later may never be spent by anyone, and some of the most valuable things — which technologies get built first, in what order, under what governance — are not storable at all because they are facts about timing and sequencing; he also says waiting is itself an irreversible intervention and has an information cost. Against the measurability point he says uncertainty cuts both ways and that the dispute can be decomposed into separately arguable cruxes: how large the risk of derailment is in the near term, how much of the eventual value gets irreversibly settled early, and how substitutable later action is for present action. Nadia's reply was that the three cruxes are not independent, so multiplying three uncertain quantities yields a number you can move anywhere you like; Stefan's reply was that this is true of any empirical question and is not a reason to stop. At that point we realised we were going in circles.

A few weeks ago the historian in the group proposed a set of distinctions that I suspect either dissolves part of the dispute or merely relabels it, and I genuinely cannot tell which. She argued that three things travel together in the longtermist writing but can in principle come apart: hinge-ness, the ratio of the expected impact of a marginal unit of effort or resources now to the expected impact of the same unit applied at a randomly chosen time; perils-ness, how much larger the probability of permanent derailment is now than at a typical time; and lock-in-ness, how large a share of the eventual value of the future gets irreversibly settled during the current window. She pointed out that these dissociate — a period could be high-peril but low-hinge (nothing anyone does moves the odds), high-hinge but low-peril (enormous upside steering with little derailment risk), or high-lock-in without high-hinge (the settlement will happen anyway and marginal effort does not steer it) — and she added two complications I had not considered. The first is a units problem: hinge-ness is defined relative to a partition of history and a unit of input, whether per century, per generation, per decade, per person, or per unit of resources, and the verdict is not invariant to that choice; per-century accounting flatters the present, whereas per-person accounting is diluted by the fact that many more people are alive now than at most earlier times, so the most important century and the most important life can come apart sharply. The second is a bankability problem: whether influence is conserved across time at some rate of return, or instead decays, is a separate empirical question that Nadia's patience argument simply assumes an answer to. My own hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that our argument is not one disagreement but three stacked and then summed into a single verdict: an empirical question about the magnitudes, a definitional question about which of these quantities was the thing we were arguing about all along, and underneath both a deeper axiological difference about what counts as improving the very long run — roughly, whether adding good lives later counts as making things better at all, or whether only effects on people who would exist anyway count, so that most of the hinge case quietly presupposes an impersonal rather than a person-affecting standard. If that is right, the stubbornness is an artifact of aggregating, and progress would consist in splitting rather than in marshalling more evidence.

I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict, and — please note this — I do not want a survey of the literature, a reading list, or citations; I want your own analysis of the structure of this disagreement, engaging the moves above as we actually made them rather than the standard versions you would expect. Concretely: which of the moves are doing real work, and is any of them answering a different question under the same name? Is the historian's three-way dissociation genuine, or do the notions collapse into one another under scrutiny — does hinge-ness, for instance, turn out to be perils-ness times lock-in-ness times something, or is one of them doing all the work? Does the units problem show that hinge-ness is ill-posed, or only that it needs an explicit unit, and if the latter, which unit is correct and on what grounds — is there a non-arbitrary choice, or does the right unit depend on the decision one is trying to make? Is the residual disagreement best described as empirical, verbal, or axiological, or as a mixture in specifiable proportions? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a decomposition and a naming of cruxes, a formal result about how such quantities behave under deep uncertainty, a track record of forecasting, a decision-theoretic argument about option value or the value of information, a reframing of the question — or is this the sort of impasse where no progress should be expected, and if so, why? Finally, if your diagnosis leaves the original question well-posed at all, say briefly where you would put your own credence on whether this is a hinge and what you would be relying on in doing so. Around 700 to 900 words of prose, please, with no headings beyond the distinctions.
