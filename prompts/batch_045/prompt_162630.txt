I'm writing the final essay for a seminar on property, historical injustice and rectification, and I'd like to work the argument through with you before I commit to it.

The case I want to use is the restitution dispute over the Benin Bronzes: the plaques, heads and other court objects carried out of Benin City by British forces during the 1897 punitive expedition, dispersed since then through auction houses, private collections and museums, and now the subject of claims by Nigeria, with some European institutions having transferred or agreed to transfer title while keeping the objects on display under loan or partnership arrangements. I want it read through the lens of Nozick's historical entitlement theory: legitimate initial acquisition, legitimate transfer, and the principle of rectification for violations of the first two. Then I want the Parthenon (Elgin) Marbles as a contrast case in the same essay, because the acquisition step there is murkier: the sculptures were removed from the Acropolis in the early nineteenth century by Elgin's agents, allegedly under an Ottoman permit whose existence and scope are disputed, sold to the British government, and held at the British Museum ever since. For the marbles, run the analysis twice — once assuming a permit existed but was granted by an imperial authority with no particular stake in the monument, and once assuming no valid permission at all. I don't want the essay to turn on settling that fact.

What I'm after in the Benin case first is a disciplined mapping. Who are the parties the theory actually recognises: the Oba and the Kingdom of Benin at the time of the taking, the British state and the soldiers who took the objects, the intermediate purchasers and donor-collectors, the present museums, the Nigerian federal government, the Edo State authorities, the Oba as a non-sovereign traditional ruler within a modern republic? What does the theory say the violation consists of — a wrongful taking, a wrongful destruction of a political order, both? What baseline does rectification aim at: return of the specific object, return of equivalent value, compensation measured by the deprivation at the time, or something directed at the wrong rather than at the loss? And what work does the intervening century do: the direct victims and the direct takers are long dead, and the objects have been conserved, published and displayed by institutions that were not themselves wrongdoers. Does a historical-entitlement view have anything structured to say about that chain, or does it just say the entitlement never passed and stop there?

Then I want the marbles to test the theory rather than illustrate it. If it yields a crisp verdict on Benin and only a conditional or indeterminate one on the Parthenon, that contrast is itself the interesting result, and I want to know whether the difference is doing real work in the theory or is merely an artefact of how contested the historical record is. In particular: can a framework built on acquisition and transfer so much as register a claim whose basis is that the object is a fragment of a dispersed whole, or that its meaning is tied to a site and a building, rather than a claim about how it was taken?

Separately, I've been trying out a three-way distinction in my notes and I want to know whether it survives contact with the theory. Call them: (a) a property claim — who holds title; (b) a custodial claim — who decides where the object physically sits, whether it travels, whether it is loaned back; (c) a narrative claim — who controls the account that travels with the object, the label text, the framing, what the object is presented as evidence of. My sense is that the live disputes sit mostly at (b) and (c): the loan-back deals and the shared-heritage framing are where the actual fighting happens. That suggests a theory organised around (a) either has nothing to say about (b) and (c), or quietly reduces them to (a). Tell me which, and tell me whether the reduction is defensible if that's what it does.

Finally, the direction back onto the theory. Is this episode a confirmation of the usefulness of a rectification principle, an anomaly for it, or a case it was never built for? Two things I'd especially like pressed. First, whether rectification here smuggles in a principle of group persistence across radical political change — the Kingdom of Benin, colonial Nigeria and the modern federal republic are not one continuous agent in any ordinary sense, and the modern Greek state is not Periclean Athens — and what becomes of the claim if no such principle is available. Second, whether the universal or encyclopedic museum argument — roughly, that a global collection makes objects accessible to more people and to scholarship, and that wide dispersal is itself a public good — has any grip inside a historical-entitlement framework, or whether it is a different kind of argument wearing the vocabulary of rights.

I know the general shape of both disputes, so don't spend the answer recounting them; use the facts only where they do analytical work. Where the theory is silent, say so rather than making it speak. If you think some other framework handles the custodial and narrative claims better, say which in a sentence or two and say what it costs — but I don't want a survey of positions.
