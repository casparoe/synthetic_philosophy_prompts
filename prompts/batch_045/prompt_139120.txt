hi — I'm a second year and I run the peer-led study sessions for the first year minds and machines module (same one I took last year and scraped a 2:1 on). Next week's is forty minutes, whiteboard, about fifteen people, mostly CS with a few philosophy and psychology types, no music theory between them, and it's on Hofstadter's knob model — the bit where a theme or a style is basically a load of knobs and creativity is a matter of finding the right ones and twiddling them.

Don't explain the model to me, I think I've got the basic pitch. I want a plan for getting it across to them.

What I keep getting stuck on when I prep is that there seem to be two different claims hiding in it. Turning knobs that are already there just sounds like searching a big space — boring, and not obviously creativity at all. Coming up with the knobs is where the interesting work seems to be, but then 'knob' is doing less work than the metaphor suggests: a knob that only turns out to exist once someone's found it isn't really a dimension of a space you were exploring. And some settings seem to re-cut the whole space — once you've decided a set of variations is going to be studies, or be in a minor mode, or be about one interval, a load of other knobs show up or stop mattering or vanish.

Then there's a question a guy asked in my session last year that I fluffed: whether 'how far apart' two variations are is even well defined. Is tempo one knob or twenty? I now suspect the answer is that it depends how you carve the space up, which would make 'that was a bolder variation' a claim about your carving convention rather than about the music. And a related worry, that maybe we're only ever reading the knobs off backwards, from variations we already admire, so the model redescribes creativity without generating any.

So: what order does all that go in, and what depends on what? What one example should carry the session — I was going to use a chord progression or a pop remix rather than anything classical, since none of them read music; is that a mistake, and what does the carrying example need to have and to not have? Which wrong ideas will they walk out with (I'm guessing 'so creativity is just brute-force search', 'the knobs are objectively sitting there waiting', maybe 'more knobs = more creative') and where in the sequence should I kill each one? One question for the end that'd show they'd got the knob-finding bit and not just the knob-turning bit. And what should I just flat out refuse to get dragged into — I really don't want to lose ten minutes to whether AI is creative.
