I convene a fortnightly seminar that puts political-theory graduate students in the same room as people who actually work on migration policy, and this term we have been running a structured exchange between two positions that normally talk past each other. On one side is an open-borders liberal: the view that a state committed to the equal moral worth of persons cannot permissibly exclude willing entrants, that border control is a form of coercion the state imposes rather than a benefit it withholds, and that exclusion therefore owes a justification to the excluded person and not merely to the existing citizenry. On the other side is a welfare-state restrictionist: the view that a generous, universalist, redistributive state depends on bounded membership not merely as a budgetary constraint but because the willingness of citizens to fund transfers for people they do not know is a fragile political achievement with institutional preconditions, so that a state may set admissions at whatever level sustains that achievement. After eight sessions neither side has moved, and I want to convert the exchange into something we could publish as the joint statement of a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

Two distinctions I have been imposing to keep the discussion honest — keep them, refine them, or replace them with better ones, but do not quietly collapse them. First, I separate three gates that are usually conflated: entry, meaning physical admission and the right to reside; access, meaning eligibility for transfers, public services and labour-market protections; and standing, meaning permanent membership and the political rights that go with it. A great deal of the argument seems to me to ride on which gate is doing the work, and I suspect each side would defend a different gate most fiercely if forced to give up the others. Second, I separate two versions of the restrictionist's empirical worry, a fiscal one about net contribution at fixed policy settings and a political-economy one about the dynamic effect of inflows on the future level and conditionality of redistribution; these have different time shapes, different evidence, and different implications for whether a temporary-worker programme is a compromise or a capitulation.

What I want is the document such a collaboration would actually produce, in my participants' voices rather than in the vocabulary of any particular school, and without a single citation or reading list — arguments only, no bibliographies. Concretely: the claims both sides can sign, including about what the other side is not claiming, and about what each accepts is genuinely hard for its own view; the residual disagreements, each stated twice, once in wording the open-borders side would accept for itself and once in wording the restrictionist would accept for itself, and never in wording one side would call a straw man; the cruxes, that is the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side; and for each crux the test, case or piece of evidence both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance so that neither can reinterpret the result afterwards. Where you think a disagreement is merely verbal, say which one and what the underlying substantive disagreement turns out to be; and for each side, name one finding that would count as evidence against that side's own position.

Please structure the response in four titled sections, in this order and at these rough lengths: "The question we were convened to answer," one or two sentences, setting out in neutral terms what the collaboration was asked to settle; "Agreed claims," eight to ten numbered claims, one or two sentences each, around 300 words; "Disagreements," a table of four to six rows with columns for the crux, the open-borders formulation, the restrictionist formulation, and whether the disagreement is verbal or substantive, around 400 words; and "Tests, with predictions recorded in advance," three or four tests or cases, each with what each side predicts and why that prediction follows from its own view, around 400 words. If you conclude that no test both sides would accept exists for some crux, say so plainly rather than inventing one.
