I've been getting into investing as a hobby and reading a lot of blog posts about it, and one of them explained something it called the "no trade theorem". This is roughly the paragraph, I've cut it down a bit:

"Economists have actually proved that rational people should never trade with each other. The argument goes like this. Say you and me start out with the same general picture of how the world works, and we each know the other one is rational. If I offer to sell you a share, the offer itself tells you something: I must reckon the share is worth less than you do. So I'd only make the offer if I thought I knew better than you, and you'd only accept if you thought you knew better than me, and we can't both be right. So no trade happens. Two things follow from this. First, the huge amount of trading we see in real markets has to be explained by people being overconfident, or irrational, or just gambling for the buzz. Second, it shows market prices already soak up all the information that's out there, which is why picking shares is a mug's game and nearly everybody should just stick it in an index fund."

Two things nag at me about it. One: the argument seems to be about why trading shouldn't happen at all, so how does he get from there to "prices are correct"? If nobody is trading on what they know, wouldn't that make prices less informative rather than more? Two: "rational people should never trade" can't be quite right, because surely there are reasons to swap stuff that have nothing to do with thinking you know better — you might need the cash, or want to be less exposed to something. But I can't tell wether the theorem is meant to cover those cases or whether it's only about a narrow sort of betting.
