I'm in a decision theory seminar and last week we did Death in Damascus, except our version had Death predicting your city correctly only 90% of the time (good predictor, not a perfect one). I tried to write out properly why everyone says evidential decision theory falls apart there, and I ended up proving something I really dont want. Help me find the exit.

(1) EDT scores an act by the news it would bring: EU(A) = the sum over states s of P(s | A) × U(A & s).
(2) Two cities, stay or flee, life = 10, death = 0, Death equally reliable whichever way you jump, ignore the bus fare. Conditional on staying, P(Death is in Damascus) = 0.9, so staying is worth 1 and fleeing is worth 9. Conditional on fleeing, fleeing is worth 1 and staying is worth 9. So each act, scored the way EDT scores acts, recommends the other one: nothing is ratifiable.
(3) The 0.9 isnt doing any real work. All (2) needed was that my act is evidence about where Death is — any better-than-chance correlation twists the ranking the same way.
(4) And every real choice has that. What I do comes out of my character and my situation, and both of those are tangled up with how the world is. Me carrying an umbrella is decent evidence that its going to rain.
(5) So no act is ever ratifiable for anybody, and EDT never gets to deliver a verdict at all — not in Damascus, not about umbrellas, nowhere.
(6) Thats absurd. EDT handles the umbrella case fine, and people who like it in Newcomb's problem obviously dont think it goes silent everywhere else.

I'd rather keep (1), (2) and (6), so I have to drop (3), (4) or (5). Which one, and what does dropping it cost me? My study partner says just kill (4), since having seen the forecast screens off the umbrella correlation, but I cant see why that move doesnt work just as well in Damascus.
