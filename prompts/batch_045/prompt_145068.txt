My dad says "he was a man of his time" the way other people say bless you, and at sunday dinner it came out three times in one night about three different guys. About Jefferson: he owned slaves, but everyone did, so drop it. About Churchill: the stuff about Indians and about Gandhi was ugly but that's how the empire talked then. About Columbus: he's a man of the 1400s, you cant hold him to 2020 standards. I tried pushing back and got nowhere, mostly because I think each time he was saying something slightly diffent, and whenever I pinned one version down he slid to the next one. So I want the whole menu.

Context for what it was supposed to do: the actual argument on the table was whether my old middle school should keep Jefferson's name. So the verdict we needed was about a name on a building, not about Jefferson's soul or about whether slavery was wrong.

Some half-baked things I came up with afterward, which you can use or throw out:

The asymmetry test. Nobody ever says "he was a man of his time" about somebody who was ahead of their time. The people around Jefferson who thought slavery was wrong get praised precisely for not tracking local opinion. But if the sentence is a causal claim — his beliefs were produced by his environment, so he's off the hook — then it should cover them too, since their beliefs had causes as well. So either the causal reading isn't what's doing the work, or people only deploy it for the majority view, which seems like a tell.

The forward test. I said to my dad: fine, then in 2125 people will say we ate animals and bought clothes sewn in sweatshops and that we were people of our time, so we're off too. He said that's different because we can know better, we have the information. I think that was him quietly switching to an epistemic reading — he couldn't have known — but I don't know how far that gets him or what it costs him.

The symmetry test. If distance discounts blame, it should discount praise, right? Do we admire anyone less for being brave in a war when bravery was ordinary then? My dad said no, courage is courage. That felt inconsistent and I couldn't say why.

The three-targets thing. There seem to be three separate verdicts getting run together: was the act wrong, is he blameworthy for it, and what do we do with the statue / the name / the holiday. My hunch is the sentence does real work on the second and nearly none on the third, but I'm not confident.

And one more: "everyone thought that then" is often just false. In a lot of these cases there were people alive at the time saying the thing was wrong, sometimes loudly. Does the sentence need that descriptive claim to be true in order to work, or is it really about what this particular guy could get his hands on?

So: lay out the precise claims the sentence could be making, from the ones that are trivially true up to the ones that are obviously false, with a verdict on each. For each reading say which of my tests it survives and which kills it. Then tell me which reading my dad was probably reaching for in each of the three cases and whether that reading actually gets him to "drop it" — and whether any version is strong enough to settle the school-name question, since that's the bit I have to go back and argue.
