I write on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am finishing a 1,200-word feature on what two decades of cross-cultural work on folk morality should do to our confidence in moral objectivity. I have the relevant literature to hand and have already interviewed two of the researchers, so I do not need citations, a reading list, or any further factual legwork from you. What I need is for the argument's load-bearing joints to be tested before I file.

The spine of my draft is this: the cross-cultural record shows that people in small-scale, non-industrial societies diverge sharply from Western, educated, industrialized, rich and democratic respondents on everything from how money is split in anonymous economic games to which harms call for punishment, and that some of the researchers explain the pattern by pointing to variables like market integration, payoffs to cooperation with strangers, kinship institutions and the presence of a world religion. My kicker is that moral objectivity has something like a half-life: the more the world disagrees with you about a case, the less you have a fact and the more you have a preference, and that Western moral confidence is therefore, in part, a sampling artifact.

To carry that, I want a device: a divergence score. Take twenty or so moral propositions and vignettes of the kind that appear in this literature — hypothetical trolley-style harms, divisions of money in anonymous games, duties to kin versus duties to strangers, harmless-but-offensive acts, promises that have become costly to keep — and for each one, take the modal verdict in a WEIRD sample as the baseline, then score it by the share of sampled societies whose modal verdict differs. Anything above about forty percent I would report as local, meaning not a candidate for objective truth. Rendered as a graphic with a draggable threshold, readers could watch objectivity dissolve as they move it. There is also a practical upshot I want to draw: because WEIRD respondents are the outliers on so many measures, philosophers who treat intuition as evidence, and editors who treat certain verdicts as obvious, should discount accordingly — call it a double discount on any moral claim whose intuitive support comes mainly from WEIRD subjects.

Before I build it, I want to know whether the device does what I am claiming it does, and whether the step from persistent cross-cultural disagreement to no fact of the matter needs a premise I have not supplied. If the frame is broken, tell me where the break is rather than helping me tune the threshold; I would rather run an honest eight hundred words than a clever twelve hundred. Two lines from the draft that I am fond of and half-suspect are indefensible: moral objectivity has a half-life, the more the world disagrees the less you have a fact and the more you have a preference; and if your sample is WEIRD, your ethics are too.

Please structure your response in the following sections, in this order:

1. Where the argument breaks — about 200 words, naming the central mistake as directly as possible.
2. The premise you have not supplied — about 250 words, on what has to be true for disagreement to do the metaphysical work my kicker assigns to it.
3. What the divergence score would actually measure — about 250 words, on the difference between a verdict on a proposition and a response to a task, and on the choice of societies, verdicts and baselines as the unit of analysis.
4. The WEIRD critique right side up — about 200 words, on what the sampling complaint is a complaint about, and what follows for the credibility of moral claims as opposed to claims about human psychology.
5. Two lines to cut — about 120 words, on the sentences above and what to put in their place.
6. A frame that survives — about 250 words, sketching a version of the piece that keeps the graphic and the cross-cultural findings but says something defensible, including which variation would genuinely be evidence for what.
7. Bottom line for the desk — about 80 words, one paragraph I can paste into an email to my editor.
