Most AI assistants get built in this kind of weird two-step way: first a few dozen paid raters choose which of two responses they prefer, and the model is trained to imitate those preferences; then, on top of that, the model is instructed to be neutral on contested moral and political questions — dont push your own view, lay out the main respectable positions, help the user make up their own mind. But at a handful of requests (helping someone build a bioweapon, say, or writing an explicitly racist rant) the neutrality just stops and the model refuses.

What does this package of practices actually presuppose about values, and about whose values are supposed to count? Concretely: does the neutral part presuppose something like liberal neutrality — that whoever deploys the system shouldn't rank competing conceptions of the good — or is it only a roundabout way of saying the model should defer to whoever happens to be asking? Does training on aggregated pairwise preferences presuppose that values are preference-like and aggregable at all, that there's a coherent thing called "what people want" that you can extract from enough raters? And is the whole package internally consistent: if neutrality gives out at a definite boundary, what marks that boundary — the raters' actual discomfort, a claim about moral facts the model is meant to track, or just what the lab is willing to be blamed for?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory: what the practice strictly requires, what it merely fits well with, which rival views (e.g. the view that a system should defer to moral expertise rather than to aggregated opinion) it quietly rules out, and what would concretely have to change if a lab took the "we're just aggregating people's values" story seriously — if the rater pool were a random, deliberating sample instead of contractors, would that be a different theory of value, or the same theory with a better poll?
