I teach a second-year philosophy of mind seminar — 16 students, one 80-minute slot, no TA — and we just went over Dennett's Quining Qualia. Half the class thinks the argument is a cheap rhetorical trick and half thinks it's obviously right, in the way that usually means nobody has actually understood it. I don't want to do another seminar on it; I want to run something.

The thing I want them to feel in their hands is the charge that qualia are an incoherent posit: the four properties they're supposed to have — ineffable, intrinsic, private, directly apprehensible — can't be held together, and every move you make to rescue the notion either turns it into something publicly checkable (in which case it isn't the special extra ingredient) or makes it so thin that it does no work anywhere. We also did the Chase and Sanborn bit, the two tasters whose reactions drift apart while nothing about the stuff in the cup changes, and one student made an objection I couldn't shake: that the case only shows we're bad at monitoring changes in our own experience over time, not that there's nothing there being monitored. I'd like the activity to make that escape hatch cost them something rather than me just arguing against it, since I don't think I argued against it very well.

What I have to work with: instant coffee, paper cups, sugar, salt, a big bag of jelly beans, index cards, a whiteboard, and sixteen people who will absolutely try to game any scoring system I invent.

So design me a runnable game. Rules, materials, a timeline, and a payoff scheme with small real stakes (the leftover chocolate is fine).

Constraints I care about:
- Nobody hears the words ineffable, intrinsic, private, or qualia until the debrief. The four properties have to come apart in what they're trying to do, not in what I say up front.
- Real private stimuli, real (small) losses, ideally built around taste so it rhymes with Chase and Sanborn. Use near-threshold differences so nobody can just name the flavour and short-circuit the whole thing.
- A mid-game twist where something changes and the group has to say, from reports alone, whether it was the experience that changed or the reporting — and discovers it can't.
- A final round where they get to design their own probe to settle that question and then have to bet on it working.

Then the debreif: map each moment of the game onto one of the four properties and onto the monitoring objection, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm expecting cheating, somebody announcing they can't taste anything, and one student who names the moral in round one), and be honest about what the game can't reproduce. My guess is it can show the posit is communicatively and evidentially idle but can't touch the residual conviction that of course there's something it's like — if that's right, tell me how to handle that in the room rather than pretending the game dissolved it. And if taste is the wrong modality for this, say so and give me the version that works instead.
