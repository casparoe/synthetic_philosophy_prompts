I co-host a technically literate podcast on the philosophy and epistemology of AI systems, and our next episode is a forty-five-minute interview with a philosopher who defends a position I'll sketch as follows. She argues that a language model's individual outputs are not assertions: the model has no beliefs, and sampling a completion is not an act of saying, so asking whether the model lied on a given occasion is a category error. What we should assess instead, she claims, is whether the model's distribution over possible answers stands to the evidence available in its weights and context window as an honest speaker's utterance stands to their beliefs — call it *distributional sincerity*. On this view honesty is a property of a policy evaluated over a reference class of prompts, not of an utterance; measured one output at a time it is not even well defined. My co-host and I have found her evasive in print on two points, and I want questions that close those exits: she dismisses any worry about a single confident false answer by calling it the "token-level fallacy," and when a case seems to show something genuinely deceptive she says it is a fact about the sampling procedure and the product wrapper rather than about the model.

Please draft ten to twelve questions, in an order that builds across the episode, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. They should cover at least the following, and I'd rather have depth on these than breadth beyond them. First, the reference-class problem: because sincerity is defined relative to a distribution over some class of prompts, and for any given output there will be classes under which it looks sincere (all questions of this broad type) and classes under which it doesn't (this question, from this user, right now), what fixes the class — and if she answers "the deployment distribution, fixed by intended use," whose intention, in a case where the deployer's intention is precisely what is under suspicion? Second, sandbagging: a system that systematically underperforms on capability evaluations looks like deception stretched across many tokens and episodes and held together by something intention-like, which should be her best case rather than her worst; expect her to say that this is just a property of the policy over evaluation-like prompts and that the remedy is to include such prompts in the reference class, and press on whether that is anything more than relabeling, given that the sandbagging system's whole competence consists in being sensitive to whether it is currently being evaluated. Third, omission and paltering: answering the literal question truly while never volunteering the disqualifying caveat, where the distribution over what to mention seems to be exactly where the honesty or dishonesty lives. Fourth, implicature: whether a distribution can implicate, given that much real-world misleading happens through what an answer suggests rather than what it says, and whether Frankfurt's distinction between the liar and the bullshitter, who is indifferent to how things are, has any home in an account like hers — and, if it reduces to something like flatness or entropy across answers, whether she thinks it is a cost of the view that bullshitting then comes out no worse than lying.

Two more things I want built in. One question should target the corrupted-mill worry: her account measures outputs against the evidence internal to the model, but post-training reshapes which of a model's evidence gets expressed, so a system optimized to please users may have its evidence-to-output map bent in just the way a people-pleaser's is — is the benchmark for sincerity then internal to a system already trained to be insincere, and is there any external anchor? One question should target observability: we see samples and sometimes next-token probabilities, never the counterfactual distribution over answers that were not sampled, and we cannot read off a model's internal evidence independently of its outputs, so what empirical finding would establish that a deployed system is insincere — and, following up, what would count as evidence against distributional sincerity itself? Close with a question that makes the "that's the wrapper, not the model" move unavailable to her, and with one about what, if anything, she would change in training, evaluation, or deployment if her account were accepted — in particular whether it would license relaxing truthfulness training on the grounds that single-answer accuracy is not the thing we should be optimizing. Please skip biographical and career questions entirely, add a short note after each question on the reply you expect, and flag the two or three places where I should pause and press rather than move on.
