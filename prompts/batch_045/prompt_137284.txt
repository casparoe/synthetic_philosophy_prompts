Consider the standard sequential observational-learning model: two equiprobable states of the world; a binary action; each agent receives a private binary signal of fixed accuracy p (with 1/2 < p < 1), conditionally independent across agents given the state; agents move once each, in a commonly known order, and each observes the complete history of earlier actions before choosing. I want to get clear about two things that I keep running together, and then about a variant I have been playing with.

First, an apparent tension. On the one hand, the belief a Bayesian observer would hold, updated on the growing action history, is a martingale and hence converges; and in the version of the model with unbounded signal strength, learning is asymptotically complete — the population settles on the correct action with probability one, and nothing that deserves to be called a cascade ever forms. On the other hand, with bounded signals a cascade forms with probability one, it may be a wrong one, and once it has formed no later private signal ever surfaces in the record. I do not think these claims contradict each other, but I want the exact hinge. What is the object that converges in the first claim, what is the object that gets locked in in the second, and which step of the argument is doing the work when one says that convergence can be convergence on the wrong value, with positive probability? Please give me that step rather than the slogan.

Second, the variant. Suppose agents do not see the whole history but only a summary of the last three actions: the tally (three–nil, two–one, one–two, nil–three), not the order, and nothing whatever about what anyone earlier did. Take p = 0.6 if a number helps.

(a) Does a cascade in the substantive sense still occur — a point after which actions carry no information about the private signals behind them — and can the process be characterised cleanly? I have got as far as noticing that the tally behaves like a Markov chain on four states, two of them absorbing, which would make lock-in almost sure but for a reason that appears to have nothing to do with martingales.

(b) Is there still such a thing as *the* public belief here? Each agent's evidence is a window, and successive windows are not nested, so there is no filtration and, as far as I can tell, no single object that plays the role the public belief plays in the full-history model. If that is right, say what replaces it and whether anything resembling the martingale argument survives.

(c) I suspect the naive calculation — treat each of the last three actions as one signal's worth of evidence and add up log-likelihood ratios — is illegitimate, since whether an observed action carries a signal at all depends on what that agent saw, which is precisely what I do not observe. How much does this break the naive picture, and does it change the answer to (a)?

Third, a taxonomy check, because I think the word is doing me no favours. I want to separate three things my notes keep collapsing: an informational cascade proper; a case in which agents' payoffs depend directly on how many others choose the same way, so that matching is rational without anyone learning anything; and a case in which agents match because their competence is judged by comparison with others, so that deviating is costly even when their private evidence is strong. Which of these is the lock-in in my windowed variant? And is the third really a distinct mechanism, or only the first with an extra term in the payoff?

Please answer in the order given, and tell me where any of my questions rests on a confusion — in particular whether the Markov-chain observation in 2(a) is a genuine alternative to the martingale picture or merely a special case of it wearing different clothes.
