I run onboarding for new engineers joining our model-monitoring team, and I've got 45 minutes next week. What I need them to walk out able to do is read a drift report and say something better than "the numbers moved." The concept is mechanistic anomaly detection: what were hunting isn't a weird input or a bad output, it's a change in which internal machinery is producing a behavior we thought we'd already characterized. The part they always get stuck on, and where I think the session lives or dies, is the identity question: when is the mechanism running now the same mechanism we wrote up six months ago?

So plan the session for me rather than giving me the talk. What has to come first and what depends on what -- I suspect I can't even get them to state "the mechanism changed" until they have an intervention-based notion of a mechanism rather than a circuit-diagram notion, but I'm not sure that's where to start. One example to carry the whole thing: I've been using a loan classifier that reviewers traced to a salary-and-zipcode feature direction, which after a data refresh returns the same approvals with that direction gone and something functionally similar rebuilt out of two unrelated attention heads -- tell me if that's the wrong example, and what a carrying example here has to have and to lack. The misconceptions in the order they'll actually form, with the point in the sequence where each gets headed off: I'm guessing "anomaly just means rare input," "same outputs and same accuracy means same mechanism," and "the mechanism is whatever's in the diagram." And one question at the end that tells me they've got the identity question rather than just the vocabulary.

Two constraints. I've decided to define identity pragmatically, by whether the safety case we already wrote still covers the new arrangement -- call that coverage identity -- and I want the plan built around it rather than around path-identity versus counterfactual-profile identity. And tell me what I should openly refuse to explain at this level.
