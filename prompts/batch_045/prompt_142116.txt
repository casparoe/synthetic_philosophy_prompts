I am one of five trustees of a small endowed charitable trust — about £180,000 of spendable income a year, distributed twice annually — whose deed instructs us to apply that income where it will do the most good that can reasonably be ascertained. I spent rather more than thirty years as an experimental physicist before retiring, and have taken to reading philosophy seriously only in the last few years, so I should be grateful for a plain argument rather than a display of erudition: we have no library access and no budget for external review, so nothing in the memo can rest on our being able to look anything up.

At our May meeting we must decide on three applications for the same £60,000. One is a hospice-at-home service, for whom the money is two years of a specialist nurse: modest, certain, easily ascertained. The other two are long-shot research groups working on poorly specified threats to the very long-term future of civilisation. Each puts its case purely in expected-value terms — a probability somewhere between one in ten thousand and one in a million of being the decisive difference in an outcome affecting tens of millions of people, against which the hospice bid is negligible. One of my fellow trustees, a retired mathematician, says this is just Pascal's mugging dressed as a grant application, and that any probability we cannot test is a number someone has made up to fit the conclusion.

The more interesting of the two groups has pre-empted him. It argues that we are wrong to scrutinise its particular number at all: we will face an indefinite stream of such applications, so the object of assessment should be our policy, not our case. It asks us to commit a fixed tenth of annual income to a high-variance strand, allocated by standing rule rather than by case-by-case judgements of credibility, on the ground that at the level of a repeated policy the small probabilities are far less likely to be sheer invention, and the aggregate case survives even if no individual application does. The trustee who dislikes this points out that we already buy insurance and once funded a pandemic preparedness project on tail-risk reasoning, so a flat refusal would be inconsistent.

Please draft the two-page decision memorandum I will circulate to the board before the meeting. It should reach a definite recommendation — decline both, fund one as requested, or adopt some capped version of the standing-rule proposal — with a time frame or review point, and it should give the strongest version of the argument it is rejecting rather than dismissing it. In particular I would like your view on whether the shift from case to policy genuinely answers the fanaticism worry or merely relocates it, and on whether the reasonably ascertained wording in our deed does any real work here or is simply a way of begging the question.
