I am an undergraduate taking a course on personal identity, and we have just finished a unit on reductionist views of the person. A popular book I picked up in the library introduces Buddhist no-self like this:

The Buddha's teaching of anatta says that there is no self. What we call a person is only a temporary bundle of the five aggregates — body, feeling, perception, volitional formations and consciousness — which causation holds together for a lifetime and which disperses at death. This is more or less the bundle theory that Hume later arrived at independently: the self is nothing over and above its experiences, so there is no deep further fact about whether you are the same person from one year to the next, and the classic puzzles about personal identity dissolve. The ethical upshot is that you have no special reason to care about your own future, since there is no one who persists to be benefited or harmed; Buddhist ethics accordingly replaces self-interest with equal concern for all sentient beings. The same idea makes rebirth tractable: with no self to be reborn, what carries over is only the causal momentum of karma, which is no more mysterious than the motion of one billiard ball passing into another.

The final two sentences are the ones I distrust — they seem to extract more from the view than it can deliver, and I cannot see why a reductionist about persons has to become impartial — but I cannot tell where the distortion starts. A friend who took this course last year muttered something about "not-self" being different from "no self", which I did not follow. Could you audit the passage sentence by sentence: what a specialist in Buddhist philosophy would accept, what is a fair popular simplification, what is an actual misrepresentation, and whether the ethical and rebirth conclusions still follow once the misrepresentations are corrected? Please answer in British English.
