Hi — I'm on the safety and capital planning committee of our regional transit authority (buses, one light rail line, and a pile of level crossings we inherited from an old freight railroad). We have to allocate a one-time 4.2 million dollar safety grant and we've somehow turned it into a philosophy seminar, so here I am.

Option A is rebuilding the Elm Street level crossing: three deaths there in twenty years, the engineers say roughly one more per decade if we leave it alone, and the rebuild basically eliminates the risk. Option B is about 200 small pedestrian retrofits scattered across the network (refuge islands, tightened turn radii, lower speed limits on the approaches) — the model says 2 to 3 statistical lives in expectation, but delivered as tiny risk reductions to a couple hundred thousand people, almost none of whom will ever notice anything changed, and where maybe two thirds of that benefit never cashes out as an actual avoided death anyone could point to. Our standing rule is cost per statistical life saved with a ceiling, and it picks B without breaking a sweat.

What wrecked the last meeting is a new member — retired philosophy teacher, and honestly insufferable about it — who said this is exactly the move Scanlon-style contractualism is supposed to block: our rule answers the complaint of whoever dies at Elm Street by pointing at the sum of a hundred thousand people's trivial risk reductions, and the principle that licenses summing them is precisely the sort of thing that person could reasonably reject, so we're not allowed to do it. Somebody shot back that B also burdens nobody above any threshold and benefits nobody above any threshold, so on her own logic B is indistinguishable from doing nothing and we may as well resurface a parking lot. A third member says the whole worry is a framing artifact: nobody is identified in advance, everyone is facing a probability, and the objector is just moved by the fact that you can point at Elm Street on a map, which isn't a moral difference. I floated a compromise nobody much liked — treat the diffuse statistical benefit as a tie-breaker between options that are each already justifiable to every affected individual, rather than as a quantity you can add up and weigh against one person's death. Meanwhile our counsel has cheerfully reminded us that whatever we write down will be discoverable after the next fatality, so reasonable-people-disagree is not an option, and whatever rule we pick will be the precedent for next year's lighting and ventilation budgets, so it has to generalize without exploding and be usable by a planner with a spreadsheet and no philosopher in the room.

So please draft the decision memo I'll circulate before the 14th: recommend A, B, or a specific split, with a time frame and a one-paragraph rule stated plainly enough to go in the minutes. Along the way: (1) put the contractualist objection at its strongest — better than our member did — and then say why it still loses, or why it doesn't; (2) say whether the ex ante / ex post distinction really does the work the third member thinks it does, or whether it just relocates the problem; (3) tell me whether a threshold or lexical-priority rule stays stable when you break the choice into a sequence of small pairwise decisions, or whether it collapses into paralysis (nothing beats nothing when every benefit is tiny) or into something worse; (4) handle the reverse direction — the retrofits each generate their own little complaints about noise, delay and lost parking, and if small things can't be summed against a death I want to know whether they can be summed at all, and which way the summing is allowed to run; (5) say what the rule lets us actually say out loud at a public meeting, both to a bereaved family and to the elderly pedestrians who don't know they're beneficiaries; and (6) tell me whether my tie-breaker idea is coherent or just aggregation smuggled in under a nicer name. If you think contractualism is the wrong frame for an authority like ours, say that in two sentences and then defend the recommendation in whatever frame you prefer — but the memo still has to land a decision, and please skip footnotes and citations entirely, nobody on this committee is going to read them.
