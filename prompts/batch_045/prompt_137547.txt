I run a monthly in-house seminar for about 16 engineers at the company I work at (75 minutes, mixed seniority). I have no philosophy background — I work on ranking and recommender systems, and the session grew out of an argument about whether the click data we collect actually tells us what our users want. Next month's topic is revealed preference: the idea that an agent's preferences, and the "utility" that represents them, are nothing over and above the pattern of their actual choices, so that any further claim about what someone really wants is either empty or a separate empirical hypothesis.

Design a session in which the participants generate real choice data for each other and then have to reconstruct each other's preferences from it, so that they walk into the difficulties rather than hearing them from me. Three things I want them to hit themselves:

1. How little a finite batch of choices pins down: several different preference orderings fit the same data, and among the utility functions that fit, some say one option is only marginally better than another while others say it is vastly better — the ordering survives, the intensity does not, and they will need the intensity for any decision that involves trade-offs or risk.
2. That the analyst has to stipulate what counts as "the same option" appearing in two different menus, and that this stipulation is doing work that the data is not doing.
3. A case where the choices are perfectly consistent with a stable ordering and we would still resist saying they reveal what the chooser wants — my candidate is a chooser who is following a rule that happens to generate a consistent-looking pattern without consulting their own wants at all (copying a specific other person, always taking the option nearest the door, deferring to whoever chose last time). If you have a better version of this case, use it.

Give me: the setup and materials; the choice tasks, using options that are cheap, immediate and real to them; the procedure and a minute-by-minute timeline; the debrief questions that map each moment onto the point it is supposed to make; and an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this. I want the debrief to end somewhere sharp rather than agreeable — force the group to say whether the utility function they reconstructed is a discovery about the person or a compressed re-encoding of data they already had, and what would count as evidence for the former.

Close with a short note on your own proposal's commitments: where this design begs the question by presupposing one answer to what "utility" is for, which version of the view it makes look stronger than it is, and what would change your mind — a consideration about what revealed preference theory is actually claiming, or an observable sign in the room — such that you would tell me to scrap this plan and run something else instead.
