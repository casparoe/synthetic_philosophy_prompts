My housemate is a plasma physicist and ever since he got me reading Hofstadter's old careenium paper — the one with a table full of magnetized "symms" careening around, walls whose bounciness tracks the world outside, and the clumps they keep forming, the "symmballs" (I've also seen it spelled "simmballs") — we've been having the same argument in a different costume every week: in some system where you can tell one story at the symbol level and another at the substrate level, did the higher-level stuff actually shove the substrate around, or is the higher-level story just a convenient way of keeping track of the substrate shoving itself around? He's been annoyingly consistent, so I started writing his verdicts down. So far: the cue simmball in a game's physics engine shoved the eight simmball into the pocket — yes ("the integrator reads positions and velocities every frame, the ball level is where the dynamics actually lives"); a chess engine's king-safety term made it play h3 rather than g4 — yes ("the search consults that number, change it and you change the move, on whatever box you run it on"); the index falling 8% is what made a pension fund dump equities — no ("the index is a summary of what the traders did, no mechanism anywhere reads the index, the fund was reacting to prices"); in a mis-compiled climate model where an off-by-one in the array indices means the weather-level animation shows a low-pressure system driving a front but what actually propagated was garbage — no ("the weather story isn't tracking anything in the machine, the indexing bug is what shoved the silicon around"); a pattern we gerrymandered after the fact, defining "the danger zone" as wherever the cue ball happened to be and then saying the danger zone caused the scatter — no ("a label stapled on afterwards, nothing cared about it, and it doesn't hold for any other break"); a tide table predicting the harbor flood — no ("the moon did it, the table is a picture of what was going to happen"); a warehouse robot fetching a box because the stock count for that SKU hit zero — yes ("the controller reads the count and acts on it, the count isn't a summary of the robot's arm"). I probbably mangled a couple of those, but you get the shape of it.

Here's the case I actually care about, and it's the first real careenium case, which is maybe why I can't predict him. Plain Hofstadter setup: no program, no interpreter, no controller, nothing in the chamber that computes or represents anything except, arguably, the clumps themselves. In the run we filmed, symmball S — a big dense clump — drifted into symmball T and T came apart. Then two things. (a) We replayed the chamber 300 times from perturbed initial conditions, all with ordinary ambient churn: whenever a clump with roughly S's coarse shape and momentum arrived, T broke, within a few percent of what the coarse-grained rule predicts, across a wide range of detailed symm arrangements and magnetisms; whenever no such clump formed, T survived. (b) In the filmed run specifically, the churn was unusually violent, and when we re-ran it forward from just before contact with S's clump dissolved into free symms (same symms, same total momentum, just not clumped), T came apart at essentially the same time anyway. So: in that run S's arrival made no difference to what happened to T, even though T's fate tracks the S-level pattern robustly across realizations.

What do you think he'll say — did S shove T apart in the run we filmed? Does either of those actually separate the rules you've identified, or is there a better case to try?
