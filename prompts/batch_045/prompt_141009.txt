I'm a journalist — science and ideas beat — and I'm working on a piece about whether secular societies can actually hold together without religion, the trust question basically. Last month I was at a panel on secularization in Northern Europe where one speaker closed with "there are no atheists in foxholes" and another said secular societies are running on "borrowed religious capital," and the two lines got welded together into the claim that the Nordic high-trust / low-church equilibrium is a fair-weather arrangement: turn up the existential pressure and the trust goes with it. Nobody stopped to say what the foxhole line was even being used to assert, and I don't want to print a piece that inherits that ambiguity. A reader also recieved a letter-to-the-editor from a guy who says the whole thing is settled by the mortality-salience priming literature, which I suspect is not quite right either, but I can't say why.

So what I'd like is a menu, not a verdict. Lay out the distinct precise theses "there are no atheists in foxholes" could be making, from the near-trivial to the strong: e.g. that self-reported belief or prayer goes up under acute threat; that threat strips away pretence and reveals what people occassionally "really" believed all along; that people *need* religion to cope when things get bad; that religion is doing some indispensable cohesion work and secular trust is what fails under stress; or that real belief just *is* whatever you fall back on under pressure. For each one say whether it's trivial-but-true, plausibly true, genuinely contested, or just false, and — this is the part I care about — which reading would actually have to be true for it to support the "borrowed capital / fair-weather trust" conclusion, and which readings are compatible with secular trust being perfectly robust. I'd also like you to separate out the prior question of what "religion makes people more moral" would even have to mean here: more prosocial toward co-believers or toward strangers, in anonymous or only in observed settings, in averages or in the tails, in behavior or in self-report. Two distinctions I've been using to keep myself honest, feel free to improve on them: *crisis piety* (a blip in professed belief or religious practice under threat) versus *crisis morality* (a change in how people actually treat each other), and *thick trust* (of people who share your commitments) versus *thin trust* (of strangers and institutions). Does the slogan, on any of its readings, give us a reason to expect *thin* trust specifically to drop in a secular society under stress, or does the evidence people cite mostly concern thick trust and self-report?

Format it as a list of readings with a one-line verdict on each, since my editor wants a sidebar and I'd rather butcher it less. Please don't give me citations or a reading list — I'll chase those down myself. What I do need you to do is flag, reading by reading, what's settled among people who actually study this versus what's contested versus where you're extrapolating past what anyone really knows; if a reading is a live dispute, tell me it's a live dispute rather than smoothing it into the consensus. And at the end, tell me what single piece of evidence would most change your verdict on the strongest reading you identified.
