I've been messing around with Avicenna's flying man argument and I think I found an angle on it that isnt just a retread, but I definately can't tell whether it's new or whether I'm rediscovering something everyone already notes in passing. Here are my rough notes / the first half of the draft.

The setup. Avicenna asks us to imagine a person brought into existence all at once, adult and rational, suspended in empty space with no sensation reaching him — no sight, no touch, limbs held apart so they never meet — and with no earlier experience to remember. Even so, he would be aware of himself. He would affirm that he is, while affirming nothing about hands or feet or a heart. The usual reading says this establishes two things: that self-awareness is primitive rather than acquired through the senses, and that what we are is not the body, since the self gets grasped without the body getting grasped.

My angle: call it the thinness trade-off. The argument earns its certainty by stripping the self of everything — sensation, memory, content — and what is left over to be certain of is a bare index. But the payoff people actually want from it is personal survival of death: the thought that I, with my memories and character, go on. Those two pull in opposite directions. The more the flying man secures, the less content the surviving thing has; the moment you give the surviving thing enough content to be me, you have left the flying man behind and are leaning on the rest of Avicennas metaphysics of the soul instead. So the argument is either certain and thin, or thick and unargued. I want to claim that this dilemma is the right frame for the whole discussion, and that most readings are attempts to dodge it.

Three stress tests.

1. Duplication. Suppose the flying man is created twice over: two subjects, intrinsically identical in every respect, in separate empty regions. Each affirms that he is. Whatever makes such an affirmation certain does not distinguish them. But then the argument is not delivering what first-personal certainty is supposed to deliver — an answer to whose self this is. It certifies that there is first-personal awareness of a certain quality, and certifies it for whoever has it, but it does not single out a subject. And that matters, because Avicenna needs souls to be many. If self-awareness is all the argument gives you, and it is the same in each twin, then it supplies no principle of individuation for the surviving selves, and it's not obvious where else in the thought experiment one would come from.

2. Gaps. Create the flying man for an instant, annihilate him, create an intrinsic duplicate ten minutes later. Each affirms that he is, at each moment at which he exists. Nothing in the awareness tells either one whether he is the same subject across the gap. So what the argument certifies is existence at a time, in the moment of awareness; it is silent about persistence. But the immortality conclusion is diachronic, so it needs the further claim that whatever is independent of matter is incorruptible. On its own, the flying man is a synchronic result.

3. Confabulation. Now build the flying man with a full set of false memories wired in at creation — a childhood, a name, a body he seems to remember — and stimulate him so that he seems to feel limbs that aren't there. He still affirms that he is. I take this to show that what survives the strip-down is not the self as opposed to the body, but the self as opposed to any attributed content at all. The properties I would normally treat as essential to me (that I am the person who did such and such, that I have this body) arrive through the very same channel as the confabulated ones, so the flying man gives me no reason to think they are really mine. What's left is the bare index, and a bare index is not obviously someone whose survival I have any stake in. A related version: assemble him gradually, limb by limb, while he floats insensate. Whenever the awareness first switches on, that only shows the body is causally needed to switch it on, not that the body is what the awareness is of — which is the distinction the defender will reach for, and which I think quietly concedes my point.

What I think survives, constructively: the flying man is best read as an isolation test showing that self-awareness has no object other than the self — it is not perception or representation of anything bodily — and that it is not learned. That is a claim about content-independence, not about substances. Compare Descartes: the cogito needs a thought with content to do its work, whereas Avicennas argument needs no content at all, which is exactly why it is harder to doubt and easier to empty out.
