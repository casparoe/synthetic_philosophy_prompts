I'm a condensed matter postdoc — I spend my days blocking degrees of freedom, integrating out fast modes, and staring at flow diagrams — and journal club last week derailed into an argument about emergence that I have not been able to let go of. I've been reading around the edges of the philosophy literature since (badly: some encyclopedia tabs, one popular book I only half trusted), and I genuinely can't tell which parts are a real disagreement and which parts are people using one word for six different things.

Here's my thumbnail of where the philosophy side is, and I'd like you to correct it where it's a caricature. As far as I can tell there are a few distinct things on the table: (i) the older derivational picture, where you reduce one theory to another by deriving the higher-level laws from the lower-level ones plus bridge principles that identify the higher-level kinds with lower-level ones; (ii) the multiple realizability worry — the same macro kind gets realized by an indefinite and heterogeneous pile of micro states, so those bridge principles can't be neat identities; (iii) the claim that even granting in-principle derivability, higher-level explanations do work the lower-level ones can't, so the special sciences aren't just convenient shorthand (and somewhere near here there's an argument, Kim's I think, that higher-level causes get squeezed out by their lower-level realizers); and (iv) the one that actually sounds like my job — the case where the interesting behavior appears only in a limit, the limit is singular, and nothing at finite system size looks remotely like it, so the derivational story supposedly breaks down where it matters most.

What I'd like is a translation. Take each of those four (fixing them first where I've mangled them) and restate them as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually think in: real-space and momentum-shell coarse graining, relevant versus irrelevant versus marginal operators, running couplings and matching at a scale, universality classes and critical exponents, error estimates with an explicit cutoff, singular limits, the thermodynamic limit, treating a cutoff effective theory as if it were fundamental. Please don't quietly smuggle in the working physicist's assumption that "derivable in principle, plus controlled error bars" is the end of the story — that's the very thing Marco (my colleague, an excellent physicist and an extremely impatient philosopher) says settles it, and I want the translation to be fair to the philosophers even if he's right. Then the part I actually care about, the residue report: what translates cleanly, what survives only with distortion, and what has no expression in my vocabulary at all? In particular, stress-test all of it on the case I keep coming back to, which I've started calling an orphan limit: suppose the only derivation anyone has of some robust macro law — a non-analyticity, or a scaling law whose exponents we can measure to six digits — goes by taking a continuum or thermodynamic limit of a family of cutoff theories that we don't actually have a UV definition of, so there is no single micro theory from which the macro law has been derived, only a limit of a family that may not exist as a theory at all. Does the philosophers' in-principle derivability condition have anything to say about that, or does it just go quiet? Same question for their distinction between the epistemic version of emergence (we can't compute it) and the ontological one (it isn't nothing over and above) — is that a distinction my vocabulary can even draw, and if it can't, is that a blind spot in how I talk or a sign the distinction was never sharp?
