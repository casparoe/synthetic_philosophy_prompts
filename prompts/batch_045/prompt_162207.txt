I've been reading LessWrong and its successor forums for something like fifteen years, and the thing I've never managed to get straight is underdetermination. Not the slogan — I grant that evidence never entails a unique theory — but which pairs of theories are supposed to count as a problem. I have fairly stable gut verdicts on individual cases, they don't obviously add up, and I'd like you to try to state the principle I'm implicitly using, or to tell me which of the verdicts has to go.

Two points of setup, so the answer doesn't get spent on things I already accept. Assume the competing theories are stated precisely, that they entail the same observation sentences, and that they are compared with all auxiliary hypotheses held fixed: I'm setting aside the familiar holism point that any theory can be protected by adjusting an auxiliary. And I'm reserving "underdetermination" for the relation between theories and evidence; what to believe next, or which research programme to fund, is downstream of that and I'd rather keep it separate.

The cases.

(1) Two formulations of the same thermal physics: one takes temperature as primitive and states the second law in terms of increasing entropy; the other takes coldness, defined as one over temperature, as primitive and restates every law accordingly, with an explicit dictionary translating each sentence of one into a sentence of the other. My verdict: these are not two theories. There is no underdetermination here, and not because the evidence happens to settle it.

(2) Two particle theories that fit every measurement to date and diverge only at center-of-mass energies above about 10 TeV, which the next generation of colliders will reach within twenty years. Verdict: not underdetermination in any sense that should worry anybody; this is not-having-the-data-yet. If someone offers it as an instance of the thesis, I think they've changed the subject.

(3) The classic grue-style pair. Every examined emerald has been green; one hypothesis says the next one is green, the other says it is green if examined before 2050 and blue afterwards. Verdict: I'm not worried, and more than that — I don't think the second hypothesis is a real rival. Not because it is disconfirmed. Because a predicate with that temporal structure isn't, I want to say, the kind of thing a law can cite, or the kind of thing the world can be one way or the other about.

(4) The apparent-age hypothesis: the world came into existence five minutes ago, complete with our memories, the fossils, and the photons in flight. Verdict: unlike (3), I'm happy to call this a genuine rival — consistent with all the evidence, not a mere redescription — and I reject it flatly. But what rejects it isn't evidence; it's something like prior improbability, or its being a wretched explanation. So: a real rival that loses, as opposed to not-a-rival-at-all.

(5) Empirical equivalence by duality. Two theories, provably identical in every empirical consequence forever, which disagree about the basic furniture: one describes a four-dimensional spacetime with gravity and no lower-dimensional fields, the other a three-dimensional spacetime with no gravity and a certain family of fields, with an exact dictionary for the observables and none for the ontology. Verdict: this is real underdetermination, and it is the case that actually bothers me. There looks to be a fact about what the world is made of, and evidence will never reach it.

(6) Divergence about regions we can never observe, not because of any duality but because no signal can ever reach us: the two theories agree about everything inside our observable patch and disagree about the distribution of matter beyond the cosmological horizon. Verdict: I want to say real underdetermination here too — and I notice that I immediately add that it doesn't matter, which I cannot square with (5), where the inaccessible claim is equally inaccessible and I very much do care. Something is distinguishing caring about ontology from caring about remote matters of fact, and I don't know that it's a distinction I can defend under pressure.

(7) The incommunicable case, which is the one I go back and forth on. Theory A says that an observer sealed inside a perfectly isolated box, who performs a certain measurement and is then destroyed together with the box and every record in it, gets outcome X; Theory B says she gets outcome Y. The theories agree on everything that will ever appear in any record anyone can share. Is there a fact of the matter here that we are merely cut off from, as in (6)? Or is a difference that can never surface in any communicable record no difference at all, as in (1)? My gut flips between those two readings, and it bothers me that I can't tell whether the operative notion is observable-by-someone or available-as-evidence-to-us.

(8) The simpler rival. Same empirical consequences as our best current theory, for all time, but strictly simpler by every measure I trust: fewer free parameters, shorter axioms, less historical accretion. Verdict: I feel real pull toward switching to it, and I simultaneously feel that switching would be crazy, which makes me suspect that my simplicity tiebreak is doing something quite different from what it was doing in (4).

(9) The bad-lot case, which I include because I suspect it doesn't belong in this box at all. Two theories on the table both fit, and my actual state is: I expect the truth to be some third theory nobody has proposed yet. Verdict: this is the situation I'm in most of the time, and it seems to me a different problem — about the hypothesis space rather than about the evidence. Tell me if I'm wrong to separate them.

Here is my tentative principle, which I don't think survives my own verdicts. Two theories are genuinely underdetermined relative to evidence E if and only if: (a) E does not discriminate them; (b) no possible extension of E would either, so the equivalence is permanent rather than an artifact of what we happen to have measured so far; and (c) they are not mere redescriptions of one another. Where they are thus underdetermined, evidence has nothing further to say and the only admissible tiebreakers are prior-theoretic: simplicity, and fit with theories that have independent support in neighbouring domains.

That handles (1), (2) and (5), I think. But it counts (3) as a genuine rival, which I deny; it makes (4) and (8) symmetric, which they don't feel; and it has no vocabulary for the it-doesn't-matter in (6). So it needs a further clause about which predicates may figure in laws, or a clause about what we're entitled to care about, or it needs to overturn one of my verdicts.

What I want from you: one principle, or at most two or three candidates, stated precisely enough that I can apply it to new cases myself, with each clause tagged to the case that forces it. Then verdicts on cases I haven't listed — including (7), and at least two cases of your own construction chosen so that they discriminate between your candidates rather than merely illustrating them. Then an honest account of which of my verdicts your best candidate cannot accommodate, and what you'd say to me if I dug in and refused to give them up.

One last thing, since from where I sit I can't tell which parts of this are load-bearing: as you go, please mark which of your claims are uncontroversial among people who actually work on this and which are live disputes — whether permanent empirical equivalence ever really arises in a mature science, whether the verbal-versus-factual distinction can be drawn at all, whether simplicity is evidence or a pragmatic tiebreak, whether dual theories describe different worlds or the same world twice. If one of your candidates rests on a contested claim, say so explicitly instead of presenting it as where the field has landed; and if my framing above smuggles in a contested assumption of its own, I'd rather be told than humoured.
