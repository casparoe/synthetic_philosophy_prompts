For a graduate seminar on the epistemology of induction I am writing the opening session, and I want the adequacy conditions fixed before anyone reads a theory. Take as background what the no-free-lunch results give us: averaged over a suitably unrestricted class of environments, no predictive or search method does better than any other, so nothing can be said for a method except relative to a restriction on the class of problems it faces. What I want is a specification for a theory of inductive bias: what it must do to tell a bounded agent which bias to adopt and what that agent may reasonably hope for from it, where the purpose is choosing and vindicating a bias rather than certifying any single prediction.

Three distinctions I want the specification to respect. First, three grades of success: beating a named rival on a named distribution; dominating every rival across a class of distributions; remaining unexploitable in the worst case over that class. These are not to be run together. Second, the gap between showing that a bias is well suited to a class of environments and showing that the world we are actually in belongs to that class; I call this the placement problem, and I want it located rather than dissolved. Third, the difference between an environment indifferent to your bias, one that merely fails to accommodate it, and one chosen by another agent who can inspect your bias and select against it.

Give me exactly five numbered desiderata, in British English, each in one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. For each: the requirement, the case that makes it a requirement, whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp, and a flag where it cannot be jointly satisfied with another item on the list. Include, if they belong, requirements about what the theory owes an agent who must fix a bias in advance, how it treats a second-order rule for switching between biases on the basis of their track record, whether it may help itself to induction when recommending induction, and what it says about the encoding-relativity of simplicity. Mark with an asterisk the requirement that does most of the work in excluding candidates. Omit any scoring of existing theories.
