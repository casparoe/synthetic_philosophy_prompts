I need help writing a eulogy and I'm kind of stuck on the structure of it, not the words. My great-aunt Dee died two weeks ago at 88 and I'm speaking on Saturday morning in a union hall — about ninety people: her old textile-mill local, the handful of remaining members of the third-party branch she helped run for thirty years, a lot of family, her three great-nieces (22 to 24) who think voting is basically astrology for liberals, and my cousin Ray, who's the county party chair and will visibly grind his teeth if I say anything that sounds like "your vote doesn't change anything." Dee voted in every single election from 1956 to last November — school board primaries, bond measures, everything — and she almost never voted for a winner: Socialist for years, then Green, then whatever was on the third line, and for the last decade she lived in a district so safe that the result was knowable in advance. She wasn't naive about this. Her line when people told her she was throwing her vote away was that a ballot isn't a lever, it's a syllable — you don't judge a word by whether it moved the building. As far as I can reconstruct her actual view: any single vote is worthless in expectation and that's the uninteresting fact; the interesting fact is that a vote only means anything as part of a record a person builds across a lifetime and leaves behind for other people to read. What you're really casting is a sentence, and the thing to be assessed is the whole run of them, not one. (This is also why she never skipped off-year primaries — she said a paragraph with holes in it is harder to read.)

The problem is her last vote. Sick, a month before she died, she voted for the mainstream candidate — the exact kind of candidate she'd spent sixty years refusing. Her old comrades are quietly hurt and a couple of them are calling it a deathbed conversion; Ray is telling people it proves she "came around"; and I will not say either of those things from the front of the room, but I also can't just skip it, because everyone there knows. So I want the argument about voting to be load-bearing in the speech rather than decorative: the difference between voting as an attempt to make an outcome better, voting as an expression of allegiance or testimony, and voting as a duty that can require swallowing the lesser evil should be what decides what I can honestly claim about her and what I have to shut up about. On her own syllable view, is there a way to make that last ballot continuous with the other sixty years rather than a break — does a final vote change the tense of the sentence rather than its content, or something like that? Or is the honest thing to admit it was a break, and then say why a reversal at the end of a sentence isn't the same thing as a recantation? I don't want her eulogized as a saint of purity (on her view the point was never purity, it was legibility — she wanted the record to say a particular readable thing) and I definitely don't want her eulogized as someone who wasted sixty years or who folded at the finish.

Practical notes: five or six minutes, spoken, and the philosophy has to be in the shape of the thing and not in the vocabulary — nobody in that room wants to hear "instrumental rationality" or "expected utility," and I'd rather you didn't name any theorist even if one is obviously relevant. No "she's still organizing somewhere," no afterlife talk at all: she was a hard atheist and would've been annoyed. Don't smooth over the fight in the family, but don't pick one either. Oh, and Ray is going to speak after me for two minutes, so ideally the speech shouldn't leave him a clean setup to say she came around. If it's useful, end your reply with a couple of lines on what you deliberately left out and why — that'll help me decide whether to keep or cut things. I've never wrote a eulogy before and I have alot of notes but none of them are in an order that works.
