I'm on the job market this year with a metaethics paper, and in two weeks I'm giving the job talk at a department whose search is advertised as "metaethics and moral psychology." The talk is "Debunking Within Jurisdiction," and the thesis is deliberately scoped rather than global. My claim is that the standard evolutionary debunking argument in its dilemma form — either the moral facts are independent of our evolved responses and the correlation between them is a lucky coincidence, or they aren't independent and they aren't the facts anyone wanted — at most establishes what I call a *jurisdictional* defeater: moral judgments whose content lies inside what I call the ancestral domain of the moral faculty survive the argument untouched, while judgments whose content lies outside that domain are left unsupported, because there is no story to tell about why selection would have tracked the truth of *those* judgments. By the ancestral domain I mean the range of social situations in which the faculty was shaped: small-band cooperation, direct reciprocity, care of dependents, and third-party responses to cheaters who can be identified and sanctioned. To decide what falls outside it I use a diagnostic I call the displacement test: a judgment is out-of-domain when the situation it concerns could not have arisen in any environment where the faculty was being shaped — institutions involving millions of people no one will ever meet, effects dispersed across centuries, agents who cannot be sanctioned or even located. On that test, kinship partiality and local fairness are in; the moral weight of national borders, duties to distant strangers, obligations to future generations, and most of what goes on in ideal-theory institutional design are out, even though an out-of-domain judgment like "we owe far more to compatriots than to foreigners" feels like an obvious deliverance of conscience, because it is an extrapolation from an in-domain psychology built for kin bands. For the debunked region I offer a replacement procedure, calibrated extension: stop consulting intuition and instead chain a small set of surviving in-domain principles — say, something about suffering being pro tanto bad and something about reciprocity — to premises that are empirically checkable from outside morality altogether, so that the verdict inherits whatever credibility the in-domain principle and the empirical premise have, and the intuition is demoted from evidence to noise. The paper has three moves: the reconstruction of the debunking argument as jurisdiction-limited, the displacement test, and calibrated extension.

The room matters, because I keep getting different objections from different people and can't prepare for all of them at once. There are two naturalist moral realists who think the whole debunking literature is overblown and who will be irritated that I concede it any force at all; they will want to know why "no available explanation of the correlation" is supposed to be a defeater rather than a research gap, and they will suspect the ancestral-domain line is gerrymandered to reproduce my antecedent politics. There is one philosopher of biology who works on cultural evolution and one visiting cognitive scientist; I expect them to attack the empirical premise rather than the epistemology — on their view the moral faculty isn't a fixed Pleistocene adaptation with a tidy proper domain, cumulative culture generates novel moral capacities, and mismatch claims of the kind I'm making are close to unfalsifiable. There is a colleague whose own work is on evolutionary debunking of religious belief, and I don't know yet whether she'll press me on why religion doesn't get the same jurisdictional escape route or on whether my apparatus hands it one. The search chair is a political philosopher who will want to know what we're supposed to *do* with a debunked applied ethics in the meantime, given that climate policy and institutional design are exactly the out-of-domain cases. And there is one departmental generalist, of a broadly constructivist persuasion, who thinks the entire framing treats moral judgment as a truth-tracking faculty in the first place and will say so. The talk is fifty minutes with roughly twenty minutes of questions, and I'm told this department's Q&A runs long and repetitive, so ordering matters as much as content.

What I want from you is a prep sheet, not a defense of the view: the eight to ten objections I will actually get from this specific combination of people — not the ones I'd get from a general-interest philosophy audience — in roughly the order they'll arrive, each one phrased the way that person would phrase it, with the vocabulary and the example they would reach for, and each with a reply I could genuinely deliver in about ninety seconds plus one honest note on where that reply is weak or concedes more than I would like. Tell me which objections I should concede in the body of the talk rather than waiting to be asked, which one I should refuse to concede because conceding it collapses the project, and which single objection the paper most needs to survive if it's going to be publishable. There is also a self-application worry I would rather not draw attention to: the in-domain/out-of-domain distinction is itself a highly theoretical judgment about a cognitive faculty, reached very recently, so by my own criteria it looks out-of-domain and hence self-debunking. If you think that is the question I should actually be most afraid of, say so plainly, estimate the damage, and sketch the least-bad reply. Please write the whole thing in American English.
