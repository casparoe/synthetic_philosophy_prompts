I'm a PhD candidate in economics and I'm presenting my job-market paper next month at a public policy school's economic theory workshop. The room will be about twelve people, seminar format, thirty minutes plus a very long question period, and it breaks down roughly like this: two behavioral economists who run incentive-compatible lab elicitations and care about elicitation machinery above all; a development economist who does survey-experimental work on subjective well-being; a health economist who builds QALY-style measures by person trade-off; one social choice theorist of the old school; and a philosopher of economics who has written on welfarism and who is the person I most want to survive. Assume they have read nothing of mine in advance.

The thesis I'm defending is that interpersonal comparisons of well-being need not be assumed as a global, all-or-nothing premise — either as a metaphysical posit about a single mental magnitude, or as the equiprobability-style identification that does the work in Harsanyi-style aggregation — but can be discovered pairwise and locally, and can fail. Call the elicitation the swap test. In stage one I fix a reference good and a reference gamble over it and use each subject's certainty equivalent to estimate her risk curvature. In stage two I ask her to name the probability at which she is indifferent between remaining in her own circumstances for sure and a lottery that, with that probability, moves her into the other person's circumstances and otherwise leaves her where she is. Her stage-one curvature lets me convert that probability into a stated level-difference between the two positions. Two people are then mutually calibrated when, after that conversion, her implied difference between her position and his equals the negative of his implied difference between his position and hers, and I take the common value as the interpersonal comparison for that pair. Calibration is chained across overlapping pairs to build a comparison graph; where chains conflict I reconcile them by least squares over log-ratios and accept that what comes out is a partial rather than a complete social ordering. The pitch is that comparability becomes an empirical relation with a testable failure mode, so the framework can represent "these two genuinely cannot be compared" instead of forcing a ranking the way a full-comparability axiom does. My sketch of the argument is: (i) the familiar complaint that utility is defined only up to positive affine transformation, so that the choice of a unit is a moral stipulation, is correct within the single-person expected-utility setting but that setting is under-described — once you let people have preferences over lotteries in which they occupy someone else's position, the identification problem is overconstrained by cross-person data; (ii) so the symmetry assumption that has to be stipulated in the aggregation theorems is instead derived from a condition that can be checked; (iii) and because the condition can fail for a pair, the resulting ordering is partial, which handles incomparability that the standard framework can only handle by stipulation.

What I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet written for that specific room and not for my advisor. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get, in roughly the order they will come, each phrased the way the relevant person would put it: the behavioral economists in the vocabulary of incentive compatibility, reference dependence, status quo bias and whether a hypothetical swap can be made binding; the health economist asking how this differs from person trade-off and what it buys over the measures already in use; the social choice theorist in the vocabulary of Arrow-style conditions and, especially, what happens to the weak Pareto condition once the social ordering is incomplete; the philosopher asking whether I have quietly changed the subject from welfare to fairness. For each objection: how much damage it does if I handle it badly, a two-minute version of the reply I should give out loud, and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede in the first two slides rather than be forced into later — I suspect there is something about risk attitudes contaminating willingness-to-swap that I should give up immediately, but you should tell me if I'm wrong about which concession to make. I also want the objection I am hoping nobody asks. Two friends at a conference dinner gave me two candidates: a behavioral economist said the whole apparatus is just envy-freeness from the fair-division literature with extra steps, and the social choice theorist said it is Harsanyi with an elicitation detour bolted on. I want to know which of those has real force, which is a cheap shot I can dispatch in a sentence, and whether there is a worse one I haven't thought of — my advisor keeps muttering that I cannot separate a person's circumstances from her preferences, and I don't yet know how bad that one is. Finally: my advisor and I disagree about how much of this is settled, so in your notes please separate what people who actually work on welfare economics and social choice would grant without argument from what is genuinely contested in the literature, and where one of my replies depends on a contested reading — whether aggregation results of that kind are best read as arguments for utilitarianism or as reductios of their own assumptions, whether fairness criteria like no-envy are welfarist at all, whether interpersonal comparability is even the right name for what I'm measuring — say so explicitly rather than presenting it as the consensus.
