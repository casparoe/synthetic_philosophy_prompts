I convene a second-year undergraduate seminar on collective decision-making — philosophy and PPE students, about 24 of them, one 75-minute slot, a room with movable chairs and no screens, and a supply of index cards, two decks of playing cards, an opaque bag, and a whiteboard. The week's topic is the Condorcet jury theorem, and I want something they play rather than something I tell them. This is live for me outside the seminar too: I sit on a faculty hiring committee that decides by show of hands after an open discussion, and after two decisions this spring that went badly and, in retrospect, predictably, I started to suspect that our procedure manufactures exactly the failure mode the theorem is about. So: design a runnable activity — a game with rules, private information, real but tiny stakes (I can offer chocolate or a small token worth a fraction of a participation mark, but not cash), a tallying scheme simple enough to run fifteen or twenty times in the session, and a debrief.

The rough machinery I have in mind, which you should improve on or scrap if it will not actually work: a hidden state of the world (say which of two urns was used, or which of two candidates is genuinely better on some criterion I can reveal afterwards), each student drawing a private signal that correlates with the truth at an accuracy I can dial, then a secret simultaneous ballot, repeated across many trials so they are looking at frequencies and not at anecdotes. Then the variants, which are where I want the real content to live: a round where everyone first reads the same short expert memo — accurate maybe 70 percent of the time — so that everyone's error shares a common cause; a round where the first four ballots are read aloud before the rest of the group votes; a round with two kinds of participant, reliable and unreliable, where it is the unreliable ones who are privately instructed to speak first, speak forcefully, and project certainty, so that the group has to decide whether to weight by confidence; and crucially an inverted round in which the private signals are right only about 40 percent of the time, so that majority rule should get worse, not better, as the group gets bigger. I have a vague memory that when voters differ in reliability the optimal weighting is not proportional to raw accuracy but to something like the log-odds of accuracy, and I would like you to build that in if it is right and tell me plainly if I have misremembered. Hard constraints: no lecture up front, nobody hears the name of the theorem or the words independence and competence until after the last round, and the effect has to show up in the numbers on the whiteboard rather than in anything I say. What they should come away having felt in their gut is that the theorem's cheerful conclusion is carried entirely by two specific properties — each voter individually better than a coin flip, and errors statistically independent — and not by any general wisdom of crowds; that enlarging the group can actively hurt; and that several of the things a conscientious committee does in order to feel rigorous (consulting an expert, discussing the case, noticing how others are leaning) are the very things that break the assumption.

For the debrief I want a moment-by-moment mapping: which round is supposed to put pressure on which premise or clause, what the facilitator says at each point, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (small-sample noise swamping the effect, someone peeking at a neighbour's card, the inverted round leaking because a student works out that their signals are anti-correlated, the confident-unreliable participants enjoying the role too much to drop it afterwards), and how to handle each. Be frank about which features of the theorem the activity cannot reproduce and which I will simply have to supply in words — I assume the asymptotic character of the result is one of them, since twenty-odd students is not a large number, and I suspect the information-pooling benefits of real discussion are another, since my signal-drawing setup gives them nothing genuinely new to tell each other. Finally, and this matters as much as the rest: separate what is settled among people who know this material from what is genuinely contested. I take it the mathematics is not up for grabs once the assumptions are fixed, and I take it to be equally settled that the conclusion flips when individual accuracy drops below chance. But I would like to be told clearly which of my further inferences are controversial — whether deliberation is net positive or negative for truth-tracking, whether the theorem is best read as a defence of majority rule or as a warning about it, what one should do instead when errors are correlated — and I do not want a contested reading presented to me, or by me to them, as the consensus. If any part of the design you give me rests on a contested interpretation, say so and say what the competing interpretation would change about the activity.
