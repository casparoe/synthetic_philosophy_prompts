I've been lurking around the rationalist corner of the internet since the Sequences days and I now haunt the comments of every "can these models actually reason" thread, which is where this started: someone posted a set of Bongard problems — the ones from the old Soviet pattern-recognition book, six little figures on the left that share some property, six on the right that don't, guess the rule — and said solving them is the cleanest remaining test of genuine concept induction, because you get twelve examples and nothing else to go on. A friend of mine said that's just overfitting with extra steps: a system that labels the held-out figures right has found a discriminating feature, not induced a concept. I think she's half right and half wrong in an interesting way, and I want to spend a year on it, so I'm applying for a one-year research fellowship and need a proposal of about four pages.

Here's the half-formed idea I want turned into one. Start with a structural point about the problems themselves: in a good chunk of them the right-hand class has no positive description at all, it's just "not that" — a catch-all — whereas in the bilateral ones both sides instantiate their own rule and the two rules are systematically related (one is the negation, or the same thing with a feature swapped, or the same relation with the roles reversed). Those two kinds of problem seems to demand different things: the catch-all kind looks solvable by one-class modelling or novelty detection without ever representing the contrast, the bilateral kind doesn't. So my first move is to split any benchmark along that axis and check whether the ordering actually shows up (and whether training on bilateral problems transfers to catch-all ones and not the other way round). Second move: the rule is radically underdetermined by twelve examples — Goodman's new riddle, grue and all that — but nobody in these arguments talks about the thing that does the work in real induction, which is some ordering over the candidate rules that makes some of the consistent predicates projectile and the rest mere curve-fitting. My suspicion is that "induced the concept" is being used to mean three separable things — found a discriminating feature, got something that supports counterfactual and generative uses, and represented the space of alternative rules with its simplicity structure — and that current systems plausibly get the first, partly get the second, and don't get the third, and that the third is the one doing the philosophical work. If you think my bilateral/catch-all cut is the wrong axis, or that it's a rediscovery of something obvious, say so and propose a better one, but I don't want a literature review — I'll fill in citations myself.

What I need the proposal to actually contain: the question stated precisely enough that a year's work could come out either way; why the two positions in that comment thread (and the standard "few-shot concept learning" framing more generally) leave it open; the method month by month for twelve months, including what I'd build myself — I have a modest GPU budget and some ability to generate synthetic figures programmatically, but I am not training anything from scratch, so the design has to be probes and evaluations run against existing open models plus synthetic sets I construct, and I want you to be concrete about how I'd construct sets that control for the things that need controlling (the discriminating-feature confound, the verbalization confound, the possibility that a model just recites a rule it can't use); what the deliverable is for people who actually build and report these benchmarks, as opposed to another philosophy paper nobody reads; and an honest failure section — both the empirical failure (the probes dissociate, passing one doesn't predict passing another, no clean story) and the conceptual one (the three-way distinction collapses, or "concept" turns out to be doing no work here), with some indication of what pattern of results would push me to which conclusion. Also tell me what to cut: I have a bad habit of scoping these things like a five-year project and then recieving a polite rejection.
