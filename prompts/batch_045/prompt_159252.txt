Not sure this is the right place, but a mate from my club who did a philosophy minor won't shut up about it and I've run out of things to say back.

His argument, numbered the way he wrote it out for me:

(1) An action only has moral worth if the agent does it for the reasons that actually make it right — doing the right thing by accident, or for the wrong reason, isn't worth anything morally.

(2) If I believe factory farming is wrong purely because you told me so, and I stop buying factory-farmed meat because of that belief, then the reason I act is just "you said so" — and "you said so" is not the reason that makes factory farming wrong.

(3) So acting on moral testimony has no moral worth.

(4) Nearly everything any of us believes about right and wrong arrived that way — parents, teachers, the internet, whatever. Almost nobody sits down and derives an ethical system from scratch.

(5) So almost nobody's morally-motivated actions have any moral worth. He bites this bullet, annoyingly.

(6) And it gets worse. Deferring to someone with lived experience — a trans colleague telling you some workplace practice is humiliating — is still moral testimony. So by (3) acting on it has no moral worth either, and you'd do better working that out yourself. He bites this one too.

(7) Here's the bit I added, which he says changes nothing and which I think wrecks the whole thing. Suppose the testimony comes from a moral oracle: a language model trained on millions of human moral judgements, with no understanding of any of it, that just tells you "don't do X". If (3) is right, acting on the oracle is exactly as worthless as acting on my mate's say-so, because either way I skipped the reasoning myself. But they really don't feel the same, and I can't say why in a form he can't just absorb into his story.

I'd rather keep (1) if I can — it does seem right to me that doing the right thing for the wrong reason isn't the same achievement as doing it for the right one — and I'd also like to keep the verdict in (6) that deferring to lived experience is fine. I don't care about (4). He says I have to drop one of (1), (2) or (3).

Answer as exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no intro and no summing up at the end:

1. Which step is actually broken, and what the flaw is — I want the mechanism, not just "reject (2)".
2. Why that step looked airtight enough to fool both of us.
3. What it costs me to reject each of (1), (2) and (3) instead, cheapest to most expensive.
4. What my oracle case in (7) actually shows, and who it helps.
5. The one thing I should concede to him up front so the rest of my reply has a chance of landing.
