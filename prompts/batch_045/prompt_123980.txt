hey — I need the spec, not the theory. Short version: I'm writing the belief-update module for an agent that lives in a simulated environment. The simulator hands it numbers it calls chances (transition probabilities for the physics, plus a few calibrated forecasters for the stuff the physics doesnt cover), and the agent has to turn those into credences it can plug into an expected-utility calculation and act on in real time without getting money-pumped. So I need a rule in the "set your credence in A equal to what you take the chance of A to be" family — the Lewis-style defer-to-chance principle with its admissible-information clause — and what I actually want from you is the *requirements list*: what any rule of that sort has to deliver before I'd accept it. Adequacy here = good enough to ship in that module, not good enough to settle metaphysics.

Here's my half-baked pile of cases that I think any version has to handle. Turn them into requirements with the consideration behind each, tell me which two of them are secretly the same requirement wearing a hat, and tell me which one of my cases is just confused:

- the last toss: a freshly minted coing, chance of heads 0.5, tossed once, then vaporised, nobody ever sees how it landed. (so: single-case chances, not long-run frequencies)
- chance laundering: a box has chance 0.4 of emitting something; downstream a machine reads that and displays either "0.4" or "0.6" on a screen, choosing which by a fair flip. All you ever get is the screen. Does deference travel through a noisy channel? Do chances about chances compose, and in which direction?
- the self-erasing bias: at t1 the chance of heads is 0.5, but there's a chance 0.5 that a mechanism fires at t2 (before the toss) and resets the bias to 0.9. So the t1 chances assign positive probability to the t2 chances being different from themselves — the t1 function more or less undermines its own authority. Which time's chances do I defer to, and what's left of the rule if I can also *learn* the entire t1 function?
- the tipster's shadow: you know the chance of heads is 0.5, you don't know the outcome, but you know for certain that in five minutes someone else will know it and will bet against you. You personally have no inadmissible information — or do you?
- two weather services: two sources, both perfectly calibrated on the historical record, tell you 0.3 and 0.7 for the same event.
- the fission box: a machine will copy you, branch weights 0.9 and 0.1, and beforehand you're asked for your credence that you'll be the 0.9 one.
- the truck on the bridge: chance the bridge goes down is 0.001 if you walk, 0.1 if you drive the truck over it. Chance of what, relative to which intervention — and must the rule mesh with decision theory, or is that not its job?
- chance one but not certain: an unending sequence of independent 0.5 events, where the chance is 1 that infinitely many of them come out heads. Does the rule force credence 1?
- the demon: an agent with complete microphysical information about a world that still has perfectly good macro-level chances. Must it defer?

Then the part I care about most: mark which requirements are non-negotiable for my purpose and which are just taste of one camp. I'm suspicious of "chances have to be readable off observed frequencies", "chances must be causally inert", and "the rule ought to be derivable from a Dutch-book or accuracy-domination argument" — but tell me which of my suspicions is wrong. I also have a hunch that full-strength deference and plain conditionalization on your total evidence can't both be fully general, because a chance hypothesis isn't an ordinary bit of evidence you can just condition on; say where exactly that bites, if it bites at all. And if some subset of the desiderata turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable, say so — that's the most useful thing you could hand me. Tell me which single requirement kills off the most candidate rules.

One line each at the end on the Lewis-style admissible-information version, a best-system/Humean story about what chances even are, a propensity view, and the "just treat the chance function as an expert and calibrate to it" reading — but keep that short, the list is what I'm paying for.
