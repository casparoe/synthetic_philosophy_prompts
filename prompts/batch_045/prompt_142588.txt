I'm trying to work out what I actually believe about distributive priority, and I would rather start from my verdicts on cases than from a theory. Take well-being to be measurable on a rough 0–100 scale, and a "unit" to be the same size of benefit wherever it lands.

(1) Ava and Ben are both at 100. A policy would move both of them to 90 and benefit no one. I think it is not better in any respect, and I would pay nothing for it. Perfect equality has no value of its own, and I don't think that Ben's having less than Ava would give Ava any complaint whatever.

(2) Nadia is at 30, Omar at 100. We can bring Priya into existence at 100; the alternative is that she never exists. Nadia's absolute position is unchanged; she merely becomes the worst off by a wider margin. I think creating Priya is fine, and I feel no pull at all toward "this is in some way worse for Nadia."

(3) One unit to Nadia (30) or five units to Omar (100): I choose Nadia.
(4) One unit to Nadia (30) or ninety units to Omar (100): I choose Omar.

So my priority weight on someone at 30 seems to be more than five times, and less than ninety times, that on someone at 100.

(5) One unit to Nadia (30), or 0.2 units to each of a thousand people all at level 100 — two hundred units in total: I still choose Nadia. I know this looks inconsistent with (4) under any principle that simply adds up weighted benefits, since ninety units to one person at 100 already beat one unit to Nadia. I cannot shake the verdict: a thousand small gifts to people who are already flourishing do not seem to accumulate into an answer to Nadia's claim, however large they sum to.

(6) One dose of a fast-acting analgesic, with an identical effect either way. The first candidate is a patient in agony now who will fully recover and go on to an excellent long life (say −40 at this moment, 85 for her life as a whole). The second is a patient comfortable now whose remaining life will be grim (60 now, 35 overall). I give it to the first. But in a different pair — a treatment that improves the remainder of a life, offered either to someone at 80 now and thereafter or to someone at 40 now and thereafter — I give it to the second. I worry that these two verdicts pull in opposite directions, one tracking the moment and the other the whole life.

(7) Before a fair coin is flipped, I can commit to give ten units to whichever of Ana and Bo ends up at 20 (the other stays at 70), or I can commit now to give ten units to Ana whatever happens. Targeting is better. But if targeting is impossible and I must commit in advance to Ana or to Bo, I am indifferent: there is no distributive reason either way, and the fact that one of the two gifts will look badly misdirected once the coin lands is not a mark against the choice I made.

My tentative principle: value an outcome by summing, across everyone, a single increasing and concave function of their well-being at the time the benefit lands, with no separate term for equality, for comparisons between people, or for complaints. Please test it against all seven cases, repair it where it fails, and state precisely what the function has to satisfy — what (3) and (4) together force about its steepness, and whether (5) forces you to abandon adding-up altogether for something non-additive (a pairwise or rank-sensitive rule, a per-person cap, a lexical element). Then say what your repaired principle predicts for a case I have not listed, and what it says about the one I keep changing my mind on: a single beneficiary, the same size of benefit, and as candidates someone whose life has declined from 90 to 60 and will stay there, and someone who has always been at 45 and will stay there. Part of me says the person at 45; part of me says the loss matters and it is the person at 60.

Please answer in at most 600 words, and state explicitly which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate.
