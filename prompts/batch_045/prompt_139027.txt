I'm a retired zoologist — birds, mostly, nothing whatsoever to do with brains — and for the last year or so I've been going along to a philosophy of mind reading group at the university. There's a move that keeps turning up in what we read about split-brain patients, and I don't have a word for it, which means I can't get a grip on what I think is wrong with it.

Here's the shape of it. Someone lays out the classic commissurotomy findings: a word is flashed to the left visual field so it goes to the right hemisphere; the patient can't say what was shown but the left hand picks out the matching object from behind a screen; asked why, the patient — well, the speaking half — comes out with some reason that has nothing to do with the word. Every verb in that description is a psychological one and every subject of it is a hemisphere. The right hemisphere saw, understood, knew what to do; the left one invented a story. You can't state the finding any other way.

Then a page later, when someone says "so are there two subjects of experience in that skull?", the same writers say: no, of course not, a hemisphere isn't the sort of thing that can be a subject, that's just a manner of speaking about information being available to one causally seperated subsystem and not to another. The subject is the patient, full stop.

What interests me is the asymmetry. The hemisphere-talk is good enough to report the discovery and to carry the dramatic conclusion — that consciousness can come apart, that unity is an empirical matter — but it's suddenly only a manner of speaking the moment it would support something people don't want, like there being two subjects, or two centres of moral concern, in one body. Nobody ever seems to run it the other way: since it's only shorthand, perhaps it doesn't show disunity either.

I see the same shape in a harmless place: a friend who used to do neurophysiology says "the visual system hypothesises" all day and then denies the visual system believes anything. There it costs nothing, because nobody is trying to count subjects. In the split-brain case the counting is the whole point.

I've heard it called a fallacy, but that doesn't feel right: it isn't a slip, the people doing it are careful and know exactly what they're up to, and the description would be unstatable without it. It's more like a licence that gets waved and then withdrawn depending on which conclusion is on the table.

So: does it have a name? I mean the asymmetry in particular, not talking about hemispheres in general. If yes, I'd want to know how near the established notions get and where mine departs from each; if there's nothing that fits, say so plainly and invent a term with a definition, and give me a couple of borderline cases so I can see the edges. And either way, tell me whether the asymmetry can actually be defended — is there a reading on which the shorthand really is evidential about disunity and really is silent about how many subjects there are, or is it just wanting it both ways? I'm not after a label to drop at the next meeting; I want to know what the thing is, what it isn't, and where it stops.
