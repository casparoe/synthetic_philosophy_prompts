I'm seventeen, and I've come to hold a view that I keep failing to talk my friends out of, so I want to know what would actually break it. The view is that generational cohorts — Boomers, Gen X, Millennials, Gen Z, and now whatever we're calling the kids after us — are not real kinds. They're arbitrary slices cut out of a continuous demographic stream, closer to star signs with birth years than to anything with genuine causal structure. When someone explains a classmate's attitudes toward work, dating, or phones by saying it's because they're Gen Z, I think the honest answer is that they were born in a particular year and then a large number of unrelated things happened to them.

So: what would count against this? I don't want a reading list or citations to studies I can't look up — tell me what a finding or an argument would have to look like in order to move me, and rank the candidates from fatal to merely awkward. Please deal with at least the following, plus anything you think cuts deeper.

The boundary problem, and how much weight it can really bear. My strongest argument is that the cutoffs are arbitrary: slide the line between two adjacent cohorts a year or two in either direction and the traits attached to them don't jump or vanish, they fade; meanwhile two people a year apart get assigned to different essences despite near-identical upbringings, while someone my own age on another continent is supposedly in my generation. A defender can reply that plenty of genuine kinds have fuzzy or conventional edges — species, mountains, recessions — so demanding a sharp seam is setting the bar in the wrong place. Is that reply fair? If it is, I think I've lost my best argument, and I want to know what I'd have left.

The identification problem. As far as I can tell, any correlation between a cohort label and an attitude can be redescribed as a fact about how old someone is right now, or about what year it is right now, and I can't see how to pry the three apart. Is there a way in principle — some case where being twenty, being born in a given year, and living in the present come apart enough to reveal which one is doing the work? Describe the case rather than assuring me that methods for this exist.

A test I invented and can't evaluate, which I'll call the shuffle test: take everyone born inside, say, a six-year window, randomly reassign their birth years among them, and see whether the generational label still predicts anything once you've controlled for current age. My prediction is that it doesn't, or that it predicts so weakly that the label isn't earning its keep. Would a generational realist accept this test in advance? If they'd refuse it, is that because it's a bad test or because it's a dangerous one?

The self-defeating version, which worries me most. Suppose cohorts begin as arbitrary but become real, because once a label circulates people adapt to it: they adopt the attitudes they're told their cohort has, institutions and advertisers treat them as a bloc, and the prediction brings about its own confirmation. On that story I'd have the origin of the kind right while being wrong that it isn't a kind now, and my view would amount to a genetic fallacy about social categories. How much of the apparent correlation could plausibly be produced this way, and how could anyone ever tell?

What my view predicts that its rivals don't, apart from the fading at the boundaries. I think it predicts that the in-between labels people invent for those born near a cutoff should proliferate without limit and should work about as well as the big ones, and that cohort explanations should travel badly between countries, since the same birth years don't line up with the same events everywhere. Are these genuine predictions, or have I built them in by definition?

Two further requests. First, be straight with me about where I'm cheating: my friends accuse me of a no-true-generation move, where every apparent cohort effect gets explained away as something else, and I want to know whether my view has been qualified into something no evidence could touch, and whether that matters for a claim of this kind. Second, I don't want a survey that ends by placing the truth somewhere in the middle. Once you've mapped how exposed I actually am, commit to a verdict: is the view, as I've stated it, more likely right than wrong, and if you had to stake it all on a single question, which one settles it? I'll add that I personally find the statistical-sounding arguments far more persuasive than the conceptual ones, which probably marks exactly where I'm most vulnerable — so push hardest there.
