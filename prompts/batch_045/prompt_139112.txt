I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of mind: 18 students, one 90-minute session, a seminar room with a projector, a laptop and my phone, but no tachistoscope, no eye tracker and no EEG. Two complications: three of the students have already taken a perception course and will recognize and name any standard illusion the moment I run it, and one participant is colorblind.

Next session we are doing the distinction between Orwellian and Stalinesque revision — the question whether, in a display where two differently coloured discs are flashed in quick succession at different locations, the subject first consciously sees the first disc and then has that experience quietly rewritten after the second one arrives, or whether the perceptual system finishes its editorial work before any of it reaches consciousness, so that only the corrected version is ever experienced. My problem is not that students resist the claim that no possible evidence distinguishes these two stories. It is that they grant it in about fifteen seconds and feel no cost. They file it as a verbal quibble about where you draw the word conscious rather than as the loss of something they were relying on.

Design an activity that makes them run into that loss before it has a name. Constraints:

Do not use the words Orwellian, Stalinesque, multiple drafts, Cartesian theater, or Dennett until the debrief; the students should be arguing about a case, not about a label.

Stage at least one live demonstration they are actually put through, and something in a second modality or on paper for the participants the visual one will miss.

Give the session small real stakes. I want each student to commit in writing, before the demonstration, both to a prediction about what they will experience and to the single piece of evidence they would accept as settling which story is true of them — and then to discover that neither the prediction nor the test can be cashed in. Tokens, snacks or an IOU are all fine; what matters is that the commitment is still unpaid when they leave the room, and that they can say why.

Follow it with a transfer round: three short cases, one in perception, one in pain (a report given at the time and revised after a later diagnosis), and one in a legal or administrative setting where what is at issue is what someone consciously experienced at a particular moment. Students should have to rule on whether the distinction does real work in each, or whether its emptiness spreads outward.

Then give me the debrief: which moments in the session map onto which part of the argument, what the facilitator says when the room splits, and how to handle the perception students who defuse the demonstration by explaining it, and the contrarians who bite the bullet on both stories at once. Be explicit about the one step the activity can only ever stage as an absence — the move from no evidence could tell us which story is true to there is no fact of the matter — and say whether, in your view, that step is one I should be asserting in the debrief anyway or flagging as a further question.

Open with a single sentence naming the session's core mechanism, of the kind I could paste into a syllabus, and then give me materials, rules, a minute-by-minute timeline, the debrief, failure modes and limits. No preamble and no restating the question.
