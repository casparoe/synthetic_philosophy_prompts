Hey — I need a hand turning a messy idea into a proposal. Context: I'm applying for a three-month funded writing slot (about 4000 words of output at the end) and I want to spend it on slurs.

My half-formed idea, sorry if its jumbled: I think the slur debate keeps arguing about what a slur contributes to the proposition expressed, while the interesting data is about three different things that don't travel together. Call them (1) derogation: the utterance says something bad about the target; (2) speaker-commitment: the utterance reveals or commits the speaker to a derogatory stance or to an ideology; (3) imposition: what the utterance does to whoever hears it — marks them as addressed a certain way, makes them complicit, puts them in a position where they now have to respond. The standard families of view — the no-content, it's-just-prohibited one; the purely expressive/attitudinal one; the descriptive-plus-expressive hybrid one — each has a clean story for two of the three and hand-waves the third. The place this shows is embeddings: negation, yes/no questions, the antecedent of a conditional, under attitude verbs, in free indirect discourse, inside reported speech, and in the metalanguage (a dictionary entry, a court transcript, a seminar on slurs). Offence doesn't project through all of those the same way, and — the bit I think nobody has worked out — it looks like derogation, speaker-commitment and imposition project through *different* subsets. Reclaimed and in-group uses are the worst case: the derogation seems to fade, the imposition doesn't always, and the speaker-commitment flips into something else entirely (affiliation? permission to be intimate?). I also want to know whether any of this changes for slurs whose target class is itself contested, where the boundaries of the group are part of what's fought over, versus ones where the classification is uncontested.

So what I want from you: a proposal that turns that into a fundable question.

- Say why the existing positions leave it open, and why my three-way split isn't just a relabelling of them with an extra box.
- Give me the method for three months. I'm imagining a fixed battery of embedding environments crossed with a fixed set of speaker/hearer configurations (in-group, out-group, reclaimed, a child who doesn't know the word, a non-comprehending speaker, acted or fictional utterance), checking cell by cell which of the three components survives. Tell me if that's the right instrument, what the obvious alternatives are (armchair cases, corpus work, asking actual speakers) and why mine beats them — or say if it doesn't.
- Say what the 4000 words at the end look like for someone who isn't me.
- Say what would count as the project failing and what's most likely to make it fail.
- And the part reviewers actually read: what would change my mind. If someone handed me which argument, I'd drop the three-way split and go back to two, or to one. Flag too where your own reasoning here is on thinnest ice.

No citations please, I'll fill those in — naming a position in plain words is fine, I don't need who said it or when.
