I cover philosophy and the cognitive sciences for a general audience. Twelve years ago I profiled the bride, then a graduate student working on the wrong kind of reasons problem: the difficulty, for any view that analyses value in terms of what it is fitting to favour or admire, of separating reasons that bear on whether an attitude is fitting from reasons that are merely reasons to get oneself into the attitude, the stock example being a demon who threatens you unless you admire him. Her own solution had two halves, and it is the second that her colleagues still argue about: a reason is of the right kind for an attitude, she argues, only if it bears on the correctness of what that attitude represents its object to be; and love does not represent its object as having merits at all, so her merits are not right-kind reasons for him to love her — the only considerations that can genuinely speak for one person's loving another are facts about the history those two people share.

I'm giving one of the toasts at her wedding next month. Half the room is her department, who will hear exactly what I am doing if I do it right, and will notice if I smuggle in a merit-based or a benefit-based reason for his love; the other half is his family, who will be baffled or hurt if it sounds as though he has no reason to marry her, or as though her being brilliant and kind were beside the point. Write me a four-minute toast that stays inside her view without ever naming it. The praise of her can be warm and specific, but it must not be offered as his reason; that has to be the shared history — they met when he wandered into her talk by mistake, looking for a session on sea-level modelling, and they have walked the same loop around the reservoir together on Sunday mornings for four years, in every kind of weather. Nothing in it should rest on how happy she makes him, or on what an occasion like this calls for. It has to end as a toast and land as affection rather than as a seminar.
