I am grading the final exam for my advanced undergraduate seminar on reference and modality. One of the questions was designed to test whether students can apply the Kripkean apparatus — rigid designation, the distinction between fixing a reference and giving a meaning, and the crossed categories of the necessary a posteriori and the contingent a priori — to a case they have not encountered before. Below are the question and one student's answer to it.

Question. The founding charter of a colony contains the following stipulation: "Let one vane be the length, at noon today, of the platinum rod in the Hall of Measures" — call that rod Keel. The settlers go on to use "vane" as a unit of length, including in hypothetical and counterfactual talk. Unbeknownst to them, Keel was cast with a flaw and has been contracting steadily ever since; by noon on Foundation Day it is already measurably shorter than it was when new, and it continues to shrink.

(a) Is "one vane" a rigid designator of a length, or does the term merely abbreviate the charter's description? Explain briefly, and say what turns on the answer.
(b) Classify the sentence "Keel was exactly one vane long at noon on Foundation Day" as necessary or contingent, and as a priori or a posteriori for the settlers who framed the charter. Justify both halves of your classification.
(c) A settler who has read the charter but has never measured Keel says: "Had the rod been cast without the flaw, it would still have been exactly one vane long at noon on Foundation Day." Is she right? Explain.

Student's answer. (a) Yes — "one vane" is a rigid designator: it picks out the same length in every possible world, and if we describe a world in which the rod was cast differently, we are still measuring in vanes, that is, in that same length. The charter's words are not a definition of the term; they only fix which length is in question, which is why "one vane" is not shorthand for the description. What turns on this is that claims about the rod's length become substantive rather than verbal: if the term were just an abbreviation of the description, "Keel is one vane long" would be trivially true, whereas on the rigid reading it says something that could have been false.

(b) The sentence is contingent: had the rod been cast without the flaw, it would have been longer at noon on Foundation Day than it actually was, and so Keel would not have been one vane long. Its truth depends on how the world turned out, so it is not the sort of thing one could know merely by reflecting on one's own convention — how long the rod is, is an empirical matter, and the settlers would need to measure it to determine whether it is one vane long. So the sentence is contingent and a posteriori, of a piece with the other famous cases: Hesperus is Phosphorus, water is H2O, and so on, where the terms are rigid and the claim is nonetheless established by experience.

(c) The settler is right. Under the supposition that the rod had been cast without the flaw, the length designated by "one vane" is the length the rod would then have had at noon on Foundation Day, since that is what the charter's description would have picked out in that situation. So even in that counterfactual case Keel is one vane long — it would simply have been a longer vane. This is just what it means to fix a reference by means of a description: the description travels with the counterfactual. The general moral is that rigidity and apriority come apart; a term can be perfectly rigid while everything we know by using it is known through experience.

Please grade this answer out of 25 and give me the specific comments I can paste onto the grading sheet: for each part, say what the student gets right, what is confused, conflated, or does not follow, and what a complete answer would have added. If two of the student's claims cannot both be true, say so explicitly, even where each sounds plausible when read on its own. I want a point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression, and I have deliberately not recorded my own view of the answer.
