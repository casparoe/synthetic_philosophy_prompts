I'm a novelist, and the manuscript I'm finishing is set mostly in the 2090s, after what my characters call the Second Expansion: the extension of full moral standing to emulated and artificially generated minds. A lot of the book turns on a retrospective judgment — my narrator looks back at the early twenty-first century more or less the way a present-day reader looks back at slave-owning societies, and takes it for granted that the later verdict is not merely different from ours but better. For that to work I need the moral epistemology underneath it to be something a philosopher could actually hold. I would rather not have a knowledgeable reader close the book feeling that my characters are trading in slogans with philosophical vocabulary stapled on.

To get the background right I have been reading a popular-audience book about moral progress, and I want to know how much of its account of the expanding circle a careful defender of that account would actually own. Compressed, the passage runs like this: human beings began by caring only for kin and band, and the history of morality is the history of that circle being pushed outward — to the tribe, the city, the nation, the race, the species. What pushes it is reason: once you grant that there is no morally relevant difference between your own kin and a stranger, consistency forces you to give the stranger's interests the same weight, and the very argument that carried you from kin to stranger carries you from stranger to foreigner, from human to animal, and in principle beyond. Feeling has never been the engine; sympathy is parochial and unreliable, and every great reform was won against sentiment rather than because of it. This is what makes the widening progress rather than mere change: later generations really do include beings earlier ones excluded, and because there is no principled stopping point, anyone who insists the circle has now reached its natural limit occupies exactly the position of the person who insisted it stopped at the tribe. We should therefore expect it to go on widening, and expect our descendants to look back at us with something like the horror with which we look back at slave-owners.

My suspicion is that this is too tidy, but I cannot say where, and I do not want a novelist's hunch to be the only thing I go on. Three things in particular bother me. First, the step from consistency forces you to extend equal consideration to therefore the circle keeps widening seems to run together two claims the view might want to keep apart — and if consistency is all one has, I cannot see why it could not be satisfied by levelling down rather than up. Second, the closing move — no principled stopping point, so anyone who claims to have found one is just the tribalist again — strikes me as proving too much, or perhaps only proving that we would not be able to see a stopping point even if there were one. Third, and this may be the novelist rather than the philosopher in me: on this picture factory farming should count as progress, since billions of animals are now inside a circle that once excluded them, even though the treatment they receive is arguably worse in absolute terms than at any earlier point; if inclusiveness and improvement can come apart that thoroughly, I am not sure what work the word progress is doing.

The reason this matters structurally is that one of my characters, a philosopher named Hallam Vey, argues against the optimism roughly as follows: every generation that has enlarged the circle has also taken its own arrangement to be complete, since nobody can see outside a circle they are inside; so the fact that we can find no principled stopping point is exactly what we should expect whether or not the circle is genuinely finished, and is therefore no evidence at all that it is not — apparent completeness is uninformative. I would like Vey to be right about something. Please go through the popular summary claim by claim and tell me what a defender would accept, what they would call a simplification, and what they would reject outright; then tell me whether Vey's argument is sound, whether it is self-undermining (if apparent completeness carries no information, why trust the retrospective judgment that earlier enlargements were genuine progress rather than mere change?), and if it survives that, whether it leaves my narrator any non-question-begging way to condemn us, or whether it collapses into the cheap thought that every age thinks itself right and therefore none is. I especially want to know what distinguishes the defensible version of Vey's point from the lazy relativist version, because I would rather cut the character than let her win an argument she should not.

One constraint: I am eight months into a fellowship at a writers' colony with no library and unreliable internet, so please work from the arguments themselves and not from sources — no citations, no reading lists, no directions to go read so-and-so. About eight hundred words, in the order I have raised things, would be ideal.
