putting together a short position note on Occam/simplicity stuff for a reading group and I want someone to stress-test it before I send it round. Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. The right normative ideal for induction is Solomonoff's universal mixture — prior weight 2^-K(h) over computable hypotheses, where K is Kolmogorov complexity.
2. Occam's razor isn't a seperate epistemic norm sitting on top of Bayesianism. It's just what falls out when you conditionalise on that prior. There's no extra "nature is simple" principle doing any work.
3. The reference-machine / programming-language dependence of K is a non-issue: by the invariance theorem two machines give complexities differing by a constant that doesn't depend on the object, so it washes out.
4. Uncomputability of the prior isn't decisive either. Normative ideals don't have to be implementable, and in practice we approximate anyway with MDL and actual compressors.
5. What gives the razor its teeth is the convergence/dominance result: the mixture converges on any computable source and beats every computable predictor, with the total error bounded by something proportional to K of the true source.
6. I dont think we have any a priori reason to believe the world is simple, and I don't want the razor to lean on one.
7. I'm also a subjectivist about priors in general: coherence is the only rational constraint, no particular prior is mandated.
8. Two verdicts: (i) 20 data points, a 3-parameter fit versus a 10-parameter fit that fits the data better — I take the 3-parameter one, and I'd take it even in a domain I knew nothing about; (ii) simplicity is a guide to which theory is true, not merely to what to predict next.
9. The objection that penalising parameters on top of the likelihood double-counts fit strikes me as confused.

Does this hang together?
