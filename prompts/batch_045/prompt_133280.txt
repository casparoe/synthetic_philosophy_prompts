I'm finishing the conceptual section of a term paper for a seminar on corruption, development and political theory, and I want the core of it stress-tested before I build thirty pages on top. Here is roughly where I've landed, in my own words:

(1) Corruption is a structural property of an office rather than of an envelope: an entrusted position is being used corruptly when a personal relation — kinship, friendship, payment, loyalty — is what settles an allocation that the position exists to settle on some other criterion.

(2) Whether a transfer counts as a gift, a fee or a bribe, and whether an office is meant to be impartial at all, is settled by local norms. I add what I call the norm-saturation condition: a written rule can serve as the benchmark against which a practice counts as corrupt only if it is saturated, i.e. only if it actually organises what officials and citizens expect, rather than merely sitting in a statute. This is why I distrust the standard cross-country indices: what they record is distance from a particular Weberian model of the state, not wrongdoing.

(3) Clientelism is corrupt, full stop, and I mean that normatively — pro tanto wrong even where everyone expects it and even where the state delivers nothing otherwise, because it converts a public good into a patron's private currency and makes access depend on continuing personal submission.

(4) Where a practice reliably gets the worst off more of what they need than any alternative on offer, I am unwilling to condemn it. That is why I do not think speed money to a clerk who otherwise never processes your file is the interesting case.

(5) The concept earns its keep as a diagnostic for institutional design — what to build, what to dismantle — not as a device for allocating blame to individuals; I would rather ask what institution would make a practice unnecessary than ask who is corrupt.

(6) A case verdict I want to keep: a city councillor who allocates scarce public housing to the people who show up at his Saturday table, in a city where the official points-based list has been frozen for years and has allocated nothing, is acting corruptly — and I want to say that even though the people he houses would not be housed at all otherwise.

What I would like is an audit, not a summary: which of these genuinely pull against each other, which only look as though they do, and for each real tension what the cheapest revision is and what that revision would cost me. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equal, and say if one of them is load-bearing for the whole paper. My own sense is that the trouble is somewhere around (2) and (3), but I cannot locate it, and I would like to hold on to (6) if at all possible since the paper turns on it. Finally, tell me how confident you are in each diagnosis and flag the single point you are least sure of.
