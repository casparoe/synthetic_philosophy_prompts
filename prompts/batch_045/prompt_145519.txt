I run a tiny AI-ethics reading group — two ML engineers, one EA guy who's been doing expected-value arithmetic on everything since about 2015, a human-rights lawyer who says "dignity" roughly every third sentence, and a couple of philosophy undergrads — and we've completely stalled on whether a deployed AI system should reason like a utilitarian or like a deontologist. Everyone just repeats their side's slogans, so for next week I want to hand out a decision tree instead: "which moral architecture are you actually proposing?", eight or nine branch nodes, about ten leaves, where every node is a concrete case or claim you can answer without knowing any of the labels, and where you only find out which position you've landed on when you hit the leaf. Start me from something all six of us already agree on, so nobody peels off at question one. And please build it from the logical space rather than from the list of names: give me the questions first, then say which combinations of answers are actually occupied by a real view (act-consequentialist direct optimizer, rule-consequentialist, satisficing or threshold consequentialist, Kantian-style constraint view, contractualist or publicity-test view, value pluralist with lexical ordering, moral-uncertainty/"parliament" aggregator, and the deferential view where the system tries to do what the humans would endorse under better information), and which cells are empty — and for each empty cell say whether it's empty because it's incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored. Also flag any two leaves your questions can't really seperate; I'd much rather you tell me they collapse than pretend they don't.

The cases I want used, roughly in this order: a triage or rationing bot that could save more lives by quietly violating a hospital rule nobody can appeal; a system that can either inflict one large harm on one user or a one-second annoyance on ten million users (does the aggregate count, and does it count all the way down to the smallest harm?); a dying user who extracts a promise from the system that, kept, makes things clearly worse; a system offered the chance to rewrite its own constraint list at runtime for a large expected gain; a population of ten thousand copies of the same system, where each individual rule-violation would be undetectable and locally beneficial; and a case where the system's own world model is known to be badly unreliable, which I take to be the strongest argument for rules in the first place. Two branching questions are definately going in there, because they're my own obsession and I've never seen them discussed anywhere: first, whether a constraint binds the system's deliberation (it must not even represent the forbidden tradeoff or compute its price) or only its action (it can calculate that violating would pay, it just mustn't), and what the difference buys you; second, whether the constraint set is frozen at deployment or open to self-amendment, and what happens to a deontological view once the system's descendants get to vote on the rules. The lawyer insists on a leaf for a view where the system models what it owes each person severally instead of aggregating, and the EA guy wants one for "maximize expected value but never act below a hard confidence floor" — include both, but tell me if on your questions they turn out to be the same leaf as something else. Keep it tight enough for one side of a handout, and no citations or further reading: half the group won't look anything up, so the tree has to stand on its own.
