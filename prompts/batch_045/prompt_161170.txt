A popular linguistics book I picked up at an airport summarizes Grice roughly like this:

'Grice's insight was that what a sentence literally says often falls short of what a speaker means, and that hearers close the gap by inference. He proposed that conversation is governed by a Cooperative Principle with four maxims — Quality, Quantity, Relation, Manner — and that when a speaker seems to break one of them, the hearer assumes she is still cooperating underneath and works out what she must have meant. That inferred content is an implicature. Because implicatures are worked out rather than encoded, they are always cancellable without contradiction: "Some of the students passed — in fact all of them" is fine, whereas cancelling what is actually said just contradicts yourself. The same story explains why "and" usually means "and then" and why "but" signals a contrast: those are defaults you get for free unless the context blocks them. So semantics hands over a complete literal proposition and pragmatics piles everything else on top. And since implicatures are not part of what is said, they are not part of the truth-conditional content either — which is why you can never be caught lying if only your implicature was false.'

Is that fair?
