Last spring I sat on a jury in a federal drug case, and one instruction we were given has been bothering me ever since.

The defendant had driven a car holding two sealed duffel bags across the border, and his account was that he had never asked what was in them and had never been told. The judge told us, in substance, that negligence would not do: it was not enough that a reasonable person in his position would have realised what he was carrying. But if we found two things, we were permitted to find that he knew — first, that he himself thought it highly probable that the bags contained drugs, and second, that he deliberately avoided confirming it because he did not want to know. I have since learned that lawyers call this willful blindness, and the instruction the ostrich instruction. We convicted, and at the time it seemed obvious.

Since then I have been reading about self-deception, and I have come across the claim that it may not even be possible: that there is no coherent state in which a person both believes something and believes its opposite, or brings it about by design that he believes what he knows to be false, and that what we ordinarily call self-deception is really just motivated reasoning, selective attention, and self-serving judgment about ambiguous evidence. If that is right, I no longer understand what we were finding when we were invited to find that he knew. So my question is about the practice rather than about my case.

What picture of belief, knowledge, and agency does an instruction like that one presuppose? I can think of three candidate reconstructions, and I would like help telling them apart and deciding which the doctrine actually needs. On the first, deliberate avoidance is not knowledge at all; it is a different failing that is equally culpable, and the law simply lets it stand in for knowledge — a normative surrogate rather than a mental-state finding. On the second, deliberate avoidance really is a way of knowing: knowledge is not a matter of what is occurrently before the mind but of how a person's attention, inference, and action are organised, so someone can know what he would sincerely deny knowing. On the third, knowledge in the statute is simply being used as a normative notion, and the instruction is not describing a mind at all. What does the two-part structure of the test — subjective suspicion plus deliberate avoidance, with negligence expressly excluded — commit the law to, and what does it leave open? Does any of the three require self-deception to be genuinely possible, or does each of them get by with a thinner story about evidence, attention, and culpability?

Two further things puzzle me. The first is that the doctrine seems to presuppose that not-looking can be something a person does rather than merely something that fails to happen: that we have the kind of agency over our own attention that makes avoidance an action. Is that presupposition as innocent as it looks? The second is an apparent inconsistency. My impression — please correct me if I have the law wrong — is that elsewhere the same legal system treats a defendant's own sincere account of what he believed as nearly decisive, and that even a thoroughly unreasonable belief can defeat a required mental element. There, the law seems to take avowal at face value; here, it looks straight through it. Is that a genuine tension, or is there a unified account on which the law is tracking the same thing in both places, and if so, what thing?

Finally: if we gave up the picture of the mind that makes self-deception possible, what would the practice have to become? Would willful blindness collapse into the recklessness or negligence the doctrine explicitly says is insufficient, or could it be rebuilt as something else — a distinct culpable failure to inquire, say — and what would a jury then be asked to decide?

I am not looking for case names or reading suggestions; I am after the implicit theory. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of what I have asked. Please also tell me how confident you are in the account you give, and single out the point in it that you are least sure of — I would rather know where a tidy story runs out than be handed one.
