I'm trying to work out what I actually believe about vast numbers of very small creatures, and my verdicts on cases don't seem to add up. (1) Paying a shrimp farm to install electrical stunners so that the animals are unconscious before slaughter: yes, worth funding, and I'd rate it a serious use of money. (2) Paying an insect-protein producer to chill black soldier fly larvae rather than boil them: I'd pay something, but perhaps a fiftieth of what I'd pay for the shrimp. (3) Spraying a wheat field for aphids, at the numbers insects come in — billions per hectare, I'm told: I feel nothing and do nothing. (4) The soil fauna lost when a meadow is paved over — nematodes, springtails, mites — which by the numbers dwarfs everything else on this list: no weight at all. (5) Picking ticks off a dog: nothing. (6) One pig in a farrowing crate versus a million honeybees in hives trucked for pollination: the pig, comfortably — and this is the verdict I trust least, given how I answered the shrimp case.

Two more features of the pattern. I won't set the probability of sentience to zero for nematodes or ticks; pressed, I'd say a few percent, and I still won't spend a penny on them. And when someone points out that paving the meadow would also prevent quintillions of future soil animals from existing — lives that on any pessimistic view would be short and bad, so that paving comes out a benefit on my own numbers — I don't accept the conclusion; I take it as a sign that I've set the problem up wrong. On evidence: an animal that learns to avoid the place it was shocked and will trade that avoidance off against a food reward moves me; mere withdrawal from a noxious stimulus does not; sleep-like states and apparently playful behaviour in bees leave me genuinely unsure, and I want to know what your account does with them.

My own guess is that what I'm uncertain about is a lineage-level matter — whether there is a persisting subject there whose life can go well or badly — and that below some level of confidence the numbers should count as one population-sized stake rather than as N individual stakes. I can't make that precise, and I suspect it is special pleading; I also find it annoying rather than convincing when I'm told that these decisions recur constantly, so that even a tiny per-decision expectation compounds.

Can you put my verdicts into reflective equilibrium? State a principle precisely, say which case buys each clause, give its verdicts on farmed crickets and on the ants in my kitchen, name the verdicts of mine it cannot keep and say which you would overturn — and tell me what would change your mind: which argument or which finding would flip you on the cases where we disagree, and where your reasoning is most likely to break.
