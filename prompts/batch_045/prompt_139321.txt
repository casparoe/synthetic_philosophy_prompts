I'm a philosophy PhD student who keeps getting dragged into the AI safety reading group in my department, and I've agreed to run next week's session on the narrow definition of the alignment problem. The explainer I was planning to hand out (one of those very long, very confident blog posts) does it like this:

"Alignment used to be defined broadly: build systems that do what is good, or what we would want if we knew more and thought longer. That turned out to be a mess, because nobody agrees about what is good. So the field now mostly works with the narrow definition: a system is aligned when it is trying to do what its operator wants it to do. This is outer alignment, as opposed to inner alignment, which is about whether the system's actual goals are the ones we were trying to give it. The narrow definition sidesteps every argument about value, since it only asks for a match between the system's intentions and the operator's wants, and what the operator wants is just whatever they say they want. That turns alignment into a normal engineering problem: specify the objective, train on it, check the system is trying to hit it. The famous hard cases — the genie that gives you exactly what you asked for, the model that games its reward model instead of doing the task — are really just cases where the objective wasn't specified carefully enough, and they're what inner alignment exists to catch."

I've got two worries and I dont trust either of them. One: this seems to collapse intent alignment into plain instruction-following, which can't be the intended reading, because the literal genie is supposed to be the paradigm of misalignment under the narrow definition. Two: the case I actually want to spend the session on is one where the operator and the people affected arent the same — a clinical triage system whose operator is the hospital trust that bought it and whose "users" are the patients — and I cant tell whether the narrow definition returns a verdict there at all without first deciding who counts as the operator, and whether deciding that quietly reimports exactly the value questions the post says it has dodged.

Could you go through the passage and separate what you'd accept, what a specialist would let slide as a simplification, and what is actually a misrepresentation? I'm especially suspicious of the outer/inner alignment sentence, which feels like it might be the wrong way round, but I'm not confident. Be explicit about where you're confident and where you think it's only a terminological squabble — and if my two worries are themselves muddled, say so plainly instead of politely finding errors to agree with me.
