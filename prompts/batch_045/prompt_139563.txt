I am a novelist. The manuscript I delivered to my editor last month turns on a minor character — a conservator of frescoes, recently widowed, entirely unafraid of scaffolding — who turns out to have what clinicians call asymbolia for pain. She feels the burning, the crushing, the tearing; she can report its location, its onset, its intensity, and its quality as precisely as any other patient in the ward; and it does not bother her at all. She has to be told, kindly, to take her hand off the hot pipe. My narrator is a physician, and in an early draft he says of her simply that her pain does not hurt, which my agent crossed out as a muddle, correctly I think. I have been trying for some months to work out what exactly is missing in her, and the attempt has turned into a short essay that will be printed at the back of the book as a note on sources. I would be grateful for your comments on it — as a philosopher, not as a copy-editor. The clinical picture in the real cases is messier than mine; I have idealised it deliberately, and I do not want the note to turn on nosography or on who first described the syndrome. I have no university library and no interest in chasing journal articles, so please argue rather than cite. What follows is the essay in full, at roughly the length it will have in print.

A note on the conservator's pain

1. An ordinary pain seems to bundle at least four things together. There is a sensory episode with a bodily location, a felt intensity and a describable quality. There is an affective tone: the episode is disagreeable, one objects to it, one minds it. There is a motivational upshot: one wants it to stop, one withdraws, one learns to avoid the circumstances that produced it. And there is something normative: the episode is bad for the one who has it, it makes her worse off, it gives her and others a reason to act. In my conservator the first item is intact and the second and third are, to a striking degree, absent. The question the novel keeps circling is what has happened to the fourth.

2. Two familiar answers suggest themselves, and I want to reject both. The first makes badness a matter of the subject's attitude: a pain is bad for you because of the stance you take toward it — you want it gone, you are averse to it, you dislike it. If that is right, then nothing bad remains in asymbolia; the sensation is present but the subject is not worse off for having it. I find this intolerable, and not only sentimentally. My conservator is quietly destroying her hands. To say that none of it is bad for her because she does not mind seems to make her welfare hostage to her sentiments in a way that would disgrace any other part of the story we tell about what is good and bad for people. The second answer makes badness intrinsic to the experience itself: the hurting just is the badness, and where the hurting is reported, the badness is there. But then my conservator is suffering, and she plainly is not; the holder of this view must say either that she is reporting wrongly about her own case or that hurting has quietly come apart from the experience she has, which looks like saving the view by emptying it.

3. The move I want to try is to stop asking how bad the episode is for her right now and ask instead whether she is being hurt, over time, by what is happening to her. Those can come apart, and asymbolia is the case that shows it. What is missing in her, I want to say, is not badness but uptake of badness. Pain, on this proposal, presents itself to its subject as a reason: it comes with what I will call the authority of the hurt, the way an experience can arrive already claiming that there is something to be done about it, and claiming this whether or not the person who has it is moved. Asymbolia is a failure of uptake, not an absence of the claim. The claim survives, which is why the surgeon has the same reason to anaesthetise her that she would have if she were screaming, and why that reason is not merely the instrumental one about her jerking her hand at the wrong moment, though that reason exists too.

4. Two invented cases, which I take to be the real test. Call the first motivated asymbolia. The subject reports no disagreeableness whatsoever, shows no distress, and will cheerfully keep her hand where it is if you do not stop her — yet she withdraws on the usual latency, acquires a durable avoidance of the relevant stimulus after a single trial, and will, if offered the choice in advance, reliably arrange things so as not to have the experience again. Aversion without any felt dislike. The attitude theorist must say this pain is bad for her, since the aversion is exactly what her theory counts. My account says the same thing, but for a different reason, and I think my reason is the better one: what makes the pain bad is the claim it makes, and the claim is intact. Call the second case hyper-symbolia: the sensory intensity is low, a mild persistent ache, but the disagreeableness is extreme, and the subject is incapacitated by it and would trade almost anything to be rid of it. Here I am in trouble. If the strength of the presented reason is fixed by the experience itself, I must say this pain is only mildly bad, which seems wrong. If it floats free of the sensory character, then I have relocated the subject-dependence I objected to in §2 one level up, into the content of the experience rather than the attitude toward it, and I have not really escaped the attitude theorist at all. I do not know how to settle this, and I suspect it is where the view is weakest.

5. What my narrator should have said, then, is not that her pain does not hurt. It is that she is not suffering, and that she is nevertheless being hurt — that there is a fact about what is happening to her that is bad for her and that she is not in a position to register, which is why the people pulling her down off the scaffolding are not being paternalistic, and why she is not being irrational, and why neither of them has the last word.

What I would like from you, concretely. First: does the distinction in §2 between a pain's badness and its disagreeableness actually hold up, or does it come apart in the hand once you press on what disagreeableness is supposed to be over and above the sensory character plus the motivation? Second: is the notion of an experience presenting itself as a reason coherent, or have I merely given a metaphor for the very motivational upshot I said was absent in my conservator? Third: I would like your verdict on whether motivated asymbolia is a genuine possibility and, more importantly, on whether a defender of the attitude theory would just swallow it and take the case as confirmation — if so, my §4 is doing no work and I should cut it. Fourth: how bad is the hyper-symbolia problem in §4 — is there a principled constraint on the strength of the presented reason that does not collapse my account into the one I rejected, or is that hole fatal? Finally, and this is the thing I most want: give me the strongest objection to the whole note that I have not anticipated, and tell me which premise it kills and whether the note can survive without that premise. If you think I have reinvented a well-known view, or a discredited version of one, please say so bluntly and without naming any names or pointing me at anything to read — being told that is worth more to me than being told I am original.
