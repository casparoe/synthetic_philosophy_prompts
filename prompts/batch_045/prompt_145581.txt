I am a PhD student in comparative cognition, and the theory chapter of my dissertation is going to have to commit to some account of what the *kinds* of minds are — the familiar four-fold ladder of Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian and Gregorian learners, or something better. Before I commit to anything I want the specification, not the literature: I want to know what I am entitled to demand of such an account. So please write the desiderata that any adequate taxonomy of kinds of minds has to meet, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are really just preferences of one camp.

Fix the purpose, so that adequacy is relative to something: I need a taxonomy good enough for two jobs. First, classification of genuinely novel systems without hand-waving — non-model organisms (the octopus, with its largely decentralised nervous system and short, largely solitary life; aneural systems such as slime moulds; tiny-brained insects that turn out to do one-shot or configural learning; corvids with manufactured tools; cetaceans and songbirds with transmitted local traditions) and artificial systems (a reinforcement learner that reaches superhuman board-game play through self-play alone; a large language model whose entire competence is extracted from a corpus of text produced by other minds). Second, explanation of which transitions are the hard ones: why within-lifetime, look-ahead-grade flexibility appears to be phylogenetically rare, and why cumulative culture — as opposed to mere transmission — looks close to unique. A taxonomy that sorts the familiar cases but tells me nothing about either of those is not what I need.

Two suspicions of my own that the list should be able to adjudicate, and which I would like treated as test cases rather than as premises:

One: the standard scheme looks to me to be running together an etiological question — how the organism's design was arrived at, whether by phylogenetic selection, individual reinforcement, cultural uptake — with a synchronic question about the mechanism that now implements the behaviour. A Popperian architecture could in principle be *produced* by cultural transmission, and a behaviour that looks Popperian could be implemented by something that is really just a very large repertoire. I want a requirement, or a set of them, that forces an account to say which question it is answering, and I want to know whether any account can answer both at once without collapsing one into the other.

Two: I want to introduce a candidate fifth kind and I do not know whether it is a kind or a degenerate case. Call them Alexandrian creatures, after the Library: systems whose competence is not the product of their own trial and error in a world, nor of instruction or tool-supply by conspecifics in a shared world, but of passive absorption of the accumulated artefacts of other minds — text, code, images. This is meant to be a genuinely different relation to other minds than the one the Gregorian kind is supposed to capture, and I suspect a good list of desiderata will either vindicate it or explain precisely why it is a Gregorian of an unusual sort. So include, as a requirement, that the taxonomy say what would count as settling whether a proposed new kind is real: what evidence, in what case, would decide it.

The distinctions I keep watching people collapse, and which I want the requirements to protect (say which of them are really load-bearing and which are only stipulative): competence versus comprehension, or genuine look-ahead versus as-if look-ahead; how a design was acquired versus how it is implemented; how plastic a system is within a lifetime versus how much of its design it got from others; transmission versus accumulation, i.e. local traditions that do not ratchet versus cumulative culture; the properties of an individual mind versus the properties of a lineage or a collective; and substrate-neutrality, that is, whether the taxonomy is supposed to apply to silicon at all.

Please also flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — that is the part I most want to see. The candidates I have in mind are: that the taxonomy be behaviourally operationalised, with no appeal to inner mechanism, against the requirement that it distinguish real look-ahead from a sufficiently large learned repertoire that mimics it; that it deliver discrete kinds, against the requirement that it handle intermediate and partial cases without ad hoc stipulation; and that it be substrate-neutral, against the requirement that it not count flexibility that is really just coverage of a training distribution. If you think the right response to one of these is to drop a requirement rather than to hold both, say which and why. Then tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding otherwise attractive accounts.

Finish with a short scorecard — a paragraph or a table, no more — on the four contenders I can see: the cumulative four-fold ladder itself, a single continuous dimension of plasticity or generality, a genuinely multi-dimensional space, and the deflationary line that the whole taxonomy is only ever a way of predicting behaviour from the intentional stance and that nothing corresponds to the kinds. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; the scorecard is for my own orientation and I will redo it anyway. No citations — I will supply those myself.
