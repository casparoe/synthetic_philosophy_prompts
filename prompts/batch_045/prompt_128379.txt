I have a personal stake in the moral-saints question, and I want a document rather than an essay.

Three years ago I sold a small business and began giving most of the proceeds away, and around the same time I left the string quartet I had played in since graduate school, on the reasoning that the rehearsal hours were not the best use of them. My cellist friend's verdict was that I had become impressive and smaller. I do not think she is wrong about the description and I do not think she is right that it counts against what I did, and after three years I still cannot say precisely why. So I am convening an adversarial collaboration, and I want you to draft the joint statement it would produce.

The two parties, neither of them a caricature:

[A] The saint-idealist: maximal moral goodness is a sound and genuinely desirable ideal for a human life, and its apparent unattractiveness comes from describing it badly. The saint need not be grim, single-minded, humorless, or incapable of friendship; saints may be rested and funny and deeply attached to particular people. Ideals also are not action-guides in the way obligations are: an ideal can mark the direction in which a life is better without licensing its own pursuit at every moment. The saint-idealist will concede that a life of joyless self-abnegation is not ideal, and denies that the pluralist's unflattering portraits are of maximal moral goodness properly described.

[B] The pluralist: even under the most generous description, maximal moral goodness is not a desirable ideal, because moral value is one value among others, and a life maximally organized around it cannot contain certain non-moral excellences — aesthetic accomplishment, absorption in an activity for its own sake, the kind of friendship that requires unearned attention — whose value is not moral value and is not cancelled by the moral gain. The pluralist will concede that those excellences are not owed to anyone and that failing to have them is not wrong, and insists instead on a distinction between what is permissible and what is worth admiring.

What the document should contain:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims: real, load-bearing, and phrased so that neither side is conceding something it would actually deny — not the sort of thing both could have signed before the collaboration began.

2. A disagreements table: for each residual disagreement, the formulation in the saint-idealist's own preferred wording and the formulation in the pluralist's, with a note from you wherever the two formulations are in fact the same claim wearing different words.

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. For each, give a case, argument, or piece of evidence both parties accept as bearing on it, and write down in advance what each predicts it will show. I want at least one crux whose test is empirical rather than conceptual; I have in mind things like whether saints are effective moral exemplars or alienating ones, and how saints and near-saints actually report on their lives in retrospect.

Two things I want forced into the document, because I think the real action is there and I have not seen them handled:

- Replaceability. The quartet did not stop playing; someone else took the chair, and the music still gets made. So the pluralist owes an answer to whether the value of a non-moral excellence is agent-relative — it matters that I am the one who is good at this — or whether it is a matter of how much of that excellence the world contains. If the latter, the saint-idealist seems to win the exchange outright. Make that argument as strong as it can be made before anyone is allowed to call it cold.

- Moral uncertainty. Suppose the saint is not certain what morality requires and must act on expected moral value across a range of moral views, some of which assign real weight to cultivation, relationship, or excellence. Does the maximally morally good life then have to contain the very non-moral excellences the pluralist is defending, as a hedge? If so, part of this dispute is not about ideals at all but about the shape of a probability distribution, and I want that part separated out and stated exactly, including what each side would have to believe about moral uncertainty for the hedge to fail.

4. Close with a short section saying which parts of the dispute are verbal, which are bedrock, and which are contingent on something we could in principle find out, and what, on your own diagnosis, would count as progress.

Format: the numbered agreed-claims section first, then the table, then the cruxes with paired predictions, then the diagnosis. No citations, no bibliographies, and no survey of what has been said in the literature — I have read it and know its shape. Aim for something I could hand to both of them without either asking for revisions.
