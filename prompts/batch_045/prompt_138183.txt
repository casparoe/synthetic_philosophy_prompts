I sit on the scientific advisory committee of a national food-safety and animal-health agency, and we are the body that decides which animal groups fall under our rules on stunning, humane slaughter and live transport. Coverage currently runs to all vertebrates and, since a revision a few years back, to octopuses, squid and cuttlefish; that last extension was adopted explicitly as a precautionary measure rather than as a finding that cephalopods are certainly conscious, which means there is no written standard anywhere in our records for what we were being precautionary about. Petitions now arrive every few weeks — industry bodies asking us to add or to exempt a group, welfare organisations asking us to extend, and several times a year a file on something for which no studies exist at all. Our decisions are legally reviewable and must be published with reasons. The committee is six people (a veterinarian, a neuroscientist, a fisheries biologist, an entomologist, an ethicist and a producer representative), we meet one day a month, and no member can realistically give a petition more than about an hour of reading. So what I need is a decision procedure we could actually run inside those constraints, not a philosophical essay on which animals are conscious.

What I want is that procedure, written so that a committee member with no philosophy training can execute it: the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as an adequate answer to each, the points at which the procedure returns a yes and stops, the points at which it returns a no and stops, and — most importantly — the points at which it has to stop and hand the matter over as a question about values rather than evidence. I also want you to mark honestly which of its steps are judgment calls wearing the costume of a test. Three things in particular have to be built in, because these are where our past discussions have collapsed. First, the evidence arrives in kinds that are not interchangeable. A taxon may have neural hardware of the sort we associate with pain in vertebrates; or it may show the flexible, trade-off-making behaviour we associate with pain — avoiding a place where it was hurt even when a food reward is on offer, tending an injury at some cost, taking an analgesic option only when injured; and very often a group looks impressive on the behavioural markers while having a nervous system organised unlike ours, or the other way round. We are also regularly reminded that reacting to a harmful stimulus is not the same as feeling it, and I want the procedure to preserve that distinction without letting it become a reason never to decide anything. So: how should we combine evidence of these different kinds, how do we avoid counting a single underlying capacity three times because it shows up in three different assays, and what do we do when the only studies available were done on a related species — at what taxonomic grain should a classification be made, and how far up or down may we extrapolate? Second, every marker we use is, taken on its own, something a clever unconscious machine could in principle be built to do. I want a step that asks whether the whole body of evidence for a taxon could be produced by a system that lacks the very thing we are trying to detect, and I want to know what kind of evidence would be needed to get past that step. Third, the costs of error are not symmetric: covering a group that is not conscious imposes real costs on producers and on our inspectors' time, while failing to cover a group that is conscious means we are authorising pain on a very large scale, sometimes across enormous numbers of animals. I want the procedure to say where the burden of proof sits, how the size of the stakes changes how much evidence we demand, and — for the direction nobody enjoys — what standard applies when a submission asks us to remove coverage from a group we already protect. Where the answer is that the evidence is insufficient, it should say what would have to change and when we re-open the file.

Then run it on three live files. (1) A petition from a welfare coalition to extend stunning and transport rules to decapod crustaceans — crabs, lobsters, prawns — currently treated as unprotected invertebrates, whose supply chains here are large and, as far as our economic briefing goes, expensive to retrofit. (2) A petition to extend the rules to the insects now being farmed at industrial scale for animal feed, mainly black soldier fly and house cricket, where the literature per species is thin or ambiguous and the number of candidate species is enormous. (3) A submission from a commercial fishing association arguing that the fish we already cover should come out of coverage, on the grounds that the brain structures which do the relevant work in mammals are absent in fish. At least one of the three should come out in a way I will find inconvenient, and I would much rather see that here than in our published reasons; for each file I want to see where exactly the procedure stopped and why. Finally, tell me what this procedure cannot do: which questions it is bound to get wrong or to leave open, and which ones we should simply stop pretending we can settle from evidence.
