I would like a number, not a survey of positions — and I would like the reasoning behind it exposed well enough that I can see where it is most fragile.

The proposition concerns Kavka's toxin puzzle. Set the case up as it is usually set up: an eccentric billionaire offers you a large sum if, at some point during the next twenty-four hours, you come to intend to drink, the following afternoon, a vial of a toxin that will make you thoroughly miserable for a day but will do no lasting harm. The money is transferred the moment you form the intention; you never have to drink, and you cannot be compelled to. Assume, as the puzzle assumes, that you know all this and that you also know that once the money is in your account you will have no reason left to drink and will therefore not drink.

Call the target claim P: in that situation, a normal adult agent can, at will, come to have a genuine intention to drink the toxin the following afternoon — an intention of the same kind, and ascribing the same attitude, as her intention to do some ordinary thing she fully expects to do — notwithstanding that she now knows she will not drink.

Two stipulations. First, P is a claim about what is possible for such an agent, not about whether it would be rational, or sane, or worthwhile, to try; if you think the two questions cannot be separated, say so and treat that as one of the sub-questions below. Second, I want P assessed not only for the original case but for two variants I have not seen discussed, since a view that handles the original case but not the variants is worth less than it looks:

Iterated Toxin. The same offer is made every morning for a year, each day's payout conditional only on that day's intention, and the offer is guaranteed in advance whatever you do. Now the agent might try to bootstrap: drink on one occasion in order to become the sort of person who can form the relevant intention at will, thereby making the remaining days collectable. Does iteration change your answer to P, and does the bootstrapping option tell us anything about what was blocking intention-formation in the original case?

Inverse Toxin. Everything is as before except that the required intention is an intention not to φ, where φ is something you would otherwise certainly do for its own sake and will certainly do once the money is in hand: you are paid to intend, on Tuesday, not to eat on Wednesday the slice of cake that will be set in front of you and that you dearly want and will in fact eat. Is P more or less plausible here, and if the number moves, what does the movement locate?

Give me your credence in P, and decompose it. I would like, at minimum, numbers on the following, each with a one-line justification, followed by an explicit statement of how they combine — conjunctively, as a weighted average over theories of intention, or otherwise — and of which correlations you are assuming:

1. Whether intending to φ, in the relevant unconditional sense, entails believing, or being disposed to believe, or accepting, that one will φ.
2. Whether agents can form intentions directly at will at all, as opposed to intentions arising only as the upshot of desire, practical reasoning, habit, or external prompting.
3. Whether the agent's prediction that she will not drink really amounts to knowledge, or whether a successfully formed intention would itself raise the probability of drinking enough that the prediction is not certain.
4. Whether what is achievable in this situation is an intention at all, or something intention-adjacent — a desire, a wish, a plan to try, a standing policy, a half-belief sustained by self-deception.
5. How much of your number is theory-relative: how much probability you assign to there being a determinate answer here that is not merely an artifact of which account of intention one adopts, and how much to the dispute being partly verbal.

Then tell me which single sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, which of your own component numbers you are least confident in and what would move it, and what would count as resolving P — what kind of argument, case, or empirical finding would actually settle it rather than restate a position.

For calibration: my own credence in P is around 0.6, and someone in my department who works on action theory puts it near 0.15. On your decomposition, which of the sub-questions above is the most likely home of that disagreement?
