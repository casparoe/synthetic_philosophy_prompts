I have supported myself by writing novels for thirty years, and a pattern in the way my colleagues reason about career decisions has begun to bother me.

Two cases. A friend was offered the choice between a modest guaranteed advance for a dependable book in a series she had already established, and no advance but a far larger share of royalties on a riskier one. She multiplied the share by her agent's estimates, found the second option worth considerably more on average, and took it. So did several of us, on the stated ground that a writing life contains many such choices and that the averages will look after themselves. She has now taken that bet four times, been unlucky twice, and cannot place the fifth book on the terms the first would have secured. Another writer of my acquaintance, who has never once taken the option with the larger average payout, has had a steadily widening career and is now offered more than either of us.

What bothers me is the direction of the argument. The defence of averaging — there will be many decisions, so expect the average — seems to get weaker, not stronger, the more decisions there are. Over many choices, the person who takes the higher average every time becomes nearly certain to end up worse than the person who does not, and the gap widens with each additional decision. That inversion is the thing I cannot name.

I should say what I take it not to be. It is not caution about large losses: no single one of these choices threatened to ruin anyone, and nobody was risking money already in hand. It is not that her estimates were poor; suppose them exactly right. And it is not a matter of spreading risk: she could not take both options and had no one to share them with.

A friend in finance, hearing this, said something about geometric averages that I did not follow, and then gave an account which assumed one is reinvesting money and can lose the whole of one's stake on any single bet. Neither is my situation. In a writing career the floor is soft: a poorly selling book does not bankrupt you, it reduces the print run, the review coverage and the shelf space available to the next one, so that the rate at which a career grows depends on where it currently is, and there is a level below which it decays slowly towards nothing without ever quite arriving.

Does the phenomenon I have described have an established name, and does the standard treatment of it survive the soft floor? If the nearest established concept fits only imperfectly, I would rather have the mismatches spelled out — including how the thing differs from ordinary aversion to risk, and from ruin in the usual sense — than be handed a label I can repeat. One boundary case matters to me especially: does any of this still apply to a writer who intends to write exactly three more books and then stop, so that there is no long run to appeal to? Please answer in British English.
