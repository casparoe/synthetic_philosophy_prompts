Sanity-check each move against the real maths as you go; I'll notice if you fudge it.

Context: I'm a PhD student in philosophy (phil of maths, drifting towards mathematical practice) and I help run a first-year module on paradoxes and infinity. We do Hilbert's Hotel and then Cantor's diagonal argument in the same week, and every single year the same thing happens in the exams: the students are totally fine with a full hotel taking in more guests, they nod along to the diagonal argument, and then they write "the hotel can't cope because there are just too many real numbers" — they've converted a diagonal *construction* into a complaint about capacity, and missed that the failure is intrinsic to the list itself, so that no rearrangement or doubling trick is going to help. I want to hand out a poem that gets the mechanics right by building the mechanics into the structure, so that the poem is the proof rather than a decoration of it.

Roughly what I have in mind, but feel free to improve on it: six-line stanzas, a refrain (your wording) standing in for whoever it is the hotel keeps finding room for. In the first stretch the refrain sits in line 1, then line 2, then line 3, as each new guest arrives — everything already in the stanza shuffling along one to make space. When the coach with countably infinitely many people turns up, do the doubling move: the refrain should now occupy the odd-numbered lines, same six lines as before, everyone else having gone to the even ones. If you can fit the infinitely-many-coaches case in as well, brilliant, but that's optional. Crucially the countable part has to show actual procedures (guest in n goes to n+1; then everyone to 2n) rather than shrugging "well, it's infinite". Then the guest whose name never ends: here the refrain has to fail in a way that *is* the diagonal. My idea was to give each guest already in the register two or three fixed epithets, and to build the newcomer by inverting the nth trait of the nth guest — so the newcomer differs from every entry on the list somewhere and therefore matches none of them, and the poem should make clear that this works against whatever list the manager happens to have ended up with, not because the list is too short. End with a short envoi outside the scheme, in the voice of the night porter, who is a finitist: he doesn't think the hotel was ever a coherent thing to imagine and thinks the register is just marks on paper. Don't let him win and don't let the manager win — leave it open, because that's honestly where the seminar ends up. One aside I half-remember, which you can use or drop: I think Hilbert's own view was that nothing like this is realised in the physical world — but don't quote anyone, I don't need citations, and if I've got that wrong just leave it out.

Constraints: around 40 lines, no footnotes, no jargon that has to be explained (if a term like "uncountable" appears it should have earned its place in the line), and readable aloud in about ninety seconds — it's going out as a one-page handout and I might read it at the pub reading group afterwards.
