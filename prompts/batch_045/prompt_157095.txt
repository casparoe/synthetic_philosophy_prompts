I'm a sociology PhD student putting together the second session of a graduate seminar I'm running on cohort analysis, and I need a diagnostic instrument rather than a literature review. The session is called something like "Do generations exist?" and it always goes badly: the demographers in the room think the question is about whether birth-year dummies survive a specification test, the cultural sociologists think it's about whether anyone identifies as a Millennial, and the philosophers think it's about whether "generation" behaves like "electron" or like "recession." Everyone leaves having restated their own view. So I want a classification tool — part decision tree, part quiz — that sorts students by what they are willing to say about cases and then tells them which position they've ended up in.

The instrument I have in mind: nine to twelve diagnostic items, each a concrete case or a forced choice between two concrete claims, phrased so that a first-year graduate student can answer it without knowing any of the position-labels and without looking anything up. Then a routing key: each item's answers move the student down a tree whose branch nodes are the claims that actually separate the positions, and whose leaves are named views. Please build the tree from the logical space first — lay out the questions and the answer-combinations they permit — and only then locate the actual positions in that space, rather than starting from a list of names and writing questions to match.

I've been drafting a three-way distinction that I want the tree to lean on, since it does much of the separating work: a "cutoff cohort" is defined by an interval of birth years; an "exposure cohort" is defined by shared exposure to some event or condition during a putative formative window; a "narrative cohort" is defined by uptake of a generational label in self-description and in how others address a group. Two pressure points should also be built into the questions. The first I call the boundary lottery: take two people born a few months apart on either side of a conventional cutoff and ask which non-trivial property — anything other than date of birth itself — distinguishes them. The second I call the diagonal problem: if cohort membership is fixed by shared formative exposure, and different domains (schooling, war, media technology, pandemic, labour market entry) have different exposure profiles with different onset years, then the slices come out misaligned, and "the cohort" is domain-relative.

Cases I want used, at least most of them: the boundary-lottery pair; a migrant who arrived in the country at twenty-four and encountered the local media and political history only as an adult; a child raised from infancy by grandparents of an earlier birth-year stratum; a birth-year stratum that shares an objective demographic location but never develops any shared self-consciousness or distinctive culture; a birth cohort that gets named, and that begins to describe itself in those terms, only in its mid-fifties; someone born in the boundary years who adopts an in-between label; the application of an Anglophone cohort label to a country with a different schooling timeline, a different internet diffusion curve and its own home-grown cohort vocabulary; a hypothetical population with no shared mass-media exposure at all; and — as a stipulated, purely hypothetical result, not something to be sourced — a large cross-national audit finding near-zero discontinuities in outcomes at the conventional cutoffs alongside strong continuous birth-year trends. I'd also like one item on whether a generation can be spoken of as a bearer of responsibility, since that's what actually animates the political arguments my students arrive with.

The leaves I need available, with one sentence each on the view's least comfortable commitment: realism about sharply bounded cohorts with genuine discontinuities; continuous-cohort gradualism on which "generation" is coarse binning of a smooth birth-year surface; exposure-profile pluralism on which there are many overlapping cohorts and no canonical one; generations as unique historical particulars, born and passing away with their defining events; generations as repeatable types that recur in a cycle of archetypes; looping social-kind constructivism on which the cohort is real and causally effective because it is classified and internalised; a more skeptical discursive view on which cohort labels are journalistic and marketing artefacts with no kind behind them; eliminativism about the term; a deflationary instrumentalism that keeps the word as a stipulated bin while making no ontological claim; and purpose-relative pluralism on which several incompatible slicings are equally legitimate for different inquiries.

Two things I especially want from the key. First, flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree cannot actually separate, rather than inventing a question to force a split — I suspect the gradualist, the eliminativist and the deflationary instrumentalist may give identical answers to every case and differ only in what they propose to do with the word, and I'd rather the handout say so. Second, give me a short appendix on the combinations your logical space permits that nobody occupies, with a one-line diagnosis of each: is the combination empty because it is incoherent, because it is merely unattractive, or because no one has bothered to defend it? Please don't spend the answer on literature review — no reading lists or citations; the questions, the tree, the key and the appendix are what I need.
