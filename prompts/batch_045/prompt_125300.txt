So — I'm a novelist, mid-rewrite on the last third of a book where one of my characters kills herself, and my editor has come back with the usual list: cut the method, don't make it look like a solution, no lingering on the aftermath, resources page at the back. She's basically reciting the standard media reporting guidelines at me. I'm not fighting her, but when I read Hume's essay on suicide — the one he never published in his lifetime, where he argues roughly that suicide offends no one, that a deliberate human act is as much part of the course of nature as anything else so it can't be an invasion of providence, that ceasing to be useful to society isn't the same as injuring it, and that the person living the life is the best judge of whether it's worth keeping — my chapter starts looking morally fine and her guidelines start looking like superstition with a helpline.

So take the 13 Reasons Why blow-up as my test case: Netflix series, 2017, a long explicit suicide scene, mental-health groups and schools saying the depiction itself would do harm, and a couple of years later, as I remember it, the scene shortened and warnings bolted on. Grant me for the sake of argument that depictions like that sometimes do raise the rate among young viewers — I know the studies are argued over, that's not my question. What I want is the mapping run both ways. On Hume's premises, who is supposed to do what here: the network, the showrunner, the critics, the audience? Which of his arguments does the contagion evidence actually touch, and which does it pass straight through? Is the duty-to-society premise the one that breaks, or can Hume absorb harm to third parties the same way he absorbs everything else, as just more consequences flowing from general laws? And what does the case show about the theory — a real anomaly, or a case it was never shaped to speak to, being a theory about an agent's duties pointed at a question about effects on other people?

One more thing I've been telling myself and want checked. I've started splitting depictions into ones that transmit reasons (they give the reader a rationale: here is why this is the sensible thing) and ones that transmit it causally, by imitation or contagion, without giving anyone a reason at all. On my split Hume's own essay is a reasons-text and the show mostly isn't — which is either a defence of Hume or a devastating one, depending on which kind the guidelines are really aimed at. Does that distinction hold up, or is it something I invented to let myself off the hook?

And when you answer, keep two columns for me: what people who actually work on Hume and on suicide prevention agree on, and what's a live fight. I've hear it said that Hume might not even have meant the essay seriously — tell me if that's a real position in the scholarship or just something people repeat.
