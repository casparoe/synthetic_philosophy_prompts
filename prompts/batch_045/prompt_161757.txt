I am applying for a one-year postdoctoral fellowship in philosophy of science and need a proposal of roughly 1,200 words. The review panel is split between philosophers of physics and philosophers of machine learning, and neither half will know the other's literature, so the proposal must be legible to both and must not rest on any empirical result I would later have to look up. Please do not include a bibliography or literature review by citation; I will supply references myself.

My half-formed idea is this. The debate between structural realists and their opponents has been conducted almost entirely over structures that arrive already stated in a formalism: a set of equations, a group of transformations, a network of relations one can write down. Both the realist's continuity claim and the standard anti-realist replies — the induction over discarded theories, and the worry that a purely structural claim is trivially satisfiable by any domain large enough to carry the right pattern of relations — are framed against that background. But an increasing amount of scientific structure now reaches us in a form no one can state: the internal variables of a trained model, which predict, generalize across regimes, and support interventions, but which are given only as a configuration of parameters rather than as a sentence. I want to know whether structural realism has anything to say about such structures, and whether they put pressure on the debate from a direction that has not been worked.

The angle I keep circling, which I can't yet make precise enough to defend, is a retrospective test. Take historical episodes of theory change where we know, in hindsight, which parts of the superseded theory were retained and which were abandoned, and where the data available before the change exists in machine-readable form. Train models on the pre-change data alone, with no trace of the successor theory, and score whether the structure they settle on is the structure that survived or the structure that was discarded. The realist's case rests on the claim that what persists across theory change is structure; if that is a real diagnostic rather than a post hoc rationalization, a structure-finding procedure with no access to the future should be able to pick out survivors in cases we can already score. If it systematically cannot, the suspicion is that continuity has been identified retrospectively, by selecting whichever relations happen to be recoverable in whatever formalism came next. That would be a result about the debate that the existing arguments cannot deliver.

Turn this into a proposal with: a precise statement of the question, formulated so that it has a determinate answer; an explanation of why both the moderate and the stronger version of structural realism leave it open, and of why the obvious alternative approaches — treating the question as one about scientific discovery generally, or as one about whether machines can understand — miss what is distinctive here; the method, set out month by month across twelve months and doable with modest compute and no proprietary models; a criterion for when the same structure recurs across two systems that share no vocabulary, which does not collapse into mere predictive equivalence; the range of possible conclusions and which considerations would decide among them; what would count as the project failing; and an honest statement of what might make it too large for one year. Include a short closing section, since the panel will ask, on what any of this would be worth to scientists and funders deciding whether to trust a variable that a model found and nobody can paraphrase.
