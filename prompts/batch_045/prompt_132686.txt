Compare the no-free-lunch theorems of optimization and supervised learning with Hume's problem of induction, treating both as egalitarian results about methods of inference — results that appear to level every candidate method down to parity with every other — and then assess whether either one has anything real to teach the other. Argue from first principles about the structure of the arguments; I am not interested in who said what, or in what the secondary literature has concluded.

To fix terms, work in the following toy setting. Let the input space consist of the four strings of length two, the label space be {0,1}, and the hypothesis space be the set of all sixteen functions from inputs to labels. A learner sees two labelled inputs, chosen in advance, and must predict the labels of the other two. Assume every learner fits the two training labels perfectly, since nothing constrains it from doing so. Now impose, first, a uniform distribution over the sixteen functions. Among the four functions consistent with any given training sample, exactly two assign 0 and two assign 1 to each test input, so every learner's expected test accuracy is one half, whatever it does and however it generalises. Impose instead an adversary who fixes the target function after seeing the learner's rule: every learner's worst-case test accuracy is then zero. In this toy, both distributions produce a tie, but they produce it for different reasons, and those reasons invite different conclusions. Begin your answer by making that difference exact, and by saying what each tie does and does not license you to infer.

Then address the following, in order.

First: state both results in your own terms, sharply enough that the respects in which they are analogous become visible. My suggestion is that the analogy runs along at least three axes — a measure over problems versus quantification over possible worlds; expected accuracy versus worst-case accuracy; and a conclusion about performance versus a conclusion about justification. Say which of these axes carries the analogy and which quietly breaks it, and whether the break is deep enough that the comparison was ill-posed from the start.

Second: the standard reply to the no-free-lunch theorems is that the uniform measure is the wrong measure, because the problems we actually face are structured, smooth, or compressible rather than drawn uniformly. Call this the structured-problems premise. Is the structured-problems premise itself an inductive assumption, and if so, does the Humean worry simply reappear one level up, as a problem about the sequence of problems rather than the sequence of observations? If you think it escapes the regress, say precisely why — for instance, whether it is meant as a belief, a posit, or a constraint on the choice of method.

Third: consider the mirror-image move. Reichenbach-style vindication argues, roughly, that if prediction is possible at all then the simple rule of enumerative induction succeeds, which grants the inductive method permission without granting it a reason. Does that argument have the same logical shape as the standard machine-learning reply to the no-free-lunch theorems? If it does, have the two literatures been making one move under two names, and does recognising that change your view of how threatening either result is?

Fourth: suppose we replace the uniform measure with a prior that assigns to each candidate world a weight decaying exponentially in the length of that world's shortest description. Say whether this is a solution to the problem of induction, a restatement of the inductive assumption in new vocabulary, or a renaming of it, and whether the no-free-lunch theorems give you any new reason to prefer it over a uniform prior, given that the theorems are silent about non-uniform measures.

Close with a verdict, kept short: state which of the two results you take to be the deeper skeptical threat, or state that the question has no determinate answer, and in either case give the single premise on which your verdict turns. Where your discussion turns on a distinction, illustrate it in the toy setting above rather than only describing it.
