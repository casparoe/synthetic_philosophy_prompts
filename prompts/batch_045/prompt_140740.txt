Hey — I'm writing a novel and there's a scene I can't get right because I genuinely can't tell which of my characters is winning the argument, and its hard to write a fight scene when you don't know who's landing the punches. Two of them, Ines and Hal, are at a rented cottage in October after the funeral of their old PhD supervisor, both a bit drunk, and they end up arguing about whether the many-worlds picture can actually account for the Born rule — that is, about whether a theory on which every outcome happens somewhere can explain why the numbers in the lab notebook come out distributed the way they do. Ines takes the physics side, Hal does philosophy of science, and neither is meant to be the fool of the scene.

Their exchange goes roughly like this (I've numbered the bits I need checked):

1. Ines: the famous version of the problem is a strawman. Nobody serious thinks you should count branches and read off fifty-fifty. Start with the easy case: measure a spin in an equal superposition and you get two branches, and nothing in the setup distinguishes them, so by symmetry anyone beforehand assigns equal credence to each. That's ordinary equiprobability reasoning, not an Everettian cheat.

2. Hal: that only works when the amplitudes are equal. The Born rule says probability goes as the squared amplitude, and amplitudes come in ratios like 1 to root two and worse, irrational ones. Symmetry can't hand you a third and two thirds. So you need something more than symmetry.

3. Ines: right, you need two more things, and they're almost platitudes. First, how you carve up the future shouldn't matter for what to expect: if one branch later splits into several sub-branches, the thing you were uncertain about hasn't changed, so weights have to add across a split. Second, a branch's weight shouldn't depend on anything beyond that branch's own amplitude — no labels, no history, no facts about which lab you're sitting in. But a quantity that adds across a split and depends only on the amplitude has to be proportional to the squared amplitude; there's essentially only one such function. So the Born weight isn't bolted on, it's forced.

4. Hal: forced as what, though? What you've shown is that if there is a weighting with those features, it's the Born weight. You haven't shown that weight is probability — the thing that should govern what you bet, or the thing that explains the statistics. And your two platitudes aren't free: "how we carve up the future doesn't matter" is close to the very thesis in dispute, because the carve-up is what's supposed to be carrying the probability. Besides, the usual way Everettians cash out "the statistics come out right" is to say that Born-typical branches are almost all of the multiverse — typical with respect to exactly that measure. So the measure is doing the work you said was being derived.

5. Ines: it isn't an extra posit, it falls out of the same two constraints. If you want a measure of how much of the multiverse is like this one, and it's a function of the state, additive, and insensitive to labels, the same argument pins it down. The rivals don't: counting branches gives you a number that depends on when you decide a branch has branched, which is pure bookkeeping with no principled stopping point.

6. Hal: here's the case I keep coming back to, and I want it answered concretely rather than by waving at theorems. Suppose the experiment is done, the result is recorded, you look at it — and then, before anybody pays out, the branches are made to recombine: the record is erased, the superposition is restored, in principle perfectly. There's one person again. Who pays? What should you have bet beforehand? If the answer is "the Born weight of the branch you were on," then the weights of branches that no longer exist are doing the work, and your story is really a story about a decomposition that was only ever approximate and temporary. And branching is only ever approximate: decoherence never completes, there's always some tiny amplitude for the branches to come back together, so the weight of my branch never has an exact value. So either your probabilities are only approximate — fine, but say so — or you have to wait for the end of the universe to define them, which is useless for deciding anything now.

7. Ines: I'll say so. They're approximate, to exactly the accuracy that branches are, and branches are as real as tables or hurricanes or anything else that's emergent and a bit fuzzy at the edges; the leftover interference terms are absurdly small. Recombination is a genuine case and I'll give you an answer: if two branches merge, the merged person's credences are fixed by the same additivity — credences add across what merged, and where outcomes are still distinct you use the weight of the branch that results. Nothing in the machinery breaks.

8. Hal: then stop calling it a derivation. What you've actually got is a package — emergent branches, weights as squared amplitudes, and weight as the thing that tells you both what's typical and what to care about — and the achievement is showing the package hangs together. But the bit doing the real work is the identification of weight with what a self-locating person should care about, and that's a philosophical posit. Meanwhile the collapse theorist who just says "one outcome occurs, with probability equal to the squared amplitude" buys the same fit to the data with less machinery.

9. Ines: except the collapse theorist hasn't bought anything, because "with probability" is exactly what needed explaining and they've simply helped themselves to it, plus a collapse mechanism and a preferred basis. And this was never an Everettian problem anyway: in any deterministic theory you can't read probabilities off the dynamics, so holding many-worlds to the standard of producing them from the bare formalism is special pleading.
