I'm a first-year CS PhD student, and for the last few months I've been building, mostly as a way of forcing myself to learn provability logic properly, a toy self-modifying agent: a few hundred lines that do bounded proof search in a weak arithmetic and then decide whether to hand their job to a rewritten version of themselves. I keep hitting the same wall, and I've tried to write out an argument for why the wall isn't an artifact of my toy but a problem about self-trust in general. The trouble is that the argument now seems to show something I cannot accept, and I'd rather find the broken step before I put any of this in a paper.

Write □φ for: the agent's own system S proves φ, and assume S is arithmetically capable enough for the Hilbert–Bernays derivability conditions and the fixed point lemma. (1) An agent counts as trusting its own reasoning only if, having derived φ, it is prepared to treat that derivation as a reason for φ, i.e. to rely on the step from □φ to φ. (2) For an agent whose reasoning is exhausted by S, relying on a principle means having it available inside S; and the only way to have the step from □φ to φ available for arbitrary φ is to have the reflection instances □φ → φ as theorems or axioms of S. (3) By Löb's theorem, for every φ, if S proves □φ → φ then S proves φ; so for every φ that S does not already prove, S does not prove □φ → φ. (4) Hence the agent cannot in general help itself to the step from □φ to φ: the only instances it can obtain are the ones it did not need, because it already had the conclusion. (5) And this is not really a problem about uniformity: nothing in (3) depends on the schema being available all at once, so fix any single φ the agent lacks and (3) still says it cannot obtain □φ → φ for that φ. Self-trust is therefore idle — it never does justificatory work at any point where the agent actually needed it. (6) But that covers every φ of practical interest: that action a produces outcome O, that the rewritten search procedure still only outputs theorems, that this term in my derivation of the grocery total is right. So no agent whose reasoning is captured by an arithmetically capable formal system can ever use its own reasoning as a reason for anything. (7) The argument also seems to generalize past the single-agent case, which is what worries me about the toy. Suppose the agent wants to build a successor whose proof system is strictly weaker than its own — my descending endorser, where the parent hands down a shrunken system through a hard-wired gate and soundness is supposed to be secured from outside by induction along the descending chain, so that the agent never represents its own soundness as a sentence at all. The parent still has to rely on the step from the child's deriving φ to φ; and (3) runs with the child's provability predicate just as well. Restricting reflection to some narrow syntactic class does not obviously help either, since the fixed point used in the proof of Löb's theorem can be built inside any class closed under the obvious operations. (8) Weakening trust to mere high confidence does not seem to help: if the agent's credence in φ conditional on its having derived φ is at least 1 − ε for every φ, and its own credence function is computable and describable in S, then the same diagonal construction runs with assigns credence at least 1 − ε to replacing proves, and partial trust collapses back into full trust or into none. (9) So the very idea of a rational agent relying on its own reasoning is incoherent. (10) Worse, (6) applies to the reasoning in (1)–(8) itself, so I have no reason to accept any of it, including (1), which I still find completely compelling when I read it on its own.

I would most like to keep (1) and (3) — (1) looks like a platitude about what distinguishes treating something as your own reasoning from treating it as a noise source, and (3) is just Löb's theorem, which I have checked in the arithmetical case — and I am much more willing to give up (2), (5), (7) or (8). Which step should I reject, why does it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost me? In particular: is there a real distinction between relying on reflection and having it as a theorem, does the probabilistic version in (8) actually collapse, and is the descending-endorser gate with external soundness a genuine repair or just (2) smuggled back in somewhere I can't see?
