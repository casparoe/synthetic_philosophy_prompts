Hi — I'm on the three-person selectboard in the town I grew up in, about 1,800 people. I got here by complaining about a gravel pit at a meeting last spring, which in hindsight was a mistake. We do everything at open town meeting, eight or nine times a year, and I've now lost three evenings to the same argument: who gets to vote on this particular question. Not who's a voter in general — who's in the demos for this decision. It keeps coming up because we're a small town where most of what we decide lands on people who aren't residents: the pit that puts silt in the river running through my parents' town downstream, a well and water ordinance that lands hardest on the year-round crew at the dairy (a lot of whom aren't citizens and can't vote in anything, anywhere), and a twenty-year bond my kids will still be paying off when they're my age.

Constraints: our town counsel is a guy two counties over that we email, the moderator has to announce eligibility out loud before the vote, whatever rule we use has to be one page in plain language and survive being complained about to the state, and realistically I get an afternoon per warrant article. So I don't want an essay on democratic theory, I want something I can run.

What I'd like is a decision procedure, in the order the questions get asked, with what counts as a good enough answer to each one, where it just stops with a no, and where it stops because the question isn't factual at all and has to go to the board as a values call (or to town counsel as a legal one, and please say which is which). I've read a little around this — the "all affected interests" idea versus the "all subjected to the law" one, the point that affectedness has no natural cutoff because everything affects everybody a little bit, and the nasty recursion where whoever decides who's in the demos is itself making a binding decision about the people they left out — so ground it in that if it's useful, but don't send me names and dates. I need the distinctions to survive, not a reading list.

Some things I want the procedure to actually handle, and tell me straight if any of these are wrong-headed:

- Being causally affected (your welfare goes up or down), being bound by the rule once it passes, and being able to see the rule as something you helped author. Are those three separate tests? Do you need any one of them or all three?
- The "you have your own town, go there" move. My instinct is that if the affected outsiders have a political community of their own that can take the issue up, that's a reason not to seat them at our meeting. I also worry that's just a machine for never seating anybody.
- Reciprocity: do we owe a vote to outsiders only if their town would seat us in the same situation? I kind of hate this but it gets said out loud at every meeting.
- The recursion. Is there any way to deal with it in practice that isn't just "use the widest electorate you can for boundary decisions themselves and write down the objection"?
- Time: people who are only physically here in July, and people who don't exist yet.

Then run it on three real ones: (1) the gravel pit rezoning, with the downstream town my parents live in; (2) the twenty-year bond bundled with closing the elementary school — does my fifteen-year-old get a vote, do the teachers who live over the line, do parents get anything extra per kid; (3) the water ordinance that raises costs for the dairy crew, some of whom are noncitizens. I have a feeling your procedure is going to tell me to do something on that third one that state law won't let us do, and if that's where it lands, say so plainly instead of building me a rule we can't use.

One of the three should come out in a way I don't like — I'd rather find out which now than at the meeting — and don't tell me all three are genuinely hard.

Last thing: for each step, tell me how much of it is actually where people who work on this have landed versus you being sensible on the fly, which step you'd drop first if someone who does this for a living read it over, and what would change your mind about the biggest call in the whole procedure. Also: say plainly what a procedure like this will never settle, no matter how carefully I run it.
