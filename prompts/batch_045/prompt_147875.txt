hey — take this one seriously and think carefully before writing anything: you're a professional philosopher of religion who also writes formal verse, and I need a poem that would survive being read aloud at the end of a seminar, not something that sounds wise for four lines and then collapses.

we're closing a reading group on al-Ghazali's occasionalism — the Ash'arite line, the attack on necessary connection in the Incoherence, the famous case of fire and the cotton, no real secondary causation, the world re-created from moment to moment, and the regularity we see being God's habit rather than a law baked into the things themselves. instead of a summary I want to end on a poem, and I want it to be a ghazal in English — loosely in the Persian/Urdu tradition, and I mean it should actually respect the constraints: 8–10 couplets, each couplet able to stand on its own, a rhyme right before a fixed refrain, and a final couplet where the speaker turns and addresses someone directly rather than just closing an image.

here's the part I care most about, and please brainstorm for a minute before you start writing: I want the form doing the metaphysical work. the refrain coming back identical every single couplet should *be* the habit — by the third or fourth time, the reader should already be leaning toward it, expecting the next line to follow the way the last one did, and that felt expectation is exactly what an occasionalist thinks necessity really amounts to from the inside. so make the refrain the regularity itself, not a slogan about the regularity.

then, once: in that final couplet, break it — withhold the refrain, or return it warped, one word off. on this view a miracle isn't a violation of a law, it's the one who usually does it this way doing it another way, and I want the break to land as a break, not as a punchline or a tidy resolution.

voice: the speaker works in a cotton workshop, tending the fire that dries the cloth — someone with every practical reason to think fire burns cotton and, on their own theory, no theoretical reason to. make them sound like a person at the end of a shift: specific, a little tired, hands doing the work. they still snatch the hand back from the flame, and the poem shouldn't decide whether that's coherent or not — leave it standing. keep it accurate too: al-Ghazali isn't denying that the cotton burns or that we see the sequence, he's denying the necessary link between them, and someone who's actually read the Incoherence should be able to tell you know the difference. no footnotes, no glossary, at most a couple of Arabic terms and only where they earn their place, nothing that needs looking up, and no devotional swell at the end — it shouldn't turn into a hymn.

so: sketch which couplet does which move first (what the opening couplet sets up, where the reader's anticipation of the refrain first becomes noticeable, what the last full-refrain couplet claims, how the closing couplet turns), then write it, 16–20 lines.
