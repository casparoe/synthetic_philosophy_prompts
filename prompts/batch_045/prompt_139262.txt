Hi — I'm a journalist, I mostly cover science and the ideas beat, and I'm finally writing the piece I've been threatening to write for a year about why so much of public life has migrated from lying to not-quite-lying: the non-denial denial, the spokesperson who answers the question she wishes she'd been asked, the university press release that says the finding in mice and lets the headline do the rest. My brother-in-law is a litigator and keeps insisting the whole distinction is lawyer folklore, and the philosophy I've waded through seems to spend nearly all its time on one case (man with an axe at the door, wants to know where your friend is) and on whether you've made an assertion when you implicate something rather than say it flat out. Before I go and interview three people who will each defend a position they've already published, I want the spec. What does an account of the moral difference between lying and misleading — or an account of why there isn't one — actually have to deliver to be worth printing? I dont mean the intellectual history and I dont mean who said what; I mean the list of cases and distinctions the theory has to get right, the reason each one earns its place on the list, and an honest flag on which entries are genuine requirements and which are just somebody's favourite view smuggled in as a constraint.

Here are the cases I want the list built out of rather than retrofitted to, roughly in the order they annoyed me. (a) The press officer who gives a literally true sentence to a reporter, knowing the reporter's desk will strip the hedge: she didn't design the false belief, she foresaw it and scheduled the briefing around it. (b) One sentence, two audiences at once — specialists who hear a hedge as a technical qualifier, lay readers who hear nothing. (c) My favourite, and the one nobody will bite on: a system that only ever emits true sentences but selects which true sentences to emit by scoring them on how many readers walk away with a false belief. (d) The old diplomatic formula where everyone in the room knows the discount rule — if there's a wrong there, is it the deception or the convention-breaking? (e) Staying silent while someone forms a false belief you neither planted nor were asked about. (f) The reverse of the usual puzzle: saying something you know is false but expecting to be disbelieved, or saying it in a form you know the hearer will mis-parse into the truth. Also, separately, whatever has to be said about blame versus wrongness — a theory that can only tell me a lie is worse, and not who is at fault and how much, is no use to me.

What I want back is exactly five numbered requirements, each one or two sentences, no preamble and no summing-up paragraph at the end; I'll write the intro and the kicker myself. For every item: the case or consideration that earns it a place, whether it's a must or a nice-to-have given what I need the theory for (good enough to structure a 4,000-word explainer around, and good enough that I could hand it to a press officer as a real guide to what she may and may not do), and — this is the part I care most about — an outright statement when a requirement is really just one camp's prejudice wearing a badge. If some pair of your five can't be jointly satisfied, say so inside whichever item it belongs in rather than in a note: my guess is that the requirements that cash things out in terms of the speaker's intention will collide with the ones that hold a speaker responsible for foreseen but unintended uptake, but you tell me. And in that same item, say which single requirement does the most work in ruling out the popular positions — I suspect one of them is the view that there's no difference in principle and another is the view that lying is worse because it invites a distinctive kind of reliance — but don't score the views for me, I'll do that in the piece. Last thing: does the list owe me a seperate answer to why anyone ever bothers lying when misleading is right there and cheaper, or is that a sixth requirement that has no business on the list?
