I am assembling a short handout for a seminar session on self-reference, and I want to close it with a sestina built on Escher's 1948 lithograph Drawing Hands — the sheet of paper out of which two hands rise, each drawing the other into existence. I am not asking for a poem that names the puzzle. I am asking for one whose form does the philosophical work.

The formal constraints:

- A full English sestina: six stanzas of six unrhymed lines in loose iambic pentameter, with the end-words rotating by the standard permutation (so the order 1-2-3-4-5-6 in the first stanza becomes 6-1-5-2-4-3 in the second, and so on), closed by a three-line envoi in which all six end-words return, two to a line, one of them ending the line and the other placed inside it.
- Take as your six end-words, in this order in the first stanza: HAND, CUFF, LINE, LOOP, PLANE, RULE. (The cuff is where the flat page turns into a limb — the level crossing; the line is at once a drawn mark and a rank in an ordering; the rule is at once a straight edge and the permutation the poem obeys.)

What the form has to carry:

In a tangled hierarchy — the idiom that grows out of Hofstadter's treatment of this print, where the drawing hands serve as a strange loop — levels trade places, and what keeps the tangle from collapsing into an outright contradiction is something the tangle cannot reach: an inviolate level that stands outside it and is nevertheless what makes it possible. A sestina has exactly this shape. No end-word keeps its rank: every one of the six passes through every line position, so the ordering of levels is scrambled from within. Yet the permutation that scrambles them is never itself scrambled; it governs the whole and is invisible from inside any single stanza. The poem should make that invisibility felt, and should make clear that the drawing hands are not a paradox in the sense of the liar or of Russell — nothing here is inconsistent — but a consistent mutual dependence that only looks impossible because we expect levels to stack rather than to close.

I also want the poem to be claimable by three readers, without adjudicating among them. Call them the anchored reading (an author and a frame outside the sheet lend the loop its consistency), the sealed reading (there is no outside: the hands constitute one another, groundless but self-consistent, the way two interlocking definitions can fix each other), and the tenant reading (every inviolate level is inviolate only relative to the system it governs, and is rented from a larger tangle that can reabsorb it). The interesting fact, and the one I care about, is that from on the sheet these three are indistinguishable: the hands cannot tell which situation they are in, and the difference shows up only from outside, across stanzas rather than within one. A reader committed to any of the three should be able to think the poem is on their side, and the poem should not say which is right.

Two further restrictions. The voice stays on the sheet — it may be the sheet's voice, or one hand's, or the rule's, but never the artist's, and it must not name Escher, Gödel, or Hofstadter, or address the reader as someone standing outside the picture. And I would rather the poem not lean on the vocabulary that would do its thinking for it: no "paradox," "recursion," "meta-," "strange loop," "tangled hierarchy," or "self-reference" anywhere in the verse.

The envoi is the one place where the hidden rule may become visible — all six words present at once, the permutation legible in a glance — but it should become visible by being shown, not by being announced: no stepping off the plane to claim authorship or to explain the mechanism.

Give me the poem only, no prefatory or explanatory prose. Someone who has spent time with strange loops should find it right rather than merely atmospheric — in particular, right about the difference between a consistent loop and a contradiction, and about the relativity of inviolability — while someone who has not should still be able to read it as a poem about two hands. I will be checking the permutation, so the end-words have to actually rotate.
