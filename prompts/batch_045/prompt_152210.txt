I'm in a reading group that meets jointly with the biostatistics department, and last week we did a session on Simpson's paradox. I came away with an argument I built myself, which I intended as a defence of the sure-thing principle and which now looks to me as though it proves far too much: either the principle is false, or no statistical comparison — pooled or stratified — ever supports any practical verdict at all. I'd like you to tell me which step to give up and what giving it up costs me.

The argument runs like this. (1) Take the sure-thing principle: for any partition of the possibilities into E and not-E, if I prefer option A to option B conditional on E, and also prefer A to B conditional on not-E, then I prefer A to B outright. The usual way of enforcing it is dynamic — someone who violates it will pay to switch whichever way E resolves, so a book can be made against them across a sequence of choices. (2) Now take a textbook Simpson's paradox: a treatment and a control, an outcome like recovery, and a stratifying variable like severity of disease, where the treatment looks better than the control within the severe cases and also within the mild cases, and worse than the control once the table is collapsed. By (1), the stratum-wise verdict is the verdict and the pooled figure is a pure artefact of the weights: whenever the two come apart, the aggregate is what you discard. (3) But (1) quantifies over partitions without restriction, and partitions are things one can manufacture. Stratify instead on a variable that the treatment and the outcome both causally influence — a common effect of the two, the sort of conditioning that produces Berkson-style distortions — or on an intermediate milestone lying on the path between receiving the treatment and recovering; on such partitions the within-stratum comparisons can be made to come out the other way, favouring the control in every stratum. (4) Apply (1) to that partition and the control is now the rational choice, contradicting (2). (5) And this is not a special feature of contrived examples: for a given treatment–outcome comparison there is normally more than one usable partition, and reversal-partitions are easy to construct, so a sure-thing principle with an unrestricted quantifier hands down incompatible verdicts about the very same choice. Either the principle goes, or it applies to no partition at all — in which case step (2) was never licensed, and neither the stratified nor the pooled numbers ever tell anyone what to do. (6) That last claim is one I cannot accept: somehow people do learn from data which of two treatments is better.

Somebody in the group said that the paradox cannot even arise for interventional probabilities, on the grounds that a pooled causal effect is a weighted average of the stratum-specific causal effects with weights that do not depend on which act you perform, and that a weighted average cannot fall outside the range of the things it averages. I could not see how that was supposed to help: steps (2) and (3) are stated over ordinary conditional probabilities, which is what anyone reading a table outside a causal-inference seminar is actually looking at, and I don't see why the weighted-average remark should stop me from applying the principle to a partition I constructed by conditioning on an effect of the treatment. So: which step do you reject? Tell me why the step you reject looked innocent, and price the alternatives for me — in particular, whether I would have to accept that stratifying on a mediator or on a common effect is as legitimate as stratifying on a confounder, whether I would have to give up the dynamic or money-pump style argument for the principle, and whether the real work is being done by some distinction between associational and interventional quantities that I haven't taken seriously. If I have to choose, I'd rather keep the sure-thing principle than keep the claim that reversal-partitions are always available, but I want to know what I'm paying either way — and I'd also like to know what my friend in biostatistics means when she says the whole thing isn't a paradox about probability at all but about which question you are asking.

Please answer in continuous prose: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, and no numbered list of your own, though you may refer to my steps by their numbers, and write it the way you would write to a colleague who is stuck rather than the way you would write on an exam script. Around seven hundred to nine hundred words.
