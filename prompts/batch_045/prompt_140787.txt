I am writing a scripted dialogue for a lecture series on the prehistory of the design argument, and one scene keeps coming out as a caricature. In it Isaac Newton, in the last years of his life, is shown three propositions from twenty-first-century cosmology, translated into vocabulary he can follow. My draft has him nodding at the first, scoffing at the second, and being baffled by the third, which is exactly what a modern person with vague ideas about a pious gentleman would write. I want the version in which his reactions follow from the details of his own theology and natural philosophy, including where those details pull him in opposite directions.

The three propositions:

(1) The numbers that appear in the laws — he knows the inverse-square rule for gravitation; tell him there are others of the same kind — sit in narrow ranges. Vary them slightly and no star ignites, no chemistry runs, and no one is there to measure anything. Some physicists treat this as evidence of design; others treat it as evidence that our universe is one member of a vast ensemble in which these numbers scatter, and that we should not be surprised to find ourselves in a habitable member.

(2) The ensemble is physically real and, by its own physics, unobservable from ours in principle.

(3) What we call matter and law are states of a computation running on hardware in a universe ontologically prior to ours; the numbers are parameters someone set.

Here is the wrinkle I want the scene to turn on. He is told three further things about (3): the simulated world exists only while the computation runs — it is not a clock that was wound and left; its laws are recomputed at every step, so at no moment does the world persist on its own; its inhabitants are rational, do successful science, are puzzled by their own fine-tuned numbers, and pray; and the programmers can override the computation and have done so. He is also told that (1), (2), and (3) are, and will remain, empirically indistinguishable from one another.

What I need from you:

- For each proposition, which specific commitment of his does the real work? And where two of his commitments conflict, which one wins for him, and why that one rather than the other? A list of relevant commitments is not the answer; the ranking is.

- The consistency question I cannot settle: he refuses hypotheses not drawn from phenomena, yet he was willing to say that the fixed stars may each be the centre of a system like ours, and he speculated about a subtle spirit pervading bodies and about the cause of gravitation. So his rejection of (2), if he rejects it, cannot be mere unobservability. What is it?

- The metaphysical question the scene hinges on: he treats space and time not as containers independent of God but as consequences of God's existing always and everywhere, and he was willing to say that infinite space is, as it were, the sensorium in which an omnipresent God intuits things directly. If that is right, can the space of proposition (3) be a space at all? Is the programmer to the simulated world as God is to nature, as a craftsman to a clock, or neither? And when those inhabitants pray, is anyone at the other end?

- The voluntarism question: the laws are not metaphysically necessary for him — gravity is not an essential property of bodies, and God could have decreed a different rule. Does that make him unusually receptive to (1), or does it dissolve (1), since on his view there need be no antecedent range of values for God to select from, only what he decreed? And if it dissolves (1), does it also dissolve (2)'s answer to (1)?

- One more consideration, which may cut either way: he devoted enormous labour to expounding the prophecies as a determinately meaningful, divinely authored text. Whether a man like that is more or less receptive than we are to the thought that the world is at bottom a code is part of what I am asking.

- Finally, rank the three by how much he would object to them, and mark which, if any, he would call impious rather than merely unsupported. He had a live vocabulary for this: much of his energy went into attacking cosmologies that explained the fabric of things by chance or blind necessity. If one of my three is, for him, a relapse into exactly that, name it and show me the mechanism by which it relapses.

Constraints: ground the predictions in his actual positions rather than in a generic early-modern theism. The gap between a Lord who governs and an artificer who finishes and leaves, and between laws as decrees and laws as descriptions, is the whole point of the scene, so do not blur it. No citations and no reading list. Where you are reconstructing something contested, say so in a clause instead of asserting it. And if some part of my setup rests on a false assumption about him, tell me which part before you answer around it.
