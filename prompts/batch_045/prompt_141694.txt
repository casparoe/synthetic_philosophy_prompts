For thirty-one years I was a research geomorphologist, and for the last four of my retirement I have chaired our county's Natural Heritage Review Board: five unpaid volunteers (a dairy farmer, a real-estate agent, a retired schoolteacher, the county planner, and myself), one staff ecologist who assembles the site file, about forty applications a year, thirty minutes per case in a public evening meeting, and written findings that are posted online and can be appealed to the county commissioners. The bylaw we administer says that a parcel is eligible for a conservation designation if, among other things, it supports a viable breeding population of a species of concern, contains a mature stand, has a substantial hydrological connection to the river corridor, and if the proposed activity would have no more than a minimal adverse impact. Every operative word in that sentence is vague, most of the criteria have several vague sub-conditions that can come apart from one another, and the board has no authority to amend the bylaw — we can only recommend amendments to the commissioners, who meet quarterly and rarely act.

In the course of teaching myself some philosophy lately I got absorbed in the dispute between epistemicist and supervaluationist treatments of vagueness — roughly, whether borderline cases are cases where a sharp boundary exists but lies beyond what we can know, or cases where the admissible ways of sharpening the term disagree, so that the statement is neither true nor false — together with the sorites difficulty, the so-called tolerance principle, and the awkward fact that the borderline region itself seems to have no sharp edge. My fellow board members think this is exactly the sort of thing that has nothing to do with a Tuesday evening in a county meeting room. I have come to think it has a great deal to do with it, but only if it can be turned into something we can actually run: I want a written decision procedure, in the form of an ordered checklist, that a person with no philosophy training can work through in half an hour with the site file open, and that ends in one of a small number of exits — a clear approval, a clear denial, a recorded finding that the criteria do not determine an answer plus an explicit statement of what we do instead, or a referral to the commissioners on the grounds that resolving the case would require making a new rule rather than applying the old one.

Please give me that procedure, with the following built into it. For each question, state what would count as a sufficient answer drawn from the kind of evidence a site file actually contains (survey counts, aerial photographs, soil maps, the ecologist's memo) rather than from information we cannot get. Say how the procedure handles a parcel that is strong on one sub-condition and weak on another, and tell us whether the dimensions are to be traded off against each other by some aggregate scoring or whether a result that clearly fails on any single dimension sinks the application regardless of the others — I want that choice made explicitly, not defaulted into. Say how the procedure handles a case where the board cannot tell whether it is looking at a genuine borderline case or a case it simply has not understood well enough. And say how it handles the chain: our docket regularly contains five parcels along a continuous gradient, each differing from the last by what any of us would call a hair's breadth, and approving number three seems to commit us on number four. I would like a step that forces the board to check, before voting, whether today's decision settles a neighbouring case by a difference too small to matter, and that requires us either to accept that consequence openly or to declare a discontinuity and give a reason that is not merely the order in which the applications arrived.

I also want the theory made honest rather than decoratively cited. Please mark, alongside the steps, which of them would have to change if the epistemicist picture is right rather than the supervaluationist one, and where the two pictures genuinely diverge in output rather than in metaphysical story: in particular, whether our written findings should describe a case as one the criteria leave open or one whose answer we take ourselves not to know, whether we should invite an applicant to come back with better evidence or tell them that no amount of evidence will settle it, and whether the honest device for an undecidable case that must still be decided is a lottery, a temporary designation with a fixed review date, or something else. If your procedure requires the board to fix in advance, publicly and for the whole year, a standing interpretation sheet listing the sharpenings of each vague term that it is willing to treat as admissible — and, if it needs them, the weights given to different admissible sharpenings — then say so, and say what we should do about the fact that we are not the body with authority to do that. Please then run the finished procedure on four cases: an unbroken 240-hectare mature forest with fourteen breeding pairs of the listed warbler; a mowed field with a roadside drainage ditch; an eleven-hectare woodlot that is plainly better than parcels we have denied and plainly worse than parcels we have approved, whose purchase option expires in six weeks; and the gradient chain of five riparian parcels at forty, thirty-eight, thirty-six, thirty-four and thirty-two metre buffers. Flag at each step where judgment cannot be eliminated but can only be relocated, tell me plainly which of the four cases your procedure answers in a way I will not like, and close by saying what the procedure cannot do — the failures I should expect it to have after a year of use.
