Please act as a professional philosopher and an experienced examiner in metaphysics who has spent years setting and marking upper-level exams, and think carefully before you write: the value of what I'm asking for lies almost entirely in the choice of crux, so a few bullet points or a short preamble (under 150 words) showing how you decided what the question should turn on is welcome before the deliverables themselves.

I'm writing the final exam for a third-year seminar on personal identity, about twenty-five students, and I'm stuck on the Parfit question. Last year I asked them about the teletransporter and the branch-line variant in essentially the form everyone asks it, and I got back twenty-five papers that all looked the same: accurate summaries of the dialectic, the claim that identity is not what matters in survival, some remarks about reductionism and the rationality of fearing death, and not a single sentence that showed me whether anyone had actually worked on the structure of the view rather than memorized it. So this year I want to build the question around a variant I've invented, and I'd like you to develop it rather than replace it with something more standard. Here is the variant: give the scanner a fidelity dial calibrated from 1 to 100. At 100, the replica who steps out on Mars has all of your memories, desires, projects, and character traits. At lower settings the replica retains your broad temperament, your values, and your longer-term plans, but progressively fewer of your specific memories, skills, and finer-grained dispositions; at 1 it is effectively a stranger who happens to share your temperament. Every setting produces a healthy, functioning person, and unless a case says otherwise the scanning destroys the original. The cases I want the question to work with are: one replica at 100 with the original destroyed; one replica at 40 with the original destroyed; two replicas at 100 with the original destroyed; two replicas at 40 with the original destroyed. What I want the students to have to confront is whether the reductionist treatment of survival — the move from saying identity is not what matters to saying what does matter is some psychological relation — has the resources to make sense of a dial at all, and what it costs it either way. If what matters comes in degrees, then whether one survives stops being a crisp yes-or-no matter, and prudential deliberation turns into a calculation about expected degree of survival, which sits oddly with the use reductionists make of their conclusion. If what matters is instead all-or-nothing above some threshold, the view has reintroduced exactly the sort of sharp boundary that identity was supposed to be providing, and it owes us an account of where the threshold sits and why. I also want them forced to say whether the dial is varying psychological connectedness or psychological continuity, since in my experience almost none of them have noticed that those are two different relations, and to say what is going on in the double cases: whether two replicas at 40 contain more of what matters than one replica at 40, or whether branching divides something fixed, or doubles it, or is simply irrelevant to how much of what matters is present.

Please give me four things. First, the exam question as it would actually appear on the paper — self-contained, explaining the dial in the students' terms, about 150 words, closed-book, forty-five minutes, 800-word limit, and phrased so that a student who writes out everything they remember about the branch-line discussion but never engages the dial cannot get more than a C. Second, a model answer of roughly 700 words that takes a definite position and argues for it rather than surveying the options, because I want to know what a first-class answer to this particular question looks like. Third, a marking scheme out of 20 marks: five must-hit points worth four marks each, each point naming the specific move a strong answer must make, with a one-line note on the way candidates characteristically get that point wrong. Fourth, a two- or three-sentence boundary note on what separates a low A from a high B and what pushes a paper down into the C range. No bibliography, no citations, and no page references anywhere: the exam is closed-book, and I don't want anything in the paper that a student would need to look up.
