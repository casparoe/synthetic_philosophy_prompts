I don't need names, papers, or citations — I only want to know what to believe and what to discount. Here is the passage, quoted as closely as I can manage:

Two players sit in front of a pot of money. On each turn the player whose turn it is can either take the larger share and end the game, or pass, in which case the pot grows and the other player faces the same choice. The game is finite — say, eight turns — and everyone knows that it is. So we can solve it from the end. At the last turn the mover takes the money: there is nothing to gain by passing when the game is about to end anyway, and both players can see this. Knowing that, the player at the seventh turn knows that passing merely hands her partner a bigger prize, so she takes as well. And so on, all the way back to the first move. Backward induction therefore shows that a rational player takes at once, and the argument needs nothing beyond the players' rationality and their common knowledge of it: because each player knows that the other is rational, knows that the other knows, and so on without end, no extra assumptions have to be made about what anyone would believe or do at a point that the argument itself shows will never be reached.

That is why the game is a paradox rather than a curiosity. In the laboratory people regularly pass for several rounds, and both players walk away with far more money than the theory says they will, so the prediction is not merely unflattering, it is empirically false, and no amount of extra sophistication about what players believe rescues it. Philosophers have sharpened the point. The backward induction argument has to say what a player would do, and would expect, at nodes which by the argument's own logic are never reached, and there is no fact of the matter about what someone would do at a decision you have already proved she will never face. The argument thus pulls the ladder up behind itself: if the first player ever did pass, the reasoning that told her to take would already have collapsed. So a rational player has no reason to take at the first node.

Game theorists eventually put this right, and the fix is a small one. Suppose each player attaches some tiny probability to the possibility that her partner is simply the sort of person who always passes, or else who mis-clicks — it doesn't matter which, since any noise of that kind does the same work. Then passing is rational, because each time your partner passes you learn something about her, and the more evidence of cooperation accumulates the more reason you have to go on cooperating; with any such probability, however small, the reputation effect carries the players cooperatively all the way to the final rounds of the game.

My own suspicion attaches to that last claim.
