I look after the internal onboarding material at a software company with engineering offices in Berlin, Bangalore and São Paulo, and we are about to start moving engineers between them for three- to twelve-month stints. The section I keep failing to write is the one about everyday local conventions: tipping in restaurants and cafés, how people queue (or do not queue), crossing the road where and when the law says you may. Our relocating engineers keep asking a question I cannot answer helpfully, and it is not really a travel question: is following one of these local conventions something you owe to anyone, or is it just prudence, politeness and wanting not to look like an idiot? And if it is something you owe, is it something you owe because you are an outsider, or just the same thing you would owe at home?

I want a decision tree, not an essay, and I want to build it from the cases rather than from a list of theory names.

**The deliverable.** An eight-question decision tree (eight internal nodes at most, six to nine leaves), plus a short companion table. Each internal node is a plain-language question about a concrete case, with two or three answer options and routing to the next node or to a leaf. Each leaf carries: the name of the position in ordinary words, one sentence on the most awkward thing that position is committed to, and one sentence on what practical stance follows for a relocating engineer (do they owe an apology if they get it wrong, and to whom; is guilt the right reaction or is mild awkwardness the right reaction; is this something the company could reasonably ask of them or only advise). Give every node an ID and every leaf a slug, because I intend to implement this as a static quiz on our wiki.

**The cases the questions have to be built out of.** Please use at least these, and feel free to add one:

1. *The last-night café.* In City A, leaving a tip in a café is expected and the law lets employers pay tipped staff below the standard minimum because tips are assumed. You are leaving tomorrow, you will never return, no one you know is watching, and the service has already happened. Do you owe the tip?
2. *The invisible pedestrian.* In City B, crossing outside a marked crossing is technically an offence, is what everybody does, and is never enforced. The street is empty, the nearest crossing is ten minutes away, and no driver is inconvenienced either way.
3. *The press-at-the-counter.* In City C there is no orderly queue; people press toward the counter and whoever is most assertive is served. An elderly person is losing out every time. Do you queue in the style you were brought up with, which means you will not be served, or do you press?
4. *The export problem.* Your habitually generous tipping in a city where it was not customary has, a local colleague tells you, become part of why prices have been rising for locals and why staff now hover for tips. Do you keep tipping as you would at home, tip as locals tip, or stop visiting that sort of place?
5. *The bubble.* A company shuttle, a serviced apartment block, an office floor where nearly everyone is a foreigner on the same rotation. Does the local convention reach inside the bubble?
6. *The shared codebase.* Two offices work in the same repository. One conventionally writes long commit messages and reviews within a day; the other merges fast with one-line messages. Whose convention binds a visiting engineer, and does local here mean the repository's convention or the city's?

**The distinctions I actually care about**, because they are the ones that seem to drive my colleagues' disagreements. Please make them branch points rather than background commentary: whether violating a convention wrongs anyone who could appropriately complain, and if so who that person is; whether the wrong depends on being observed or on expectations being formed, so that a perfectly invisible violation is different in kind; whether it matters that you will never return and never meet these people again; whether the arbitrariness of a convention (which side of the road, whether a tip is ten or fifteen percent) makes compliance more binding or less; and whether the fact that your single act makes no difference to whether the convention survives at all removes your reason to comply.

**Leaves that must be reachable**, named in plain language with no jargon I would have to look up: the view that conventions which solve a coordination problem create a genuine duty to comply while most people comply; the view that the duty comes from the expectations the convention creates and the disappointment or friction of flouting them; the view that it is a matter of fairness to the people who sustain the practice, so that the visitor is a free-rider if they take the benefit without the cost; the view that nothing moral is at stake at all, only prudence, politeness and self-presentation; the view that a guest owes compliance to hosts as a matter of respect, regardless of what the convention says; the view that you should follow local practice because locals are better placed to judge how to get what they want, which cuts out when you know better; the view that only the underlying values matter, so the local convention binds merely as a means and can be substituted; the view that some conventions should be flouted and, where you have any leverage, eroded; and the deflationary view that there is no fact of the matter about whether the visitor must, only social meaning and the reactions they will get.

**The companion table.** Before the tree, lay out the logical space: the questions, the combinations of answers they permit, and which combinations are actually occupied. For each unoccupied combination, say briefly whether nobody is there because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it simply has not been worked out — I would bet on a genuinely empty cell around the claim that conventions bind everyone while also holding that an entirely invisible violation harms nobody, and another around any local convention binding without qualification once you admit that some conventions systematically disadvantage a vulnerable group. Then flag any two leaves your tree cannot separate, especially the fairness-to-participants view and the expectations view, which both say tip in the last-night café; tell me whether they come apart anywhere in the cases above, whether what separates them is only a difference in the reason rather than in the recommended action, and whether that difference matters for my purpose. Finally, note the answer patterns that land a reader in no cell at all, and what I should print for those readers.

**House rules.** No citations, no bibliography, no named philosophers and no labels I would need a reference to decode; I have no way to check them and my readers will not want them. No question may ask a reader to self-report a theoretical commitment, so nothing like do you think conventions create moral obligations or do you consider yourself a relativist: sort them by what they say about the cases. Please also keep the legal question explicitly separate from the moral one throughout, since one of my six cases involves a rule that is on the books and universally ignored, and I want to know whether that fact is doing any work. Use City A, City B and City C rather than naming real places; we have got local facts wrong in onboarding material before and I would rather the cases were schematic than wrong.
