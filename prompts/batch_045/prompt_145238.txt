hey, I'm stuck on something in the theory of legal rules and I keep going in circles, so I wanted to get an intuitive explanation rather than a formal one.

Here's a made-up constitution, the Acharnean Charter, whose amendment machinery is deliberately nasty:

- Art. 100. The Charter may be amended by a two-thirds vote of the Assembly.
- Art. 101. Art. 100 may be amended only by a three-quarters vote.
- Art. 102. Art. 101 may be amended only by a three-quarters vote held at least four years after the last amendment of Art. 101, and Art. 101 may never be amended so as to set its own threshold below three-quarters.
- Art. 103. This Article governs only amendments proposed after this Article has itself been in force for two years.

Now the things I can't get straight.

First, hierarchy. In the old puzzle this is modeled on — a constitution with two amendment thresholds, where the higher one is itself amendable — it looks like whether a given amendment is valid is just a matter of whether the right procedure was followed at the time, and the "higher" clause is higher only because the amendment of the lower one had to go through it. So is legal hierarchy here anything more than a bookkeeping fact about which validity-conditions happened to be satisfied? If so, what does it even mean to say Art. 102 is entrenched — is that a legal claim or a prediction about what officials will treat as valid? Relatedly: if the Assembly one day passes by 3/4 an amendment that rewrites Art. 101 to require only a simple majority, and everyone goes along with it, did something illegal happen, or did the Charter just change? I sense this is the point where the Hart-style thought kicks in — that the ultimate rule of recognition is a social fact and can't be legally regulated from inside — but I want the gut-level version of why that matters for these clauses specifically.

Second, timing and self-application. There seem to be several distinct questions I keep collapsing into one: is the validity of an amendment assessed by the procedure in force when it's proposed, when it's voted on, or when it takes effect? Can a single statute do two things at once — lower the threshold and then, in its second section, exercise the newly lowered threshold? If yes, is that different in kind from doing it in two statutes a week apart, or is the difference purely cosmetic? And Art. 103 is my attempt to make the self-reference indexical: a rule that governs only amendments proposed after it has been in force for two years. Does that include an amendment to Art. 103 itself, and does the answer change if the amendment is drafted to take effect in three years?

Third, the boundary between amending and replacing. Suppose the Assembly uses the amending power to repeal the amending power entirely (or to make itself the sole, unfettered source of law). Is there a legally visible difference between exercising a power and abolishing it? People reach for the omnipotence paradox here — can an omnipotent being make a rule it can't later change — but I'm never sure how good that analogy actually is, since legal powers come with a point and a jurisdiction in a way that omnipotence supposedly doesn't. Also, whatever we say about abolishing, what about *replacing*: at what point is the thing that emerges no longer the Charter but a successor document, and who would there be to settle that?

And a distinction I invented while thinking about this, which may be confused: call it first-order vs second-order self-amendment. Changing Art. 100's threshold is first-order; changing Art. 101, i.e. the rule about how the threshold-rule may be changed, is second-order. Art. 102 tries to be second-order-about-itself. Is there a principled reason the regress stops somewhere, or does it just stop wherever the social practice happens to stop — and is there any version of the staircase that is genuinely impossible to amend rather than merely costly?

So: what I'd like is an explanation of what's actually going on in these cases — what the real fault lines are (validity vs efficacy? identity of a norm over time? the difference between a rule's content changing and the rule going out of existence?), where the genuine disagreement is, and which of my four worries above are the deep ones and which are pseudo-problems I'm inventing by drafting bad clauses. Please argue for a view rather than surveying options, and don't give me anything to go read — I'm after the intuition.
