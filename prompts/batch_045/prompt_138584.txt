I'm 68, retired from running a hydrology lab, and I've got myself volunteered as the (unpaid, and only) compliance person for a small medical NGO running clinics in two countries, one in West Africa and one in Southeast Asia. So about ten times a week I get an email asking: is this payment, gift, or favour ok? A fifty-dollar expediting fee to get a vaccine consignment released at the border; a village chief presenting a fieldworker with a goat; the district health officer asking us to put his cousin on the payroll; a drug company paying for our staff to attend a conference at a nice hotel; cash "transport money" given to patients so they'll actually show up for treatment.

I've been reading about corruption and I'm sick of two things people keep saying. First, the textbook definition where corruption is misuse of public office for private gain — that covers almost none of my cases, since half the people we deal with aren't officials in any useful sense and the gain often flows to a clinic or a family rather than to a person. Second, when in Rome, do whatever the local norm is — which I don't accept but also can't yet argue against properly.

So: I don't want more theory, I want a decision procedure I can run in five minutes on my phone. Give me the questions in order, what counts as an adequate answer to each one, where the procedure definately stops with a no, and where it hands the decision up to our director because it's a values call and not a factual one. And please give me at least a couple of distinctions that aren't in the standard compliance handbook. Three I've been chewing on and can't make work: the difference between a transfer that buys a specific act and one that buys a standing disposition to be helpful, where the return is vague and indefinitely deferred; whether a gift corrupts more when the recipient can't easily say no to the giver later, i.e. the exit costs are lopsided; and whether it matters that refusing makes the patients worse off rather than making us worse off. Use whatever actually does the work and throw out the rest — and tell me if I'm seperating things that should be kept together.

Then run the procedure on three of the cases above, your pick of the hardest ones, but include at least one where the answer it spits out is one I won't like. Finish by telling me plainly what the procedure can't do.

Under 700 words, bullets rather than essay prose, and no citations or further reading — no library access here and I don't want any.
