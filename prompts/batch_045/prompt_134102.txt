I am writing a policy paper on the taxation of large inheritances, and I keep running into the same picture on both sides of the argument: society is described as a footrace in which some children begin well down the track because their parents were wealthy, and a tax on estates is then defended as moving those children back toward a common start, or attacked on the ground that a head start is mere luck and that there is nothing unfair about luck. I want to audit the picture rather than deploy it.

My suspicion is that the race image is carrying a substantive assumption that is false: that there is one race with one finishing order, whereas a modern economy is better described as a set of partly parallel, partly incommensurable tracks whose prizes are substantially positional. If that is right, then equalizing the start is not even well defined — equalize at which moment, relative to which competitors, on which track. My candidate replacement is that very large inherited wealth is less like a head start in a race and more like being handed a share of the scorekeeping: durable influence over which games get played, how they are scored, and who is in a position to enter them.

Please audit both images, in the following sections:

Mapping (about 150 words): an explicit correspondence table for the race picture — what plays the role of the runners, the starting gun, the head start, the lanes, the rules, the referee, the baton, and the prize — and then the same for my scoreboard picture.

Where the race mapping holds (about 100 words): the features of inheritance and labour-market competition it genuinely captures.

Disanalogies (about 250 words): the places the race mapping breaks, sorted into ones that matter for the tax conclusion and ones that are merely cosmetic, with a sentence on why each of the load-bearing ones matters.

What the picture imports (about 100 words): the normative commitments the analogy brings in silently — for example that what justice requires is an ordering rather than a threshold, that a start can be located at a single moment, and that market outcomes track effort closely enough for a head start to be the thing to object to.

Verdict (about 200 words): whether the race picture supports any inheritance-tax conclusion once the load-bearing disanalogies are on the table, and whether my scoreboard replacement does better or simply reproduces the same defect one level up, by turning an argument about desert into an argument about power that may prove far more than I want it to. If neither works, say what would.
