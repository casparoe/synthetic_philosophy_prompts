I work on the ethics of blame, and I have been defending a version of the standing-forfeiture view that I would like stress-tested. Call it the implicated-norm account. In outline: standing to blame is not a global property of persons but a norm-relative and time-relative one; a blamer forfeits standing only when her own conduct, unacknowledged and unrepented, implicates the very norm she invokes in blaming — not merely when she is flawed, and not merely when she has violated some unrelated norm; and what forfeiture defeats is her authority to make the demand, not the truth of the judgment she expresses and not the propriety of the response in every other sense. So the adulterer retains standing to blame tax evasion, while the adulterer who blames adultery does not; the blamer who has owned the same fault and changed regains it; and a hypocrite's blame can be entirely deserved, even perceptive and useful, while still being something she has no standing to do.

What I want is a map of the view's exposure: what would count against it, what it predicts that its rivals do not, and where it may have been insulated by its own qualifications.

The pressure points I can see, and want developed rather than merely listed, are these. First, the weakness-of-will worry: much ordinary hypocrisy consists in accepting a norm and failing to comply with it, so if the account explains forfeiture by the blamer's failure to treat the norm as binding on her, it may simply misdescribe the central case, or fail to apply to it at all. Second, and this is where I suspect the real trouble lies: how finely is the invoked norm typed? If the account may individuate norms at whatever grain a case requires, it can accommodate any distribution of intuitions and predicts nothing — I want to know whether the grain problem is a fatal defect, a reason to expect an independent criterion of norm-individuation, or merely a research programme. Third, the apparent inversion: the person who has been through it — the recovering wrongdoer — is often accorded *more* standing, not less, which may mean the account gets the direction of the effect wrong, since greater implication should mean lesser standing. Fourth, asymmetric severity: the habitual liar who condemns a murder, or the shoplifter who condemns a fraud. If the account must deny standing here, it is committed to something I find unacceptable; if it blocks the extension, I want to know on what principled basis. Fifth, the directed case: a fraud victim who has herself defrauded others blaming the person who defrauded her, where the wrong was against *her* — and alongside it, third-party blame, where the hypocrite speaks not for herself but for someone else wronged. Sixth, the practical and political cost: hypocritical blame is frequently the most informed blame there is, and a forfeiture view hands wrongdoers a ready deflector and can be used to silence precisely those with the most to say; I want an honest account of whether that is an objection to the view or merely an abuse of it. Seventh, the generality worry: if nearly everyone is implicated in nearly every norm one might invoke, the account threatens to generalise into a near-universal loss of standing — a quietism about blame that I suspect is a reductio, but which I can imagine being defended.

Please rank these from fatal to merely awkward, distinguishing what a defender of the account should concede as genuinely counting against it from what she can reasonably dismiss, and say plainly whether the view as stated risks anything at all. I would also like your view on two further questions: whether cheap verbal acknowledgment restores standing, which would seem to make standing hostage to a performance rather than to conduct; and whether the account predicts anything about the blamee's response — whether someone blamed without standing owes no answer — given that people blamed by hypocrites do in fact often feel guilt and apologise. I am most moved by cases in which the hypocrite's blame is not merely apt but needed, and least moved by appeals to the blamer's sincerity. I do not want a bibliography or a reading list; I know the literature. I want the arguments.
