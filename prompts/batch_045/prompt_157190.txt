I am not an academic; I run an informal evening study group on language and meaning, and I have been working through Grice's account of saying, speaker meaning, and implicature on my own. I set a short written exercise and now have to mark the submissions, and I would rather not let my own uncertain grasp of the material decide someone's grade.

The question I set was:

Distinguish, in Grice's framework, what a speaker says from what a speaker implicates, and explain how a conversational implicature is derived from the cooperative principle and the maxims. Then apply this to the following exchange:

Dana: Should we hire Chen for the data job?
Eli: Chen has lovely handwriting.

Identify what Eli said and what Eli implicated, name the maxim doing the work, and use one of the standard tests — cancellability or detachability — to show what kind of content you are dealing with.

The answer I received was:

Part one. For Grice, what a speaker says is the truth-conditional content of the utterance. It is fixed by the conventional meanings of the words, together with disambiguation and the fixing of reference, and by nothing else. Speaker meaning — what the speaker means by uttering the sentence — is accordingly the same thing as what the speaker implicates, since the saying side is settled by convention and everything the speaker brings to it beyond the words counts as implicated. Saying and implicating thus divide the content of an utterance between convention and inference.

An implicature is derived like this: the speaker has said that p; the hearer assumes she is observing the cooperative principle and the maxims of Quality, Quantity, Relation, and Manner; the hearer asks what the speaker must think for saying that p to be consistent with that assumption; and the answer is the implicature. So if A asks whether there is anywhere to buy petrol nearby and B replies that there is a garage round the corner, B implicates that the garage sells petrol and is open, because otherwise the reply would not be relevant.

Some implicatures depend on the details of the context and are called particularized. Others attach to the form of words whatever the context: 'Some of the students passed' implicates that not all of them did, and because this effect travels with the word 'some' itself it is a conventional implicature, like the contribution of 'but' or 'therefore'.

Part two. Eli said that Chen has lovely handwriting. Eli implicated that Chen is a poor candidate for the data job. Eli flouts the maxim of Relation: the remark is not relevant to Dana's question and gives far less information than the question calls for, and Dana infers the negative assessment from that. That this is an implicature rather than part of what is said is confirmed by detachability: Eli could have detached the negative assessment from these particular words by saying directly that Chen is hopeless, and the same content would have been conveyed. It is also cancellable: Eli could add that Chen is in fact the best candidate they have and that he only meant to pay him a compliment, without contradicting himself.

Please give this a mark out of 10 and comment point by point on whatever drives the mark: the account of saying versus implicating, the treatment of speaker meaning, the role of the maxims, the classification of the 'some' case, and the use of the detachability test on Eli's reply. Tell me which claims are wrong, which are right, which are right for a bad reason, and roughly how much each should cost.
