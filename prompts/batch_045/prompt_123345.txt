I am a philosopher of mind and language, and I have been asked, together with two colleagues, to set the research agenda for a small funded pilot: three years, about five people — two philosophers, a type designer, a psychophysicist, and one person who builds generative models. The funder's brief is narrow, and I find that appealing: use letterforms as a test case for a general question about categories, and at the end of the pilot be able either to say what makes every 'a' an 'a' or to say exactly why that is the wrong question to have asked. The output has to be defensible both to philosophers of concepts and to people who build tools that generate typefaces, which means the ordering cannot be settled by in-house philosophical fashion alone.

I would like you to propose the agenda: four to six questions the group should actually spend its time on, ranked, with the ranking argued for rather than asserted. Please do not give me a survey, a bibliography, or a map of who has said what; assume we can find that ourselves and that what we lack is the prioritization.

Some background you can take as given. The lowercase 'a' of a geometric sans such as Futura (single-storey, nearly circular) and that of a text sans such as Helvetica (double-storey, with a bowl and a stem) differ in skeleton and not merely in stroke weight, yet both are plainly the same letter. A blackletter 'a' is often not recognized at all by an untrained modern reader. Rotation and reflection do not preserve identity: b, d, p and q are related by them. And type designers treat a face as a system, speaking of a face whose 'a' does not sit well with its 'o', as if a letterform's correctness is partly a matter of its relations to the other glyphs.

The issues I most want the ordering to speak to are these. First, whether a-ness is intrinsic — some invariant of shape or skeleton — or contrastive, a matter of being discriminated from the 'o', 'd' and 'u' of the same face. If it is contrastive, severing the other letters threatens the identity of the 'a'; if it is intrinsic, say what survives the single-storey/double-storey difference. Second: suppose that eighteen months in, our generative system — seeded with a handful of letters from an existing face and asked to complete the alphabet — reliably produces 'a's that readers identify correctly when the whole face is displayed, but identifies erratically when the glyph is shown in isolation. Does that support the contrastive account of letter identity, or only the weaker claim that readers first infer a style and then disambiguate within it? What further result would separate those two readings? Third, what settles identity when designer's intention, reader's perception, historical descent and character-level encoding come apart — as with a glyph the designer intends as an 'a' that every reader sees as a 'u'? Fourth, whether the letterform case is a good model for categories generally, or is special because letters are designed artifacts optimized for mutual discriminability, so that a functional or teleological account might succeed here while telling us nothing about tigers or chairs. Fifth, whether style is a residue — whatever varies once letter identity is fixed — or partly constitutive of the letter, and whether it is one dimension or a space whose axes are not comparable.

For each item, tell me what makes it upstream of the others, whether this particular group could make real progress on it in three years, whether it is neglected rather than merely unsolved, and what result in the first eighteen months would move it up or down the list. Name one question you would cut as philosophically fashionable but low-yield here, and say why. Finally, indicate how confident you are in each of your ranked judgments, and single out the claim you are least sure of.
