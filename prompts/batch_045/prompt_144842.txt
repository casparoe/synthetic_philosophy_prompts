I am a graduate student in psychology, and I have built an argument about heritability whose conclusion I cannot accept, even though every step looked defensible when I wrote it down. I would like a diagnosis.

Two stipulations first, so that we are not talking past each other.

- Read heritability in the broad sense: H² = V_G / V_P for a given trait in a given population at a given time — the proportion of the phenotypic variance attributable to genetic variance. I know that actual estimates lean on assumptions (no genotype–environment correlation, no confounding of shared genes with shared environments, and so on), and I am happy to grant every one of them here, because my worry is conceptual rather than empirical.
- Call a trait intervention-proof in a population if no change operating only on the environment of the individuals in that population could alter any individual's value for the trait. Intervention-proof, not merely intervention-resistant: this is a claim about what is possible, not about what is cheap, legal, or likely.

The argument:

(1) H² is the fraction of the differences among individuals in the population that is due to genetic differences, and the remaining fraction 1 − H² is due to environmental differences.

(2) Let a public-health programme remove an environmental source of variation in the trait — say, it equalises access to some relevant resource across the population — and suppose it succeeds: afterwards, no one in the population differs from anyone else with respect to that environmental factor.

(3) The programme shrinks V_P by exactly the variance that factor contributed, and it leaves V_G untouched, since genotypes are fixed at conception and cannot be altered by anything done to the environment afterwards.

(4) So H² rises: the same numerator over a smaller denominator. A programme that removed every environmental source of variation would drive H² to 1.

(5) If H² = 1 for a trait in a population, then all the differences in that trait are genetic and the environment contributes nothing to them; the trait is, in that population, entirely genetic.

(6) If a trait is entirely genetic in a population, it is intervention-proof there: since genotypes are fixed at conception, no later environmental change can make a difference to anyone's value for the trait.

(7) So an environmental intervention that succeeds perfectly — one that removes all environmental variance in the trait — makes that trait intervention-proof. Moreover, by (3) and (4), the more successful an environmental intervention is, the more intervention-proof, and hence the less environmentally malleable, the trait becomes.

(8) Iterating (2) through (7): once a population has been environmentally equalised with respect to some factor, environmental policy touching that trait can do nothing further — and this holds for any trait and any environmental factor. In particular, a population fully equalised with respect to nutrition would have height at H² = 1 and so could not be made taller by any nutritional change; and the secular increases in average height that many populations showed over the twentieth century, usually attributed to diet and disease burden, would have to be impossible in any well-nourished population.

I cannot accept (8). What I want to keep is: (1), which just looks like the definition; the empirical claim about height in the final clause of (8); and the thought that a successful equalising programme really does raise H² without changing anybody's genes.

My supervisor says the trouble is all in (3), because V_G is itself a function of the environment: one and the same set of genotypes can generate different amounts of phenotypic variance, and different orderings, in different environments, so you may not hold the numerator fixed while you alter the denominator. My housemate says (3) is fine and the trouble is the move from (5) to (6), because H² = 1 is a statement about a ratio of variances and says nothing about what an intervention could do to the mean.

Please name the step you would reject and say what kind of mistake it is — an equivocation, a scope or quantifier shift, a modal slide, a false invariance assumption, something else. Finally, give me a single paragraph, fit to drop into my paper in place of this argument, stating what H² does and does not entitle you to say about the likely effect of an environmental intervention on a trait.
