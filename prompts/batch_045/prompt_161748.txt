hey, I'm in a seminar on post-war European political thought and we spent all of last week on Jaspers and on whether a nation — as opposed to the individuals making it up — can be guilty for something the grandparents' generation did, and whether whatever that is can reach a generation that wasn't alive yet. I genuinely can't tell what I think, so could you write me a dialogue between two people who disagree about it properly? One of them holds that there's a real sense in which the "we" persists across generations and can carry something guilt-like (not criminal guilt — more a standing stain or obligation that doesn't evaporate just because everyone who did it is dead), and the other holds that guilt is tied to agency and dies with the agent, so the later generation can inherit duties — reparative, memorial, institutional — but not guilt.

I'd like them to actually fight over these rather than talk past each other:

- Our tutor invented this case and I can't stop thinking about it: one living person who is a descendent of both sides — grandchild of a perpetrator on one side, grandchild of a victim on the other. Call it the double-descent case. If guilt transmits down a line, what does that person have? Guilty and owed at once? Does one cancel the other, or do you get some weird superposition?
- The renunciation move: suppose a later generation formally repudiates the whole thing and gives back everything it inherited — land, pension funds, university endowments, the lot — so nobody is materially better off. Does the "we" dissolve then, or was membership enough on its own? And flip it: a population that keeps the benefits but has basically no continuity of identity with the perpetrators (new constitution, different language, mostly different people) — is benefit sufficient to carry it, or does it need identity too?
- The asymmetry that bugs me: if guilt can be inherited, can the standing to forgive be inherited as well? Why is it that the debt doesn't seem to die with the victim while the guilt is supposed to die with the perpetrator? That looks like a double standard and I want to see one of them actually defend it instead of just asserting it.

Make them both good — the one denying collective guilt shouldn't be some cold-hearted randian, and the one defending it shouldn't get by on vague talk about solidarity. They should steelman eachother's strongest version and then answer it. End on something I can still write about rather than wrapping it up neatly, and please don't include any bibliography or citations, we're not marked on that part.
