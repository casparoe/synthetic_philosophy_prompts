I build compartmental epidemic models of the ordinary mass-action kind — homogeneous mixing, a fitted contact parameter, compartments for susceptibility and infectiousness — and I use them for two very different things: forecasting a few weeks ahead within an outbreak that is already under way, and comparing interventions that would themselves change the contact structure, such as school closure or vaccinating a high-contact subgroup first. When I worry in public that the homogeneous-mixing assumption is false, colleagues wave a principle at me. The version I keep meeting comes from a widely read economics blog I would rather not name, which summarizes Friedman's classic 1953 essay on the methodology of positive economics — the one Samuelson nicknamed the F-twist — roughly like this:

"Friedman's point is that the realism of a theory's assumptions is simply irrelevant to its scientific status. A theory is an instrument for generating predictions about the phenomena it was built to explain, and the only legitimate test of it is whether those predictions come out right. He actually goes further: because a powerful theory works by stripping away everything except the handful of forces that do the work, the more important a theory is, the more false its assumptions will be. So the standard complaint that economics rests on fictions — that firms maximize profits, that people have stable and complete preferences — is not a criticism at all; it is a sign that the theory is doing its job. And since assumptions are only instruments, they cannot be tested. Asking whether firms really maximize profit is like asking whether a map is really the territory. A model is confirmed or refuted wholesale, by its predictions, and never by inspecting its parts."

Something here makes me uneasy, but I cannot locate it: my instinct is that the "cannot be tested" claim is the weak joint, though I can't say why. Go through the summary claim by claim and tell me which parts a careful defender of the original essay would accept, which are tolerable simplifications of a technical thesis, which a defender should reject as misrepresentations, and whether my colleagues' conclusion — that I should stop worrying about my mixing assumption — follows from the parts that survive correction.

The reason this matters to me is a rough taxonomy I have been using on my own models. I sort deliberate falsehoods into three bins. Screened-off falsehoods: false, but the quantity I care about barely moves if I put the omitted complication back in. As-if surrogates: false as a description of the individual units, but backed by a separate story about why the units will behave as if it were true anyway — a selection, learning, or market-discipline argument that does the real explanatory work. Scaffolding: false, implausible as description, and there simply because the model is intractable without it. My hypothesis is that the F-twist, charitably reconstructed, licenses the first two bins but not the third, and that the blogger's "never inspect the parts" formulation conceals the difference, because in practice you cannot tell which bin an assumption belongs to without attempting the de-idealization you have just been told is unnecessary. I also suspect the relevant dividing line is not prediction versus explanation but rather use within the regime the model was fitted in versus use in evaluating an intervention that perturbs the very feature that was idealized away.
