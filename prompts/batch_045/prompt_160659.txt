I run a discussion group at the local library — fifteen-odd people, none of them philosophers, mostly retired teachers and a couple of engineers — and we've now had the same argument three sessions running, so I want to build something to break the deadlock. The argument always has the same shape. One person says: of course I could have done otherwise, I could have taken the other job, nothing forced me. Someone else says: that's not what I care about at all, what I care about is whether the decision came from me rather than from how I was raised or what mood I was in or some advert I half-remember from childhood. Then a third person says neither of you has got it, the real question is whether it makes any sense to feel guilty about it afterwards, or whether sitting there deliberating beforehand was pointless because it was already settled. They agree to disagree and go home, and nobody notices that they may each be describing a different thing they want out of free will, and that the usual labels — compatibilist, incompatibilist, libertarian, skeptic — cut right across whatever it is they're actually disagreeing about. There's that old slogan about the varieties of free will worth wanting, and I want to take it completely literally and build an instrument out of it.

So: write me a which-free-will-do-you-actually-want quiz. Eight or nine questions, each one a concrete little scenario with two or three answers to pick, phrased so that someone who has never heard of a Frankfurt-style case or of leeway versus sourcehood can answer honestly and without guessing which box it puts them in — no question should just ask whether you think you have free will, and no answer option should be a label. A few scenarios I'd like used or riffed on: an app that has predicted every one of your choices perfectly for a year but you feel entirely autonomous; a woman raised in a strictly religious household who has reflectively and wholeheartedly endorsed the values she was taught; a man who spent ten years deliberately reshaping his own habits and temperament; someone whose deliberation contains a bit of genuine randomness that occasionally makes her act against her own considered judgment; two people who end up selfish, one manipulated last Tuesday and one shaped slowly since childhood; a guy who picks the unhealthy option because the cafeteria put it at eye level. Then a scoring key that maps patterns of answers onto positions: classical leeway-style incompatibilism, reasons-responsive or sourcehood compatibilism, a hierarchical deep-self view, a diachronic self-creation or autonomy view, an answerability or attributionist view, revisionism, hard incompatibilism, and the view that there's nothing here worth wanting and we should reorganize our blaming practices anyway.

Two more things that matter for how I'll use it. First, I've started describing the debate to my group as being about two separate jobs that free will is supposed to do — a backward-facing one, making praise and blame and guilt and pride actually fit the person afterwards, and a forward-facing one, making deliberation non-pointless and leaving the future genuinely open for the person deciding — and I'd like every leaf of the quiz tagged for which of those two jobs it delivers, with the positions that deliver only one flagged loudly, because I suspect that mismatch is the entire source of my group's talking past each other. If there's an established pair of terms for that split, use it instead of mine; if not, tell me whether my version holds up as a distinction or collapses under pressure. Second, I don't want the key to just be a list of the views people already defend: I want you to build the tree from the logical space — the questions, and the combinations of answers they allow — then place the actual positions in it, and mark the combinations where nobody lives and say why (incoherent? just unattractive? merely unexplored?). Also tell me which single question does the most sorting work, which one turns out to be useless because everyone answers it the same way, and which two positions your quiz genuinely cannot separate, plus what extra case you'd need to pry them apart.
