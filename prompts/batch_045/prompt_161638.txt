Please act as a professional philosopher — someone with real expertise in the philosophy of mind, emotion, and fiction, who also understands something about how virtual environments are actually built and moderated. Think the problem through before you write: I would much rather you consider and reject three or four candidate concepts than settle on the first one that rings a bell. I'm not asking for references, reading suggestions, or citations — I can't chase them up, and they wouldn't help me; I want the carving of the thing itself.

The context: I handle safety and moderation for a social VR platform, and I keep running into one configuration that I have no clean vocabulary for.

First instance. A player walks out onto a walkway the designers have built to look like cracked glass over a long drop. They hesitate, hands go out for the rail, some refuse to continue. If we attach a small in-world cost to turning back — a failed objective, a respawn timer — most of them go on. If we attach a real-money reward to crossing, all of them cross. Two minutes later, in the post-session chat, the same player writes that obviously it wasn't real and there was zero risk, and they aren't being defensive or ironic about it.

Second instance. In a ranked mode where a character can die permanently — a character the player has spent months building — players report sweating and nausea, and they buy protective items with real money. The same players, asked directly, will say the character is a bundle of numbers and that losing it harms nobody. What's striking is what the intensity tracks: it scales with the stakes inside the world (ranked versus unranked, permanent versus respawning loss) and barely at all with how photorealistic the rendering is. And it doesn't habituate. People with two thousand hours flinch as hard as newcomers.

Third, and this is the part I most want handled: it isn't belief. Asked mid-session whether there is any chance of being physically hurt, they say no, and they act on it — they will take the headset off to answer the door with a threat still on screen, they'll let a child watch, they don't call anyone. So the reaction isn't tracking a credence about real danger, and it isn't confusion, and it isn't mere startle either, since people spend money to avoid the outcome.

What I need is a name, if there is one, for this: a bodily and practical response that behaves like fear, or like valuing something, while coexisting with a settled, action-guiding belief that nothing real is at stake, and with no experienced conflict between the two. I expect the philosophy-of-fiction literature has something in this neighbourhood, but I've been told my field's own notion of presence or immersion is the wrong grain: it explains how strong the illusion is, not how the illusion sits beside knowledge that it is an illusion.

I also need the edges, because I have to write policy with this. Specifically: (a) What would have to change for this to stop being the special state and become plain fear — a real person behind the avatar making a credible threat? Consequences that survive logging off? A clinical exposure session where the fear is the entire point? (b) What would make it mere imagination or play-acting, with no claim on us at all? (c) When the in-world stake is convertible into real money, is the response about the world or about the money? (d) Does participation break the analogy to whatever established concept you reach for? In a film or a novel the consumer is a spectator and nothing they do is part of the story; in VR the player's action is part of the fiction, and some of the other characters are driven by real people with real intentions. Is that the same phenomenon with a louder body, or a different one?

In my notes I've been calling it stake-locking: the stake drives everything while you're inside the world, nothing at all once you're out, and the subject never registers the mismatch as a conflict. I'll drop that label immediately if there is a proper one. Either way, give me a definition tight enough that someone could argue with it, and tell me where it stops applying — I need to be able to write, without contradiction, both that a user was genuinely frightened and that nothing was done to them.

Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no concluding summary: (1) the nearest established concept and what it genuinely covers; (2) the respects in which what I've described is not that; (3) the strongest rival candidate and why you rejected it; (4) your recommended term — mine or yours — with your definition; (5) the two or three boundary cases that decide whether a given incident falls inside it.
