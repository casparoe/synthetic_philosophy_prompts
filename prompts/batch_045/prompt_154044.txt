ok so you're a professional philosopher who's taught Descartes to undergrads a bunch of times and you're a bit sick of the standard moves. Take a few paragraphs to brainstorm before you commit to anything, and please don't be diplomatic — I want you to actually pick a side and defend it.

I want you to compare two readings of the cogito. Not the historical question of what Descartes meant — I want the question of which reading is actually the better one.

Reading one, the existence reading: what the argument delivers is an existence claim. There is thinking going on, so there is something that thinks, and that's the Archimedean point, the one thing the deceiving-demon scenario can't touch. Existence does the heavy lifting, and the self, or at least a thinker, falls out of it.

Reading two — this ones my own invention, I haven't seen it anywhere, so don't waste time trying to place it: call it the standpoint reading. What the cogito really secures isn't existence at all but the availability of a standpoint. The certainty isn't that something exists; it's that there is something it is like to be the subject of these very thoughts, and that this is given to whoever is doing the reasoning in a way that can't become a further object of doubt — even if the thing occupying that standpoint is destroyed, swapped out, or never had any persistence in the first place. On this reading the cogito is basically a consciousness-first result, not a self-first one, and Descartes slides over the gap between there being a point of view and there being a thing that has it.

Compare them against these pressure cases, and be disciplined about where each is simply silent rather than forcing it to speak:

1. A demon world where the demon creates and annihilates a thinker every instant, each one getting exactly one thought. Does the cogito still secure anything, and what?
2. Someone mid-fission, about to become two psychologically continuous successors. What does the cogito secure for them, and does either reading have anything to say about who they will be?
3. A subject who runs the reasoning with no first-person concept available: not I am thinking but there is doubt ocurring now. Does the cogito still deliver, and does the difference in what it delivers count against one reading rather than the other?
4. Descartes own restriction that the certainty holds only while the thought is actually being had. Both readings need to explain why the conclusion goes cold the moment you stop attending, and I suspect they explain it for seperate reasons.
5. Whether either reading can bear the downstream weight Descartes puts on it — the real distinction between mind and body, and eventually getting the external world back.

Then, the part I care most about: what is the single strongest objection to each reading that the other one doesn't face? Which do you think is closer to right? And what would have to be shown — what kind of argument, or what kind of case — to flip you to the other one? If you think reading two collapses into some position that already exists under another name, say which and why.

No footnotes, no reading list, no name-dropping papers. Just the argument.
