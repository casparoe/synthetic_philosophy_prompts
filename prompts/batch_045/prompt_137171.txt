Hey — I'm running a 75-minute session for about 14 upper-level undergrads in a war-and-peace seminar. No real money, index cards and dice only. I want them to feel, before I name anything, why a threat of mutual destruction is only credible if the threatener has partly lost control of it, and why buying that credibility costs you safety. No lecture up front, and they shouldn't hear the words deterrence or brinkmanship until afterwards.

Here's the mechanic I sketched, which I'm not sure actually works. Two teams, six rounds, each round both secretly choose Back Down / Hold / Escalate. A shared risk counter accumulates the sum of both sides' Escalates over the whole game (not the max), and I roll at the end of every round: under the counter, catastrophe, big negative for everyone. Winning = the other team backs down first, worth noticeably less than catastrophe costs. Plus each team gets one delegation card, playable at the start of any round, handing their remaining choices to a device or to a hawkish subcomander who always Escalates. And halfway through, one team gets a counterforce option that knocks out part of the other side's ability to escalate.

Does that mechanic actually generate the paradox, or is it just Chicken with a dice roll? I suspect the counterforce card is doing the wrong work — it may make the strong side plainly safer, when what I want them to stumble on is that gaining a first-strike capability can leave you worse off, because it hands the other side a use-or-lose pressure and lowers the threshold for fighting lower down the escalation ladder. Fix it: rules, materials, payoff numbers that make the credibility problem bite, a timeline, how I rig the room, and a debrief that maps what happened back onto the two paradoxes. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong (I can see teams splitting the difference or not taking the risk counter seriously), and be honest about which part of the idea the game can't reproduce at all and I'll just have to say in the debrief.
