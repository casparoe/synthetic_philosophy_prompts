I have been arguing, on and off for about four years, in a small online group that originally formed to read the asymmetry argument for antinatalism and has long since outgrown it. I should say at the outset that I have no academic background: I left school at eighteen, I work in freight forwarding, and everything I know about this subject I picked up from that group and from arguing with it. What I want from you is not a verdict on whether we should stop having children. It is a diagnosis of why the argument never moves.

The argument as I have come to understand it runs like this. If I bring a child into existence, that child will suffer, and that suffering is a real harm to someone. If I do not bring the child into existence, the suffering that child would have had is not a harm to anyone, because there is no one there for it to be bad for. Meanwhile, if I do bring the child into existence, the happiness they enjoy is a real benefit to someone; but if I do not, the happiness they miss out on is not a loss to anyone either, again because there is no one there to be deprived of it. So creating someone buys them a benefit whose absence would have cost them nothing, and inflicts on them a harm whose absence would have spared them everything. Add the empirical claim that even a good human life contains a great deal of ordinary suffering, and the conclusion is supposed to follow.

What I find frustrating is that the same four moves repeat endlessly and nobody shifts. Someone says the two halves of the asymmetry are independent claims bolted together, and that you can grant both without any reason against creating following, since a reason needs someone to have it. Someone else says that if you reverse the second half you get a duty to have as many children as possible, which is absurd, so the asymmetry is doing all the work and cannot simply be asserted. Someone points out that if non-existence is better for the child, then non-existence has to be a condition the child could have been in and compared against, and there is no such condition. And then there is the plain disagreement about how much suffering an ordinary life actually contains, which seems to me to be a factual matter that nobody in the group is in a position to settle.

Three cases keep coming up, and I suspect they are where the real disagreement is hiding. First: suppose I could create a child whose life is certain to be mildly good, except for a one-in-two-hundred chance of a life dominated by unremitting agony. Everyone in the group agrees I should not. But if we all agree, that intuition cannot be what divides us, so what is? Second: suppose I can either pay for a treatment that will spare an existing five-year-old years of pain, or use the same money to create a child who would have a decent life. Everyone agrees I should treat the five-year-old. The antinatalists say this shows that creating has no positive value; the rest say it shows only that we should relieve suffering first, which nobody denies. The whole dispute seems to replay inside that disagreement, which makes me think it is not really about children at all. Third: if you could ask the particular person, before they existed, whether they would accept the life in question or prefer not to be, and they would accept it, does that settle anything? My side says no, because the only person who can answer is the person who already wants to go on living; the others say there is nobody else to ask.

My own hypothesis is that the force of the argument comes from a prior and quite separate asymmetry, one about reasons rather than about value: that pain is an emergency we are required to prevent, while happiness is a bonus we are merely permitted to provide. Grant that and the conclusion follows almost mechanically; refuse it and the argument has nothing to grip. On that reading the fight is not about procreation, and not really about the asymmetry either, but about a background picture of what we owe each other. A second, less confident suspicion: the argument talks throughout as though it were about the particular child I might create, but its conclusion is about whether there should be any children at all, and I cannot tell whether that is a feature or a fault.

So: what kind of stalemate is this? Is it a verbal dispute about what better and harm mean, a substantive dispute that happens to be hard, a clash of starting intuitions that neither side can argue for, a hidden empirical question about the texture of ordinary lives, or two different questions being pursued under one name? If it is several of these at once, I would like to know which is doing the most work. And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress here: a distinction, a test case that separates the positions, an agreement about what question we are asking, a decision to stop expecting argument to do work only temperament can do? If your honest answer is that no progress should be expected, I would rather be told that plainly, with the reason, than given an optimistic list of things that would not in fact help.
