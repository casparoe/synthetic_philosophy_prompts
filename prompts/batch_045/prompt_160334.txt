I'm a PhD student in political theory working on the state's interest in which children come to exist, and I keep tripping over a cluster of institutional arrangements whose implicit moral theory I cannot reconstruct cleanly. Let me give you a stylized composite of things I have seen in several public health systems, since that is the object I want diagnosed. A publicly funded fertility service offers IVF together with preimplantation genetic testing, but funds testing and selective transfer only for conditions that clear a severity threshold expressed in expected-QALY-lost terms; it declines to fund polygenic scoring. For any child actually born with one of the funded-screened conditions, the same system guarantees a lifetime support entitlement, and the size of that entitlement is indexed to how severe the condition was rated to be on the very scale that justified funding the screening. Meanwhile the clinics involved will report polygenic risk scores to parents for common diseases but not for educational attainment or height, and regulation caps the number of embryos created in a cycle at a fairly modest number. So, in shorthand: the state pays to prevent the birth of children with certain conditions, pays again to support the ones who are born anyway, indexes the second payment to the rating that justified the first, refuses to pay for selection within the ordinary range, and refuses to let anyone create enough embryos to make even the permitted selection precise.

What I want is the implicit theory, stated as sharply as you can manage, with three things kept apart: what the package strictly presupposes, what it merely sits well with, and what it is actually inconsistent with. I think the hard part is the metaphysics of benefit: since the child who results from a given transfer is not the child who would otherwise have resulted, it is unclear in what sense selecting one embryo rather than another is good for anyone, and I would like you to say which principle about selecting among possible people the practice has to be committed to, and whether that principle is the same one the rest of the health system uses when it weighs QALYs. Four tensions I suspect but cannot place: first, a health economist in my department argued that a fully compensating entitlement removes the welfare difference that was supposed to justify screening, so the two halves of the scheme defeat each other — I cannot tell whether that is a genuine reductio or a stable design feature, and I would like you to tell me which. Second, the severity rating looks like a cardinal claim about a life going less well, yet self-reported well-being among people with several of these conditions is often close to population averages, so I suspect the rating is secretly a resources or opportunity metric wearing welfare vocabulary. Third, the disease-versus-traits disclosure asymmetry seems to presuppose a line between a child's absolute prospects and competitive or positional advantage, which a straightforward maximizing principle of the kind that goes under the name procreative beneficence cannot obviously draw. Fourth, the embryo cap restricts the option set in a direction that same maximizing principle would want loosened.

Tell me which of those four are real and which are my confusions, and where the practice is simply underdetermined — several theories would license it — rather than inventing a tidier theory than the practice deserves. Then say concretely what would have to be added, dropped, or rewritten if the operative view were replaced by, in turn, a maximizing procreative beneficence view, a sufficiency view on which parents owe the child an adequate life but not the best available one, and a permissive view on which there is no duty to select at all. Finally, be explicit about the epistemic status of your reconstruction: mark the step in it you are least confident in, distinguish the places where you are reporting what the practice entails from places where you are defending a view of your own, and say what argument or empirical finding would make you withdraw the central claim of your answer.
