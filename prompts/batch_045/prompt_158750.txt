I'm on the volunteer investment committee of a small perpetual scholarship fund — about $9M, we meet four times a year, we have to pay out somewhere near 5% a year in scholarships no matter what the market did, and the founding donor's family is in the room. None of us are finance people except one retired actuary. Our current "policy" is a gut-feel 60/40 plus whoever sounds most confident that quarter.

We've been reading about the Kelly criterion / maximizing long-run geometric growth, and it's clicked for us that for a fund meant to last forever what matters is the growth rate of the corpus, not next year's expected return. But I can't hand a committee a formula. Please turn it into a decision procedure we can actually run in a 90-minute meeting on any proposal we get: the annual equity-vs-cash allocation, but also one-off skewed things — a donor challenge grant we could lever up on, a program-related investment that either returns 3x in six years or loses most of the principal, an offer to buy a building with a mortgage. I want it as questions in order, each with what counts as a good enough answer, where we stop with a no, and where it explicitly hands the decision back to us as a values call rather than a fact (I'm guessing the payout floor and the time horizon are in that category — tell me what else is).

Two things have to be built in. One: we almost never have probabilities, just an actuarys range and some vibes, so the procedure needs to say what to do when the input is "somewhere between 30 and 60%", and be honest about which steps are judgment with numbers attached. Two: our positions aren't independent — everything correlates with everything, and with the years the scholarships come due — so "size it as if it were the only bet" needs correcting, and I want to know how much the correction actually matters in practice.

Then run it on three cases: the annual allocation; the 3x-or-bust program-related investment our director is in love with; and a donor match that expires in 90 days. I want at least one case where the procedure gives us an answer we'll hate. And be straight about what maximizing geometric growth can't tell us — I suspect it has nothing to say about how bumpy the annual scholarship budget is allowed to be, and I want to know the other blind spots too.

It needs to be in a form I can put infront of the board president, who will ask why this isn't just expected return, and will want a two-sentence answer.
