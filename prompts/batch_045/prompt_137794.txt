Write a short story, between 1,200 and 1,800 words, in the third person and past tense, that dramatizes a problem in the theory of mental content known as teleosemantics. The story should be fiction from its first sentence to its last: no preface, no afterword, no footnotes, and no passage in which a character or the narrator steps back to explain the philosophy. Nothing should be cited, named, or alluded to from the real philosophical literature, and no character should invoke any real philosopher, book, article, or school of thought as an authority. If the dispute is recognizable to a reader who knows the literature, it should be recognizable only from how it is staged.

The material you should build the story around is as follows. Two field researchers are studying a migratory moth whose most important neural structure, a small paired organ they call the spindle, was shaped over thousands of generations by one selective pressure: detecting the wingbeat of a parasitoid wasp that used to hunt the moths in flight. The spindle's firing drove a reflex that makes a moth fold its wings and drop into the leaf litter. That wasp was wiped out within the researchers' own lifetimes, but the field station holds archive recordings of its wingbeat, and the researchers can play them back. When they do, the moths drop.

Three complications should arrive in the story at different points, and each should change what the researchers take to be at stake. First, the spindle now fires reliably to something else: the low infrasound that precedes violent squall lines in the region. Moths that drop before a squall survive; moths that do not are killed in enormous numbers. This new use has never been fully ratified by selection — a minority of broods still respond to the old wingbeat frequency and not to infrasound, so the shift is real but incomplete, and nobody can say when it began or when it will be finished. Second, in a nearby population the spindle turns out to be developmentally a fusion of the ancestral wasp-detector and a separate organ that was selected for something else entirely, measuring barometric drift for the timing of migration. In these moths a single state type has two ancestral jobs. Third, an island population, founded long ago by moths that never encountered the wasp, has a spindle produced by a different mutation with no wasp in its history at all, and it also drops to the archived recording.

Give the disagreement practical consequences rather than letting it float. A seismic survey using airgun arrays is scheduled to run during the moths' migration week. Whether the researchers file for an injunction, and on what grounds, depends on whether the moths are, in the relevant sense, getting the world wrong and being killed by their own error, or whether they are responding exactly as their states now bid them to respond to an environment that has merely turned hostile. Their conservation mandate compounds the problem: they are funded to preserve the ancestral migration, not the storm-sheltering behavior that is quietly replacing it.

Do not let either researcher win, and do not let them split the difference or agree to disagree in a way that dissolves the question. Avoid the technical vocabulary of the debate — content, representation, proper function, teleology, selected effect, teleosemantics — and find ordinary language for the disagreement instead. The final sentence should be a concrete image rather than a question or a thesis.
