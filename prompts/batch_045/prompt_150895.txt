Had a three-hour argument with my friend last night about whether a bigger population is actually better, and we got nowhere. Every time I raised a problem he'd say that's a different question, and every time he raised one I said the same thing back, so I want to start over at the top: before we argue about whether more people is good, I want the spec. What would a position on this have to get right to count as adequate?

Purpose matters here, so let me fix it: I don't need something that ranks abstract possible worlds against each other. I want a theory good enough to tell an actual finance ministry whether to spend money on baby bonuses this decade, and roughly how much. If the theory is fine for cosmology but useless for a budget line, say that.

Here's the stuff that actually came up, which I think has to show up somewhere in the list:

- The pension thing. More workers per retiree sounds great, except those workers retire too, so it looks like it just pushes the problem one generation down rather than solving it. I don't know if that's decisive or just a bad version of the point.
- His ideas point: more people means more potential Einsteins, more specialization, bigger markets, more innovation. I said that's about scale and institutions, not headcount as such, and that it depends on whether the extra people get educated and employed. Whoever's right, the theory has to keep "more total output" separate from "more output per person."
- His weird one about thresholds: that below some size a society can't sustain certain things at all — a full research university system, a serious military, a language community, even some skilled trades where the tacit knowledge dies out if there aren't enough practitioners to pass it on. I actually think this is the strongest thing he said, but I don't know if it's a real desideratum or just a prejudice of his.
- The identity mess. A child brought into existence isn't made better off by existing in the way a policy usually makes someone better off, so "more people = more welfare" can't straightforwardly mean good for the extra people. But my friend says that objection proves too much and would also make it fine to shrink to nothing.
- The repugnant conclusion thing — total welfare views apparently imply that a gigantic population of lives barely worth living beats a smaller population of excellent lives. He says fine, that's not repugnant. I say that shows the metric is broken.
- Who pays: the costs of more people land on the people doing the childrearing, and the benefits land on everybody. That seemed like a big deal to me and he waved it off as a separate question.
- Timing: bonuses might just make people have kids earlier instead of later, so births go up this year without anyone having more children in the end.
- Composition: pronatalist incentives recruit particular kinds of parents, so a theory that only counts heads is blind to which heads.
- Nonhuman animals: more people means more habitat converted and vastly more livestock, and if you count them the sign might flip entirely.
- If it's headcount that matters, why is the policy fertility rather than immigration — and if the answer is that it matters that they're ours, the theory owes an account of why.

So: give me the requirements list. For each one, the case or consideration behind it, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of one camp — especially tell me which of the things I listed above are genuine desiderata and which are the prejudices of somebody who's already picked a side. Then flag any pair of requirements you suspect can't be jointly satisfied, because if the list is internally inconsistent that's the most useful thing you could tell me. And tell me which single requirement does the most work in knocking out popular views.

At the end, briefly — a table is fine, don't spend more than a quarter of the answer on it — how total-welfare, average-welfare, person-affecting, and "humanity as a value in itself" style views do against your own list.
