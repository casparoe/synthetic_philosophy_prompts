hey — I'm working on a long blog post on Samuel Scheffler's afterlife conjecture, and I want to push it up against a rival view I've been kicking around. Roughly, Scheffler's idea (from Death and the Afterlife, based on his Berkeley Tanner Lectures) is that a lot of what we value in our lives depends on the assumption that humanity will keep going indefinitely after we die; that's supposed to explain why the doomsday scenario — everyone dies thirty days after you do — and the infertility scenario feel like catastrophes far out of proportion to the deaths or the lost welfare they involve. Call the rival the testamentary view: what matters isn't that humans as such keep existing, but that what we make and care about finds uptake — that at some point there are valuers who can recieve it, understand it, and carry it forward. On that view a civilization of digital successor minds, uplifted animals, or aliens who dig up our archives and genuinely get what we were doing would mostly satisfy the condition, even with Homo sapiens gone.

I don't want general pros and cons lists, I want the cases where the two come apart. So: run both views over these six scenarios and give me a markdown table, one row per scenario, with exactly these columns:

- scenario — short label, don't re-describe it
- afterlife_conjecture — how much value/meaning is lost on that view; use exactly one of: none / small / moderate / severe
- testamentary_view — same, same four-word scale
- diverging — yes or no: do the two views actually give different verdicts here, or are they just describing the same loss in different words
- intuitive_fit — which of the two verdicts matches your own pre-theoretic reaction to the case
- reason — one sentence max, the crux

Scenarios:
1. Doomsday: an asteroid kills everyone thirty days after your death. You die when you would have anyway.
2. Infertility: no more humans are ever born, everyone alive now lives out a normal lifespan, humanity winds down over about a century, nothing else about those lives gets worse.
3. Digital descent: biological humans die out within two centuries, but we're gradually replaced by successor minds that inherit our archives, keep our languages and music and maths alive, and clearly value them.
4. Alien uptake: humanity is wiped out by a plague next year. Ten thousand years later a distant alien civilization recovers our data caches, deciphers them, and finds real value in what we made — no shared biology, no causal continuity of practices, no memory of us as ancestors.
5. Hostile successors: humanity continues indefinitely and flourishes, but within three generations every value we hold is repudiated, our books are burned, and our works are reinterpreted as crimes.
6. Quiet amnesia: humans survive indefinitely, but the population drops to a few thousand, all records are lost, and nobody remembers that anyone lived before them.
