Our reading group is closing a six-week run on The Mind's I, and the last session is the dialogue in which Einstein's brain, kept alive in a vat and questioned through its electrodes, gets pressed on whether it is still anyone — and, more sharply, on whether an imagined book recording in advance every question it could be asked, together with the reply it would give, would be saying the same things it says. Half the group thinks the book plainly isn't thinking and the brain plainly is; the other half thinks the only difference left is speed, and that speed can't be where consciousness lives. Someone is also keeping the brain alive in order to ask it these things, and I'd rather the poem didn't let that drop out of frame. I want to close with a poem rather than with a summary, which is why I'm writing here.

What I'd like is a pantoum, five quatrains, in the voice of the brain. The form is the point, so hold to it strictly: the second and fourth lines of each quatrain reappear as the first and third lines of the next, and the final line of the poem is the poem's first line again — which means that after the opening stanza almost nothing in the poem is new, and the reader watches a small, fixed stock of lines get recombined under pressure from questions it cannot decline. That recycling is meant to carry the argumentative weight of the book: is a finite table of replies, reshuffled into new contexts, a mind, or only the appearance of one? Please have the poem actually work through the serious replies rather than gesture at them — that a book's entries are answers only for someone already asking and reading; that a table has nothing it is like and no one the word I could belong to; that any book complete enough would have to contain entries about its own entries and so never closes; that the brain's replies are produced by an active, causally integrated process rather than retrieved from storage; and that if we would not say consciousness drains out of a brain slowed down a millionfold, the slowness of the book cannot be what disqualifies it.

The closing return of the opening line should carry the verdict. Either it comes back having become an answer to something it could not have answered the first time — the same words now meaning more, because of everything that has passed between their two appearances — or it comes back flat, recognisably the same line doing the same work, and the reader hears in that flatness the answer instead. Whichever you take to be the honest ending, commit to that one rather than hedging between them. And keep it a poem: no stage directions, no epigraph, no prose gloss afterwards, nothing that needs a footnote for a reader who hasn't read the dialogue — it should also work for someone who doesn't know the piece, as a poem about being asked questions in a language you cannot stop answering in.
