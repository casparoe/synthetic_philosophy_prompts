I work on an incident-review team at a hospital system that uses a language model to draft replies to patient messages and to flag the ones a clinician should look at. Our postmortems are saturated with intentional language: the model assumed the chest pain was musculoskeletal, the model never registered that the patient was on an anticoagulant, the model wanted to be reassuring and so softened the recommendation. Someone finally challenged the practice, and our head of model evaluation defended it with an analogy I keep turning over.

His claim is that attributing beliefs and desires to a model is no more mysterious than attributing them to a corporation. When we say the firm believed the acquisition would clear regulatory review, we are not positing a group soul; we are using a description that earns its keep because (i) it predicts what the firm will do, (ii) it explains what the firm did better than any inventory of what its employees individually believed, and (iii) the states we attribute are not the states of any single member. He says all three hold for the model in exactly the same way, so the attributions are in equally good standing, and he adds that the standard presentation of the intentional stance — on which even a thermostat can be described as having beliefs — shows that the bar was never high to begin with.

Our compliance reviewer says the analogy is backwards, for two reasons. First, corporate attributions are anchored: there are board resolutions, memos, recorded decisions, employees who actually had the thoughts, and above all a legal person that can be fined and sued, so there is a place where attributions get audited and a place where the buck stops. Second, cheap attributions to a model are a way of moving blame away from the people who chose, deployed, monitored, and overrode it and onto the artifact.

A wrinkle I noticed and can't place: a firm persists through turnover and has legal continuity, whereas a model gets copied, forked, fine-tuned, and quantized, and its instantiations begin each call with nothing but the context window. The model, as it figures in our reports, seems to name a type with ephemeral instances rather than an individual with a history.

I'd like an audit of the analogy. Give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the members, the charter, board resolutions, institutional memory, legal personhood, liability, and the audit trail. Then separate the disanalogies that matter from the ones that don't, sorted by the three jobs we actually use these attributions for — predicting what the system will do on the next case, explaining a failure well enough to fix it, and allocating responsibility for an outcome. Two points I specifically want addressed: whether the intentional stance is even the best stance available here, given that for a firm we have nothing better while for a model we also have the design stance (architecture, training objective, weights) and increasingly circuit-level accounts that may outpredict our mentalistic talk; and whether the non-reducibility of group states to any single member's states is a point in the analogy's favor or quietly imports a grounding requirement that the model cannot meet. Does my colleague's conclusion survive? And if the corporate analogy fails, is there a better source domain? I've been wondering whether a model is closer to a firm's archived institutional memory — every memo it ever produced, searchable but speakerless — than to the firm itself, and whether it should be thought of as more like a population than an organism.
