My friend and I argued late into last night about burnout in the tenants' union campaign we are both volunteering on, and every turn of the argument got closed with the same line: the sentence that opens Epictetus's Handbook, "Some things are in our control and others not," which in the modern Stoicism he follows circulates as the slogan that some things are up to us and some are not. He was using it to argue that since whether the ordinance passes is not up to him, letting his mood depend on it is a category error, and that the right posture is to withdraw concern into his own effort and character. I had two objections I could not make precise enough for them to land. First, that "up to us" may not mean causally producible by me acting alone but something like dependent on my own judgment and assent, in which case whether I keep showing up is up to me in a way the vote is not, and the slogan by itself says nothing about whether an outcome outside my control can still be a legitimate object of concern. Second, that a two-way split seems to leave no slot for outcomes that depend jointly on many people's wills and so are partly but not wholly mine, and I could not tell whether that is a defect in my reading or in the slogan.

Could you set out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses that opening line might be expressing: causal production by me, counterfactual dependence on my will, moral responsibility or attributability, immunity to being hindered by others, what can be rationally demanded of me, and anything else that belongs on the list? For each, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false. What I most need is the last part: which of those readings would actually get my friend his conclusion about withdrawing concern, and whether any of them leaves room for the move I wanted to make, of pursuing an external outcome that is not up to me while withholding the judgment that securing it is mine to do.
