I am writing a paper on split-brain cases and the unity of consciousness, and I want to lean on a passage from Leibniz's *Monadology* (1714) to motivate one of my premises. The passage is §§13–14:

13. This variety must involve a multiplicity in the unity or in that which is simple. For since every natural change takes place by degrees, there must be something which changes and something which remains unchanged, and consequently there must be in the simple substance a plurality of conditions and relations, even though it has no parts.

14. The passing condition which involves and represents a multiplicity in the unity, or in the simple substance, is nothing else than what is called perception. This should be carefully distinguished from apperception or consciousness, as will appear in what follows.

A few sections later he adds that we experience this multiplicity in ourselves when we find that the most trifling thought of which we are conscious involves, in his words, "a variety in the object".

The premise I want to extract is this. Call *subject-unity* the relation that makes experiences the experiences of one and the same subject, and *stream-unity* the relation that makes them co-conscious, i.e. parts of one stream of experience at a time. Contemporary argument about commissurotomy patients, I want to say, conflates the two, because it tacitly accepts what I call the Integration Condition: stream-unity is exhausted by functional integration, so that once integration between the hemispheres is gone, any remaining experience must be distributed over two subjects rather than belonging to one subject whose experience has lost its stream-unity. Leibniz looks like a clean counterexample in principle: since a monad is simple and, as he says elsewhere, has no windows, the unity of its perceptions cannot be achieved by integrating parts, so it would have to be *unity by simplicity* — subject-unity and stream-unity both holding in the absence of any integration at all. If such a position is coherent, the inference from dissociation to two subjects rests on a premise nobody has defended.

Two questions about the passage I cannot settle, and on which my use of Leibniz turns.

1. Is the multiplicity in the unity of §§13–14 a multiplicity of represented contents *within* a single perceptual state — so that the unity at issue is the unity of one intentional state, and §§13–16 say nothing about what makes a succession of perceptions the perceptions of one subject? Or is it a multiplicity of perceptions, so that Leibniz is offering an account of subject-unity? The argument from gradual change in §13 pulls towards the second reading; the "variety in the object" of §16 pulls towards the first, on which my reading collapses. Which is right, and can the text decide it?

2. Leibniz separates perception from apperception and admits perceptions of which we are not conscious. Is he thereby committed, or merely permitted, to allow a single monad two streams of apperception that are not co-conscious with one another? If so, my contrast is misdrawn: unity by simplicity would be compatible with a divided subject, and the Leibnizian position would grant the dissociation literature everything it wants rather than nothing.

I am not asking for secondary literature — I have no way of checking it — so please argue from these sections and from the metaphysics of monads generally. Please also be explicit about the status of your reading: which claim in it you are least confident of, where it turns on a translation or on a contested interpretive decision rather than on the text, and what would convince you that the opposite reading is correct — a particular sentence of Leibniz's, a structural feature of his system, or a flaw in the argument I have sketched.
