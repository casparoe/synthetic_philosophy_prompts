I'm a third-year cognitive science major, and I'm the undergraduate representative on my university's Advisory Committee on Student-Facing Algorithms. This term I'm also taking a seminar on representation in minds and machines, which turns out to be unfortunate timing, because the case in front of us is close to a direct application of the reading. The counseling center and the first-year writing program jointly want to license Lark, a student-success assistant from a vendor called Fenwick Analytics, that would read students' weekly reflection posts and essay drafts and assign a risk score routing some of them to a human advisor. Fenwick's safety case does not rest mainly on outcome data. It rests on what they call internal evidence: their interpretability pipeline has isolated a direction in the model's activation space that they interpret as tracking acute distress; clamping that direction moves the model's outputs the way you would expect if it really were a distress feature; and suppressing the direction suppresses the routing behavior. Half the committee takes this to be a stronger guarantee than a behavioral audit, on the grounds that behavior only tells you about the inputs you thought to test, whereas a genuine internal posit would license extrapolation to the ones nobody thought to test. The other half takes the pipeline to have produced a useful redescription of behavior we already measured, and thinks that calling the direction a distress feature adds nothing we are entitled to rely on.

At our last meeting one member proposed a compromise criterion, and I have been turning it over ever since (including at breakfast with my roommate, who interns at a lab that does this kind of work and got annoyingly evasive when I asked her). The proposal: require Fenwick to recover the same interpreted feature in a second model that matches Lark's behavior on our audit set but was trained from a different initialization, with a different architecture and a different data ordering. If the pipeline finds such a direction in both, and it survives the same interventions in both, we treat the feature as real enough to lean on; if the two models' versions of the feature disagree, or if a fine-tuned variant of Lark produces the same routing behavior while the pipeline reports the original direction as inert, we treat all interpretability claims as description only, whatever Fenwick calls them. I think that is roughly the right test, but I genuinely cannot tell whether it settles anything or merely raises the bar on a question that was never empirical to begin with.

What I need from you is the decision memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting, addressed to the committee and written so that both Fenwick and our most skeptical faculty member could actually reply to it. It should have a bottom line: license as proposed, license with specified conditions and a deadline, or decline. I lean toward licensing with conditions, but argue for whatever you think is right. Along the way I want three things done explicitly. First, say what the committee would be committing itself to about whether these internal states are representations, and whether the decision even requires settling that question or can be made while leaving it open. Second, define any technical term you rely on (feature, direction, clamping) in enough plain language that the two humanities faculty on the committee can follow the argument. Third, give the strongest available version of the losing side's worry about untested inputs and unknown unknowns, and answer it rather than restating our position in a louder voice. Aim for roughly 900 to 1,200 words, and keep it free of citations, footnotes, and suggested reading: our packet goes out to people with no library access, so anything that depends on a source is useless to us.
