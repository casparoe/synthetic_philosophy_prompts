I'm a senior in high school working on an independent research paper about the species problem for my school's academic journal, and I'd like your help with one section of it. I don't need sources or citations — I have those — I want the reasoning.

The section is a thought experiment: imagine Darwin could be shown the evidence available now but not in his time, and then asked what a species really is. The evidence I have in mind is threefold. First, that for most of the history of life organisms were single-celled, and that such organisms routinely pass genes sideways to organisms we would classify as belonging to other species, or even to much more distant groups, so that for a large part of life's history the genealogy looks less like a branching tree than like a net. Second, that once we could compare whole genomes, the groups we get depend heavily on what we measure and where we draw a line: populations that look nearly identical to us and live alongside one another sometimes turn out never to exchange genes, while populations we would have treated as separate species turn out to interbreed freely and swap genes over long stretches. Third, that organisms can now be made rather than only found — genomes edited or written from scratch, an extinct form partly reconstructed from the genome of a living relative — so that whether a creature counts as a new species or as a modified member of an existing one can start to look like a design decision.

Assume he retains what he in fact held: that species are not fixed or separately created kinds, that the difference between a species and a strongly marked variety is one of degree, and that the word is largely a convenience for talking about sets of closely similar organisms. The live options, as I understand them, are that species are real units in nature and exactly one account of them is correct; that species are real but there are several genuinely different kinds of species and which one we invoke depends on what we are investigating; or that species are not real divisions of nature at all but a useful way of carving up a continuum.

My questions: which of these three would he most likely have settled on once he had absorbed that evidence, and would any of them have forced him to revise part of his own account of how new forms arise, or is the question of what a species is detachable from the mechanism for him? I have also been playing with a framing of my own — that the disagreement between the three options is really a disagreement about what the category is supposed to do for us, whether it is supposed to count kinds, track a lineage's history, or explain what keeps a group distinct — and I would like to know whether Darwin could have accepted that framing or whether it would have struck him as changing the subject. Please also say plainly where you think he would have been genuinely torn rather than where you are just uncertain yourself.

Begin your answer with a single sentence giving your overall verdict, then give the reasoning. No preamble and no restating my question.
