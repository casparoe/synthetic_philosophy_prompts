Hi — I'm the person on my team who keeps getting voluntold to write the "is this actually fair to users?" section of our model spec, so sorry in advance for the length. I work in applied RL, post-training / RLHF-ish side: we train against several reward heads at once (helpfulness, harmlessness, latency/cost, plus a learned per-user satisfaction model) and every few weeks we have to collapse them into one scalar objective to actually optimize. I'm comfortable with Pareto frontiers and dominated return vectors, linear scalarization and the fact that it can't reach non-convex parts of the frontier, mixed/stochastic policies and the convex hull of achievable returns, constrained MDPs with a hard safety threshold, and distributional RL with CVaR-style risk-sensitive objectives; I know roughly what the von Neumann–Morgenstern independence axiom says but I've never read any philosophy of it. Our current aggregation rule is a Nash-bargaining-style product over gains above a disagreement point (we use the last deployed policy as the disagreement point) with a lexicographic safety constraint bolted on, and it works fine, which makes me suspicious of anything that claims it's irrational.

What I want run through this vocabulary is Harsanyi's aggregation theorem. My half-remembered version: if each individual's preferences over risky prospects satisfy the vNM axioms, and society's preferences over those same prospects also satisfy the vNM axioms, and society respects unanimity (or at least can't dissent when everyone is indifferent), then social welfare has to be an affine / positively weighted sum of the individual utilities — and this is supposed to be an argument for utilitarianism. Restate that as precisely as you can in my terms, and please don't stop at "weighted sum = linear scalarization": I want to know what plays the role of a social alternative, what a lottery over social alternatives corresponds to in a training pipeline, what corresponds to an individual vNM utility function, and which assumption is actually doing the work (independence at the social level? unanimity over risky prospects? a fixed set of people? interpersonal comparability?). A few things I specifically want in the residue report: (1) people keep calling this utilitarianism, but the weights look undetermined to me — does getting equal weights need a whole separate argument, and does that separate argument have any counterpart in my world (something like sampling a uniformly random user, or a "you don't know which user you'll be" version of the disagreement point)? (2) our rule is Pareto-efficient but deliberately nonlinear and has a hard threshold — what exactly does it violate, and is the theorem silent or hostile about it? (3) we evaluate distributions, not means — a policy that occasionally goes very wrong is unacceptable even at a higher mean — so risk-sensitivity isn't sitting inside the utility curvature the way vNM wants; does the theorem just go quiet there? (4) the population isn't fixed: a decent fraction of accounts in any deployment window are new, so the set of "individuals" changes between the two things I'd want to compare. (5) I vaguely recall an objection about ex ante vs ex post Pareto — someone said Diamond, but I might be misremembering, so don't lean on it if I've got it garbled — and I want to know whether it has an analogue in my setting (expected experience under the lottery vs. what actually happens to the person who got served this rollout).

Then the part I actually care about: the verdict on the residue. Which parts translate cleanly, which only after you smuggle in something my framework doesn't assume, and which have no expression at all in it — and for each, is that a hole in my vocabulary or a sign that the theorem is less of a result than it's advertised as? Please flag the step in your translation you're least confident in, and say what would change your mind about that verdict: what argument or consideration would flip you from "your vocabulary is missing something real" to "the theorem is basically linear scalarization plus an assumption you'd never grant", or the other way round.
