Me and two friends have this argument that's been going round in circles for about a year — one of them is doing a part-time philosophy diploma, the other just likes arguing — and I've finally tried to write down what I actually think instead of just reacting to whatever was said last. Bit of background: I'm not academic at all, I work in transport planning, and I got into this after my aunt told me she was offered a university place at eighteen and turned it down, and that she's glad she did. She never left the village she grew up in, she's never had any money, and I genuinely couldn't tell whether that was her talking or the village talking. So here's roughly where I've landed: (1) I don't think you can just take what people say they want as the last word — if someone's spent their whole life with most of the doors shut, the fact that they now say they're happy with what they got isn't much evidence that it's what's good for them. (2) But I also think outsiders are terrible at this kind of judgement, and when people in a situation say collectively what's good for them — disability activists saying they aren't suffering, for example — then we should basically defer, and I think the nothing-about-us-without-us idea is right. (3) I get irritated when people call an unusual or expensive preference a waste; if someone genuinely wants to spend their life in some religious vocation, or on a hobby that costs a fortune, and nobody is forcing or tricking them, I think that's a real preference and should be treated as one. (4) I think there's a real chance, not a negligible one, that some of the moral things I'm most sure about are the sort of thing people will look back on the way I look back on my great-grandfather's views about women working, because every generation seems to have been totally certain of something that later looks like bias. (5) At the same time I'm very sure about a few specific moral judgements and I don't think "maybe you're the biased one" is a reason not to act on them — I'm vegetarian, I give money to a charity that funds surgery in poor countries, and I feel not remotely shaky about either. (6) I'm happy to call someone's moral view biased when it lines up suspiciously well with their own interests — the guy arguing that inheritance tax is theft, or the professor who thinks philosophy funding must be protected — but I roll my eyes when people dismiss a view as just an evolutionary or cultural byproduct, which in my experience always gets aimed at religion or nationalism and never at whatever the speaker happens to believe. (7) The rule of thumb I came up with myself for the (1) cases, which I've started calling the door test: I trust a preference more the longer it survives the person believing the door is genuinely open, and I trust it less the more it appeared or got stronger right after the door shut. Which on my own aunt seems to tell me to discount what she says, and that feels wrong to me, but I can't say why.

Can you audit this for me? What I'd like is the tensions ranked by how serious they actually are, not a list where everything conflicts with everything, and I especially want to know which pairs only look like they're in conflict and come apart when you look properly. For each real tension, tell me what would have to go and what it'd cost me to give it up, and then say which single change would clean up the most. Also, two things I can't work out on my own: does (4) pull against the very thought that my great-grandfather was wrong, or is that a fake problem; and is the door test actually doing any work or is it just (1) with extra steps? I really can't tell which of these I'd be willing to loose, and I'd rather not be told I have to drop whichever one you think I'm most attached to.
