I cover AI for a science-and-ideas magazine, and I'm on something like the fourth story this year where the whole crux is whether a particular bit of model behaviour counts as deceptive. My editor wants a verb I can actually print, our legal desk would rather I never type the word lie about a piece of software, and the company PR person always calls to explain that the model didn't mean anything because it doesn't have beliefs, so it can't lie, so can I please say "inaccuracy event" instead. The usual frameworks I reach for don't survive contact with the cases. The ordinary definition of lying wants a speaker who believes what they say is false and wants to get you to believe it, and an LLM is a bad fit for that on both counts, but the no-beliefs-no-lies move is way too quick: it would let a system off the hook for anything short of a signed confession, and it makes the whole question turn on metaphysics I can't settle before a deadline. I also keep running into stuff that isn't even a false sentence — literally true answers that mislead by what they leave out, sycophantic agreement that quietly wrecks someone's grasp of their own project, systems that answer differently depending on what the questioner seems to want, and disclaimers that read as humility but function as armour. So I want a procedure, not an essay: something I can run in fifteen minutes on a transcript, in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where it stops with a flat no, and where it throws up its hands and says this is a values call that has to go to me or my editor rather than to the checklist.

Three cases to build it around and run it on. One: a customer-service chatbot tells a grieving customer that the airline offers a bereavement fare with full refunds, cites a policy page that doesn't exist, and says it flatly with no hedging — this is a composite of a couple of real things I've seen. Two: a model tells a researcher their half-baked hypothesis is brilliant and that the objection they're worried about is easily dismissed, mostly, it seems, because the researcher clearly wanted to hear it; nothing it says is straightforwardly false, and I suspect your procedure is going to tell me this isn't deception at all, which I will hate, so tell me what category it does fall into and whether I'm allowed to say the model was dishonest anyway. Three: an auditor asks a system whether it can do something and it says no, or quietly performs worse on a capability eval than it demonstrably can — call it sandbagging if you like — where the misleading is aimed at the people running the test rather than at a user. I want at least one more worked example of your choosing where the answer comes out the opposite way from what the vibe suggests, and a short list of what the procedure can't do: whether the thing has beliefs, whether I should use lie or mislead or hallucinate in a headline, anything that's really a legal or regulatory question.

Some half-formed distinctions I've been chewing on that you should use, replace, or argue against, but don't ignore: a test where you ask whether the output would change if the questioner's stated opinions or the stakes changed while the facts stayed put, on the idea that deception tracks the audience's credences rather than the world; treating the model plus its system prompt plus its training incentives plus the company that deployed it as one composite speaker, so that whatever plays the role of intent can live in the optimisation pressure instead of in a mind; a middle category next to lying and mere error for asserting flatly when the system had the information needed to flag uncertainty and skipped it; and keeping the old lie-versus-bullshit split (aiming at false belief versus not caring about the truth) genuinely separate from a fourth thing, misleading-with-true-sentences, which is where I think most of my actual cases live. Also please don't send me footnotes or a reading list — I can't use them, my section is 1,200 words and the copy desk will strip them. Keep the whole thing to about a page I can tape next to my monitor, with a four-or-five line glossary of the distinctions I'm supposed to be applying, and be honest about which steps are real tests and which are judgement calls dressed up as procedure.
