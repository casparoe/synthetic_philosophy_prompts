I hold the following view about the original/derived intentionality distinction: there is no such thing as original intentionality. Every case of aboutness is inherited — from a designer's intentions, from a training regime, or, in our own case, from natural selection, which is not an agent and has no intentions to lend — so the difference between "original" and "derived" is at most a difference in how long and how opaque the causal history is, not a difference in metaphysical kind. Searle is right that syntax alone doesn't suffice for semantics, but on this view the point is inert: what supplies semantics is always history, and there is no privileged stopping place at the skull. This is roughly Dennett's line — the two-bitser argument — plus a case I don't think the literature settles: an orphaned lineage, a chain of systems each trained largely on the outputs of its predecessor, where no designer's intentions are reconstructible from the end state and no one is currently interpreting it. I want to say such a system's internal states are genuinely about things, and that nothing in the original/derived distinction blocks that.

What would count against this view? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: the arguments, cases, and results that should move me, including ones a defender of my view would concede rather than dismiss. I'm especially interested in whether history-of-selection accounts can make room for misrepresentation without collapsing into a success semantics that cannot accommodate error, whether "derived from" needs a terminus or can be circular, and whether the view buys its answer by making content relative to an interpreter's stance. Please also assess honestly whether the position has been qualified to the point where no possible finding could tell it apart from Searle's, and, if so, whether that is a defect in a claim of this sort.
