ok so context: im in an AI ethics / philosophy of technology seminar and we just had the week on the narrow way of stating the alignment problem — intent alignment vs value alignment, i.e. is the target "does what we specify" or "does what we actually want". The final paper is "take one work of fiction or film and read it through one concept from the course", and half my section is doing HAL from 2001, which my TA says she has graded forty times already, so I want something else. I want to do W. W. Jacobs's short story The Monkey's Paw (1902), because on the surface it looks like the cleanest possible instance of the distinction: the paw gives the Whites exactly what they ask for and it ruins them.

But I cant hand in "the paw is intent aligned but not value aligned" as a whole paper, that's one paragraph. Here's what I have so far, and I'd rather you argue with it than agree with it.

First: is the paw even intent aligned? A wish like "let us have two hundred pounds" is compatible with an indefinite number of ways the world could go, and the paw reliably lands on the worst one. So either (a) intent alignment is unachievable in principle, because any specification you can actually write down is underdetermined and filling in the gaps secretly requires the whole value function — which collapses intent alignment into value alignment and makes the narrow definition kind of pointless — or (b) the paw is just a bad interpreter, a specification-gaming system that a better one would fix, and the story is about a design failure rather than about the concept. Which does the story actually support, and is there a third option I'm missing?

Second thing I keep circling back to: most of the damage seems to come from irreversibility, not literalism. There's no undo, no check-back, no refusing after the fact, and the third wish only happens because the second one cant be taken back. If the paw were corrigible — reversible wishes, or a thing that paused and asked whether this is what you meant — the plot largely wouldn't happen. So is the real moral about a missing design property (no off switch, no deference) instead of about the gap between what's specified and what's wanted? And can I argue that without the paper turning into a different paper?

Third: whose intent? Morris says the fakir put the spell on the paw to prove something about fate punishing people who meddle with it. If that's right, the paw is perfectly aligned with its designer's objective and merely indifferent to its users'. My TA keeps saying intent alignment means the system does what the principal intended, but here designer and user come apart badly and the artifact seems to serve the first. Is that a genuine problem for the narrow definition, or is it just the ordinary principal-agent thing in a horror costume?
