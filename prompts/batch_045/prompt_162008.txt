I sit on my school's academic honor board, which decides cases by majority vote on a short list of separate yes/no questions rather than on a single verdict, and there is a pattern I keep running into that I cannot find a name for.

Here are two instances. In one case the five of us voted separately on whether a student had opened a file she was not authorized to open (yes, 3–2), whether she knew at the time that she was not allowed to (yes, 3–2), and whether she should be found in violation (no, 3–2), even though the third question was supposed to be settled by the first two. Each of us individually voted consistently; the mismatch appeared only in the totals. The same shape turned up when our science-fair panel voted on whether a project's method was sound, whether its data supported its conclusion, and whether it should advance: the first two passed, the third failed.

The part I actually want to ask about is a second layer. When I pointed out the mismatch in the honor-board case, we voted on how to handle it, and a majority of us endorsed each of three things that could not be done together: that where the sub-questions have clear majority answers we should follow where those answers lead; that a direct vote on the verdict should stand; and that in this particular case the verdict vote should not be overridden. So the board's answer seemed to depend on whether we asked a question or read it off the ones we had already answered, and the same dependence reappeared when the question was about the procedure itself rather than about the case.

I am not after a label. I want to know what exactly this phenomenon is and what it is not: how it differs from the familiar paradox where a group's preferences cycle among options (this doesn't feel like cycling, since nothing here rotates), whether the second layer is genuinely a further phenomenon or just the first one applied to itself, and where the edges lie — whether it depends on the questions being linked by a rule at all, or whether any set of separate majority votes can come apart this way. If the second layer has no name, I would like a proposed one, a definition tight enough to separate it from the first, and a case that sits on the boundary between them.
