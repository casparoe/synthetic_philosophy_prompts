me and two friends have a little reading group that's been stuck on promising for like a month now — the really basic question of why saying "I promise" does anything to you at all, and why the thing you end up with seems to be owed *to the person you promised* rather than just being a general duty floating around. We keep going in circles on whether promising is the exercise of some normative power to change someone else's normative situation, whether it's really just a useful convention that we've collectively decided to run (the Hume-flavoured story), or whether it's about handing someone assurance inside a relationship of mutual recognition. One of us also keeps insisting that the interesting bit is the promisee's right to release: that what you owe is *directed*, so only the promisee can let you off, and no amount of compensating them counts as a release. I want to write something that makes that tangle funny, so here's the ask.

Write a news article, headline plus roughly 650 words, in the voice of a slightly breathless fintech/regulatory trade newsletter (think a payments-industry trade rag that covers "compliance innovation" without ever asking whether the thing being innovated should exist), reporting on the launch of a startup called PromiseSwap. Here's the seeding I have in mind, use it or improve on it: users log a promise into the app; the platform matches it to a vetted "surrogate performer" who actually does the promised thing; the promisee is pushed a notification and a small "fidelity credit," and the platform's terms treat the credit plus surrogate performance as discharging the promisor. The newsletter should report, straight-faced, that PromiseSwap has also launched a second product for promisees: you can sell your right to release a given promise in advance, in "pre-consent bundles," so that promisors can buy their way out before the fact. Please invent your own names for the founder, the regulator, and any critic (make them clearly fictional — nothing that could be mistaken for a real philosopher), invent a couple of customer testimonials, and include one short sidebar of your choosing (an "excluded categories" box, a poll, a pricing table, whatever serves the joke).

The laughs have to come from actually getting the philosophy right rather than from generic startup mockery. So the piece should exaggerate real features of promising, not invented ones: that the obligation is directed and only the promisee can release it, so a firm that sells absolution is selling something it doesn't own; that surrogate performance can remove the *harm* of a broken promise while arguably leaving the *wrong*, and that sometimes the point was that *I* pick you up at the airport, not that a stranger does it better; that a promise to do what you were going to do anyway still binds, which is a pricing problem for the app; that you can't promise to do something wrong or to feel a feeling, so the terms of service have to carve those out; and that if the practice only has the force it has because of a convention we maintain together, then a private company scaling that convention into a market is trying to keep the benefit while quietly dismantling the institution — maybe charge users a "convention maintenance fee" for that. And the pre-consent bundle stuff should work as a slow reductio: if everyone has already sold their standing to complain, there's nobody left to promise *to*, and the reader should feel that without anyone in the article saying it. Keep the irony deadpan — nobody in the piece should figure out what's wrong, but a reader should be able to.

Don't hedge or give me a menu of options here: commit. Write the piece from inside one positive account of promising (convention-based, fidelity/mutual-recognition-based, or normative-power-based — pick the one you think makes the best satire and stick to it consistently), make your choices about tone and structure definite, and then add a short note after the article, 120-150 words, saying which account you wrote it from, where you exaggerated, and which contested reading you leaned on hardest (e.g. whether the right to release is inalienable, or whether substitute performance ever discharges a promissory obligation). That note is where I want you to be self-critical rather than defintely right — I'd rather know what the joke is costing you than be told it's all fine.
