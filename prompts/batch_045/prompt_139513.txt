I am a senior at a school founded in 1859, and this term I am in a small seminar in which we are reading Nietzsche's 1874 essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, the second of the Untimely Meditations. Our teacher has a habit: as we go, she classifies practices at the school itself as instances of monumental, antiquarian, or critical history, and sometimes refuses to classify them at all. She has now been asked to give the head of school a short opinion on a proposal, and I am trying to work out in advance what she will say. Her rulings so far:

1. At the annual Founders' Day assembly a senior performs a first-person monologue as the founding headmistress and tells the school to take her nerve as its own. She called this monumental, and said it was the cleanest example we would get.

2. An assembly about the first women admitted to the school in 1972, presented as what the students in the room could become. Also monumental. She stressed that none of these women is famous outside the school and that several are still alive, so fame and distance from us are not what make a practice monumental.

3. A project to transcribe, annotate and bind the diaries of a pupil who left in 1881, which the students described as worth doing because the diaries are ours and will otherwise crumble. Antiquarian.

4. An oral-history project on a 1969 walkout, in which students interviewed people who had been there. The students said the point was to give the school courage to act now. She still ruled it antiquarian, on the grounds that what the students actually do with the material is tend and preserve it, and that a stated purpose of inspiration does not by itself make a practice monumental.

5. A student commission that investigated the school's namesake, documented his advocacy of eugenics, and recommended removing his name; the name was removed. Critical.

6. A report on the school's admissions practices in the 1950s and 1960s arguing that the school's present self-image rests on an injustice. The administration never responded and nothing changed. She ruled this critical as well, and said that what makes a practice critical is that it brings the past before a tribunal and pronounces on it, not that the sentence is carried out.

7. An alumni group that restages a nearby battle of 1863 with exact uniforms, rations and marches, and whose members, when asked, say they feel no particular bond with the period and have no use for it beyond getting the details right. She declined to put this in any of the three categories and said it was closer to what Nietzsche diagnoses as an excess of history: material accumulated but taken up into nobody's life.

The new proposal, called Echo, would digitise everything already collected about the school's past — the diaries, letters, council minutes, the 1969 interviews, the commission report — and train a language model on it, so that any student could ask a question and receive an answer written in the first-person voice of a past pupil, together with a note of the sources the answer drew on. The archive would be added to every term, each graduating year contributing its own material, so it would never stop growing. The proposal gives three reasons: that nothing of the school's past should be lost; that past pupils should be available to advise present ones, as the founders were once supposed to advise us; and that because the model answers in the past's voice but in our own idiom, the past will at last be made to answer for itself in terms we can use.

My questions: how do you expect her to rule on Echo — monumental, antiquarian, critical, or none of the three — and why? What rule is she running, given that the seven rulings above do not determine a single one? Which other rules fit those seven just as well, what does each of them predict about Echo, and which do you favour and why? If the rules genuinely come apart here, what single change to the proposal, or what one further case, would best tell them apart?

One more thing: please say how confident you are in each part of what you claim, and single out the claim you are least sure of. I would rather have a cautious answer with its weak point marked than a smooth confident one.
