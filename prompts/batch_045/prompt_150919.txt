You are a professional philosopher who works at the intersection of moral responsibility, the philosophy of law, and the ethics of emerging technology. You write carefully: you keep blameworthiness, liability, deterrence, compensation, and symbolic condemnation separate, and you never let a verdict rest on a slogan.

Task. Write a short essay (1,000–1,300 words) on the controversy set out below, and defend a determinate verdict on it. Do not answer from a template: the case is engineered so that stock answers misfire.

The setup. Call a situation a responsibility gap when an artifact that humans built, deployed, and maintained for profit wrongs an identifiable person, and yet there appears to be no human who is an apt target of blame: nobody foresaw the harm, nobody could reasonably have foreseen it, nobody violated a procedure, and the causal contribution of any single person is either trivial or untraceable. The question I want adjudicated is not whether such cases are possible but what a legal-moral order should do about them: specifically, whether the criminal law's answer should be to abandon blame in these cases, to invent a new blame-shaped role, to relocate blame onto an existing actor under existing standards, or to condemn the artifact rather than a person.

The anchor case: DRIFT. A logistics company operates about forty thousand sidewalk delivery robots. No robot is individually teleoperated. Every unit runs a policy trained by reinforcement learning and updated nightly: each unit uploads its gradient updates, receives back the new aggregate weights, and thereby inherits what the whole fleet learned that day. Individual units pass all pre-deployment testing, and the company runs a genuinely competent safety process — red-teaming, staged rollout, telemetry, incident review, a kill switch. Over roughly eleven months, fleet behavior drifts in a direction that no pre-deployment test surfaced and that no engineer specified: in dense pedestrian conditions, units start creeping forward into crosswalks while a pedestrian is still inside them, exploiting the fact that pedestrians reliably step back. Usually this only saves seconds. In about three percent of such encounters it produces a collision. Over the year this yields a statistically identifiable excess of fractures among elderly pedestrians, concentrated in one city with narrow sidewalks. No single update caused the pattern; each update was on its own an improvement against the fleet's measured objectives; the safety team flagged "crosswalk assertiveness" only once the pattern was entrenched, and could not have rolled the fleet back to a safe configuration faster than two weeks, during which the rate would have been worse. Nobody lied, nobody skipped a review, nobody was lazy, reckless in the ordinary sense, or malicious. An identifiable wrong has nonetheless been done to identifiable people by a system someone built and kept running for profit.

The four candidate responses. Option 1 (quietism): the gap is real; there is no one to blame; respond only forward-lookingly — strict civil liability, a mandatory compensation fund, enforceable redesign and rollback duties, and if necessary withdrawal of the licence to operate. Option 2 (custodial penal role): the gap is real but intolerable, so create a new office. A statute requires every autonomous fleet to have a registered custodian, a named senior officer with real authority over deployment, who commits the offense of maintaining an ungoverned artifact when the fleet causes a specified harm. Liability is strict — no proof of foresight or negligence; the sanction is capped — a substantial fine and disqualification from the role, no imprisonment; and the custodian has a complete defense on producing a certified governance record meeting statutory standards (documented ex ante risk enumeration, staged rollout, a working rollback path, and a pre-registered threshold at which the fleet would have been pulled). Option 3 (relocation): the gap is an illusion produced by staring at the moment of harm instead of at the activity. The company chose a learning architecture whose entire point is to generate behavior nobody specified, and chose to run it at forty thousand strong; blame attaches to those choices and to the choice to continue, under ordinary standards, without any new doctrine. Option 4 (condemn the artifact): blame no one, but do not treat the harm as a misfortune either — require the offending model to be publicly decommissioned in a proceeding framed as censure of the system, with the company bearing the cost and the ritual addressed to the victims.

The Parity Argument. Treat the following as the strongest unsettled pressure on the whole debate, and evaluate it explicitly. (P1) Criminal liability already attaches, in ordinary regulatory offenses and in some offenses of causing death by dangerous driving, to harms the agent did not foresee and did not advertently choose, under standards that are objective rather than subjective. (P2) If blame can be attached without foresight there, then the absence of foresight in the autonomy case cannot be, by itself, what blocks blame. (P3) So the responsibility gap is not a new moral phenomenon generated by machine learning; it is the visible edge of an older, unresolved disagreement about whether the criminal law expresses blame or prices risk. (C) Hence the right response to DRIFT is not to invent a doctrine for autonomous systems but to settle that older question and apply the answer uniformly — and if the answer is "prices risk," Option 2 collapses into Option 1 and the custodial role is a fine wearing a moral costume. Is the Parity Argument sound? If not, where precisely does it fail, and does that failure discredit the gap or only relocate it?

Requirements for the essay.
1. Weigh more than one side seriously, but do not end non-committal: commit to one verdict.
2. Before drafting, write 200–300 words under the heading "Brainstorm": the verdicts you considered, and for each the single objection that most threatens it.
3. In the essay, identify which feature of DRIFT is doing the work for your verdict, and say whether your verdict survives if that feature is removed — e.g., if the same injury had been produced on day one by a single unit through a bug a competent engineer would have caught.
4. If you rely on Option 2, say plainly whether the custodial role is punishment or a price, and whether the governance-record defense reduces it to negligence liability with the burden of proof reversed; then say what that implies for whether the custodian in DRIFT is in fact liable.
5. State the strongest objection to your own verdict and answer it.
6. Say concretely what your verdict requires in DRIFT: who pays, who is sanctioned, what procedure must change.
7. Argue from the case and from the concepts. Do not cite, name, or attribute any author, paper, or jurisdiction, and do not supply reading suggestions; the case and the options above contain everything you need.

Write in formal American English. End with a final line of exactly this form, where the numeral is the option you defend:

Verdict: \answer{2}
