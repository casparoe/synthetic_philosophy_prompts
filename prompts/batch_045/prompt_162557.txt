I teach a grad seminar on wellbeing and public policy and I keep tripping up on the World Happiness Report, so let me ask you as if you were a colleague in the next office. The practice: every year a UN-backed report ranks something like 140 countries by averaging people's answers to one life-evaluation question — picture a ladder from 0 (the worst possible life for you) to 10 (the best possible life for you), which step do you stand on now — and then explains the gaps between countries with a handful of circumstances (income, health, having someone to count on, freedom, generosity, low corruption). Bhutan pushed through a UN resolution back in 2011 inviting governments to give happiness and wellbeing more weight in how they measure development, and a fair number of states have since built wellbeing dashboards, budget targets, even happiness portfolios around numbers of this kind. What I cant pin down is what the whole package — a league table, plus governments treating movement in it as an achievement — has to assume. Don't just recite the report's own rationale for using life evaluations instead of emotions, I've read that part; I want the implicit theory, what it quietly rules out, and were the practice is philosophically underdetermined.

Please answer as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, no preamble and no closing line:

1. Reconstruct the theory of happiness the practice needs. Is it genuinely committed to happiness as a reflective judgment about your life rather than as a feeling, or could a plain hedonic view, or an objective-list view treating the scores as a handy proxy, license it just as well? Say which parts strictly presuppose the judgment view and which merely sit comfortably with it.

2. The ladder asks people to place themselves against the best life they can imagine for themselves, and never uses the word happiness. What does the practice have to assume about that top rung — that it is stable, that it is comparable across persons and cultures — and what happens to the measurement if people quietly revise it downwards, the adaptive-preference case, or upwards?

3. Here's a test of my own that I rather like: count a policy as a real wellbeing improvement only if the country's score would still have risen had the government never announced a wellbeing target and nobody knew their answer was being tracked. Does the practice presuppose that self-reports are publicity-proof in this sense, and can a life-evaluation question meet that demand, given that it records a judgment people partly form from cues about what answer is expected of them?

4. A ranking of averages is zero-sum: one country's climb is another's slide. Does a state that sets out to top the table thereby commit itself to something positional about wellbeing, and is that a discovery about wellbeing or an artifact of the league-table format? Does targeting the average score rather than the rank fix it, and is either consistent with also using the number as a measure of how people's lives are going?

5. Suppose we swapped the judgment view for an experience-based one — affect sampled through the day or reconstructed from yesterday, weighted by person-hours. What would actually have to change: the question, the cross-country comparisons, the weighting, the ranking itself? And would my publicity test from item 3 be easier or harder to pass under that replacement?
