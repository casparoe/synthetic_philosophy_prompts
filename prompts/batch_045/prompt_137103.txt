I'm writing a novel set mostly in a livery stable upstate (long story — my agent talked me out of the version set in an antique map shop), and one of my characters, Nell, who runs the place, has a bad habit of settling arguments with stuff she half-remembers from a philosophy minor she never finished. There's a scene I love and can't quite make work: a buyer contracted for "one white horse" for a wedding shoot, Nell's foreman sent a grey one, the buyer is furious, and the foreman's whole defense is that a grey horse is still a horse and the contract says horse. Nell says no — I asked for a white horse, and a grey one no more answers to *that* than a black one would, and if you think it does you've mixed up what I asked for with what you happened to put on the trailer. And then, insufferably, she quotes this, which I found in a translation online (it's from the "White Horse" chapter of the Gongsun Longzi, the Warring States text attributed to Gongsun Long):

[Advocate] "'Horse' is what designates shape. 'White' is what designates color. What designates color is not what designates shape. Therefore I say: a white horse is not a horse."
[Objector] "If there is a white horse, one cannot say there is no horse. If one cannot say there is no horse, does that not mean there is a horse? Since having a white horse counts as having a horse, how can the white [one] not be a horse?"
[Advocate] "If one seeks a horse, yellow and black horses may all be delivered. If one seeks a white horse, yellow and black horses may not be delivered. Suppose a white horse were [just] a horse. Then what is sought [in the two cases] would be the same. If what is sought were the same, then 'white' would not differ from 'horse.' If what is sought does not differ, how is it that yellow and black horses are acceptable [in one case] and unacceptable [in the other]? That acceptable and unacceptable are mutually opposed is clear. Therefore yellow and black horses are the same in that they can answer to 'there is a horse,' but cannot answer to 'there is a white horse.' This is a white horse's not being a horse: it is certain."

Here's what I can't work out, and I need to before I can decide what Nell gets to say. (1) Does the "may be delivered / may not be delivered" bit actually prove anything about horses, or only about requests? It looks to me like it shows that *asking for* a white horse and *asking for* a horse are different requests — which the foreman can happily grant while still saying he delivered a horse — and not that a white horse isn't one. If that's right, Nell's speech is a cheat and I have to cut it. (2) I genuinely can't follow the step "If what is sought were the same, then 'white' would not differ from 'horse'": how does sameness of what's sought get you to sameness of the two words? Is there a suppressed premise, and is it the same suppressed premise as in the shape/color bit at the start, or a different one? (3) Is the objector's "having a white horse counts as having a horse" ever answered anywhere in the dialogue? My paraphrase of what comes later is that the advocate goes on to say something like: horse without white is horse, white without horse is white, it's only the two together that give you the compound name, and whiteness on its own doesn't settle on any particular thing the way a white horse does — but I don't see how that's a reply rather than just restating his thesis in fancier words. (4) The thing that would actually save my scene: my own twist on the case. Suppose Nell had asked for "a horse" in the contract but everyone knew she needed a white one for the photos, and the foreman brings a black one — she still didn't get what she sought, but now the words she used were the foreman's words. And suppose instead someone delivers a carved wooden horse: the "seeking" test says no to that too, which makes me suspect the test proves too much and can't tell apart a modifier that narrows within the kind (white) from one that kicks the thing out of the kind (wooden). So: what exactly is the argument in that passage an argument for, stated as precisely as you can, what is the strongest version of it, what is the honest objection to it that a smart reader will raise, and which of those — if any — can I put in Nell's mouth about grey horses, black horses, and wooden ones without her sounding like she's bluffing?
