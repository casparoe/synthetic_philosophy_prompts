I've been reading the rationalist blogs since the Sequences were still being posted, mostly lurking, and there's one move I see people make constantly that I've never actually cashed out: "that's a skyhook," "you need a crane there," "that's greedy reductionism." I nod along every time, but I don't think I could tell you what any of it commits you to, and I suspect I've been nodding at vibes.

I'm an SRE. I write postmortems, I own a couple of services, and the vocabulary I actually think in is: root cause vs contributing factors, causal chain, blast radius, dependency graph, interface and contract, black box, leaky abstraction, tech debt, "magic" as a term of abuse, debuggability and observability, five whys, retry storms and cascading failure, and "we stop drilling down when we hit something we can file a ticket against." I'm fluent in that and basically illiterate in philosophy.

So: restate the crane/skyhook distinction and the good-reductionism vs greedy-reductionism contrast in exactly that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Not an analogy with a footnote attached — a working translation. And where you have to bend either side to make the pieces fit, say which knob you turned and why.

Three things I specifically want you to run through the translation, because I think they're where it either earns its keep or falls apart:

1. Licensed stopping points. In my world, "it's always DNS," "the vendor's SDK did something weird," "the clock on one node was off" are perfectly respectable places to stop, because the postmortem's job ends at an actionable remediation, not at the bedrock of reality. But if "unexplained explainer" is close to the definition of a skyhook, then most of myfield runs on skyhooks and the concept has no teeth. So: where does Dennett say the regress stops? Is that stop the same kind of thing as my "file a ticket against it" stop, or a different animal entirely? If different, say exactly how.

2. The vertical axis. The stock examples all seem to be top-down: mind, God, elan vital, the ghost in the machine. But in my experience the tempting pseudo-explanations are often bottom-up ("it's all just voltages on a wire, there's no such thing as 'the service'") or sideways ("leadership deprioritized reliability"). Does the up/down metaphor survive translation into a dependency graph, or does it quietly smuggle in a hierarchy my vocabulary doesn't have and maybe doesn't want?

3. The crane-shaped skyhook. Here's a pattern I keep hitting and have no name for: a component with a clean interface, good docs, observable inputs and outputs, and nothing inside it holding it up — a vendor ML model sitting in the request path, say. In my vocabulary the contract is what makes something a legitimate part of the system; I can rely on it without knowing how it works. The crane/skyhook distinction seems to be about the insides. Is "has a contract you can depend on" a *different* distinction that people around here routinely conflate with "is a crane," and what breaks when the two get merged?

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about:
- What translated cleanly.
- What translated only with distortion, and what you had to give up to make it go through.
- What has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all.
For each of those three: is it a gap in my vocabulary, or a sign that the original distinction is doing less work than the way it gets deployed in comment sections? In particular, steelman the view that "skyhook" is just "hypothesis I disapprove of plus a feeling that it's unearned," and then tell me whether, given only my vocabulary, there's any non-social test that would seperate a crane from a skyhook. If there isn't one, say so plainly rather than inventing a test you don't believe in.

Two constraints:
- Don't hand me a reading list or tell me to go look at chapter N of some book; describe whatever example you need in your own words. Assume you have no way to look anything up.
- Give me at least one case where actually running the translation changes what I'd write in the "Action Items" section of a real postmortem — a case where thinking this way would make me do something different, not just describe the same thing in different words. If it never cashes out that way, I want to know that too.

Aim for roughly 1000-1400 words, sections with headings, no padding, and please don't flatter my framework by deciding in advance that it's adequate.
