I'm writing the framing section for an internal document on scalable oversight, and I keep going back and forth on one particular claim, so I want your number on it together with the derivation rather than a survey of the literature. Take the standard debate setup: a human judge who is weaker than the systems being overseen, two AI debaters that make claims about a hard question and cross-examine each other, and a judge who picks a winner; the safety case for the whole thing rests on the thought that whatever the honest debater says is easier to defend than whatever the deceptive debater says. Now restrict attention to the regime that actually matters for oversight. The question is one the judge could not check alone — a technical or specialized empirical matter, say whether an apparent error in someone's cost-effectiveness model is a real error, or whether a given laboratory protocol carries a particular off-target risk — and both debaters are drawn from the same near-frontier model family, trained on overlapping data with overlapping objectives. Here is the proposition: in that regime, the fraction of questions on which the judge ends up endorsing the true answer after a full debate is no better than the fraction on which the judge endorses the true answer after simply reading one model's answer along with the reasoning it offers, where "can't tell" counts as a miss. In other words, the debate add-on buys nothing, or less than nothing.

I am at about 0.55 on this; a friend who works on debate is at about 0.25; and I would like to know where that gap lives. Give me your own number and decompose it. At minimum I want: (a) the probability that the asymmetry itself holds in principle for questions of this kind — that for the true answer there exists some short chain of sub-claims a competent judge can check, and for the plausible false answers there does not; (b) the probability, conditional on (a), that two debaters from the same model family actually realize that asymmetry rather than sharing a common-mode blind spot located in exactly the region where the judge is weakest; (c) the probability that cross-examination informs the judge rather than degrading into a regress in which every supporting sub-claim is itself contested and the judge is left adjudicating disputes about how to adjudicate; and (d) the probability that questions of this kind even have the determinate, checkable answer that debate is supposed to converge on, as opposed to being judgment calls under uncertainty where the honest answer is hedged and the dishonest one is crisp. Add whatever terms you think I have omitted. Two I have been worrying about and do not know how to price are the rhetorical cost of hedging — the honest debater is penalized for saying something qualified and boring — and something I have started calling the asymmetry of surprise: the true answer is often the surprising one, and a judge who penalizes whichever side bears the burden of surprise will systematically penalize it for being true. I have also been going back and forth on whether the judge's own errors are independent of the debaters' errors, or whether all three fail together.

For each term give a number and one line of justification, then say how they combine into your headline figure. Then tell me which single term your number is most sensitive to, which term you are least confident in, what one experiment or argument would move that term most, and — between me at 0.55 and my friend at 0.25 — which of these sub-questions is the most likely home of our disagreement.
