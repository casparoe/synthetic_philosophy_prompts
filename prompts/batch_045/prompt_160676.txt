My friend Ravi and I had a proper shouting match at a barbecue on Sunday and we're still not really speaking, so I've decided to settle it by making other people act it out instead of by arguing more.

Ravi is on the board of his neighborhood association and he's been fighting a 40-unit building on a corner lot ten minutes from the train. His line is: nobody here is against housing, we just think the character of this particular block is worth protecting. My line is that that sentence is what self-interest sounds like when it puts on a cardigan. We went round on it for an hour and got exactly nowhere, because every time I said "that's your equity talking," he said "no, it's an aesthetic preference, I liked these houses before I bought one," and I honestly could not pry those two apart and neither could he. That's the bit I want people to feel.

So: I co-run a monthly urbanism thing, half tenant-union people and half policy-nerd types, usually 18-22 of us, 90 minutes, folding chairs in a church basement, no projector, budget of basically nothing. Design a session for us where the room runs into Ravi's problem itself instead of hearing me talk about it.

What I'm picturing, but you fix it if you have something better: a staged hearing over a paper map of maybe eight or ten blocks, one of which has the good school and the transit stop. Rules, materials (index cards and poker chips are fine, I don't want an app), a timeline with minutes on it, and no lecture up front — nobody should hear "NIMBY," "right to the city," "exclusionary zoning," "positional good," or the homevoter thing until the debrief.

Three things I definately want built into the mechanics rather than into what I say afterwards:

One, the payoff for living in the good block has to be positional, not absolute. Whatever chips people score for amenity access should be worth more when fewer players hold them, so that blocking the building doesn't create anything, it just defends a rank. Give me the actual payoff table so I can check you didn't sneak in a flat bonus.

Two, hand out roles and make several of them people who don't currently live in the voting district: someone who grew up there and got priced out and now lives forty minutes away but whose kid still goes to school there, a night-shift orderly at the hospital in the district who can't afford to rent in it, someone on the housing waitlist, an adult kid who wants to move back, a small landlord with one duplex. Everyone argues for "the neighborhood," nobody is allowed to argue for themselves — and then run the identical proposal twice with a different franchise the second time (block-only, then everyone with any connection to the district) so the outcome turns on who counts as the community rather than on the merits. If the vote doesn't actually flip in the room, tell me what to do instead, because I suspect it sometimes won't.

Three, the thing I actually care about: after each person speaks, they privately write down their real reason on a card from a fixed list — aesthetic, financial, fear of change, loyalty to neighbours, fairness to outsiders, other — and everyone else writes down what they think it was. Tally hits against guesses at the end and put the number on the board. I want that gap to be the moment the room notices something, not a number I explain.

If you think adding a re-randomization at the end (people get reshuffled into new roles and scored again) sharpens this or wrecks it, say which and why, and leave it out if it wrecks it.

Then a debrief that maps specific moments onto specific distinctions: the same proposal flipping with the franchise goes to the boundary problem, the stated-vs-guessed reason gap goes to why neither side of my argument with Ravi can audit the other, and the priced-out parent versus the waitlist person versus the hospital worker goes to three different claims that all get called "a right to the city" — a right to be housed somewhere decent at all, a right not to be pushed out or to come back, and a right to be present in and shape the central district. Say which of those three the game shows no amount of new construction can satisfy, and which one it shows can be satisfied, and be honest if the upshot is that Ravi and I were both talking past each other.

Also: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (I can already see two of our regulars meta-gaming the chips and one person taking the whole thing personally because she's literally on a zoning board), how to handle it, and which part of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and I'll just have to say out loud myself.

Last thing, and I mean it: tell me where your design loads the dice against Ravi. Which bit of the scoring quietly assumes a contested empirical claim — about whether new market-rate units loosen anything upstream, about who actually turns up to hearings, whatever — and what result in the room would make you tell me to scrap the session and run something else entirely. And flag the part you're least confident about, the bit you'd expect to go flat with this particular crowd rather than with students.
