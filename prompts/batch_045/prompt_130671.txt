A chain of reasoning keeps converting a modest empirical claim into a global scepticism about perceptual justification, and I cannot locate the step that does the converting.

(1) Say that an experience is cognitively penetrated only if, with the distal stimulus and the state of the sense organs held fixed, a difference in the subject's prior beliefs, concepts, desires or expectations still makes a difference to what the experience is like.

(2) Suppose penetration so defined is actual. The case I cannot shake involves two experienced birders who have each spent years searching for a different one of two nearly indistinguishable warblers, one of which shows a faint buff patch on the flank. Looking at the same bird in the same light, each reports seeing her own species' flank patch, and each describes the patch as visually insistent in the way something unexpected is insistent. Call this the two-birder case.

(3) If an experience is penetrated, then part of what fixes its phenomenal character is the subject's prior states, so the experience is not a pure delivery of the world to the subject.

(4) An experience cannot confer justification on a belief if part of why the experience is as it is derives from that very belief or from the states that produced it; the justification would run in a circle.

(5) But every experience a mature perceiver could appeal to is penetrated by something — a standing expectation about how things of that kind look, a theory about what is likely to turn up, a trained habit of looking where one has learned to look — since nobody meets the world with an empty head.

(6) So no perceptual experience ever confers justification on any belief, and perceptual foundationalism is false.

I want to deny (6). I take (1) to be the correct definition rather than a stipulation I can bargain over, and (4) reads to me as a truism, although perhaps that reading is exactly the illusion. Something appears to shift between (3) and (5), or again between (5) and (6), but I cannot say what. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it commit you to?

Please answer in four named sections, in this order: "Diagnosis" (about 150 words), naming the step you reject and stating precisely how it fails, distinguishing clearly between a scope or sense shift, an equivocation, and an outright false premise; "Why it looked sound" (about 100 words); "The price of the alternatives" (about 200 words), taking premises (1), (4) and (5) in turn and saying, for each, what a theorist gives up by denying it and what the most attractive version of that denial would look like; and "Verdict" (about 80 words), including a judgement on whether the two-birder case, as I have described it, even satisfies (1).
