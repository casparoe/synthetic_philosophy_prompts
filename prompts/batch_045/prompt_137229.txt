Working title: Equality of re-doability?

1. The standard menu and my target. Egalitarians standardly ask whether the proper currency of justice is welfare, resources, or capabilities. My friend defended the capability approach associated with Sen and Nussbaum: what matters is people's real freedom to achieve valuable functionings, not merely their holdings or their mental states. I grant that this is an advance over welfare and resource metrics, but I argued that all three currencies are too synchronic. They ask what a person can do or be at a time, or what resources she commands, but they neglect the diachronic cost of revising a practical identity or a central plan once it has been adopted. My positive proposal is that we should equalize a fourth thing: equality of re-doability. By re-doability I mean the socially and materially conditioned cost to an agent of reversing or revising a major commitment or plan when the agent's own ranking of the relevant options has changed.

2. The central distinction. I distinguish two kinds of revision cost. First, exogenous revision: the agent switches because the external payoff or feasibility of the old plan has worsened. For example, a musician becomes a carpenter because automation destroys the market for live music. Here I claim the agent may reasonably bear the costs of switching; this is a form of ordinary option luck or market risk. Second, endogenous revision: the agent switches because her own conception of the good, or her judgment about what makes the activity worth doing, has changed. For example, the musician no longer values music and comes to care about carpentry, even though music remains as feasible and as well paid as ever. Here, I argue, the costs of revision are not a matter of her responsibility in the same way, and justice requires that these revision costs be equalized, not merely that the agent have the bare capability to switch.

3. The Palimpsest Case. Ada and Bede both have the same formal capabilities: each can be a musician or a carpenter, and each has the resources, training, and physical ability to attempt either. Ada is raised in a community that treats both careers as ordinary options; if at thirty she abandons music for carpentry, she pays moderate retraining costs and keeps her relationships. Bede is raised inside a rigid musical dynasty. He too can afford carpentry training and has the ability to do the work, so on a standard capability metric he has the capability to be a carpenter. But if he switches, he loses his family, his community, and his default place in the social world. His material costs are similar to Ada's, but his re-doability is far lower because the revision costs are much higher. I claim Ada and Bede are not equally free with respect to the space of lives, even though their capability sets look alike.

4. The Marx connection, paraphrased rather than quoted. In his 1875 Critique of the Gotha Programme, Marx objects to a principle of equal right that measures everyone by a single equal standard, namely labor contribution. His complaint, as I understand it, is that any such standard is defective because it regards inherently unequal individuals only from one side, as workers, and ignores their differing needs and circumstances; the remedy, in his picture of a higher phase, is to replace contribution with need. I want to press a further step. Marx's insight is not only that a single contemporaneous standard ignores need; it is that any single contemporaneous standard is blind to the diachronic problem of re-measuring a life when the person herself has changed. Equalizing need at a time still asks what a person needs now; it does not ask what it would cost her to become a different person with different needs. Re-doability is meant to capture that missing dimension. My notes therefore treat the move from contribution to need as a half-way house, and argue that the full lesson of the equal-right discussion points toward a metric sensitive to the costs of re-orienting a life, not just to the satisfaction of a given set of needs.

5. Worries I can already see. First, reductionism: a critic will say re-doability is just resources plus capabilities plus some welfare facts, not a new currency. Second, responsibility: if Bede's community is voluntarily rigid, is it unjust that Bede faces high revision costs, or is that simply the price of other people's associative freedom? Third, measurability: how could we compare Ada's and Bede's re-doability without an implausibly fine-grained interpersonal metric? Fourth, paternalism: does equalizing endogenous revision costs require the state to take a view of which changes of heart are authentic?
