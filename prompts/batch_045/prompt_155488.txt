It's almost always a conversation stopper. Last week at a conference an AI-safety panelist used it to wave off a question about whether choosing to build an autonomous targeting capability into a weapons system is itself a political decision, rather than a neutral capability that some government might later misuse. I want to file something sharper than "well, it depends," so I need the actual options.

Can you lay out, as a menu from the boring to the wild, the precise claims that line could be making? The ones I can think of: a causal claim (artifacts don't make anybody do anything); a responsibility claim (praise and blame attach to users and nobody else); a design claim (features are engineering tradeoffs, not values smuggled in); an authority claim (choices about artifacts belong to engineers and buyers, not to the people who live near them); and what I'd call the consent gap — that a design choice counts as political when the people who end up bearing its costs were never in the room, like a face-recognition system tuned to work well on one group and badly on another. Add any I've missed.

For each one: is it trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false? And the bit I actually care about — which of these would really support brushing off the targeting question, and which only look like they do? Also: which reading is a panelist in a hurry most likely to have meant, and does that reading survive once you notice that declining to build the capability is also a decision somebody makes?
