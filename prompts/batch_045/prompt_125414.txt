I teach a graduate seminar on risk regulation, and I keep failing to convince my students that the precautionary principle is anything more than cost–benefit analysis wearing a safety helmet. Every example I bring — a pesticide, a food additive, an industrial process — gets dissolved: they re-run the case with a slightly more pessimistic utility for death or a more conservative prior, expected-utility maximization then delivers exactly the precautionary verdict on its own, and they conclude the principle was redundant all along. So I want a case constructed to order, built so that this particular move is unavailable.

The specifications:

(a) Realistic and small-scale: something a city council, a water utility, a hospital board, or a school district could genuinely face this year. No existential risk, no science fiction, no unfamiliar technology. The uncertainty should concern whether a familiar intervention carries a harmful side effect, not the fate of the world.

(b) Well-characterized numbers, stated explicitly: a probability interval for the suspected harm, a magnitude for it, and a magnitude for the benefit forgone if the intervention is rejected. Put the probability somewhere between 1 in 20 and 1 in 5, so no de minimis threshold applies; make the harm finite and modest — serious illness or a handful of deaths, not catastrophe — and make the uncertainty the ordinary bounded kind that lets an expected-value calculation go through cleanly. The benefit forgone should itself be a health benefit, so the disagreement isn't merely about monetizing life. In short, deny yourself the two things the principle is usually accused of exploiting: a vast unknown tail, and a probability too small to estimate.

(c) The verdicts: expected-utility maximization must clearly favor adopting the intervention; the precautionary principle must clearly favor not adopting it. Choose one formulation of the principle, state it in a single sentence, and apply it consistently.

(d) Robustness, so my students can't deflate the case: the expected-utility verdict must survive every probability in your stated interval and at least a threefold change in how the harms and benefits are valued relative to one another. The precautionary verdict must not be reproducible by expected-utility reasoning with a different prior, a different utility for death, or a different discount rate. If it is reproducible, say so plainly — I would rather have an honest report of failure than a case that cheats by stipulating the verdict.

(e) The point of the exercise: I want to isolate whatever the principle tracks that expected-utility reasoning doesn't. My hypothesis is that it is something relational or deontic — who imposes a risk on whom, whether the risk-bearers consent, whether they can be compensated afterward, whether they also receive the benefit — rather than something about magnitude or probability. Build the case so that this feature, and only this feature, does the work. Avoid resting the case on irreversibility alone, since my students will price that as option value; if it is your best move anyway, explain why it can't be priced here.

Deliverables, in this order:

1. One sentence: does the case work? I'll read the rest in light of your answer.
2. The scenario: one paragraph, no more than 150 words. Name the decision, the decision-maker, the people who would bear the suspected harm, and the people who would get the benefit — concretely enough that the relational structure is visible.
3. Your one-sentence formulation of the precautionary principle, plus the expected-utility calculation with your numbers.
4. About 100 words on why each approach gives the verdict it gives.
5. About 80 words: which feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to both verdicts when you remove it — say, by giving the risk-bearers ex ante consent, or by making them the same people as the beneficiaries. If removing the feature doesn't collapse the disagreement, the feature wasn't doing the work and the case needs rebuilding.
6. About 80 words: the strongest objection a cost–benefit analyst would make against your case, and the best reply available to the precautionary side.

Keep the whole answer under 700 words, and don't pad the scenario with atmosphere — I need the structure visible.
