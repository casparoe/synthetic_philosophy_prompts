At a talk last week a visitor presented a model of a day-ahead electricity market that assumes every generator bids its true marginal cost and that demand is infinitely divisible and completely price-inelastic. Someone objected from the floor that real generators shade their bids and that the assumptions are just plain false, and the speaker killed the question with that Friedman line — "the more significant the theory, the more unrealistic the assumptions" — and went on to the next slide (apparently Samuelson nicknamed this move the F-twist). It felt like a brush-off, but I couldn't say why, and I don't want to just repeat "correlation is not causation"-style scepticism back at him.

So give me a menu, not a verdict: the distinct precise theses that sentence could be stating, from the trivial to the radical. I have my own rough taxonomy of the ways an assumption can fail to describe reality, and I want you to use it or fix it: (i) plain omission — it leaves things out, so it's "false" only in the sense of incomplete; (ii) Galilean-style idealization — false, but the model is meant to tell you what happens when you put the friction back in; (iii) as-if surrogate — it isn't describing any mechanism at all, it just attributes to the system the pattern maximization would produce; (iv) error compensation — the prediction comes out right because two falsehoods cancel; (v) soak-up — the falsity gets absorbed into a fitted parameter, so the model tracks one regime fine and tells you nothing once the regime shifts.

For each reading of the slogan say which kind of unrealism it's celebrating, whether it's trivial, substantive-but-defensible, or actually false, and wether it would support the speaker just moving on. My hunch is that he needed (iii) or (iv) and the slogan really only covers (i) and (ii), and that "significant" is doing sneaky work (wide predictive range? compression of information? causal depth?) — tell me where that hunch is most likely to break, and what would change your mind about which reading the line is best given. End with \answer{...} naming the reading or readings that would actually license his brush-off, or "none" if the slogan doesn't reach.
