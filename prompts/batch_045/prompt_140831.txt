The half-formed idea is about the free will debate. The standoff between compatibilists and incompatibilists is normally presented as a disagreement about whether the ability to do otherwise survives determinism, but I suspect a large part of why it never moves is that both sides assume 'could have done otherwise' has a single fixed truth condition that doesn't move with context. That looks false to me. 'Can' and 'could have' are modals, and on the standard semantics they quantify over a modal base and ordering source supplied by the context of utterance, and that context includes what is at stake for the person asking. We are lax about what someone could have done when nothing much hangs on it and we tighten up when everything does. If something like that is right, three things might follow, and I can't tell whether they are the same thing said three ways or three separate projects. First, an explanation of the persistence of the debate: the philosophers arguing about blame and resentment are using a high-stakes 'could', the ones arguing about deliberation and what it's like to face an open choice are using a low-stakes one, and each side's intuitions are correct about its own context. Second, the substantive question then becomes which stakes-profile is the right one for the practice we actually care about, and the answer may differ between the backward-looking practices (blame, resentment, praise, holding to promises) and the forward-looking ones (deliberating, forming intentions, trusting your own future execution) — so that compatibilism might come out true of one family and false of the other, which is not a conclusion I have seen defended. Third, the way it all collapses: if it is the moral stakes of blame that raise the bar, then the incompatibilist gets her conclusion by construction and the whole story is circular, or else it just relocates the original disagreement one level up, to a fight about which stakes are the privileged ones.
