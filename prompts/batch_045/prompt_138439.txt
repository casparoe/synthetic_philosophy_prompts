I teach a first-year seminar on the ethics and political theory of corruption, and we have spent most of the term on the question of whether the anti-corruption norms that get exported by Western states and development institutions are really universal or are a parochial preference for impersonal, rule-governed, bureaucratic order wearing a moral costume. I want to end the term with a film rather than another case study — Coppola's The Godfather (I and II, which almost all of them have seen anyway) — but I don't want to screen it as mere illustration, and I don't want a reading that tells them what they already think. The frame I have been using in the seminar is a three-way distinction, which I suspect does more work than the literature's own vocabulary: first, transactional corruption, a discrete and more or less priced exchange that leaves the underlying rule in place but gets it applied to the wrong person; second, clientelism, a durable and asymmetric personal bond in which protection, jobs, licences and favours are traded for loyalty and deference, where nothing is individually priced and where the currency is the relationship rather than the payment; third, something I have been calling gift-sovereignty, an economy of obligation in which what is transferred is not a benefit at a price but an unpayable debt: the return is deliberately left unspecified, the donor's standing is constituted by the debtor's continuing indebtedness, and either to refuse the gift or to offer to settle the account straight away counts as an insult or a declaration of hostility. My suspicion is that almost everything the film actually shows us belongs to the second and third categories rather than the first — the wedding-day petitions, where a favour is granted before any return is mentioned and the return is summoned only later and indefinitely; the business with the horse, and the accompanying line about an offer that cannot be refused; Michael's early disclaimer to Kay that the family is his family and not him, and the final shot in which she is shut out of the room; and, in the second film, the Nevada senator and the Senate committee hearing. A colleague thinks my third category is just clientelism with added romance, and that the film is straightforwardly a case study in the second; a student argued in the opposite direction, that the film's real claim is that the American side — the police captain, the senator, the other families' judges and union officials — is enmeshed in exactly the same logic, so that the contrast the film draws is not between corrupt particularism and clean universalism but between a version that admits what it is and a version that has to hide.

Here is what I want from you. First, take the distinction seriously enough to test it: does the Corleone economy really run on the second and third logics rather than the first, and does it matter? In particular, I want you to press on a puzzle I keep circling. The standard working definition of corruption — roughly, the abuse of a position of trust for private advantage — seems to presuppose a background of impersonal rules that are supposed to be applied, and a role whose holder is supposed to apply them; but the Corleones hold no public role at all, at least early on, and the world they operate in does not obviously contain the impartial rules that a bribe would distort. So either our confidence that they are corrupt shows the standard definition is too narrow and needs to be stated in terms of something like the illegitimate privatisation of a normative order, or it shows that our judgement was never really about corruption and was instead a verdict on the legitimacy of a rival jurisdiction — in which case the term is doing rhetorical work and the seminar's central question has quietly been begged. Second, give me three scenes you would actually put in front of undergraduates, described in your own words and without worrying about exact wording, and say what each one is supposed to make them see. Third, tell me where the film resists the frame: I would rather hand them a reading that admits the anti-corruption lens may be the parochial thing here, and that the film may be a tragedy about the costs of loyalty and about what a man becomes when he takes over his father's obligations, rather than a text about corruption at all. And end by saying what the film knows that the political-theory literature on clientelism and universal standards does not. I do not need reading lists or citations, and I would rather you did not try to reconstruct dialogue verbatim; I am after criticism, not documentation.
