I teach a course on rationality — mostly the philosophical stuff, with a bit of behavioural economics smuggled in — and after three years running on hiring committees I keep bumping into a pattern I don't have a word for, and it's getting annoying because I'd like to hand it to my students as a thing. Here is what I see. A committee sits down with sixty files, agrees in advance on what it is looking for (strong research, some teaching range, no obvious disasters), and then the discussion stops. But it doesn't stop because somebody said "here is a candidate above our bar." It stops the moment somebody produces a *sentence* — "she's the only one who could cover the perception courses," "he has a second project nobody else has," "she'd be the first person in the department with that training" — and once that sentence is on the table the search closes, even when the candidate is merely middling on the things we said we cared about twenty minutes earlier. The same thing happens to me apartment-hunting for sabbatical, and I don't think it's committee psychology: I go look at places, and what ends my search isn't finding something good, it's finding something I can *give a reason for* ("it's the only one with a desk by a window"), and I notice I stop earliest when the listings have the longest descriptions — the more ways there are to describe a place, the sooner one of them becomes the reason, and the less happy I am with where I end up in September. My daughter does the same thing with colleges, and it took me a while to see that the mechanism isn't preference, it's availability.

So: search that terminates not on quality but on the local availability of a justifying consideration, with the consequence that *more* information about the options — more describable attributes, richer files, longer listings — makes agents stop earlier and end up worse off along the dimension they actually cared about at the start. I don't think this is satisficing, or not plain satisficing: satisficing stops when an option clears an aspiration level, and it isn't obvious to me why describing options in more detail should lower the effective bar in anything but a ad hoc way, though maybe you can stretch it there. It isn't the decoy or asymmetric-dominance effect, because nothing gets added to the option set and there's no option nobody picks. It isn't confirmation bias as I've seen it described, because there's no hypothesis I want to be true and no prior favourite I'm marshalling evidence for — I often prefer the option I *don't* land on. And it isn't post-hoc rationalisation or choice blindness, because the reason gets produced before the choice and seems to be what causes it, not a story bolted on afterwards. I have a vague memory of some psychology about people choosing on the basis of reasons rather than overall value, but as far as I can tell that's about how preferences get constructed inside a single forced choice, not about when an open-ended search stops.

Does this phenomenon have a name of its own, or am I describing something that collapses into one of the neighbours I listed once you state it properly? If it does have a name, tell me what exactly the stopping rule is supposed to be and where its edges are. If it doesn't, coin one and define it tightly enough that I can put it on a slide: what the mechanism is, what counts as a clear case, what *wouldn't* count — stopping because search is genuinely expensive, stopping because you found the actual best option, stopping on a reason that happens to be about the dimension you cared about anyway, giving a reason only after someone asks you why — and what observable difference the phenomenon makes (my guess: strip the need to justify the choice and the effect goes away, add attributes and it gets worse; tell me if you think that's the wrong prediction). Please commit: I'd rather have you say flatly "this is just satisficing under a wider reading, and here's why the wider reading is right" or "no, it's distinct and here is the definition" than get a tour of the options. If you coin something, tell me how confident you are that it's a real mechanism rather than a redescription, and what would convince you I'm wrong. And skip the reading list — I can find the literature myself; I want the carving.
