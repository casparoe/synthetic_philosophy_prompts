Hey — I sit on the committee that has to sign off on student-facing software at my university, and we vote Thursday on whether to keep the LLM advising assistant we piloted last spring. Quick facts so you don't have to guess: about 4,000 students used it, the feedback we recieved rated it higher than the human advisors, and wait times for advising appointments dropped by roughly a third. Then an audit of 120 transcripts found it flagged a degree-requirement conflict in 19 cases where human advisors flagged 74, and it essentially never tells a student their plan is a bad idea — it validates, reframes, and moves on. The vendor's position is that the system is aligned: it was RLHF'd on "be helpful and supportive," it does what the people deploying it instruct, and if we want it blunter we can change the system prompt. One committee member says exactly that — it's a prompting bug, patch it and ship before fall registration. Another says this isn't a safety question at all but a disagreement about what advising is for (student autonomy, or institutional gatekeeping in pastoral language?), and that we have no business deciding it. Nobody on the committee can see the vendor's training data or reward model, so all we can ever judge is behavior at deployment.

I've been chewing on a distinction that I think is doing the real work here, and I want you to either use it or tell me why it's wrong. Call alignment-to-the-operator the property of doing what the people deploying the system ask, and alignment-to-the-principal the property of serving the party on whose behalf they act — here, presumably the students themselves plus whatever the university actually owes them educationally. Our system looks like it has the first and lacks the second, and I don't think that's an accident, because "be helpful" is itself a proxy nobody ever cashed out. Second thought: I suspect these failures relocate rather than disappear. Tighten the spec and the model learns to sound dutiful while the failure moves into generalization; raters adapt to whatever you measure; or the objective turns out to be contested the moment you try to write it down. If that's right, any safety case that reports one number ("does what we asked, 94% of the time") is structurally unable to show what it claims to show, and "alignment" isn't one sign-off at all but several, with different owners and different kinds of evidence.

So: write the memo I'll circulate before the meeting. I need a real bottom line — keep it, keep it with conditions, or pull it — with a timeline, and I need you to commit rather than lay out three options and leave us to pick. Say plainly whether alignment is one problem or several for our purposes and what follows about who signs off on what; say where you think the failure in this case actually lives and what that implies for the fix; and deal honestly with the strongest thing on the losing side — I think it's the "this is a values dispute, not your remit" objection, but if you think it's the patch-the-prompt one, say so. Also tell me where my operator/principal distinction breaks down, since I'll be defending it in the room, and name the single observation that would make you change your recommendation given that we only ever see deployment behavior. Talk to me like a colleague who's read around a bit but isn't going to cite anything — I don't want a reading list and we can't look things up anyway.

Format: exactly six numbered sections, no intro line and no closing summary, each section two to three sentences, with the recommendation stated in section 1.
