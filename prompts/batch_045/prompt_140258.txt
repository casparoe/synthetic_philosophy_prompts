I run a hardware store and I sit, as a volunteer, on the seven-person board of Cascade Ridge Search and Rescue. I have no background in philosophy, but I've been arguing about this with two friends for weeks — one says we are about to punish a man for bad luck, the other says a good process is a good process and the family is owed more than a shrug — and I'm the one who has to draft the memo before our meeting in twelve days, so I'd like it written properly.

Here is the situation. Our field manual tells a leader to adopt the first search plan that reaches a seventy-percent probability of detection within the first hour, and then to stop planning: it says explicitly that we should not keep looking for a better plan, because every minute spent planning is a minute the missing person is getting colder. It also sets a ninety-minute "hold" window before a segment is abandoned and the search is widened. In January, a leader named Maya followed that rule exactly, found the missing hiker alive at fifty-two minutes, and in March the county commissioners gave her their Lifesaver of the Year award; there is a plaque on the wall at the station. In February, a leader named Dan — a twelve-year volunteer with no prior incidents — followed the same rule on a very similar call, held his segment for the full ninety minutes, and the hiker was found dead three hundred metres outside it, dead of exposure, with the medical examiner's estimate of time of death falling inside the hold window. At minute sixty-two Dan radioed dispatch to ask whether he should widen early, and the dispatcher — a volunteer who was simultaneously coordinating a second, unrelated call — told him to hold the segment; he held. The manual also says the field leader has final authority on scene, which is the sentence the family quotes in its letter to us, and which the local paper quoted under the headline asking why we stopped looking. Since then a donor has offered the organization a quarter of a million dollars for thermal drones, on the condition that the board state in writing that the manual's threshold rule contributed to the death; the donor turns out to be the dead man's uncle, which we only learned after the offer arrived.

One board member is circulating a motion to suspend Dan pending a full review. A second says we cannot put a plaque on one wall and a suspension in the other for the same conduct. A third, who works in data analysis, has proposed a new standing procedure instead: re-run every serious incident in our incident simulator, feeding it only what the leader knew at the moment the plan was fixed, run that plan against a large set of simulated hiker locations drawn from our historical distribution of where people actually end up, and grade the plan on its average detection time — then train on, or if necessary discipline on, the grade rather than the outcome. I like the sound of this, but it worries me too, since our historical distribution was itself produced by searches that ran under the same rule, and since Dan's grade would depend on where in that distribution the real hiker happened to be. To complicate matters, two of our seven members are active field leaders who could find themselves in Dan's position, and one is the county's appointee who is mainly worried about the insurance pool and the newspaper.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate in advance of the vote. It should take a clear position on four things: what to do about Dan; what to do, if anything, about Maya's award and about the manual's rule; whether we can accept the donation on the terms offered; and whether to adopt the simulation-based grading procedure, and if adopted, how much weight it should carry. It needs a concrete recommendation with a timeline rather than a menu of options, because four of seven votes are needed and I have to whip them. And it has to treat the strongest case for sanctioning Dan honestly — the family's letter is genuinely good, and the argument that a rule nobody is ever accountable under is not really a rule is the one I can't get out of my head — while still telling me what you think we should actually do and why.
