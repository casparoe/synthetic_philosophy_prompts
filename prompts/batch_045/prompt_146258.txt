I work on the negotiation layer of a system in which autonomous agents — often two instances of the same container image, with identical code, weights and inputs — bargain with one another over a fixed, finite number of rounds for a surplus that grows the longer neither of them grabs it. This is not academic for me: we are deciding whether the protocol needs a built-in commitment device (escrowed deposits, an irrevocable lock action) or whether two agents with identical decision procedures can be relied on to hold out against each other. So I built the following argument with a friend who works on decision theory, and I now cannot accept its conclusion but cannot locate the mistake.

The game I will use is a simultaneous-move cousin of the usual centipede. Two agents face 100 rounds. The pot starts at 10 and grows by 1 after every round in which both choose PASS. In any round: if both choose PASS, the pot grows and play continues (or, if this was round 100, the pot grows once more and is split evenly); if both choose TAKE, play ends and the pot is split evenly; if exactly one chooses TAKE, play ends with 60 percent to the taker and 40 percent to the other. Call this the Simultaneous Centipede. Call the Duplicate variant exactly the same game with exactly one difference: the two agents are exact duplicates — two instantiations of one decision procedure — so that whatever one outputs in a round the other outputs too, and both know this and know that the other knows it. Now the argument.

1. In the Simultaneous Centipede, reasoning from the last round backwards: at round 100 TAKE weakly dominates (54.50 against 43.60 if the other takes, 65.40 against 55 if the other passes), and if the other takes at round k+1 then TAKE at round k is better whether she takes (half of the pot beats 40 percent of it) or passes (60 percent now beats 40 percent of the grown pot, since the pot is always at least 10). So TAKE in every round, and the outcome is 5 each.
2. The Duplicate variant differs from the Simultaneous Centipede in exactly one respect: each agent's move is perfectly correlated with the other's. The rules, the pot, the payoffs and the information are the same.
3. What it is rational for me to do depends on my payoffs and on what my move brings about or makes more likely by causal routes. The fact that my move is correlated with another agent's move — that it is evidence of it — is not itself a reason to do or avoid anything.
4. In the Duplicate variant, my passing does not cause her to pass and does not make her passing more likely via any causal route; the correlation is a consequence of our shared constitution, which my choice does not affect. So by premise 3 the correlation gives me no reason I did not already have in the Simultaneous Centipede.
5. So rationality requires TAKE at round 1 in the Duplicate variant as well: 5 each.
6. But in the Duplicate variant both agents know that the plan PASS in all 100 rounds is available to each of them individually, that it yields 55 each, and that if either executes it the other does too — the very correlation that premise 4 declares irrelevant is what guarantees the plan works. The argument therefore concludes that rationality requires each of two identical agents to take 5 in a case where 55 apiece is available to each by a plan each can execute alone and each knows the other will execute. That I cannot accept.
7. And it seems to prove too much in both directions. If premise 3 is right, then the intuitive story about the centipede — grab now because otherwise she grabs first — is doing no work at all, since it is absent in the Duplicate variant and the conclusion survives; the backward induction paradox would then not be about pre-emption or the self-undermining character of backward induction at all, and every diagnosis framed in terms of common knowledge of rationality, off-path beliefs or zero-probability nodes would be treating a symptom. But if premise 3 is wrong and correlation can be a reason, then the verdict in the ordinary Simultaneous Centipede is not robust: by my arithmetic, a prior of only about 2 percent that the other agent is a mirror-type already makes PASS at round 1 the better bet, which would make the standard result a knife-edge resting on exact independence rather than a result about rationality.

I have three candidate escapes and trust none of them: reject premise 3 and accept that evidence can be a reason, which looks like it commits me to one-boxing and to some ugly conclusions where the correlation comes from a common cause I cannot influence; reject the Duplicate variant as described, on the grounds that PASS throughout is not even a Nash equilibrium there (a unilateral TAKE at round 100 is worth 65.40 if the other keeps passing), so the case only looks different because I have let the deviation propagate along the correlation, which is the very thing in dispute; or reject something about the move from last-round-first reasoning to a requirement at round 1, which a friend describes as confusing a claim about the path with a claim about the strategy profile, and which I do not fully understand.

Please answer as a decision theorist or philosopher of rational choice who has genuinely worked on this material. Before committing to a diagnosis, reconstruct both variants in your own notation and run the backward induction yourself rather than trusting my numbers — tell me if my specification or my arithmetic is wrong, including the 2 percent figure. Then name the step you reject, explain why it looked innocent, and price the alternatives: what exactly does each rejection commit me to elsewhere in decision theory, and which of my three escapes is closest to right? I am most attached to the claim that TAKE in round 1 is correct in the ordinary Simultaneous Centipede and to the claim that TAKE weakly dominates at round 100; I am least attached to premise 3. Finally, tell me how confident you are in the diagnosis overall, single out the claim in your answer you are least sure of, and say separately where competent people would still disagree even after the diagnosis is accepted.
