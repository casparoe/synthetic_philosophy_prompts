Animal-welfare law and ordinary moral bookkeeping both run on a bright line: everything on the vertebrate side — with cephalopods pulled in later, by amendments to research rules that otherwise protect only "all living vertebrates" — counts as a protected animal, to be anesthetized, housed, justified before an ethics committee and counted in the statistics, while everything on the insect side matters not at all. The crickets raised for feed, the caterpillars killed in harvesting, the colonies destroyed with pesticide are a resource, and someone who objects is treated as having made a joke. What interests me is that every candidate ground for the line looks continuous rather than discrete: nociception, integration of information across sensory modalities, flexible learning including reversal learning, motivational states that can be frustrated, trade-offs between competing aversions, a model of the body, something functionally like a global workspace. Creatures have these to different degrees and not in any fixed order, and at the bottom of the scale a thermostat has one or two of them in degenerate form — it registers a state of the world and corrects for error — with nothing anyone would call experience. So: what does the bright-line practice actually presuppose about moral status, and what does it merely fit well with? Does it strictly require a threshold in the underlying property, or would a graded view on which status saturates very fast, or on which very small degrees of status are simply negligible in any cost-benefit calculation, license exactly the same line? Does it require status to be a single scalar at all, given that the underlying capacities don't move together, so that "more sentient than" may not even be a well-defined ordering across cases? And is the line doing moral work at all, or only administrative work — a speed limit adopted for legibility and enforceability, with the moral reality underneath still gradual? The part I genuinely cannot settle: on the numbers I half-remember, insects killed in farming and pest control run to the trillions per year, and if each has some small chance of a small nonzero degree of sentience, then expected-value reasoning of the kind I was raised on seems to push insect suffering to the top of every cause-priority list; but I also suspect that summing speculative probabilities over astronomical headcounts is precisely where that style of reasoning eats itself. Say whether anything principled blocks the aggregation or whether the conclusion is merely costly, and be clear throughout about how confident you are in each claim, singling out the point you are least sure of.
