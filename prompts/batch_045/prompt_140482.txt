I'm writing a novel set in an isolated valley, and I've painted myself into a corner. Two villages on opposite rims of a gorge; a fever a generation before the book opens left most of the lower village deaf, so the two sides run everything through a code of mirrored shutters that everyone on both sides was born into. Nobody agreed to it, nobody remembers it starting, and a big part of the book is about which of their shared practices have any grip on them. I keep wanting to narrate that some practice is a convention and others aren't — a habit, a rule somebody enforces, a thing they do because they think it's right — and I realized I don't have a spec for what that claim is supposed to buy. My writing group says just use the Lewis-style thing, a regularity everyone conforms to, expects the others to conform to, and prefers to conform to given that the others do, but that feels like it decides my cases by fiat before I know which cases matter.

So: write me the requirements list. What does an account of convention have to deliver for me to call the practices in my valley by that name — which cases it has to sort correctly, which distinctions it can't collapse (convention vs mere regularity, vs a command backed by force, vs a norm people enforce, vs something they'd do anyway for independent reasons), which bits of the story it has to be able to accommodate, with the case behind each requirement. The cases I actually need handled: the mirror code itself, where a second, equally workable code existed and this one just won; the ledge lamps, where a stubborn tenth never light them, everybody knows it, and the system runs fine anyway; the water-turn rule, where the two villages' interests genuinely conflict and one of them is getting the worse deal; the black doors, where every household keeps its door painted black because each believes the others would sanction any other color, though in fact nobody cares and nobody has ever enforced it; the left-hand ledge, the only passable one, which everyone walks on and where there was never any alternative worth coordinating around; the dawn bell, which everybody hates and everybody still keeps; and the herders and their dogs, who time their movements around each other down to the minute, with only one side having anything like expectations about the other's expectations. Tell me too whether how a practice got started belongs in the test or only how it's kept going, since I have a chapter where the code shifts over one winter without anyone deciding anything.

Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just the taste of one camp, and flag any pair you think can't be jointly satisfied — that's the part I most want, because if the list is impossible I'd rather know now. One purpose-of-the-theory thing specific to me: I want a test a reader could in principle apply from what's on the page, from behavior and what people say out loud, without access to anyone's real preferences or to whether they all truly know what the others know. Say whether that observability condition is a genuine desideratum or my own novelist's prejudice, and if it's genuine, what it costs — I suspect it collides head-on with the bit about conditional preferences. Then tell me which single requirement knocks out the most candidates. A short scorecard at the end: how Lewis's own regularity-and-expectations account does, how the accounts that build sanctioning into the definition do, and how the evolutionary or reinforcement-style pictures that don't ask for beliefs at all do. Keep the weight on the criteria, the scorecard can be a few lines.
