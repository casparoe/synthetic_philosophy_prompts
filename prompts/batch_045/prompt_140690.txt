Putting together the reading list for my grad seminar on comparative development — the why-are-some-countries-rich one, you know the genre — and before I assign another three hundred pages of it I want the spec rather than the theories. What would an explanation of cross-national income differences actually have to get right to be worth anything? Write me the desiderata list.

Purpose, so you can argue about which demands are real: I want a theory good enough that (a) I'd let a student spend five years testing it and (b) I'd be willing to tell someone allocating aid money 'this is our best guess, and here's how much to trust it.' If some requirement comes out of one of those purposes and not the other, say so.

Cases I want attached to each requirement, and I'd rather have argument than inventory:

- The separation cases. Most of what we correlate with prosperity travels in a bundle, so I want a requirement that a theory say what it predicts where the bundle comes apart: a place with extractive-looking politics and a resource windfall, a place with decent formal institutions and terrible geography, a place that got rich fast while keeping institutions the institutions-camp would call bad. Give the case behind the requirement, not a list of countries.
- Onset, persistence, and catch-up. Sustained growth starting somewhere, gaps that just sit there for a century, and then episodes of rapid convergence and of growth that collapses. I think these are three different explananda and that a lot of the literature quietly swaps between them; write that in as a requirement to declare which one is being explained, and tell me if you think it's a fake distinction.
- Level versus growth rate. Something can explain why a place is rich without explaining why it keeps getting richer. Is respecting that a genuine desideratum or pedantry?
- Within-border variation. Same formal institutions, same national culture, big and stable income gaps between regions. Whatever the theory is, does it have the resources to handle that, or does it just get to change the unit of analysis? I want your view on whether this is a requirement or a cheap shot.
- Exogeneity. The cause can't just be income wearing a hat. But I suspect the truly exogenous candidates are the ones that can't explain much, and the ones that explain a lot are partly effects. If that's a real trade-off, make it explicit as a pair of desiderata that pull against each other and say which one you'd drop.
- The 'five-year minister' test: its favoured causes mostly move over generations, while the people who'd act on it have an electoral term. Is being usable at the timescale policy actually runs on a requirement, or a category error imported from the policy world? This is the one I'm least sure about.
- Risky prediction. It should be able to state something it would get wrong. Half the literature explains every outcome after the fact; say how you'd write that into the spec without it becoming a demand for a randomised experiment.

Then flag any pair you think can't be jointly satisfied — that part is the real result, not the checklist. No citations and no reading list, I know the literature; I want the criteria and the reasoning behind them. Finish in three or four lines on which single requirement does the most damage to the institutions / geography / culture families respectively. Don't be polite about it.
