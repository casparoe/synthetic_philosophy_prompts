I spent thirty-four years as a spectroscopist in a government materials laboratory, which is to say that I spent them reading pictures: a spectrum is a picture that behaves rather better than a sentence, and I have never quite lost the habit of trusting a trace on a chart more than a paragraph of prose describing it. Since retiring I have been working through the philosophy of science in a fairly unsystematic way, and I have lately become interested in Otto Neurath and his campaign for a picture language. As I understand him, the claims are roughly these: that a great deal of what science needs to convey to non-specialists can be conveyed with a small stock of standardised pictograms rather than with sentences; that amount is to be shown by repeating an icon at fixed size rather than by enlarging it, so that quantities can literally be counted off; that pictures stand a better chance of being understood across a language barrier than words, and that he had a slogan on his office wall to the effect that words divide while pictures unite; and yet that he was no foundationalist, and did not think the picture language was, or could become, a self-standing notation into which everything else might be translated once and for all. He thought of it, I believe, as a helping language, and he is known for the image of a ship that must be repaired plank by plank while it is at sea. Please take the summary above as authoritative for the purposes of this exercise, and tell me in the note if you think I have got him wrong.

What I would like is a short story, 1,200 to 1,600 words, in British English, that puts one specific structural difficulty in such a system on display. My own suggestion, which you should feel free to sharpen rather than merely illustrate, is this: in a notation where amount is shown by how many times a mark is repeated, the case of nothing at all is shown by there being no mark, and no mark is exactly what the chart also shows when nobody looked, or when the record has gone missing, or — the case I care about most, and the one a laboratory person would insist on — when the instrument could not have seen anything anyway because the result sits below the method's detection limit. Zero, unsampled, and not detectable collapse into the same blank square. A picture can show a thing, or several things; denying that the thing is there seems to require a further convention, and every such convention is a new sign that someone has to be taught, and that can be confused with the marks it resembles.

The constraints I would ask you to respect:

1. Set the story in the present day, in and around a working environmental monitoring programme — a catchment, a water-treatment works, a shift rota, a wall chart — with nothing speculative or futuristic in it, and with no equipment failure, villain, or accident of misreading doing the work. The trouble must come out of the notation itself.
2. The system in the story uses a small fixed set of pictograms, quantity by repetition, and no words, numerals, or sentences. It may not rescue itself with arrows, crosses, strikethroughs, or colour coding unless the story introduces one of these explicitly as a new sign, with the cost of teaching it made plain.
3. The message that has to get through is a negative one that matters: a set of upstream sampling points came back clean after something that might have contaminated them, and someone downstream has to decide what to do on the strength of that. The three-way collapse described above should be felt by the reader before any character names it.
4. Nobody may solve the difficulty by inventing a clever pictogram that dissolves it. If the characters adopt a new sign, the story should make clear that what they have adopted functions as a word — a convention agreed between people, not a picture of the thing it means — and this is where I would like the story to meet Neurath's own view that the picture language was a helping language rather than a replacement, and his anti-foundationalism: a tool you repair in the course of using it, not a foundation you can get underneath.
5. No character is a philosopher and no character lectures; the reader should be shown the difficulty rather than told it. One dry professional sentence, spoken in the course of the job, may carry the weight if it earns it.
6. Do not put any sentence into anyone's mouth as a quotation from Neurath's writings or from anyone else's; paraphrase rather than invent. And please append no bibliography or suggestions for further reading, since I have no way of checking them.

After the story, add a note of no more than 300 words covering three things: first, which of the claims about Neurath the story leans on, and how confident you are that you have characterised his position correctly; second, where the story takes liberties with what he actually proposed, or rests on a contested reading of him, including whether the collapse of blankness I have described is a real limitation of such systems or one that practitioners already had a convention for, and whether you are guessing there; and third, the single point in the whole response about which you are least confident, and why.
