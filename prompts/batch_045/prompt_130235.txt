I do robust and model predictive control for industrial processes — LQR, receding-horizon stuff, reference governors, disturbance rejection, the usual — and I know roughly zero philosophy and only whatever ML I've picked up from colleagues. Those colleagues keep talking about "approval-directed" agents as an alternative to "goal-directed" ones and I want to know if that distinction is real in my vocabulary or if it evaporates on contact with it.

My own attempt, tell me where I'm cheating: a goal-directed agent is MPC with a terminal cost on state — needs a dynamics model, plans, and will do strange things to intermediate states to hit that terminal cost. An approval-directed agent is a reactive controller with a stage cost on u given the current x, no model, no terminal cost, and a cost function I can only sample along the trajectory I actually executed. So the safety case for approval-directedness is just: short effective horizon, no planning, hence no instrumental convergence.

What I want is the residue report, and I have three candidates:

1. The thing that gets approved isn't an actuator command at the plant's timescale, it's often something temporally extended — write the report, run the experiment. So myopia is relative to the granularity of the decision variable, and that granularity gets coarser as systems get more capable. Is approval-directedness then just goal-directedness with a short horizon — a dial, not an architecture?

2. Power-seeking: in my world controllability is handed to you by whoever bolted on the actuators. You don't acquire it and you certainly don't want to. There's no vocabulary for an agent increasing its own control authority. Real gap in the framing, or confused talk?

3. The approver is a sensor whose reading depends on the controller's output, whose transfer function is unknown and drifts, and which can't be queried off-policy — asking "would you have approved the other action" is a different question. Cost oracle, sampleable only on-path, with a bias term that's a function of u. Does control have anything for a reference signal generated inside the loop by something the controller can influence, or is the failure mode simply unrepresentable there?

And: is what doesn't translate a defect in my vocabulary, or a sign the approval/goal line is blurrier than it's made out to be? Where exactly does it stop being a distinction?
