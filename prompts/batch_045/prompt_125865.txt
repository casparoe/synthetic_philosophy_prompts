hey — I'm teaching an undergrad seminar, cross-listed cogsci and philosophy, 18 students, one 80-minute slot, and I want the session on Dennett's four kinds of minds (Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian) to land as something they feel rather than as four definitions on a slide. So: design me a game where the four kinds show up as four different restrictions on how a team is allowed to learn, and where students run into the differences before anybody names them. Nobody should hear the word "Popperian" until after they've felt what it's like to be one.

Constraints: no real money, nothing beyond normal classroom stuff (index cards, envelopes, a deck of cards, a timer on the projector). Four or five teams of 4ish, all doing the same underlying task, so the only variable is the learning rule they're stuck with. I'd like the environment to have a hidden payoff structure that shifts once, partway through — I think that's where the interesting differences bite: who can re-learn at all, who pays for it in actual trials, who is wrecked because the rule they inherited was built for the old regime. But if you've got a better structure for exposing the distinctions, take it.

One bit of extra machinery I want built in: I've been playing with what I'm calling a Baldwinian creature — it inherits neither the answer nor a procedure for finding the answer, only a heritable bias about where in the space of options to start looking. That nudge changes which trials it happens to run first, and so which reinforcements it happens to collect. Give one team that and only that, then tell me honestly whether the game can actually distinguish a Baldwinian creature from a plain Darwinian one inside a single session; if it can't, say so and say what you'd change. Related: does the taxonomy have a real slot for a creature that learns mainly by being taught — another agent choosing which hypotheses you even get to test, and in what order — or is that just a Gregorian creature with a particular tool? That's the bit I'm genuinely unsure about, so don't flatten it.

What I want them walking out seeing: that these aren't ranks on a smartness scale but differences in where the selection happens and who pays for a bad guess — in the genes, in overt behaviour across a lifetime, inside the head before acting, or in a toolkit borrowed from a culture that was under no obligation to understand it. And that a Gregorian creature can be right for reasons it can't state, which is also how it ends up wrong at scale.

Sections, please, in this order:
