I teach a six-week module on evidence and uncertainty for the master's students in science writing — I do it most years between deadlines, and this time I have about fourty exam scripts to get through by Friday. The last question on the paper was on the Clifford / James fight over the ethics of belief, and I deliberately built it around a case I made up rather than around God or the shipowner, because if I used the usual examples everyone just rehashes the seminar back at me. Here is the question, followed by one student's answer, typed out more or less as written with the name removed.

QUESTION (short answer, 25 minutes): We read Clifford's The Ethics of Belief and James's The Will to Believe. Consider the following case in two variants. Dr. Nwafor runs a small disease-surveillance project that depends on a loose network of clinics in another country sending her their weekly case counts for a full year. The dataset is worthless unless most of them report honestly and on time, and whether they will is not something she can find out in advance.
Variant A (trust up front): her decision whether to send the clinics a letter this week expressing full confidence in them — and whether inwardly to believe now that they will come through — is causally loaded. Clinics that sense they are being audited quietly stop reporting; clinics treated as partners tend, disproportionately, to become reliable. The letter is due this week.
Variant B (the sequencing lab): same project, same stakes, but now the question she has to settle this week is whether the country's only sequencing lab will still be funded and running in six months — a fact entirely unaffected by what she believes or writes.
For each variant, say whether James's machinery licenses the belief: the genuine option (forced, living, momentous), the claim that our passional nature not only lawfully may but must decide when the question cannot by its nature be settled on intellectual grounds, and the cases where faith in a fact helps create the fact. Then say what Clifford would say about each. Where the two variants come apart, say exactly what does the work.

STUDENT ANSWER: Clifford's rule is the strict one: it is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone to believe anything upon insufficient evidence. His shipowner is the test case — the man talks himself out of his doubts about his ship rather than following them up, and for Clifford that is the crime, not the sinking itself: the belief was never earned, and anyway no one'sbelief is a private matter, since our credulity leaks into what we do and into what everyone around us will believe later. James's reply is that this is too simple. His thesis is that when an option is genuine, meaning forced, living and momentous, and cannot be settled on intellectual grounds, our passional nature not only lawfully may but must decide it, because leaving the question open is itself a decision and risks losing the truth just as much. He is not saying anything goes: he rejects the Pascal's wager style argument, and he insists both hypotheses have to be live for the person choosing. He also has the cases where faith in a fact helps create the fact — the climber who has to believe he can make the jump, the social cases where trust has to come first — and he thinks inquiry itself runs on that kind of precursive faith.

In variant A I think James clearly licenses the belief. The option is forced, since the letter either goes out this week or it doesn't and not sending one is the same as sending a different one; it is momentous, since the whole dataset depends on it; and it is living, because Nwafor can genuinely see the clinics as partners. Clifford would object that she is manufacturing a belief for the project's sake, but Clifford's real target is acting on a belief you haven't checked, and since we can't believe at will anyway, what he can reasonably demand is that she keep checking: if she believes and audits quietly at the same time, Clifford has nothing left to object to.

In variant B I think James licenses it too, and for much the same reason. Whether the lab survives is not something Nwafor can settle from her desk this week, so the question is intellectually undecidable for her, and her passional nature must decide. Since the project goes better if she proceeds as though the lab will be running, and belief is what lets her act at all, she ought to believe it. James's point is that in these situations the evidence is never going to be in on time, so what matters is which belief lets you act; a belief that keeps the project moving beats paralysis, and Clifford's principle applied strictly would just paralyse anyone who ever has to decide anything.
