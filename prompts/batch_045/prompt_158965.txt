hey — I need help designing one seminar session and I've got a fairly specfic ask.

Setting: final-year undergrads, PPE/politics mix, about 16 in the room (4 groups of 4), one 90-minute slot. Politically mixed, and not just left/right: several of them are from countries where they think the current government is wrecking democracy, and several think that claim is hysterical. That disagreement is the raw material — I want them to run into it, not hear me describe it.

Topic: democratic backsliding. What counts as it, who gets to decide, and whether the concept is anything more than a partisan label wearing a social-science badge.

The core activity I have in mind: anonymised composite country vignettes — a list of institutional changes over roughly a decade, no country names, no party names, obviously invented — and students score each one for how much backsliding it involves. The twist I want built in: at minute zero each student privately ticks which of two policy bundles they'd rather see enacted. They then get a packet where two vignettes are introduced with one line saying the changes were made by a government elected on the bundle they picked, and two saying the other one. Identical institutional changes, different label. I want each student to compute their own within-subject gap and discover it, rather than me announcing it from the front.

What I need from you:

1. The mechanics. What's on the minute-zero slip; how I get the right packet to the right student without anyone clocking what's going on; the four vignettes (6–8 institutional changes each — realistic but deliberately unidentifiable composites, and please don't build them so they depend on getting the details of real cases right, I'll swap in actual cases later if I want); and the scoring scheme. Include a short banned-words card for the definition-writing phase (no country or leader names, no "populist"/"authoritarian", no unanalysed "rule of law"), and tell me how to keep the word "backsliding" itself out of the room until the debrief — call it "Type-Q" or whatever you like. Pre-register one scoring scheme, not a menu of three.

2. A round that does the "who decides" part. My idea: each group takes the role of a different umpire — the domestic opposition, a regional monitoring body issuing an annual score, a comparativist writing ten years later — and re-scores one vignette from inside that role using a deliberately restricted evidence packet (opposition press clippings / formal legal texts only / election outcome data only). Design those packets and say what each role structurally cannot see, so the divergence comes from epistemic position and not just from opinion.

3. Something properly in the weeds, so it isn't just "everyone is biased, discuss." Two gadgets of mine I want built into the materials: (a) the ratchet condition — roughly, a reform is backsliding-relevant only if it both raises the vote share the opposition needs to take power and is insulated against reversal by a future majority of different composition, so the test isn't "did the rules change" but "could the losers undo it after winning"; (b) what I call plebiscitary laundering — an incumbent puts an institutional power-grab to a referendum and wins it, so now every external critic is objecting to a vote rather than to a government. I want a vignette or trap case that forces each group's own rule to return an answer on the referendum case. Tell me honestly if either gadget is doing less work than I think it is.

4. A payoff structure that needs no money. I was thinking a trap-case tournament: each group writes one case designed to break another group's rule, groups earn chips for surviving attacks, the surviving group gets to name the concept at the end. Fix the actual rules of that — order of attacks, time limits, what counts as a successful break — and tell me how it will degenerate if I let it run.

5. A debrief that maps specific moments onto specific parts of the problem, what will predictably go wrong (I'm most worried about the student who has read the literature and says the magic word in minute three, and about the whole thing landing as lazy both-sidesism), and a frank note on what the activity cannot reproduce. My own suspicion is that the big one is sequentiality: real erosion is a process where each step is locally defensible and nobody ever sees the completed list of changes, and the activity hands them exactly the information a real actor doesn't have. Say what I have to supply in words because the game can't get it across.

6. Two commitments I actually want rather than options. First: what result you'd expect at the reveal, and what I should do instead if the framing gap comes out flat. Second: your own verdict — one verdict, not "it depends" — on whether "democratic backsliding" is more than a partisan label, together with the strongest thing you can say against your own verdict.

No bibliography or reading list, and don't build anything that leans on my getting attributions or dates right; I'll add the readings myself. Don't put anything in quotation marks from a real text — paraphrase if you need to. And flag the places where you're just guessing about how 20-year-olds will actually behave in a room.
