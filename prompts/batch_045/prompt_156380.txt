Dear colleague — I need your help with a novel, and I'd like you to work here as a moral philosopher who is also at home in social choice theory and the history of health economics. Please think about this carefully before you write: begin by sketching privately, for your own use, the structure of the decision — who the decision-makers were, what the feasible set really was, what each available procedure presupposed about the information it had, and which comparisons each procedure performs silently, whether or not anyone admits it. Then discard the scaffolding and send me one finished piece of continuous prose, somewhere between twelve hundred and fifteen hundred words, with no headings, no bullet points, no boldface, no tables, and no citations or suggestions for further reading: the way you would write to a colleague who had asked you a genuinely hard question.

The book is set in Seattle in 1962 and centers on the committee that decided which patients would get the first chronic dialysis slots. The facts, which I have established in my own research and which I would ask you to take as given rather than to verify or dispute, are these: chronic hemodialysis had just become technically possible; a Seattle center had a handful of machines and far more candidates than slots; selection proceeded in two stages, a medical screen that eliminated most applicants and then a social-worth stage in which an anonymous lay committee ranked the survivors by their contribution — dependents, employment and taxpaying, emotional stability and cooperativeness, prospects for rehabilitation, participation in community and church life; the arrangement was described in a widely read magazine article and provoked public revulsion, and the committee acquired a theological nickname in the press; it is said to have fallen back, after the outcry, on random selection among the medically eligible; and about a decade later Congress extended Medicare to anyone with end-stage renal disease, which dissolved the scarcity that had made the ranking necessary in the first place. My narrator is a nurse who sits in the room as a nonvoting observer and keeps a private ledger, and I want the arguments in that room to be ones a real philosopher would recognize, not stage villainy.

What I want from you is an analysis of that episode through the lens of interpersonal utility comparison, and I want it to run in both directions. First, in the theory's own terms, what was the committee actually doing: were they comparing utility levels, or gains, or units; what numeraire did they adopt without saying so; and what informational commitment does each of the procedures available to them — ranking by social worth, ranking by expected medical benefit, a lottery — actually require? I have been using a distinction in the manuscript between denominated comparison, where you fix a unit in advance, such as life-years or quality-adjusted life-years or dollars or chances, and compare magnitudes in it, and rank-only comparison, where you order candidates without attributing magnitudes to anyone. Tell me what each of these buys and what it costs, what the social choice literature implies about how little can be done with purely ordinal and noncomparable information, and whether rank-only is even genuinely available to a committee facing this choice or whether it collapses into a denominated comparison the moment anyone asks how much better one candidate is than another.

Then three problems I keep running into in my drafts. The first is whose utility is being compared: the patient's own, including what the experience of dialysis would actually be like for her; the family's; or the committee's, on the reconstruction where each member imaginatively occupies each candidate's position, the way an impartial observer is supposed to. Would that reconstruction have produced a different ordering than the social-worth ranking did, and is it psychologically available to actual people in an actual room, or only to a theorem? The second is my nurse's ledger, which has two columns: what the patient stands to lose in her own terms, and what others stand to lose if she dies. Is that a coherent distinction, or does it collapse? I can see a double-counting worry, since the losses to others should already appear in those others' own utility functions once the utilities are fully comparable, and an opposite worry that dropping the second column simply ignores harms everyone in the room can feel; I would like you to say which of these is the real problem and where the baseline should be set, since gains measured from each patient's own status quo seem to give a different ordering from gains measured against a common reference point such as a fair innings. The third is whether the lottery escapes comparison at all: it presupposes equal claims within the pool, it still requires someone to decide who is in the pool, and it converts a comparison of magnitudes into a comparison of chances, which changes what is said to the loser even if it does not change who lives.

Finally, what the episode does to the theory. Does the recoil tell us that these comparisons were epistemically unavailable, that we cannot make them, or morally unavailable, that we may not, and can those two claims be told apart in a case like this? Does the congressional solution of 1972 show that the problem of interpersonal comparison is usually a scarcity problem in disguise, and if so, what is lost or merely relocated when the scarcity is dissolved? And be plain about where the lens is silent, especially about the expressive dimension: what it does to a person to be told, more or less in so many words, that she is less worth saving than someone else. I am not asking you to decide who was right. I am asking you to tell me what my characters are entitled to believe, which intuition belongs to which kind of person in that room, what a real committee in 1962 would and would not have said aloud, and above all which words I must not put in anyone's mouth because they would resolve something that has not been resolved. An honest account of where this lens distorts the case is worth more to me than a tidy verdict.
