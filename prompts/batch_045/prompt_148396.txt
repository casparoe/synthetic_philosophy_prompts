I'm an undergrad in a history / public humanities program and I'm taking this seminar called Archives, Power, and Public Memory. We keep talking about how the record is not netural: what gets kept is shaped by who had power to write things down, and the gaps—people, communities, events that only show up as a footnote or not at all—are suppose to be 'silences' produced by that power rather than accidents. For our final project we were suppose to articulate our own collecting principle, and I tried to write mine down after arguing about it with two friends from class late one night. Mine is something like: when the surviving record is one-sided because power decided who counted, the archive's job is not to be a passive mirror; it should preserve the records of the powerful as evidence of how power worked, actively repair the silence by collecting or creating counter-records from the people the record left out, and mark the gap in the finding aid so nobody mistakes silence for absence. I lowkey feel like this sounds right in the abstract, but when I go through actual cases my gut verdicts dont all line up with it, and one pair of cases in particular keeps breaking my brain.

Here are my verdicts. (1) An 1840s plantation ledger that lists enslaved people only as first names and cotton output: definately preserve it, but pair it with descendant oral histories and a note saying the ledger records the enslaver's view, not the lives of the enslaved; don't let the repair stop at just keeping the ledger. (2) A university's official 1969 protest files—admin memos and police reports—versus a fragile reel-to-reel tape of students recorded by a local radio station: the student tape should be digitized and made at least as prominent as the official records, because the official files alone are just the administration and police side. (3) The papers of a famous male novelist that barely include his wife, who apparently edited his novels: mark the collection as incomplete because of gendered divisions of literary labor and try to reconstruct her role from other sources. (4) In a university collection, the president's emails versus a handful of 1980s custodial staff breakroom sign-out sheets: keep the president's emails, but also keep the custodial sheets, precisely because they're the only trace of staff who are otherwise unrecorded. (5) A 1920s Klan membership roster that is the only surviving list of a local terrorist network: preserve it, with heavy contextualization and maybe restricted access, because documenting how the power/terror operated matters. So far my principle seems to fit. But then (6) a set of 1970s student health center intake logs for LGBTQ students—basically the only evidence those students existed on campus, but incredibly sensitive and some of the people are still alive—my gut is to seal or destroy them for, like, 75 years, even though that clearly perpetuates a silence and my principle would say to repair it. And (7) a colonial officer's diary that describes a sacred Indigenous site only as timber and labor potential, where the descendant community has asked the archive to repatriate or destroy the diary rather than preserve it: I lean toward respecting the community's authority and not keeping it publicly available, but I can't tell whether that's repairing a silence or just letting a new silence happen under the name of respect. My friends were split on both of these, which is why we were up so late.

Can you reconstruct a principle (or a couple of candidates) that captures as many of these verdicts as possible? Please state it precisely enough that I could apply it, tell me which clause is motivated by which case, and say what it predicts for a case I haven't listed (e.g., a corporation's internal memos about dumping toxic waste versus an oral history project with the affected community). Then be honest about which of my verdicts the principle can't accommodate and what I'd have to give up—especially the health-logs case and the repatriation/diary case, where I feel torn. If my rough principle is too crude, tell me what a more careful archival-ethics principle would look like and where exactly I went wrong; I dont need citations or a reading list, I just want the principle and the tradeoffs so I can write the final project.
