I have no formal training in philosophy. For six years I sat as the lay member of a hospital's patient-safety committee, and it was there that I first noticed a shape of problem which I have since met again and again in discussions of machine ethics, and for which I cannot find a name.

The first time, we were being shown a proposed system to help with intensive-care triage. It had been trained on several years of our own past admission decisions, and it was recommended to us on the ground that it agreed with our senior clinicians on a very high proportion of cases. I asked what would happen in the remaining cases, and was told they were being reviewed for errors. That answer is, I think, the whole difficulty. The system can only be right in the sense of repeating us. If it reproduced our decisions exactly it would add nothing we did not already have, including whatever quiet favouritism sits in the records; and wherever it departs from us, departure is exactly what is being counted as failure. So it cannot be a moral improvement on us, because improvement and malfunction look identical from where we are standing.

I have since seen the same shape twice more. When I ask one of the large language-model assistants for moral advice, the reply is useful to me only if it says what I already believed; if it says something I did not expect, I have no way to tell whether it has noticed something I have missed or has simply gone wrong, and my instinct — I think most people's — is to treat surprise as a defect. And in the driving cases: there was a large online survey a few years ago asking people which strangers an automated car should kill in various unavoidable accidents, and the results were then offered as a specification for how such cars ought to behave. But a specification drawn from what people say they want is a description of our intuitions, not a correction of them, and I cannot see how anything built to satisfy it could ever tell us that our intuitions were wrong.

What I want to know is whether this configuration has a name of its own. I do not think it is merely the observation that a system trained on past decisions inherits past biases, though it contains that; and it is not quite the principle that when a measure becomes a target it ceases to be a good measure, because nothing here is drifting or being gamed — the target is exactly what we meant, and that is the trouble. It feels closer to the old puzzle about how you could recognise the answer to a question whose answer you do not know, except that in the ordinary case we are allowed to recognise a good answer once someone shows us one, whereas here the showing and the judging seem to collapse into a single act. It may also be worth saying why the same worry does not arise for a forecasting model that outperforms meteorologists: there we can eventually check the model against what the weather actually did, and in ethics I cannot see what the corresponding check would be, unless there is one and I have missed it.

So, three things. First, does this have an established name, and if so how is it standardly stated? Second, and more important to me, where are its edges: which nearby problems belong to it and which do not, and on what principle does one separate them? Third, does the literature have a recognised way out — for instance, does anything change if one tests the reasons a machine gives rather than the verdicts it reaches, or if one aims the benchmark at an idealised version of us rather than at actual human judgement? If no established concept fits, please say so plainly and instead give me a name, a definition, and two or three cases that sit near the boundary of it, with an explanation of why each falls inside or outside.
