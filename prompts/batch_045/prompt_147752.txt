I have been reading about a proposed norm in Bayesian epistemology, and I have hit on a class of cases that I cannot classify. The norm, as I understand it, is this: a rational agent's current degree of confidence in a proposition, on the supposition that her later self will assign that proposition some specific probability, ought to equal that later probability. Equivalently, her current confidence should match her expectation of what her future confidence will be. I believe this is called the Reflection principle and is associated with Bas van Fraassen, though I may be misremembering the details. My difficulty is not with the basic principle as it applies to cases where I simply expect to learn more, or to have more evidence, later on. It is with cases where the future confidence is produced by a process I now regard as epistemically defective.

Let me give two concrete instances. First, suppose I now have strong, excellent evidence that a proposition A is true, and I am 90% confident of A. I also know for certain that tomorrow I will be given a drug that will permanently erase the specific experiences and records that constitute that evidence. I know exactly what the drug will do: after taking it, I will have no memory of the evidence and no way to recover it, and my confidence in A will settle at 50%. If I apply the Reflection principle naively, it seems to say that my current confidence in A, on the supposition that my future self will be 50% confident, should itself be 50%. But that would require me to discount my present evidence entirely and drop to 50% right now, merely because I know I will later forget why I believe A. That strikes me as wrong.

Second, suppose I know that next week I will be subjected to a prolonged and irresistible propaganda campaign that will raise my confidence in A to 80%, even though I now possess excellent evidence that A is only about 30% likely. Again, the principle seems to tell me that, conditional on my future self being 80% confident, I should already be 80% confident. Yet here the future change is not forgetfulness but a predictable corruption of my judgment by a process I now regard as unreliable.

These are not cases where I expect to receive new evidence I lack; nor are they cases where I expect my future self to have strictly more, or better, evidence than I have now. They seem instead to be cases of expected epistemic corruption or anticipated future irrationality. My questions are as follows. Does this pattern have an established name in Bayesian or formal epistemology? How is it related to the Reflection principle itself, to the standard objection that Reflection goes wrong when an agent expects to forget, and to the Dutch-book arguments sometimes offered in defense of Reflection? If there is no established term, please coin one, define it precisely, and give boundary cases that distinguish it from (a) merely forgetting evidence, (b) rationally updating on new evidence, and (c) ordinary cases where a future self has superior evidence. I am not asking for bibliographies, citations, or further reading; I want the conceptual carving: what exactly this phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie. A bare label would not satisfy me.
