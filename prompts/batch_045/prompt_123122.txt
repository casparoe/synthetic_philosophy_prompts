I am putting together a first-year seminar session on complicity, and the reason it has stuck with me is not really academic. Last month I resigned from a university committee that was about to approve a partnership I thought indefensible; a senior colleague told me, not unkindly, that I had accomplished nothing, since the vote was eleven to one and the outcome was never in doubt, and that if I had wanted to change anything I should have stayed and made the case. I have been going round in circles about whether he is right, and I keep reaching for Le Guin's 1973 story The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas, which I have now decided to teach instead of the bystander case study I first planned (too lurid for first-years, and the facts of it are contested anyway). I would like you to read the story through the philosophy of causation by omission: the city whose happiness depends on one child kept in filth and darkness, the citizens who are shown the child once they are old enough to understand and who mostly come to acquiesce, and the ones who simply leave, each alone, without knowing where they are going. What, on the best available accounts of causation by omission, are the stayers and the walkers actually doing, causally speaking?

Two features of the story seem to me to make it a sharper test case than the standard examples, and I want your reading to take them seriously. First, unlike the usual collective-omission cases (emissions, bystanders, a vote decided by more than one ballot), Omelas is rigged so that each individual stayer is a difference-maker: the terms of the city are said to be such that were the child brought up out of that place into the sunlight, washed, fed and comforted, all the prosperity and beauty and delight of Omelas would wither and be destroyed in that day and hour — so had any single citizen acted, the child would not be suffering. On a counterfactual or difference-making account the causal question about the stayers is therefore easy, and the familiar excuse that one person's omission makes no difference is simply unavailable. Which makes me wonder whether the causal verdict is doing any moral work here at all, since the difference each citizen can make is real but exercising it destroys everything else they love. It also looks like a rigged comparison between rival theories: the bond between child and city is a stipulation, very nearly a spell, rather than a causal process, so a production or conserved-quantity theory that refuses to treat absences as causes at all will say the stayers cause nothing, and I cannot tell whether that is a point in the story's favour or evidence that the story loads the dice against that theory. Second, the walkers-away do nothing for the child: they leave it where it is, so on any counterfactual test their walking away is causally inert, and yet the story plainly invites us to think that the exit means something. I have been toying with two ways of resisting that verdict and I want you to assess both. The first is contrast-sensitivity: perhaps what the walkers cause is not the child's suffering but some more finely individuated effect — the suffering as ratified by this population, or the continuance of a city that still includes them. But if contrastivism can always recover a difference by redescribing the effect, is the causal label just smuggling in a moral verdict? The second is a distinction I have been calling constitutive versus causal dependence: the city's acquiescence is not an effect of each citizen's staying but is partly constituted by it, so a walker changes what the city is rather than causing anything to happen — and then the questions are whether an account of complicity that never asks about consequences can still be normative, and whether the walkers can coherently be said to be aiming at that change at all.

I would like the reading to be honest in both directions and to end by saying what the story adds to the debate that the arguments alone do not. Where does the causal lens actually illuminate a moment? I am thinking especially of the reader's position, since the narrator keeps challenging us to believe in the child and to accept or refuse the bargain, which makes putting the book down itself the available omission — an audience's omission rather than an agent's — and of the closing refusal to say whether the place the walkers go to even exists, which leaves them acting with no information about consequences at all. And where does the lens distort the story? It is, after all, an anti-utilitarian parable about whether a happiness bought at that price can be accepted at all, and it may be that treating complicity as a causal question is exactly the move the story was written against; I would rather be told that than be handed a tidy reading. Finally, if you were teaching it to first-years, which two or three moments would you put in front of them and what would you ask about each? Describe them rather than quoting — we will have the text in front of us, and I would prefer my notes not to depend on exact wording.
