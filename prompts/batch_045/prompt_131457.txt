You are a professional philosopher with research-level command of collective action, game theory, and institutional design, and with some experience of advising bodies that must actually pass, enforce, and later defend rules. Please think carefully, and work in two stages.

Stage one. Before you build anything, brainstorm openly for a page at most about what a race to the bottom is. My impression is that several quite different mechanisms get bundled together under that label and under the Moloch slogan my younger colleagues keep sending me, and that the remedy suited to one is useless or harmful for another. Tell me which are genuinely the same phenomenon and which merely look alike. In particular: give me your view on whether a competition that injures its own participants is a different animal from one that injures people outside it, and whether the second deserves a different name and a different remedy; and distinguish a race in the absolute level of some input from a race for position in a ranking, since it seems to me that the first can be merely wasteful while the second cannot be won by anyone no matter how much is spent on it. If you think my framing is muddled, say so in one sentence at the outset and then answer anyway: I would rather have a usable procedure with a caveat attached than a critique with nothing attached.

Stage two. Turn the result into something my committee can run.

I chair the Research Culture Committee of a national learned society in the physical sciences, roughly eighteen thousand members. My own career was forty years of experimental work in condensed-matter physics; since retiring I have been reading philosophy on my own, which is to say badly and without supervision. Assume no technical vocabulary: if a distinction is doing work in your procedure, define it in the terms in which you use it.

Three or four times a year our committee receives a petition signed by some fraction of the membership asking Council to impose a binding constraint on everyone, in order to stop what the petitioners call an arms race. Success rates in the national grant scheme have fallen below ten per cent and reviewers are drowning; author lists on some papers run to thousands of names; my early-career colleagues now submit professionally designed portfolios for a travel prize that once required a page of text; and every few months someone proposes a new attestation, ban, or cap. Council has made us the gate through which these petitions pass, and has told us to stop improvising.

So: give me a decision procedure that four volunteer scientists with no economics training can run in ninety minutes, four times a year, and whose output is a one-page recommendation that Council can put to a membership vote. For each step, state the question, state what would count as a sufficient answer to it, and say where the procedure can stop. Some petitions should be refused outright at an early step; some should be sent to Council unresolved because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts — what the discipline is for, who should bear the cost of stopping — and I want those hand-off points marked explicitly, since we have been quietly making value judgments while calling them technical ones. Any step that requires a number we could not obtain within six weeks should say what to do instead, because we will usually have no number. Every recommendation should carry a sunset clause and a named observable quantity that would tell us, at review, whether the constraint worked.

I have a first-pass set of tests of my own, sketched from my reading; please use them as raw material — keep what is real, discard what is confused, add what is missing, and order them:

One, the exit test: can an individual member decline to escalate without forfeiting something they need? If yes, the competition is voluntary and we should not bind anyone.
Two, the winners test: would those currently winning the race also sign the constraint? If not, suspect that we are being asked to protect a losing faction rather than to rescue everyone.
Three, the displacement test: if we cap one input, does the competition reappear in another, and is the new one cheaper or dearer to monitor?
Four, the ratchet test: once an escalated level is reached, does it persist after the pressure is removed? My instinct is that persistence strengthens the case for acting early, but I can also see the opposite reading, so please settle it or tell me it is genuinely unsettled.
Five, the jurisdiction test: what fraction of the competing population can we actually bind, and do the unconstrained outsiders decide the outcome anyway?
Six, the enforcement test: does policing the constraint itself become a new arms race of evasion, credentialling, and monitoring?
Seven, the measurement test: can we state now what we would look at in three years?

Then apply the finished procedure to three petitions, and check it against them before you finalise it: revise the procedure if a case breaks it.

Petition A: cap the number of principal-investigator grant applications any member may submit in a twelve-month period, at two.

Petition B: require every member submitting a referee report to our journals to attest that the report was not generated by a language model, on pain of a two-year reviewing ban.

Petition C, from a large group of early-career members: abolish the judged early-career impact prize that this committee currently administers, and replace it with a random draw among applicants who clear a stated threshold.

I expect the procedure to give us an answer on Petition C that we will not welcome. I would rather you told us so plainly than softened it.

Finally: tell us where the procedure cannot be trusted — the kinds of petition it will systematically over-regulate or under-regulate, and the two or three situations in which it should decline to give an answer at all. Do not give me a reading list, citations, or a bibliography; I cannot chase them up usefully and I do not want them. Argue in your own words, from the theory, and tell me when you are guessing.
