I write about science and ideas for a living, and while reporting a feature on big, irreversible life choices I drifted into accepting a position I first thought was overstated: that for the class of options L.A. Paul calls epistemically transformative — options whose central outcome you cannot evaluate in advance, because what matters about it is knowable only by having it — standard expected-utility decision theory is not merely hard to apply but ill-posed, and that none of the obvious repairs actually work. The repairs I have in mind are the ones I keep being offered: treat the object of choice as a lottery over the person you become and let the machinery run over that enlarged outcome space; reclassify the choice as a purchase of information and price it with the usual value-of-information apparatus; fall back on an objectivist account of well-being so that the outcome is good or bad for you whether or not you can imagine it; sample the experience where sampling is possible; or simply insist that ignorance of one's own future tastes is ordinary empirical uncertainty of a kind we muddle through constantly. Each of these looks to me like it relocates the difficulty rather than meeting it.

I would like the case against me, properly organised. Ranked from fatal to merely awkward, what would have to be true — or what would have to be shown — for the ill-posedness claim to be false? Please separate, as I have to for the piece, the defeaters a sympathetic defender of this position would herself concede from the ones she would dismiss as resting on a misdescription, and say why she would dismiss those. In particular I want to know what the position stands or falls with: whether it collapses if the special first-personal character of experiential knowledge is denied; whether it is really a thesis about whose preferences have normative authority; whether it presupposes that decision theory owes us synchronic, action-guiding advice about one-shot, non-repeatable choices at all, or whether it is only visible once you demand that; and how much of it survives if the value of discovering who you become turns out to be just another term in an ordinary expected-utility calculation rather than a distinct kind of reason. I also want the consequences it is committed to that might be unacceptable on reflection — above all the worry that if the argument works for becoming a parent it also works for ordering an unfamiliar dish, and either that generality is a reductio or the argument has been quietly trading on the stakes all along. And please tell me honestly whether the position has been qualified into something unfalsifiable, given that any third-personal evidence about how the experience will feel can in principle be waved away as not being the relevant kind of knowledge; if it has, say whether that is a defect here or whether insulation of that sort is what we should expect from a claim about the limits of a formalism.

Three concrete test cases, since I would rather test the claim than admire it. First, suppose a well-validated predictive system — call it a preference oracle — that, from longitudinal data on people like you, produces a calibrated probability distribution over your post-transformation preferences and over whether you will later endorse the choice, with reliability comparable to good economic forecasting. Does that close the gap, or does the defender say, coherently, that it is still not the thing you needed to know? Second, suppose a reversible sampler: a technology that gives you the phenomenal character of the experience for an hour and then withdraws it, leaving your life and your deeper dispositions untouched. Third, the empirical finding I would find most destabilising: that people facing transformative choices predict their later attitudes about as well as they predict ordinary ones, and that retrospective endorsement tracks what third-personal information predicted ex ante. What does each of these do to the position, and which of them is decisive rather than merely awkward? The thing that would move me most, personally, is a demonstration that the framework yields determinate advice in a hard case that matches what we would independently count as wisdom — if the best it can offer is the recommendation to decide whether you want to be the kind of person who finds out, I will start to suspect I have been persuaded by a restatement of the problem.
