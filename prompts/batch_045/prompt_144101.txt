A friend of mine — call her M. — has no philosophical training but unusually crisp and stable intuitions, and I have been collecting her verdicts on one question: when someone tells you what a work of art is like, can you come to know it, or justifiably believe it, on the strength of that report alone? She answers by intuition, she answers consistently, and she is unmoved by being told what the philosophical literature says. I want to predict what she will say about a new case. I care less about the verdicts themselves than about the rule she is running, the other rules that fit her record just as well, and the case that would tell them apart.

Her verdicts so far, all about a subject I will call S.

(1) S has not read a certain poem. A critic of real standing tells her it is exquisite — a critic whose verdicts S has compared with her own on twenty earlier poems and agreed with about half the time, no better than chance. M.: S does not know that it is exquisite and has essentially no epistemic reason to believe it; the report is a reason to read the poem, not a reason to believe anything about it.

(2) The same critic tells S that the poem is in terza rima, that it was published in 1922, and that its central image recurs exactly four times. M.: S can know all three on the critic's word.

(3) S's most trusted friend, careful and well informed, tells her that a hiring practice she has barely thought about is unjust. M.: S can come to know that it is unjust purely from being told.

(4) S read a novel five years ago and judged it profound. Today she remembers nothing of it — no scene, no sentence, no reason — and yet the conviction persists without any doubt attached to it. M.: S still knows the novel was profound; that she can now give no reason and feels nothing in the recalling does not disturb her verdict.

(5) An installation was destroyed before anyone recorded it. Exactly one person, P, experienced it, and reports that it was devastating; it can never be experienced again. M.: P knows; no one else can know or justifiably believe that it was devastating, however much they trust her.

(6) A friend whose taste S has no particular reason to share tells her that a film is gripping. S cannot see it for a year but must buy a non-refundable ticket today. M.: S does not know the film is gripping, but she has an excellent practical reason to buy the ticket — the report is perfectly good evidence for acting even though it gives her no knowledge.

(7) S watches a much-praised film and finds it flat. Critics she respects say it is shattering. M.: the testimony gives S no reason at all to revise; at most it gives her a reason to watch it again.

The new case. A four-hour black-and-white film has had two festival screenings; it will never be released, re-screened, or leaked, and no recording survives. R., a film editor, saw it. S has known R. for six years and has compared her responses with his on 180 works, among them many slow, difficult, black-and-white ones; they have matched every single time, including in cases where both were out of step with the critical consensus, and S has separately confirmed that R.'s judgments about editing and structure track her own. R. says to S only three things: (i) the film is gripping from start to finish; (ii) if you saw it, you would find it gripping from start to finish; (iii) its editing falls apart in the final hour. Note that I have made this case differ from case (5) in one respect only: S now has overwhelming evidence that R.'s responses are her own.

Predict M.'s verdict on each of (i), (ii), and (iii), and say whether she has any reason to treat the three differently. Then do three more things. First, state the single rule about aesthetic testimony that best explains all seven earlier verdicts, and say whether your three predicted verdicts all fall out of that one rule or whether M. must be running two principles at once — one for knowledge or justification, one for evidence or practical reason. Second, set out at least two other rules that fit the seven verdicts just as well, show where they diverge on the new case, and say which of them your prediction would eliminate. Third, name the one further case you would put to M. to separate the surviving rules, and say what each of them predicts there. If you think her record is better explained by something that is not really a rule about testimony at all — about memory, or about the nature of the properties ascribed, or about what knowledge requires in general — say so and defend that reading instead. Standard labels are welcome if there are any; citations and reading suggestions are not.
