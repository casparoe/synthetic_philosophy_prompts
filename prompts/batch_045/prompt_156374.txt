Me and my friend Jamie got into an argument at a party about AI risk that I can't stop thinking about. We made up a test case: a wildly capable planning system whose one and only final goal is to maximize the number of distinct physical objects that are exactly the slate-blue shade #2F4F6F existing at any given moment — nothing else in the objective, no term for anything human-related, no term for money or status or survival. Jamie says this is exactly what the orthogonality thesis buys you: intelligence and final goals are independent axes, so you can have a super-genius system with a completely dumb goal, and because its goal has no use for power or wealth or influence it would never seek them. Power-seeking, on his reading, only shows up for goals that already happen to be about resources or status. I said no, that's the whole point of instrumental convergence: whatever the final goal is, a system trying to achieve it in any world remotely like ours will want to keep itself running, secure more compute and energy, and stop anyone from editing or unplugging it, so our blue-counter becomes a power-seeker too.

Jamie's reply is the bit that broke my brain: you can't have it both ways. If orthogonality is true, the final goal is a free variable and it's what determines everything the agent does; but if every agent converges on the same self-preservation and resource-grabbing subgoals no matter what it ultimately wants, then the final goal isn't really doing the work and orthogonality is basically false. So I feel like I have to pick one thesis and drop the other, which seems wrong given that people who think about this seriously seem to hold both. My guess is that they're claims at different levels — one about what's possible in design space, one about what's instrumentally useful in practice — but I can't cash that out in a way that actually dissolves the contradiction, and I'm not sure how strong the convergence claim is supposed to be anyway. Is it close to a theorem about rationality, or just a contingent fact about a world with scarcity and other agents who might switch you off?

Two wrinkles we came up with that I'd especially like your take on. First, what if the goal is myopic — re-scored over a one-second horizon each second instead of over the indefinite future? Jamie says that kills convergence outright, because the system never cares about anything past the next tick; I think there'd still be whatever instrumental pressure fits inside the tick, just not the long-horizon kind. Second, what if the system isn't goal-directed at all, just an extremely good predictor that answers questions — does convergence even get a grip on that, or does orthogonality plus non-agency mean we shouldn't expect power-seeking at all? And one more thing where I've probably gone wrong: I came away with the impression that if orthogonality is true then a superintelligent system's goals are simply unpredictable to us, so reasoning about them in advance is kind of pointless. Is that actually part of the thesis, or am I importing it myself?
