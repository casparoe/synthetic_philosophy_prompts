I retired four years ago after a career in sensory physiology — thirty-odd years working on rod and cone function in nocturnal mammals — and I have since been auditing a continuing-education seminar in bioethics, which is how I came to be appointed the "community scientist" member of our teaching hospital's Novel Interventions Committee. The committee maintains two lists: interventions we classify as restorative, which the hospital funds and our clinicians may prescribe, and interventions we classify as elective, which a patient may obtain privately but which we do not pay for. What has struck me in my first year is that we are far better at producing verdicts than at stating the rule we are running; the seminar readings draw a tidy line between restoring normal function and improving beyond it, but our own decisions seem to me to be consistent with several different lines at once, and I would like that made explicit before our next meeting.

Our recent verdicts, in brief: a cochlear implant for a toddler with congenital profound sensorineural hearing loss went on the restorative list, with fairly short shrift given to the argument that deafness is a cultural identity rather than a deficit; deep brain stimulation for advanced Parkinson's tremor, restorative; testosterone replacement for a sixty-eight-year-old man with serum levels in the lowest decile and documented fatigue, restorative, but with the explicit proviso that the dosing target is the range typical of healthy men in their forties rather than their twenties; modafinil for residents on night float who have no sleep disorder, elective; standard walking prostheses for a bilateral below-knee amputee, restorative, while the carbon-fiber running blades the same man wanted for competition were elective, five to two, despite his argument that they merely restore the locomotive range everyone else enjoys; growth hormone for a boy with idiopathic short stature — no deficiency, no disease, predicted adult height near the third percentile — elective, four to three, over a dissent holding that severe short stature is a real social and psychological disadvantage and not a bare preference; and a hippocampal memory prosthesis for a man with lasting impairment after viral encephalitis, restorative only after the manufacturer agreed to a firmware cap holding performance near the median of the age-matched normal range, with the minutes recording that we would not have funded an uncapped version.

Next month's case is the one I cannot see our way through. A thirty-four-year-old wildland firefighter with early-stage retinitis pigmentosa — measured acuity 20/60, pronounced night blindness, expected to progress — is being offered enrollment in a trial of a camera-based cortical visual prosthesis. In the trial data it brings daytime acuity to roughly 20/25, still below her premorbid 20/15, and because the camera's sensor extends into the near-infrared it also generates a false-color overlay that gives genuinely useful vision in darkness and through smoke. The manufacturer can switch that overlay off in firmware, but only at the cost of dropping measured acuity to about 20/50, since the device's contrast processing draws on the additional spectral channels: the supra-normal effect is not a detachable extra but is causally entangled with the therapeutic one, a "bundling" problem I keep raising in the seminar and cannot get anyone to rule on. Without the implant she will lose her field certification within the year; a desk assignment exists, but it ends the career she wants. A related case is already queued behind hers: the same prosthesis for a sixty-one-year-old colleague whose night vision has declined to the ordinary extent for her age, with no ocular disease at all.

What I need from you, in under 500 words and in three short labeled sections rather than a single block: first, the two or three rules our precedents are jointly consistent with — a pathology-or-medical-need rule, a restore-to-the-normal-range rule, an opportunity-or-positional-advantage rule, or something I have not thought of — and what each of them predicts for the firefighter, including whether the "normal" in question is indexed to the species, to an age cohort, or to a person's occupational role; second, your recommended classification and the closest analog among our own precedents, together with an honest statement of the principle we would be adopting under each of the alternatives, including funding her with the overlay disabled or declining outright; third, the single further case whose verdict would best separate the surviving rules, and whether my queued sixty-one-year-old actually does that work or merely repeats what the amputee's blades already told us.
