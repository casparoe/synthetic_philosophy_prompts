I'm putting together a short magazine explainer on voting and collective choice, and I keep tripping over this thing called Sen's liberal paradox. A popular book on social choice that I'm using as background sums it up like this — I'd paste the passage but its on my other laptop, so this is close to verbatim:

Two housemates share one copy of a racy novel. The prude would rather nobody read it, but if somebody has to, he'd sooner read it himself than let his housemate be corrupted by it. The housemate, who is no prude at all, most wants the prude to read it, would rather read it himself than have nobody read it, and likes the nobody-reads-it option least. Now give each of them a say over exactly one purely personal matter — whether *they* read the book — and add the rule that if both of them prefer one arrangement to another, that's the arrangement. Those three commitments — a sliver of personal liberty, respect for unanimous preference, and no limits on what preferences people are allowed to hold — are jointly impossible: any rule satisfying all three generates a cycle in which every arrangement is beaten by another, so in the end the book goes unread. The author calls it Arrow's theorem with a rights condition bolted on, says the lesson is the same one Arrow drew (get consistency and you need a dictator), and adds that Sen's own way out was to drop the unanimity condition, since rights ought to trump efficiency. He closes by calling the result devastating for libertarians, because it shows that even the most minimal anti-paternalist politics contradicts itself.

My gut says the dictator line is where he's gone wrong — isn't that Arrow's conclusion rather than Sen's, and wouldn't an actual dictator violate the liberty condition anyway? And the "so the book goes unread" bit smells like a misunderstanding of what a cycle is. But I'm not confident about the rest, and I don't want to print something that makes a political theorist snort-laugh. Also, one thing I can't tell: whether the paradox is supposed to be a problem *for* rights, or a reductio of the whole way of modeling rights as decisiveness over outcomes.

Can you go through it more or less line by line and tell me what someone who actually works on social choice would accept, what they'd call a fair simplification for a popular audience, and what they'd say is just wrong? Where would a defender of Sen's result say the book has misrepresented it, and does the closing claim about libertarians, or the claim that rights should trump efficiency, follow from anything in the accurate part? If the paradox really is best read as a reductio of the framework rather than of one of the three conditions, say so and tell me what that reading costs — I'd rather get that right in the piece than repeat the tidy version.
