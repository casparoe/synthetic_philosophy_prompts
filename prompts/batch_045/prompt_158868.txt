I sit on the clinical ethics committee of a 600-bed teaching hospital, and we are jointly reviewing a proposal with our new clinical-AI oversight subcommittee. The vendor, Meridian, wants to pilot a system that generates what it calls an extrapolated preference report for patients who lack decision-making capacity and have no available surrogate or advance directive — the "unbefriended" patients, which is a real and growing category on our wards. The system is trained on a patient's own prior digital traces (messages, calendar entries, purchase history, portal messages, clinic notes) together with a population prior, and it runs in either of two modes. In conservative mode it only fills in missing information and discounts statements made under acute pain, delirium, sedation, or untreated depression — the patient's underlying evaluative standards are held fixed. In revisionary mode it also revises those standards themselves, on the theory that a person better informed and more experienced would not merely apply her current values to better facts but would come to have different ones. The vendor's own validation study compared system output against wishes later stated by patients who regained capacity, and reports 71% agreement overall, with disagreements clustering heavily in end-of-life decisions — precisely where the reports would matter most. Our index case is a 74-year-old woman, Mrs. Halvorsen, three weeks post-stroke, no surrogate, no directive, whose retrievable traces contain a decade of repeated statements that she would never want to be a burden and would rather die than live in a nursing home, alongside a two-year stretch of severe, untreated depressive episodes in which many of those statements were made.

What has split our committee is not the familiarsubstituted-judgment question but what happens when substituted judgment runs out. I want the memo to take up three things specifically. First, the anchoring problem: at what point does an idealized version of Mrs. Halvorsen stop being a version of her at all, and is there a principled stopping rule or only a stipulation the vendor has baked into a parameter? Second, order sensitivity: on the one test case we ran, revising her aversion to being a burden before revising her fatalism about her prognosis yielded a recommendation for continued aggressive treatment, while reversing the order yielded a comfort-focused plan. If the endpoint of extrapolation depends on the sequence of revisions, then a single confident recommendation misrepresents the actual output of the procedure, and I want your view on whether that is a defect in this system or a feature of the idealization idea itself. Third, what I think of as the ventriloquism failure mode: whatever the procedure's theoretical standing, a confident paragraph in the chart will displace clinical judgment and family deliberation rather than inform it, and we should ask whether our safeguards can realistically prevent that.

Please draft the memo I will circulate before our joint meeting two weeks from Thursday. It should be about 900 to 1,200 words, open with a clear recommendation — authorize the pilot, authorize in conservative mode only, or decline — and then give the reasons in a form the vendor and the clinical leads could actually respond to, including any conditions with dates attached (for instance, what the system would have to demonstrate about path-independence or about how it handles statements made during depression, and by when). It should also give the strongest consideration on the losing side its full weight rather than disposing of it in a sentence, and it should close by naming what evidence or result would most change your recommendation. Please do not spend the memo restating that substituted judgment is our legal standard; assume we know that, and tell us what to do in the cases where there is no one left to substitute.
