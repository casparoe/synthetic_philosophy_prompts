I have been reading about negative utilitarianism and the suffering-focused ethics that descends from it, and rather than testing it against invented trolley cases I want to see how it handles a real episode: the campaign to eradicate Guinea worm disease. The facts, as I understand them, are these. Guinea worm is contracted by drinking standing water containing water fleas that carry the parasite; roughly a year later a mature female worm, up to about a metre long, emerges through a painful blister, usually on the leg or foot, over the course of weeks. It rarely kills, but it incapacitates people for weeks or months, often at planting or harvest time, and the emergence is accompanied by a burning pain that sufferers try to relieve by immersing the limb in water, which is precisely how the parasite re-enters the water supply. There is no vaccine and no curative drug. The campaign, led since 1986 by the Carter Center together with WHO and national ministries, took the disease from an estimated 3.5 million cases a year across roughly twenty African and Asian countries to somewhere around ten to fifteen human cases a year in a handful of remaining countries, and it did so without any medical technology at all: filtering drinking water, keeping infected people out of water sources, applying the larvicide temephos to ponds to kill the water fleas, paying for case reporting, and containing cases. Since 2012 the programme has been complicated by the discovery that the parasite is now circulating mainly in domestic dogs and some cats, apparently because they eat fish containing the larvae, so the response now includes burying fish waste, tethering dogs and cats during the transmission season, and experimenting with veterinary deworming. What struck me is that the campaign's own publicity measures its achievement in years of suffering prevented rather than in deaths averted, which sounds like it was tailor-made for the view I am interested in.

So here is what I want: a disciplined mapping of the case onto suffering-focused ethics, running in both directions. On the theory's side, I want to know who counts as a patient and what counts as harm — the human pain and incapacitation obviously, but also the tethered dogs, and, if the view really is about suffering wherever it occurs, the enormous numbers of water fleas and other aquatic invertebrates killed by larvicide, which matters a great deal if one assigns even a modest credence to invertebrate sentience. A friend who works in global health says the endgame of the campaign is terrible value for money: the marginal cost per case averted when only a dozen cases remain is astronomical compared with, say, palliative care or deworming programmes, and standard burden-of-disease metrics score a low-mortality, high-pain disease in remote villages poorly because they weight death and lost productivity. Does suffering-focused ethics vindicate the campaign where those metrics do not, or is it simply relabelling the same calculation? And what does it say about the animal leg of the programme — the tethering, the fish-waste burial, and the option, which has been floated in similar eradication contexts, of culling reservoir animals?

Then I want the case turned back on the theory. Two things in particular seem to me to be genuinely awkward for it, and I would like you to say whether they are fatal, illuminating, or simply outside its scope. First, a beneficiary problem: the people spared in the 2020s are largely not the people who would have been spared in 1986, and the payoff of eradication is mostly located in unidentified future people, so it is worth asking whether a view that, on some formulations, has no room for benefiting merely possible people can say why the campaign was worth doing at all. Related to this is what I would call the extinction premium: is permanent eradication worth more than the sum of the cases it prevents, because it forecloses the risk of future resurgence, and does a version of the view that counts only actual experienced suffering have to condemn the last decade of spending while a version that counts risks has to explain why the same money should not have gone to larger catastrophic risks instead? Second, a population offset: preventing incapacitation lets people farm, survive, and, over decades, plausibly raises the number of people who exist, each of whom will suffer somewhat; if the asymmetry is right, preventing suffering is what matters and adding worthwhile lives is not a compensating gain, so I want to know whether this offset is a real cost the view must book, and whether it threatens to cancel the campaign's benefits. Please be explicit about where the theory gives a determinate verdict, where it is genuinely silent, and what, if anything, this episode should change in the mind of someone who already finds negative utilitarianism attractive.
