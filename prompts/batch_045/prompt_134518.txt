I'm a software engineer by trade — I build data pipelines, no philosophy background beyond a couple of half-finished online courses — and last spring I was picked by sortition for my city's charter review commission, which among other jobs has to decide what recommendation we attach to proposed changes in who counts as a voter in municipal elections. I ended up being the one who keeps the spreadsheet of our decisions, and going back through it I've realised we've been consistent in our verdicts without anyone being able to say what rule we're actually applying. The record so far: we kept the voting age at 18 for mayor and council but approved letting 16- and 17-year-olds vote for the school board only, on the reasoning that they are the ones who have to live with what that board does; we approved the municipal vote for non-citizen residents with at least two years' residence and rejected it for shorter-term residents, and rejected it again for non-resident property and second-home owners who pay city property tax but sleep somewhere else (roughly: you vote where you live); we approved the vote for people held in jail awaiting trial but rejected it, six to three, for people serving felony sentences, with the dissent saying we were punishing people twice; we approved automatic registration and mandatory time off work to vote but rejected a modest fine for abstaining and rejected publishing the names of non-voters, the chair's line being that we can clear the path but we cannot punish people for staying home; and we rejected seven to two a proposal that a parent or guardian could cast one extra ballot per child under sixteen, the majority saying a proxy ballot is the parent's judgment and not the child's. When I've raised the pattern at meetings, three different members have offered three different glosses on it — one says if the city can coerce you then you get a say, another says the vote belongs to people who are in it for the long run and will still be here, a third says it tracks who can form an actual judgment on the question being asked — and none of them has been able to explain why the others are wrong.

The item in front of us now is a citizen-initiated charter amendment we can't amend, only recommend for or against, with two hundred words of explanation. It would (a) let anyone sixteen or older who has lived in the city thirty days and intends to stay register and vote in all municipal elections, candidates and ballot measures alike, citizen or not, replacing the current eighteen-and-over, citizens-and-two-year-residents rule for city elections; (b) add an official "none of the above" option on every municipal race, counted and published like a candidate, where a majority "none of the above" leaves the office vacant and forces a special election within ninety days; and (c) move any registered voter who skips two consecutive municipal elections to inactive status, requiring re-registration before voting again — no fine, no published list, and you can do it at the polls on the day. Note that part (c) is satisfied by turning up and voting "none of the above".

What I need from you is this: lay out the two or three (or four) candidate principles that fit our track record just as well as each other, including any I haven't thought of, and say what each of them predicts about each part of this package; give me your best guess at what we should recommend if we want to be consistent with ourselves, with reasons for preferring that over the alternatives and with the closest analog among our own precedents identified; tell me honestly where our precedents genuinely underdetermine the answer and which principle we would be publicly adopting with a yes versus a no, especially given that the parts pull in different directions and we have to vote on it as one thing; and name the further case — the variation on this proposal, or the separate proposal — that would best tell the competing principles apart if we could get it in front of the commission next month. Please don't give me reading lists or citations; I have no library access and I need two hundred words I can stand behind at a public meeting.
