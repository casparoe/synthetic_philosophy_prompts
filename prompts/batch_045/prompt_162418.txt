I run the talks programme for a small local history society, and I also sit in on a reading group that's slowly grinding through philosophy of history — we did Carr a while back (the bit where the facts of the past only become facts *of history* once some historian calls them up, which struck me as obviously right and also obviously a bit of a dodge), we've got the old Ranke idea that you can just show the past as it actually was on the reading list, and someone keeps saying we should do Hayden White next. Anyway, there's a thing I keep noticing in the actual history books I read that I don't have a word for, and Google hasn't helped.

Take any two recent books on the same event — say the end of a communist regime, or a revolution, or a company's collapse. At the level of the individual sentence they agree almost completely: dates, who was in the room, what the decree said, what the harvest or the balance sheet actually was. The footnotes check out. But the books tell completely different stories. One starts in 1956 and the other in 1985. One treats the harvest figures as the thing that moved history and the other treats three people's bad decisions in one week as the thing that moved it. One gives a famine four pages and the other gives it a subordinate clause ending in a semicolon. One ends when the wall comes down and the other runs four years past it. And nobody ever argues about the page-count thing directly, even though it seems to me to be doing most of the work. Now the part I actually want a word for: when a reviewer says a book has left something out — a faction, a province, a whole decade — the author very often fixes it in the second edition. The material goes in, sometimes as a couple of new paragraphs, sometimes as a long discursive footnote. And the story doesn't change. At all. The new fact gets absorbed into the existing shape without disturbing it; it sometimes even makes the shape feel more solid, because now the author has obviously dealt with the objection. But the reviewer's original complaint is still as live as it was before, and it was never really about the missing item anyway. I've now watched this happen enough times that I treat it as a rule: correcting the factual content of a historical account and changing what the account is *about* are, as far as I can tell, entirely decoupled operations — and working historians seem to know this perfectly well while continuing to argue as though the facts were going to settle something.

So: is there a name for that, and a literature on it? I mean specifically the absorption property — you can add true material to an account without the account having been wrong about anything, and without its overall claim changing. I don't think I'm after "historical facts are selected" (sure, but selection is about what gets pulled in, whereas this is about what happens to material *after* it's been pulled in), and I don't think I'm after underdetermination of theory by evidence either, because in science new data sometimes does force you to drop the theory and I genuinely can't think of a case where a corrected detail forced a historian to retell the story. Please don't just hand me a label — I want to know whether this is one thing or two or three (the space-allocation thing may be separate from the absorption thing), what the nearest established concepts are and exactly where each of them falls short of what I've described, and what a case where correction *did* force a reshaping would even look like, so I'd recognise one if I saw it. And if there's nothing established, what would you call it and how would you define it so it doesn't just collapse into "narratives have shapes, obviously"?
