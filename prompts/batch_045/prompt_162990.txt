I work in confidential computing and protocol security — remote attestation and enclaves, reproducible builds, proof-carrying code, fair-exchange and gradual-release protocols. My game theory stops at Nash equilibrium and the barest notion of a commitment; I have never read this philosophical literature, so please carry that side of it.

Here is the dispute I want translated. In the program-equilibrium setup, two players in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma do not choose actions. Each submits a program that can read the other's source code and then outputs an action, and an equilibrium is a pair of programs such that neither player gains by submitting something else instead. There are such equilibria in which both sides cooperate — for instance a program that cooperates just in case the other program is exactly itself, or one that cooperates just in case it can prove, inside its own proof system, that the other program cooperates. One camp reads this as showing that rational one-shot cooperation is possible after all: the familiar impossibility was an artifact of making agents choose actions rather than choose policies that condition on the other's policy, and nothing outside the game is needed — no repetition, no enforcer, no irrationality. The other camp reads it as relocating the problem: which program to submit is itself unmodeled and unselected among enormous numbers of equilibria, most of them bad; the cooperative constructions are brittle under small perturbations; and the assumption that each side can read the other is doing all the work.

I suspect the two camps are relying on different notions of what it is for one program to read another, and I want the translation to pry them apart: legibility, where I inspect the other party's code and thereby predict what it will do; verifiability, where the other party hands me something I can check — a proof, an attestation, a hash over a reproducible build — without my predicting anything; and identity-matching, where I can tell that the other party runs the same program I do without understanding it at all. Which of these does each construction actually use, and which of the three can my field really deliver?

Two further constraints. First, use as your running test case a variant in which the players are asymmetric: one has a strictly larger proof-search or verification budget than the other. Where does the cooperative outcome go, and, in my terms, what has gone wrong? Second, my field carries a standing intuition that simultaneous exchange cannot be conjured from nothing — that you need a trusted third party, or an optimistic protocol that merely bounds the counterparty's advantage, or a probabilistic gradual release. If that intuition is roughly right, tell me whether the program-equilibrium result contradicts it or whether the two results are about different things; if I have over-generalized it, correct me.

Answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion: (1) the base mapping — what a program, an equilibrium, and a defection are in my vocabulary; (2) the two camps' disagreement restated in that vocabulary; (3) what translated without loss; (4) what you had to distort, stretch, or silently idealize in order to translate, including where the asymmetric-budget variant lands; (5) what has no counterpart at all in my vocabulary, together with your verdict on whether that residue is a blind spot of my framework or a sign that the philosophers' dispute leans on an idealization it never acknowledged.
