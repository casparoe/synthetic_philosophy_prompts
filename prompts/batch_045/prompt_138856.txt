I am helping to put together a short teaching dialogue for an internal decision-making handbook at a small aerospace company; it will be read both by engineers on a flight-readiness review board and by two philosophers on the company's advisory panel, so it needs to be technically serious but not jargon-only. My own draft was useless — the Bayesian kept saying "just pick a prior" and the imprecisist kept saying "you can't," and nothing moved — so I would like you to write it instead, with both characters equally competent and neither reduced to a slogan.

The case they are advising on: a reusable upper stage that has already flown once can either be reflown or replaced with a newly built one. Two inspection teams have looked at the same microfracture data and disagree about the metallurgy: one team's model implies a failure risk on reflight of around 7%, the other implies around 20%, and nobody has a defensible probability that either team's method is the right one. Before deciding, the board can run a cryogenic cycling test. Both teams agree that fleet history pins the base failure rate somewhere between 6% and 9%, but they disagree sharply about how informative the test is: on the first team's model a "pass" roughly halves the risk and a "fail" pushes it toward something like 60%, whereas on the second team's model the test tracks almost nothing and moves the number by less than a point either way.

The two advisers are Marit Vasse, who holds that rational credence is always a single number, and Daniel Okonjo, who holds that it can be a set of probability functions. I want them to fight over at least these four things, with each of them given their strongest version:

First, the diagnosis. Vasse says the board's spread is really second-order uncertainty about which sharp model is correct, and that a single sharp probability over the joint space — a weighted mixture over the two teams' models — represents it perfectly well, with no loss. Okonjo says the spread is not a mixture: the board has no defensible weights, and averaging with made-up weights manufactures precision the evidence does not support.

Second, the phenomenon where conditioning on each cell of a partition widens the interval: before the test the board's interval for failure is comparatively narrow, but whichever result comes back — pass or fail — the interval afterward is wider, so the board knows in advance that learning the result will make it less precise. Vasse needs a story about this that isn't just denial; Okonjo needs to say why it isn't a reductio of his view.

Third, the worry about responsiveness to evidence: updating every function in the set by conditioning can leave the set wide no matter how much data arrives, since the set may contain some function that anticipated whatever data showed up, whereas a sharp mixture over the same hypotheses would converge. Okonjo should say what the imprecise framework is for, if not for tracking evidence.

Fourth, the decision rules. Have them work through what the set is supposed to do when it doesn't single out an action: picking the option with the best worst case over the set, versus permitting any option that maximizes expected value according to at least one function in the set and isn't beaten by another option under every function in the set. Vasse then presses the point that whichever rule Okonjo picks, the board's actual choice will agree with some sharp function, so the sharp function is doing all the work and the set is idle machinery — and she adds the stronger argument that no rule for a spread-out agent can be both safe and non-timid, because any such rule either turns down wagers that every function in the set rates as favourable or accepts a package of wagers that loses money whatever happens.

Please write it as a dialogue of roughly 1000 to 1400 words with minimal stage directions. Both characters should respond to what the other actually said rather than to a rehearsed script; each should make at least one real concession; there should be at least one moment where one of them reformulates the other's position and the other accepts the reformulation as fair; and define any technical term in passing when it first appears, since the engineers in the audience will not know it. Do not let either side win by stipulation or by an appeal to what everyone in the field supposedly accepts, and end with the two of them locating the precise point at which they still disagree rather than resolving the dispute.
