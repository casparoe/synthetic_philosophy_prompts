I'm a philosopher who has been asked, somewhat outside my competence, to sit on a committee reviewing how regulatory agencies here should quantify the benefits of a proposed rule. Before I sign anything, I want to understand what the method we already have is committed to, because several of its features look to me as though they pull in different directions. As I understand current practice, it has roughly these parts.

A benefit is measured by willingness to pay: what people would pay for the improvement in question. Costs are priced the same way, as what those same resources would have fetched elsewhere. When a rule takes away something people already have — a bus route, a clinic, access to a stretch of river — the loss is still priced as what they would pay to keep it, not as what they would accept as compensation for losing it; the willingness-to-accept figures are set aside as inflated or incoherent. For goods that have markets, the willingness to pay is read off behaviour, from wage premiums or house prices; for goods that have no market, it is elicited by asking people in surveys what they would pay. Risk to life is priced differently again: a single figure per statistical life is applied uniformly to everyone, even though every other benefit is priced by a willingness to pay that plainly scales with income. And benefits that fall far in the future are discounted, so that a hundred lives saved a century from now count for much less than a hundred saved today.

The method's defenders say that preferences are revealed by choice, and that utility is nothing but a device for representing choices — not a quantity in the head that could be measured or compared across persons. I want to know what this package actually presupposes. Does measuring in money require exactly the cardinal, interpersonally comparable utility that the official story says we have no access to, given that a dollar is worth less to a rich person than to a poor one? Does the refusal to use willingness-to-accept commit the practice to treating the existing distribution of entitlements as the morally correct baseline — and is that commitment required by the framework or merely convenient for it? Is the uniform price on a statistical life in genuine tension with pricing everything else by income-sensitive willingness to pay? Does the discounting require a pure preference for the present, which I gather the framework officially disavows, or can it be derived from an empirical claim about people who have not yet revealed any preferences? And is there one theory of welfare under which all five features hang together, or is the practice an alliance of several theories that would come apart under pressure?

Finally: I am being asked to evaluate three proposed amendments — measure losses by willingness to accept and gains by willingness to pay; divide every willingness-to-pay figure by the payer's income, so that money buys no extra weight; or abandon money as the unit and rank rules by their expected effects on self-reported well-being instead. For each, tell me whether it is a repair internal to the existing view or a rival to it, and what would have to change elsewhere in the practice if it were adopted. I would rather have the argument than a reading list.
