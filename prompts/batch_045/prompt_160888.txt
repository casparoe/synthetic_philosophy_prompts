I work as a software engineer, with no formal training in philosophy, and an argument at work about our benefits policy has turned into an argument about something more general: when someone's life goes badly, what do the rest of us owe them, if anything? Below are my own verdicts on a set of cases, and then my attempt at a principle. I would like the principle made precise, tested against the cases, and repaired or replaced, plus its verdicts on cases I have not listed. Please do not give me reading suggestions or citations; I am after the principle, not the literature, and I have no way to look anything up.

My verdicts, given on instinct and in the order I thought of them:

1. Born with a condition requiring lifelong medication, cannot work full time. Yes, the costs should be covered, and this feels clear.
2. The same condition acquired at forty-five after smoking since fifteen, in a household where both parents smoked and advertising was targeted at the neighbourhood. Yes, covered. But it feels different from case 1 in a way I cannot articulate, and I cannot explain why that difference does not change the verdict.
3. Lost their savings gambling, now cannot pay rent. No compensation for the loss. This also feels clear, though I would still not let the person starve in the street.
4. Took a below-market salary plus equity at a startup, understood the odds, equity ended worthless, no savings at fifty. Yes, help, at least partly. I know this is a voluntary gamble exactly like case 3 and I cannot say what separates them; the startup case feels like a shared risk and the casino case does not.
5. House destroyed in a flood whose likelihood had tripled because of emissions produced by other people; insurance either unavailable or priced out of reach. Yes, help.
6. Same flood, but cheap insurance was widely available and the person declined it after repeated explicit warnings. Much weaker claim than case 5, and weaker than case 3 in principle, yet I am far more willing to make an exception here than in the casino case, which makes no sense to me.
7. An engineer with native talent, working no harder than her peers, earning five times as much. No, do not take the surplus away. But the argument that covered case 1 seems to require taking it: both are brute facts about how someone was built.
8. Someone who deliberately cultivates an expensive taste over years, so that they now need something costly in order to be content at all. No compensation, and the case feels easy.
9. A child whose parents spent everything on a failed scheme. Help the child; the parents, no.
10. A drug prescribed in the late nineties, later shown to cause a chronic illness; the patient chose to take it on the information available at the time. Yes, compensate. This was a chosen gamble that lost, which by my own lights in case 3 should mean no compensation, but I am unmoved by that.

My tentative principle: a bad outcome is compensable just when the risk that produced it was bundled into an option the person could not have declined without losing access to something they were entitled to pursue, or when the magnitude of the risk was not reasonably discoverable at the time of choice; and separately from all of this, anyone below a floor gets rescued however they got there, with the floor doing humanitarian work rather than compensatory work.

What I would like from you:

State a principle, or a small set of rival principles, precisely enough that I could implement it as a function over a case description: what fields the input needs, what it returns, and where it returns something like undefined because the case description does not supply enough information. Tell me which clause is motivated by which case. Tell me honestly which of my verdicts each candidate overturns, and in particular which of cases 3 and 4 has to go, and whether case 7 can be saved alongside case 1 at the cost of complexity or only by giving one of them up. Say whether my rescue-versus-compensation split is doing real work or merely hiding the hard question behind a floor. Say whether my bundled-versus-separable cut is genuinely different from the familiar distinction between what people chose and what merely happened to them, or just a redescription of it in engineering vocabulary.

Then give the verdicts of your favoured candidate on cases I have not listed: a risk that was discoverable in principle but only with expertise the person could not reasonably have acquired, such as a contract whose terms conceal the exposure; a choice that later turns out to have been heavily shaped by a genetic predisposition the person did not know they had; a risk bundled into the only available route to a basic good, such as the only job within reach requiring a dangerous commute; a gamble that fails only because of how other people independently acted, as when a competitor launches first; a case where compensating would predictably change future behaviour; and a risk shared identically by everyone in a group, where only some lose. Finally, give me one case where the principle you favour returns an answer you think I would find repugnant, since I would rather find that out now than after defending it.
