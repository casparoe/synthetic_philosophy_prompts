Me and my friend got into a massive arguement last night about my aunt, and I realised we were going in circles because we never got clear on what we were even asking of a view. She's 79, moderate dementia, happy most of the time, still lights up for music and her dog, and about six years ago — fully competent, fully informed, no pressure from anyone — she filled out a directive refusing antibiotics, ventilators, feeding tubes, the lot, on the grounds that she'd read that dementia is basically a long humiliation and she wanted none of it. Now she has a urinary infection that three days of antibiotics would clear up, and my friend says that directive is obviously binding and I say obviously not, and we spent two hours swapping between she decided and she's fine now, with my friend treating she decided as if that settled not just whose call it is but how strong the reason is. So I want to do this backwards.

Before I look at any of the standard positions again, I want the specification: what does a theory of precedent autonomy — of whatever authority an earlier competent self has over a later incompetent one — actually have to deliver to count as adequate? Fix the purpose as: something a hospital ethics committee or a family at a bedside could use in real time with imperfect information, not something that settles the metaphysics of personal identity. Give me the requirements list with the case or consideration behind each one, and label which are non-negotiable and which are really just the Preferences of one camp or another. The cases I want it to earn its keep on: my aunt (contented patient, clear directive, mild reversible illness); the standard dementia case where the directive and the clinical picture line up and nobody argues; the easy cases where the earlier self obviously speaks — knocked out for surgery, delirious for two days, back to yourself on Thursday; the case where the later self is competent again and repudiates the whole value system the directive came out of (religious conversion, gender transition, someone who becomes a different kind of person politically), so both selves are competent and informed and the only thing that changed is the person; the Ulysses-ish case where the entire point is to be bound against your own predictable future weakness; the case of a 24-year-old writing binding instructions about the body of an 84-year-old she has never met; and the asymmetry I noticed mid-argument, that a directive refusing a treatment gets honoured much more readily than one demanding a treatment everyone now regards as futile — I can't tell whether that asymmetry is a datum a theory has to explain or just an institutional accident. It also has to say something about the epistemic situation: the directive is vague (no heroic measures), it conflicts with what the daughter says her mother would want now, and the patient is in the middle zone where she can still voice preferences but inconsistently.

Three candidate requirements of my own that I want you to either promote or throw out. One: my friend invented a clause during the arguement — if I later revoke this, dont believe me, I'll only be doing it because I've become the kind of person who's afraid of dying — and I think a theory has to handle directives that anticipate and discount their own later revocation, which feels different from the ordinary Ulysses setup. Two: must a theory distinguish a later self who repudiates the earlier conclusion from one who repudiates the earlier grounds — someone who now thinks that whole long-humiliation picture was just bad newspaper reporting about dementia, versus someone who still believes every word of it and simply doesn't care any more? Is tracking continuity of grounds rather than continuity of content a genuine desideratum, or am I overcomplicating things? Three: is it legitimate to demand that an account not presuppose an answer to the general question of what makes someone the same person over time, or is that just the reductionists rigging the game? And the part I most want: where the list turns on itself. Which pair of requirements do you suspect can't be jointly satisfied — my guess is something like a competent person's say-so about her own body binds, full stop, against what we owe the person in the bed tracks her interests now — and which single requirement, if you insist on it, eliminates the largest number of the usual positions. Then briefly, a paragraph is plenty, how the main families do against your list: the strict earlier-self-speaks view, the current-best-interests view, the would-the-later-self-endorse-it view, and any graded psychological-continuity view you like. Please skip reading lists and paper titles, I can find those myself.
