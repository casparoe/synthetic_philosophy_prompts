I've been listening to a finance/econ podcast, and one episode was on "why smart people shouldn't trade." It summarized something called the no-trade theorem roughly like this:

"Milgrom and Stokey proved mathematically that in an efficient market no trade can ever happen. The intuition is simple. If I'm willing to sell you a stock at price p, that must be because I think it's worth less than p; if you're willing to buy, it's because you think it's worth more than p. We can't both be right. And since both of us are rational and looking at the same information, we should arrive at the same estimate of what the thing is worth — that's basically Aumann's agreeing-to-disagree result, that rational people with the same information can't disagree. So the only thing that could ever make people trade is one of them having private information the other lacks. But then the other one should reason: the very fact that you're offering to trade tells me you know something, so I'd be taking the losing side. So nobody trades. The upshot is that real-world trading volume is either irrational noise or people with liquidity needs, and it is mathematically impossible to consistently make money by trading on information once prices are public."

Later in the episode the host applies it to two cases: (1) a volume spike right after an earnings announcement — "most of that is people being irrational, since the news is public by then"; and (2) a bet between friends about who wins a game — "if you disagree about the outcome and you've both watched the same season, one of you is being irrational, which is why friendly bets are sucker bets."

Someone in the comments also said the theorem shows that when a used-car buyer and seller agree on a price it must be because one of them has private information, and that it doesn't matter if the seller has genuinely never heard of the kind of defect the car has, because "not knowing about a possibility is just assigning it a very low probability."

And I've seen the result described elsewhere as the formal version of "there's no such thing as a free lunch," and as the thing that makes the efficient-markets hypothesis work.

Something here definately feels off to me. I keep thinking the actual assumption is stronger than "rational and have the same information" — something about priors? — and I dont see how "no mutually profitable speculative trade" turns into "volume is noise" or "you can't make money from information." But I've never read the paper and I don't know the literature.

Can you go through the summary more or less bit by bit and tell me what someone who actually works on this would accept, what they'd wince at, and what is just wrong? Specifically: (a) is the "both rational, same information, so we'd agree" step the real assumption of the theorem, or is it a different result that's only in the same family? (b) how much stronger is the real assumption than plain rationality — is it doing all the work, and should it be read as a claim about rationality at all, or as something else in disguise? (c) does the conclusion really rule out profiting from information, or only something narrower — and where does trade driven by risk-sharing or hedging rather than by disagreement fit? (d) do the host's two applications survive once the summary is corrected? (e) is that comment about the used car right, or does unawareness break the model in some way? And finally: given that the assumptions are pretty idealized, what is the theorem actually taken to show — is it a claim about real markets at all, and does it end up supporting or undercutting the efficient-markets story?
