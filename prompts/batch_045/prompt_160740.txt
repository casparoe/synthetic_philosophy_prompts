I'm in a small reading group that has been working through Kierkegaard's polemic against "the press" and "the public," and what keeps striking me is how precisely his diagnosis describes the *machinery* of a newspaper — or, in our own day, a comment section — rather than any actual crowd. The claims, as I've been summarizing them for myself, go roughly like this: the public is not a people, a community, a congregation, or even a gathering, but an abstraction that the press brings into being and that exists nowhere else, since a hundred thousand readers alone in their own rooms are more fully "public" than the same number packed into a square; the public has no situation and no actuality, and precisely for that reason it is never answerable for what it thinks; the press levels everyone not by decree but by abstraction, so that no one is anybody in particular and no one need be; everything it touches is turned into chatter, and chatter is forgotten by tomorrow; and the only thing that can counter it is the single individual — the person who is somebody in particular, who holds an opinion in his own life and can therefore be held to it. We also spent one whole evening on his corollary that you cannot simply *announce* the truth to people who are already inside the public, but have to reach them indirectly, which is why he published under invented names and insisted the books belonged to those names rather than to him.

For our last session I want to hand out a piece of satire built out of that material. What I'd like is the founding prospectus of a new daily newspaper — the advertisement a paper prints to announce itself — written in the puffed-up register of an 1840s periodical prospectus (numbered sections, ornamental subheads, testimonials, a subscription form at the end), about 700 words, and entirely straight-faced: the paper should sell exactly what Kierkegaard says the press produces, presenting each feature above as a benefit to the subscriber. So it should promise relief from being anybody in particular; guarantee that no reader will ever need to be in an actual situation in order to hold an opinion about one; boast that nothing printed in it will be remembered the following day, and that this is a feature, since nothing that is forgotten can be answered for; flatter the reader's resentment of anyone who still is somebody in particular; and offer, as a premium tier available to literally one subscriber at a time, "The Individual" — unshareable, impossible to hold by proxy, and requiring the subscriber actually to live what he reads, since the paper cannot perform that part for him. It should also advertise its own anonymity, its refusal to be answered, and its plan to publish no events at all but only what everyone is saying about them. The humor has to come from getting the diagnosis right rather than from inventing vices the press doesn't have in this account: leveling should work the way he says it works — by abstraction, by people mutually cancelling into a something that is nothing, which is nevertheless everywhere — and not by censorship, propaganda, or a tyrant; and the paper's cheerful indifference to whether what it prints is true should be traceable to the claim that truth is a matter of how one exists, and so cannot be possessed by a crowd. The subscription form should do its share of the work, with a space for the subscriber's name that the prospectus politely discourages him from filling in.

Keep it deadpan throughout — no winking at the reader, no modern asides, no stepping out of the prospectus's voice — but legible enough that someone who has never heard of Kierkegaard can feel that what is being sold is the subscriber's own disappearance as a person. Please don't put any verbatim quotation from Kierkegaard or anyone else in it; paraphrase, in the paper's own voice, the claims it is trading on. And if you can manage it without breaking character, let the thing implicate itself at the close: the machine that manufactures the public is, after all, a piece of printed paper read alone in a room, and so is this one — addressed, as it must be, to a public that does not yet exist and that its own publication is about to bring into being.
