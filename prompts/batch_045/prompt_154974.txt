I am a PhD student in philosophy, and I am putting together the comparative section of a seminar paper on whether any major non-Western tradition is usefully classifiable as a form of consequentialism. My supervisor suggested Buddhist ethics as the test case, and a friend sent me the relevant chapter of a popular introduction that she likes. It reads as follows:

"Buddhist ethics is straightforwardly consequentialist. The Buddha's own criterion for an action was whether it reduces suffering, and karma is simply the mechanism that tracks an action's consequences for the agent: acts that make things better generate merit, acts that make things worse generate demerit. Because what matters is the relief of suffering rather than conformity to rules, the precepts against killing, taking what is not given, and false speech are useful rules of thumb rather than prohibitions, and the Mahāyāna skillful-means literature draws the conclusion explicitly. In the best-known example, a ship's captain discovers that a passenger intends to murder everyone on board, and so kills him, accepting the karmic cost and even a rebirth in hell, because the alternative is far worse. A Buddhist is therefore committed to exactly the trade-off that consequentialists are committed to: one life may be sacrificed for many, and the precept against killing has to give way. The doctrine of no-self reinforces this. If persons are not ultimately real, there is no deep difference between my suffering and yours, so the only thing that can matter is the total amount of suffering in the world."

I can feel the pull of this, but it also seems too fast, and I would like to know where it goes wrong before I build an argument on top of it. Two things in particular bother me. First, everything I have read about karma stresses that it is intention — cetanā — that makes an action karmically significant rather than its outcome; but if that is right, the captain story seems to cut against the chapter, since he knowingly takes on demerit for the sake of a better outcome. Second, I am unsure how "suffering" is being used: the chapter reads dukkha as roughly pain or distress, whereas in the passages I have looked at it seems to cover the unsatisfactoriness of everything conditioned, and the aim is not a lower total but the cessation of it.

I also tried to test the reading with a case of my own: someone tells a lie that prevents a killing, but does so partly from compassion for the intended victim and partly from resentment toward the killer. On the chapter's account this looks like an easy case of right action; on an intention-based account it looks mixed in a way the chapter has no vocabulary for. Is that a fair test, or am I rigging it?

Could you work through the passage claim by claim — what a specialist would grant, what is a defensible simplification for an introductory chapter, and what is simply mistaken? Specifically, I would like to know whether the no-self point actually supports aggregation across persons, since I have heard both that the reductionist view of persons pushes toward something like impersonalism (which would help the chapter) and that Buddhist thought has no single subject of welfare across which anything could be aggregated; and whether the alternative to the consequentialist reading really is a virtue-ethics reading, or whether that is a false dichotomy. Where the scholarship is genuinely unsettled, please say so rather than giving me a tidy verdict — I need to know which parts of this I can safely lean on in the paper.
