Hi — I manage the eligibility desk for our county rental assistance program. Me and two other people decide what our own program rules mean, several times a week, and whatever we say is what the front desk does until somebody higher up tells us otherwise. None of us are lawyers. County counsel is part time, she's in the building maybe twice a month and she reads what we've already issued, after the fact. The state auditor reads our denials once a year. So what I need is something we can actually run in about twenty minutes, on paper, that ends in one of three places — apply reading A, apply reading B, or stop and send it to counsel — and that tells us which questions to ask in what order, what counts as an actual answer to each one, and where it's really just handing the question to a person because it's a values call dressed up as an interpretation.

The part I can't get right is the old text-versus-purpose thing. I know the slogan version — if the words are clear, stop; if they aren't, ask what the rule is for — and it doesn't help, because every fight we have is one level below that. Here are the shapes they actually take:

- Our rulebook opens with a "purpose and intent" section, but it was written for a different, older program, and after the 2015 renumbering most of the provisions it points at don't exist anymore. It still says things like the program exists to keep county families housed. How much weight does a purpose statement get when it's visably orphaned from the text it was written for?
- One of our rules was lifted almost word for word out of a model code, and the model code had two carve-outs that our board didn't adopt. Does deleting them mean something, or did nobody think about it?
- The intake software has implemented one reading of a rule for years, nobody still here knows who chose it, and flipping it would mean reopening a few hundred closed cases. Is that a fact the procedure can use, or is it inertia with a nicer name?
- The two purposes in our purpose section pull against each other in most real cases: get money out the door fast, versus make sure it goes to the people it's for. "Look at the purpose" isn't an instruction when there are two.
- And the unit question. Does the text/purpose test get applied to a single word, to a whole sentence, or to the rule as a chunk? With "resided" or "household" it definately seems to matter which you pick.

Two more things I want an honest answer about. One: our denials get audited and our approvals get audited, but they cost us different things — a wrong approval is an improper payment we may have to eat, a wrong denial is a family that may lose housing while the appeal grinds for four months. I suspect that asymmetry isn't allowed to be part of interpretation at all, but I want the procedure to tell me that rather than quietly deciding things for me. Two: we have an internal convention that we won't read a rule in a way that makes things worse for a protected class without counsel signing off. Is that a tie-breaker inside the procedure, or a veto outside it?

Some constraints, so the procedure isn't fantasy: we can't call the drafters (gone, term-limited, or dead) and the board minutes are thin, so there's no real legislative history to go dig up; our rules get reauthorized every two years, so arguments about a text freezing in 1970 while the world moved on are weaker here than in the stories people tell — say whether the procedure should even ask about drift. And it has to be runnable by someone whose training is a two-week onboarding.

Then please run the whole thing, start to finish, on three live headaches:

1. The income rule says count income "received during the month." A client gets a retroactive Social Security back payment covering fourteen months, arriving as one check this month. Count all of it or one fourteenth of it? The software has exactly one field.
2. "Any person who shares the dwelling and contributes to household expenses" counts as a household member. A client's live-in caregiver is paid through a state waiver program, and part of the pay is room and board. Caregiver in or out — and does the answer change depending on whether it helps or hurts the client?
3. To qualify you must have "resided in the county for twelve consecutive months immediately prior to application." A woman leaving a domestic violence situation spent six weeks in a confidential shelter whose registered address is in the next county over, then came back. On paper her twelve months has a hole in it.

I have a feeling the procedure is going to tell me to deny number 3 and I am going to hate that, so if that's what it says I want it said plainly, along with what I'm actually supposed to do instead of quietly fudging it.

And tell me what the procedure can't do: where it will misfire, which distinctions it drops and whether dropping them costs us something real, and which steps are judgment calls we've all agreed to call procedure.
