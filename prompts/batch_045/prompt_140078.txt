I'm running a 90-minute reading group session on conditionals next month for maybe eight grad students — they've all had intro semantics, a couple are syntax people who've read some Kratzer-flavoured stuff on modals, nobody has actually read the conditionals literature. I want to hand them a one-page flow chart at the start that sorts them into a position by what they say about cases, rather than a list of isms I read out. Can you build me the tree?

Rough spec:

Start from something everyone in the room will grant. I was going to enter at "some conditionals with false antecedents are plainly true and others plainly aren't, so this can't just be a truth function" — tell me if that's the wrong door and why.

Then six or seven branch nodes, no more. Each one is a concrete case plus a question with two or three answers, phrased so somebody who has never heard of a selection function or an ordering source can answer it. For each node give me a paragraph on why that question goes there and why not the more obvious question someone would have put at that spot — I want to be able to defend the chart when someone challenges a node in the room.

The cases I want doing the work (invent fresh props; the textbook ones are banned — the Oswald/Kennedy thing, the Bizet-and-Verdi thing, "if kangaroos had no tails they'd topple over"):

- an indicative conditional whose antecedent the speaker already knows the answer to: the doctor who has already read the scan saying to the patient "if the scan shows a tumour, we'll need to biopsy it". I think this is the node that decides whether your context set is allowed to contain stuff the speaker knows is false, but you tell me whether it's really doing that work or whether it just looks like it is.
- a pair where an indicative and a counterfactual with the same content come apart: one where the indicative sounds fine and the counterfactual bad, and one the other way round. At least three nodes total should be carrying the indicative/counterfactual contrast specifically, since that contrast is the whole reason I'm running the session.
- a Sobel-style (or reverse-Sobel-style) sequence rigged so that what similarity would keep fixed and what the conversation is treating as up for grabs come apart — a hiring committee or a council vote, where tacking a clause onto the antecedent flips the verdict even though nobody would say the extra clause makes the world less similar. Use that to test whether the ordering source is allowed to be supplied by what's under discussion rather than by similarity, but don't make the node just ask "is similarity context-dependent?", because everyone says yes to that and it diagnoses nothing.
- a might/would asymmetry under indeterminism: the coin wasn't flipped; "if it had been flipped it might have landed heads and it might have landed tails" seems fine, "it would have landed heads" seems bad. I want that to be the node that seperates views with a unique closest world from views without one, if it really does.
- something embedded: a conditional under "the probability that...", or under a quantifier, or with an epistemic modal in the consequent. Pick whichever you think is most diagnostic, and tell me honestly whether that node separates positions or just separates people who've heard of the triviality results from people who haven't.

Leaves: I need about ten. The ones I care about having on the page, in whatever non-specialist words you'd use: the strict-conditional/necessity view; the variably-strict similarity view in both its flavours (unique closest world vs. ties or no closest world); an ordering source supplied by context or premisses rather than by similarity; "indicatives and counterfactuals have the same semantics and the difference is epistemic or pragmatic"; "counterfactuals are semantically their own thing"; and a non-truth-conditional suppositional leaf. Add others if you think they belong, but cap it at ten or eleven and give each leaf one line on its most awkward commitment.

Two things that matter to me more than the leaves themselves.

Build it from the space, not from the names. Give me the questions first and the combinations of answers they actually allow, then drop the real views into the cells, and for every cell nobody is standing in say which it is: incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unoccupied because nobody wrote the paper. I especially want your read on whether anyone can hold both "the probability of a conditional is the conditional probability" and "conditionals have truth conditions that vary with similarity", and on whether the "no semantic difference and no pragmatic story either" cell is really empty or just empty-looking.

And be honest about where the tree fails. Flag any pair of leaves your nodes can't pull apart — I suspect the two variably-strict flavours and the context-supplied-ordering view collapse into each other somewhere near the Sobel node, and I'd much rather see that flagged than see you invent a node that "fixes" it. Also give me three or four control cases where every mainstream leaf gives the same verdict, so people can check whether they understood a node, and tell me what I should say to somebody whose answers land them in no cell at all: is that a genuine position or did they just misread a case?

It's a one-page handout, so the chart itself has to be ascii or a bullet tree. And don't tack a reading list on the end — there won't be time for it.
