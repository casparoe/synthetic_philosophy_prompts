For rather more than thirty years I worked in satellite remote sensing of the atmosphere — microwave sounding, retrieval theory, and the inverse problems that go with them — and since retiring I have been sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group at the university, generally in the role of the person who supplies the examples from physics. The group is convened by Dr. Miriam Hallam, a retired philosopher of science. Over six weeks on the underdetermination of theory by evidence she has ruled, case by case, on whether the evidence of the day underdetermined the theories in question. Her verdicts so far:

— The wave and corpuscular theories of light, before the mid-nineteenth-century measurements of the speed of light in denser media: underdetermined. Two live research programmes, each able to accommodate the optical phenomena then in hand, and it was a new measurement rather than a re-reading of old data that broke the tie.
— The caloric theory against the early mechanical theory of heat, before the measurements of the mechanical equivalent: underdetermined. She treats Rumford's cannon-boring experiments and Davy's ice-rubbing experiments as suggestive but not decisive on their own.
— The island-universe hypothesis against the account of the spiral nebulae as nearby nebulosity inside our own Galaxy, before Cepheid variables in Andromeda returned a distance: underdetermined.
— Wegener's drifting continents against the permanentist orthodoxy of the 1920s: not underdetermined. On her reading, the geodetic and geophysical data available then were positively adverse to the rates of motion his account required, so this was not a tie that a later observation merely happened to break; and the theory that eventually carried the day had not yet been formulated.
— Kelvin's estimate of the age of the Earth against the far longer timescales the geologists were asking for: not underdetermined. She holds that the thermal evidence of the period did not tie but came out on Kelvin's side; the error lay in an auxiliary assumption — that the Earth has no internal source of heat — and she expressly does not count "one can always rescue a claim by adjusting an auxiliary" as underdetermination.

Next week it is my turn to present a case, and I should like to know what she will say about it before I put it to her. It comes from my own former field, and I will describe it as I mean to describe it to her.

In the early 1990s two groups independently produced records of upper-tropospheric humidity over the tropical oceans from the same satellite microwave radiances. One record shows moistening across 1987–1993, the other shows drying; the difference matters because it bears on the strength of water-vapour feedback. Both records fit the radiances to within their stated noise. Both groups used the same radiative transfer forward model and the same spectroscopic parameters, and — this is the feature I want to stress — they would have agreed in advance about what any specified state of the atmosphere would radiate, and about what any instrument, flown or unflown, would register given such a state. Their disagreement is entirely about which atmosphere actually obtained, and it traces to the constraint each group imposed when inverting: one regularized toward a moist adiabat, the other toward a radiosonde climatology. No observation available in 1993 would have separated them; an instrument with a different weighting function, flown later, did. At the time, though, both groups took the disagreement to be a genuine factual one about the tropical atmosphere and published it as such.

I genuinely cannot tell whether my professional instinct — that this is an ill-posed inverse problem rather than a rivalry of theories — has anything to do with the distinction she has been drawing, and I would rather find that out from you than in front of her. I have no training in philosophy, so state the rules in plain terms rather than in the vocabulary of the literature; if a rule has a settled name, give the name in a clause, but do not lean on it.

Please answer in exactly five numbered points, each of one or two sentences, with nothing before the first point and nothing after the last. Points one to three should set out the three rules her record is most compatible with, in the order of how well you think each fits it, each with what it predicts about my case. Point four should give your best guess at her verdict, the reason for preferring it, and which of her five earlier verdicts is the closest precedent — saying plainly if that precedent is in fact a poor one and why. Point five should give one further case, real or constructed, that would separate whichever rules your guess in point four leaves standing.
