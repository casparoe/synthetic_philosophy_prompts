I'm finishing a chapter on the formal structure of the betterness relation and would like an audit of the position I've ended up in before I commit to it in print. Where I've landed:

(1) Betterness is asymmetric and transitive; equally good is an equivalence relation; and the two compose, so that if A is better than B and B is equally good as C, then A is better than C. I treat the whole package as a non-negotiable constraint on any acceptable axiology.

(2) Micro-indifference: if A and B differ only in that one person is slightly better off in B, by an amount small enough that she could not notice the difference, then A and B are equally good. The thought behind it is that a change no one can discern, and which is bad for no one, should move the outcome in neither direction.

(3) No thresholds in aggregation: for any positive improvement to a single person's welfare, however small, there is some finite number of people such that giving that many people that improvement makes the outcome better. Small gains cannot be permanently inert.

(4) Endpoints can differ across a spectrum: an outcome in which everyone lives in severe pain is worse than one in which everyone leads an ordinary decent life, and the two can be linked by a finite chain of outcomes each of which differs from its predecessor only by an imperceptible change in the welfare of one person.

(5) Some comparisons are indeterminate: for certain pairs there is no fact of the matter, neither better nor worse nor equally good, the items are on a par. Parity is not transitive: A on a par with B and B on a par with C while A is better than C is meant to be coherent. The indeterminacy is metaphysical rather than epistemic, but I don't think it threatens realism, since whether A is better than B is not constituted by what anyone would choose, judge, or be disposed to say.

(6) Methodologically, my warrant for transitivity is pragmatic: an agent who accepted a cycle in betterness could be exploited, and a theory of value whose adherents are systematically exploitable is defective. I also want the theory to guide action; it cannot simply fall silent when an agent must choose between two options.

Please tell me where this set is in trouble, ordered by severity, and for each problem say what specifically has to give and what the cheapest repair would cost me. I am unsure which commitment is actually doing the damage, since (2) on its own only ever compares neighbouring outcomes and says nothing about chains; I'd like that settled rather than assumed. I also want to know whether the non-transitive parity in (5) is a resource for repairing the rest or an additional liability, whether (5) and (6) can both survive, and whether repairing one problem makes another worse. Finally, flag any pair here that only looks like a tension but dissolves once it is stated precisely.
