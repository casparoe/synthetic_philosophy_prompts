Last night I had a long argument with an old friend who works on large language models, and I have agreed to do something rash about it: a seventy-five-minute guest session for his lab group, whiteboard only, no readings assigned. His position, stated with some confidence, was that the poverty-of-the-stimulus argument died the moment systems trained on raw text with a generic architecture and no hand-coded grammar started producing structure-dependent generalizations. If more comes out than anyone put in, he says, there is no gap to explain, only a stale claim about mid-century linguistics. I said the argument was never about the volume of the data but about the kind of evidence available to a learner, and that a system's success shows at most that some bias or other was sufficient, never that no substantive bias was needed. We went in circles, partly because my vocabulary and his kept sliding past each other.

So please plan the session for me rather than giving me the argument. The audience is about fifteen graduate students and engineers who are fluent in transformers, training objectives and scaling behaviour, and who have had no linguistics and no philosophy. My friend will be in the room and has been promised a fair hearing, so the plan has to give his objection its real weight somewhere in the hour instead of steamrolling it.

What I want from you:

First, the sequence. I want to hold three claims apart from the start: the gap thesis, that what learners reliably end up able to do outruns what their evidence determines; the origin thesis, that the difference is made up by something the organism brings to the data rather than by the data; and the domain thesis, that what is brought is rich and peculiarly linguistic rather than generic learning machinery. My aim is that by the end they can say which of the three a language-model result actually touches. Relatedly: I am tempted to withhold the word innate until they have the gap thesis in their own terms, making them work first with something like an inductive bias not acquired by the learner's own learning procedure from the data it sees. Tell me whether that is the right order, where the translation should happen, and what the nativist is claiming over and above the merely architectural sense of bias.

Second, the carrying example. The two I would have reached for are the case where children form questions by fronting the auxiliary of the main clause rather than the first auxiliary in the string, and the case where contraction across a silent position is blocked, as with want to. Choose one or propose something better, and say what such an example must have: a missing piece of evidence that no further quantity of the same kind of data supplies. And what it must lack: dependence on contested claims about what children actually say, and any need for syntactic formalism on the board.

Third, the misconception map, in the order these will surface and with the point in the sequence where each is headed off. My own guesses, which I would like you to correct and add to: that more data just is more evidence; that if a network learns a pattern from a corpus then the corpus was sufficient; that innate means present at birth, hard-coded or unlearned, so that anything learned from data is thereby not innate; that the argument is a gaps argument that shrinks with every improvement in models; that it is the no-free-lunch result in philosophical costume; and that children's input is not in fact poor but statistically rich.

Fourth, where in the hour I should concede the most that the machine-learning results genuinely establish, and how to stage that concession so it does not collapse everything built before it.

Finally, one question I can put to them at the end that would show the idea had landed rather than that they had learned a slogan, ideally one that cannot be answered by saying that more data would fix it; and the things I should name out loud as deliberately left out at this level.
