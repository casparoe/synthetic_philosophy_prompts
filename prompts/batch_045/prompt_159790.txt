hey — I'm in a seminar this term on memory and public space (something like PHIL 340, "Monuments, Memory, and the Public") and the final lets us do either a normal paper or one "creative option." I want to do the creative option, but I really don't want it to be another lazy both-sides joke about "cancel culture vs. history" — our prof would definately tear that apart, and honestly so would I.

So: could you write me a piece of satire in the form of the minutes (plus attached resolutions, maybe a short appendix) of a university committee — call it the Standing Committee on Commemorative Coherence — that has been asked what to do about a donor's statue on campus. Deadpan bureaucratic voice, 800ish words, numbered minutes, no winking, no ironic scare-quotes inside the document itself. The comedy has to come from the committee being completely sincere and procedural while reasoning itself into conclusions that are obviously monstrous.

The philosophy it needs to actually get right, so that someone who works on commemoration ethics reads it and thinks "that's fair," not "that's a strawman":

- the split between backward-looking reasons for honoring (gratitude, fidelity, promises made to or about the dead) and forward-looking ones (what the monument expresses and does now, to the people who have to walk past it), and the committee blithely putting both on one decision-theoretic scale;
- the expressive point that a statue isn't just a record — it's the university speaking in the present tense, so leaving it up is an act rather than a mere omission, which is what makes the "we couldn't have known better" defense unstable;
- that defense taken seriously and then pushed somewhere bad (if ignorance excuses the original honoring, does it also excuse the original deed? does it excuse us toward whoever we wrong now?);
- the asymmetry people cite between tearing down a statue and simply never having erected one;
- contextualization as a third option — plaques, counter-monuments, re-siting — and why it tends to satisfy nobody, since it keeps the honoring in place while claiming to suspend it;
- who has standing to decide: the university, the honoree's descendants, the descendants of the people he harmed, current students — and how asking that question ends up biting the university, since its own founding money is entangled with him.

Two bits of apparatus I invented for this, please use them and play them straight:

- honoring amortization: the honoring-force of a monument is a stock that depreciates across generations, so removal is just writing off a fully depreciated asset, and the committee can compute a "residual honoring value" and put it in a spreadsheet next to the maintenance budget;
- honoring leakage: honoring spills out of the statue into everything around it — the square, the street name, the endowed chair, the scholarship — so no partial fix ever fully contains it, and every fix has to be bigger than the last one.

The reductio I'm after: by the end the committee should propose something a normal person would recoil from but that follows cleanly from its own premises — e.g. a mandatory sunset clause on every honor on campus (statues, named buildings, endowed chairs all expire after N years unless renewed by whoever has standing by then), or deliberately perishable "provisional plinths," or pre-committing now to dishonoring ourselves on behalf of a future we can't predict. Pick whichever you think is both funniest and most rigorous. It should still be readable by someone who hasn't read any of the literature.
