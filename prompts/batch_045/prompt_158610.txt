Here's the question I set:

"Galen Strawson argues that moral responsibility is impossible, via what he calls the Basic Argument. (a) Set out the premises of the argument and say precisely which kind of responsibility it is meant to rule out. (b) Does the argument depend on the truth of determinism? Explain why or why not, including how Strawson deals with the indeterminist alternative. (c) Give the strongest objection you know of to one of the premises, and assess how well Strawson can answer it."

And here's the answer, transcribed more or less as written:

The Basic Argument has three steps. First: what you do, in any situation, you do because of the way you are — your actions flow from your character, values and motives, none of which you chose. Second: to be ultimately morally responsible for what you do, you have to be ultimately responsible for the way you are, at least in the crucial mental respects. Third: you can't be, because being responsible for the way you are would require you to have brought yourself into being that way — you'd have to be, in the old phrase, causa sui — and that's impossible. Every act of self-creation would have to be performed by someone who already had a character, so you'd already have to be responsible for that character. Strawson adds that he doesn't even need the regress: the first act of self-creation is already impossible, because it would have to issue from a nature you hadn't yet given yourself.

The responsibility he's targeting is responsibility in the strong, desert-entailing sense — the kind that would make blame warranted just because someone did something wrong, not merely for forward-looking reasons like deterrence or reform. That's the notion retributivism needs.

(b) No. Strawson is agnostic about determinism and the argument is built to run either way. If determinism is true, everything traces back to factors outside your control. But indeterminism doesn't help either: if your decision wasn't determined by the way you were, then nothing caused it and it merely happened to you, which is luck rather than agency. So the argument is neutral on the determinism question.

(c) The strongest objection is Frankfurt's. On his hierarchical account, you're responsible for an action when the desire that moves you to it is one you identify with — first-order desire meshes with higher-order volition — and that doesn't require self-creation at all. Strawson's reply is that whether you identify with your desires is itself a function of how you already are, so the mesh is just one more thing that happens to you and can't give you ultimacy. I think this reply is decisive: any account that grounds responsibility in facts about the agent's psychology inherits the problem, and Frankfurt's does.

Still, the Basic Argument is less damaging than it sounds. Strawson's own position is a compatibilism of the reactive attitudes: the practice of holding one another responsible is vindicated by the role it plays in our interpersonal relationships, and the argument only shows that an inflated metaphysical gloss on that practice — desert, retribution — is unavailable. So ordinary responsibility ascriptions are fine; what the argument kills is a philosophical theory of them, not the practice itself.
