I'm a senior at a large public high school, and I write the humor column that runs on the back page of our student paper. For the issue that comes out just before winter break, I want to run a piece that satirizes something I've been reading about on my own: Nietzsche's thought experiment of the eternal recurrence — the version in which a demon tells you that this life, down to its smallest detail, will repeat forever, and you are asked whether you could affirm that.

What I have in mind is a satirical memo from my school district announcing a new mandatory "Recurrence Readiness" pilot, written in the deadpan, mildly thrilled register our administration actually uses in its emails to parents: a short announcement, a bulleted list of program components, an FAQ, and a sign-up link that does not work. Our faculty advisor is strict about length, so please keep it to roughly 700–800 words.

The target is the self-help version of the idea — the "live so that you would will this life again" life-audit — and the way it converts a demand for greatness into an optimization problem. Two wrinkles I want built into the fiction, because they seem to me to be where the thought experiment is least examined:

- Memory. Everyone I ask imagines the loop as starting over with amnesia, the way it does in films. But if you do not remember the previous go-rounds, then it is not your recurrence you are affirming, only a copy's; and if you do remember, what you are being asked to affirm is eternal boredom rather than your life. I would like the district to have settled this by fiat — full recall, for the sake of "audit integrity" — and to be entirely oblivious to what that ruling does to its own program.
- Other people. The loop is supposed to contain exactly this life, which includes everyone else in it. Willing my recurrence means willing my sister's worst year, and my grandmother's last one, an infinite number of times. I want the memo to process this as an administrative inconvenience: a consent form, a parental-signature line, an opt-out that no one explains. The joke should land without any character stating outright that the "test" is really a question about what you are permitted to will on behalf of other people.

The reductio I am after is this: because students are scored on recurrence-readiness, the rational strategy is to make one's life as small, safe, and repeatable as possible, so that the highest score in the school goes to the flattest possible Tuesday — the opposite of the greatness the idea was supposed to demand. Please let that follow from the program's own logic rather than being stated as a moral.

Constraints: keep the voice deadpan and never wink at the reader; a reader should be able to tell what is being ironized without being told. Aim the humor at getting the view right rather than at a caricature of it, so that an adult who has actually read Nietzsche laughs rather than winces. Invent the officials; keep real people out of it. Do not quote Nietzsche, and do not include citations or suggestions for further reading.

After the piece, please add a note of about 200 words saying which reading of recurrence you have built the satire on — the thought-experiment reading or the cosmological one in Thus Spoke Zarathustra, since I gather there is real disagreement about whether the latter is meant as a physical theory at all — where the piece knowingly exaggerates or simplifies, and what a defender of the reading you did not use would say that would make you rewrite it. In that note, please also name the one place where the satire's own reasoning is weakest, so I can decide whether to keep that beat.
