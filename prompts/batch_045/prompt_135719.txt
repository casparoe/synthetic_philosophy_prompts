I'm the student representative on my university's committee on automated assessment, and we have to rule on a proposal next week. I'd like a prediction grounded in our own track record rather than a general discussion of AI supervision.

Our published decisions so far, in short form:

1. Approved: a model that graded short-answer physics problems, after an audit on 200 answers found it agreed with two human markers as often as they agreed with each other; every grade carried the rubric item applied, and disagreements were reviewed.
2. Approved: a code assistant in a research lab, with no measurement of the suggestions themselves — each edit was accepted or rejected line by line by the researcher with a logged reason, and the finished pipeline passed its test suite and replicated.
3. Rejected: model-written feedback on draft essays. Students and instructors, blind, rated the comments as helpful as human comments — but the model could not be made to say which passage any given comment was about, and roughly one comment in twelve invoked a rubric criterion the essay did not address.
4. Rejected: a commercial AI-text detector reporting a 1% false-positive rate on its own benchmark, where the vendor would not disclose training data or let our staff run an adversarial test.
5. Approved with conditions: a tutoring bot confined to hint sequences, with monthly log review, after a term in which its users' exam scores were non-inferior.
6. Rejected: an automated literature screener with 96% recall against human screening, where the audit sample came from the same corpus the tool had been tuned on and it gave no reason for any inclusion.

The new proposal: 'span-locked scoring' for lab reports. For each rubric criterion the system quotes a span from the report and assigns a verdict; a second, weaker model, shown only that span and that criterion — never the report, the other criteria, or the first model's verdict — must reproduce the verdict; items on which the two disagree (about 35%) are routed to a human marker. On the items it scores itself, its grades agree with two human markers as often as they agree with each other. In a test where staff planted twenty reports containing plausible-looking but misleading passages, the checker caught eighteen.

What will we decide? I'm less interested in the verdict than in the rule we are running — something like 'approve when audited outputs match the human baseline on independently drawn samples' versus 'approve when every step is one a competent reviewer could endorse from the artifacts, without redoing the work' — and I suspect both fit decisions 1 through 6, since each rejection can be read either as a missing or contaminated audit or as an unauditable process. Tell me which rule you think we are actually applying, what it predicts here, which precedent is the closest analog, what we would be committing to if we went the other way, and which single piece of further evidence — a re-run, or a variant of the proposal — would best separate the two rules.
