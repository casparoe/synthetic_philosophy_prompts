I am writing a long essay on the proverbial claim that revolutions devour their own children, and I want the French sequence from June 1793 to July 1794 to do real argumentative work in it rather than serve as decoration. So please analyze that sequence through the lens of the candidate mechanisms I sketch below, tell me which of them the case actually discriminates between, and say what the case does, in turn, to each of the three standard interpretive frameworks: class struggle, ideology, and contingency.

The facts I am working from, so that nothing needs reconstructing: on 2 June 1793 the Convention, surrounded by Hanriot's National Guard, decreed the arrest of roughly twenty-nine Girondin deputies; Brissot, Vergniaud and the rest were guillotined on 31 October. In March 1794 the Hébertists, the dechristianizing, sans-culotte-aligned ultra-revolutionary flank, attempted an insurrection at the Cordeliers and were guillotined on 24 March. Twelve days later, on 5 April, Danton, Desmoulins and the Indulgents, who had been campaigning for an end to the Terror, went to the scaffold on charges of royalism and corruption. On 10 June the Law of 22 Prairial abolished defence counsel and witnesses before the Revolutionary Tribunal, capped trials at three days, restricted verdicts to acquittal or death, and, without saying so explicitly, stripped Convention deputies of the immunity that had previously required the Convention itself to vote before one of its members could be tried. Monthly executions in Paris rose from 155 in Germinal to 796 in Messidor, while acquittal rates stayed near a quarter of those tried. In late June the French victory at Fleurus removed the military emergency; in late July Robespierre, Saint-Just and Couthon were arrested and executed, and the apparatus of the Terror was then dismantled by the men who had staffed it.

Here are the mechanisms I want distinguished, each of which could in principle generate the observed pattern:

One, a purity spiral of competitive outbidding, in which the currency of political legitimacy is revolutionary virtue, moderation is indistinguishable from treason, and the standard ratchets because no faction can bid lower than its rivals without being consumed by them.

Two, coalition contraction: revolutions are won by broad and internally incoherent coalitions, each purge removes a flank, and each removal raises the stakes of being next, so the winning coalition narrows monotonically toward a rump clique that is finally too small to defend itself.

Three, instrumental autonomy: the revolutionaries build repressive instruments with their own personnel, budgets and esprit de corps, and the moment those instruments are legally unshackled from control by their creators, they acquire the capacity to eat them.

Four, personnel selection: revolutions select for traits that are adaptive in the seizure of power and maladaptive in exercising it, so the pattern reflects who the process promotes rather than any dynamic it sets in motion.

Five, the hypothesis I am partial to and most want stress-tested: that the visible presence of partly disloyal actors inside the ruling coalition is what makes a leadership's restraint credible and gives it an off-ramp, so that eliminating them destroys the capacity to de-escalate even when the leadership wants to, and the purge continues for commitment reasons rather than ideological ones.

Six, the null hypothesis: prominent people die violently in violent times, we mostly study the revolutions that became violent, and the narrative shape of a devouring revolution is something we impose after the fact.

What I want is a disciplined mapping rather than a survey. For each mechanism, say what the agent is, what the action variable is, and what it predicts about four features of the record: the ideological direction of successive purges, since the Hébertists and the Dantonists were killed within a fortnight of each other in opposite directions, which does not look like a spiral; the tempo, whether it should accelerate smoothly or jump at institutional changes such as Prairial; who survived and why, which is the cleanest test of the selection story; and how the sequence should end, whether by coup from inside the governing committees or by pressure from below. Tell me which of the six the case rules out, which two or three it leaves standing, and where the evidence is genuinely ambiguous. I am especially interested in what the acquittal rate and the immunity clause do to the instrumental-autonomy story, and in how much of the arc the Fleurus victory explains away as exogenous shock.

Then turn the analysis around. Which of the three interpretive frameworks does this case most damage, and does any mechanism travel better across cases than the others? And since my essay's thesis needs to be more than an anecdote, tell me what the comparison class should be, what a revolution that did not devour its children would have to look like, and what observation inside the French case would have counted against each mechanism. Finally, be explicit about where all six are silent, including whether anything here explains why Thermidor came in July rather than in September. I would rather you mark the silence than make a mechanism speak.

Please answer in continuous prose, roughly twelve hundred words, reasoning from the record I have given rather than from any reading list.
