hey — putting together something like a long memo on secession for a group that's advising a constitution-drafting process, and before I write any of it I want the spec. Not "should Catalonia be allowed to leave" — I want the requirements list: what would a theory of secession have to deliver to be good enough to actually be written into law, i.e. administrable by an electoral commission and a constitutional court, not just defensible in a journal?

Concretely: it has to resolve, before the vote happens, who the demos is, what territory is on offer, what the threshold is, and what happens to the bits that vote against. So give me desiderata that bite under that constraint, with the case behind each.

Cases I want requirements anchored to, at least loosely: Scotland, Quebec, Catalonia — plus the awkward ones: a holdout county that votes to stay but is geographically stuck to the new state; an enclave or exclave; a region whose departure would leave the rump state economically or militarily non-viable; a diaspora that identifies with the nation but doesn't live there; a subgroup that wants to seceed from the secession and maybe rejoin the parent state; a secession that succeeds or fails purely on whether other states recognise it; and a "nation" with no thick shared culture that just wants out over fiscal transfers.

Things I specifically want you to think through rather than assume:

- the franchise–territory circularity: who gets to vote depends on what unit is seceding, but what unit secedes is supposed to be settled by who votes. Does any theory actually break that circle, or does every one of them smuggle in a prior answer about the unit (usually an existing administrative border)?
- the reiteration / Russian-doll problem: whatever right you posit has to say what it says one scale down. Let the holdout secede from the secession and you get a regress; don't let it and you've denied the holdout the exact right you just asserted. Which horn is less bad, and why?
- the veto-holder question: does the rump state's population get a say (defence, debt, shared infrastructure, transfers)? If yes, secession collapses into a grant; if no, into a unilateral right.
- the trigger/authority split: who may authorise a referendum, who has the right to secede, and what counts as a legitimate grievance (remedial-only vs. any democratic will) are three different questions. Keep them apart and say which one a given theory is actually a theory of.
- the serial-referendum problem: what's the shelf life of a lost vote, and of a won one? Anything that says "the demos decides" owes an answer about a demos that changes its mind, or one that was panicked, bribed, or badly misinformed.

Also mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of one camp (nationalists, unionists, international lawyers, constitutional judges). Flag any pair I probably can't have together — my guesses are that "the demos draws its own boundaries" and "everyone materially affected gets a say" can't both hold, and that "no veto for the rump state" is in tension with "succession is settled in advance" (debt, treaties, UN seat, central bank) — but you tell me. And name the single requirement that knocks out the most popular theories; I suspect it's the holdout problem killing pure plebiscitary views, but I want your take.

A short table scoring the three big families — remedial-right-only, plebiscitary/primary-right, and the negotiated-constitutionalist "you need the parent state's consent" view — is fine, but keep it to a few lines. Alot more than that and you're spending words on the wrong part; the criteria are the deliverable.

At the end, say how confident you are in the list overall and single out the one requirement or claim you're least sure about.
