Hey — journalist here, I mostly cover science and ideas, but I got pulled into a long read on consent and deception and I think I've been leaning on an analogy that's doing more work than it deserves. The setup I keep circling: a hospital network I've been reporting on rolled out a machine interpreter for pre-op consent conversations, and in at least one case it rendered a sentence about the risk of permanent nerve damage into something close to the opposite; the patient signed, the surgery happened, and now everyone is arguing about whether she actually consented. Separately I've been looking at dating-app fraud where the lie is about something like age or marital status rather than about what the act itself is. In both, the move my lawyer friends keep making — and the one I've been making in my drafts — is that consent is basically a contract: offer, acceptance, and if you were lied to about something material, or you lacked capacity, or someone leaned on you, then the agreement is void or at least voidable, so there was no real consent. Their pitch is that contract law has already spent a long time separating deception about what the act is from deception about why you're doing it, and has machinery for duress and capacity, so why reinvent the wheel.

Can you audit the analogy for me? I want the mapping explicit — what plays the role of offer, acceptance, consideration, capacity, duress, material misrepresentation, void versus voidable, third-party enforcement, remedies — and then the places it breaks, sorted by whether they actually threaten the conclusion or are just decoration. Things I'm tripping over: a contract creates a bargain a third party can enforce and a court can award damages for, whereas consent seems to do nothing but change the moral status of an act; contracts are roughly discrete and bind you going forward while consent is ongoing and revocable in the middle, so I don't know what the analogy even says about someone who says stop ten seconds in — breach, or something it has no vocabulary for; contract law assumes a meeting of minds between two parties, but in the interpreter case nobody was lying and the distortion came from a relay neither of them chose, so it's unclear whose misrepresentation it is or whether "material" applies at all; and the frame seems to quietly import that there is a settled list of disclosable facts and one signing moment that settles everything. Which of those matter for the question I care about?

Does the conclusion I've been using it for — a lie about a material feature of what you're agreeing to means there was no valid consent — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? And give me a better fit if there is one (permission slip, waiver, license, promise, something else entirely), because I'd rather print an analogy I can defend than a clean line I can't. Tell me too if the contract framing is the wrong place to start altogether. Please answer in American English, and feel free to be blunt where my framing is definately confused.
