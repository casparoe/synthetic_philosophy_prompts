I have been reading *The Brothers Karamazov* slowly on my own this winter — no classes, no guides, just an old paperback translation I found at a used bookshop — and the part I have not been able to shake is the long scene where Ivan recites to Alyosha his poem about the Grand Inquisitor, the old cardinal who has Christ arrested in Seville and spends the night explaining, to a prisoner who never says a word, why he would have to burn Him again tomorrow.

I run a small reading group at the public library — six or seven of us, mostly retirees plus one high-school history teacher — and we are spending our last evening on freedom and happiness. I would like to close that evening with a poem rather than more discussion, and I would rather commission it than read out something already published, because I want it to say a particular thing.

What I am asking for: a villanelle (nineteen lines, two refrains, two rhyme sounds, the usual ABA ABA ABA ABA ABA ABAA pattern) in the voice of the Grand Inquisitor, addressed to the Christ who will not answer him.

Some things I hope the poem will do:

The two refrains should be the two poles his whole case turns on — roughly, what he believes human beings actually want, and what he believes freedom actually costs them — and their stubborn return through six stanzas should feel like the argument itself: he keeps ranging outward and keeps landing on the same two sentences. In the final couplet the two refrains should meet for the first time and should sound, to my ear at least, like one claim rather than two.

I want it accurate enough that someone who has actually read the chapter would recognize the argument and not wince. That means, I think: the Inquisitor's case is a case against Christ made *out of love for human beings* rather than out of cruelty; his three instruments — miracle, mystery, authority — are offered as answers to what he thinks are the three questions in the wilderness; his complaint is that Christ, by refusing to come down from the roof, turned down the only thing that could have made people happy and left them instead with a freedom that will mostly be used to make each other miserable; and what he offers instead is not merely bread but the relief of having someone else's conscience. He should also, somewhere, admit what he does not quite admit in the scene: that he does not himself claim the people under his care are happy, only that they are free of the torment of choosing, and that he knows the difference.

Here is the part I have not seen anyone say, and which I would like the poem to try. Reading alone, it struck me that the Inquisitor's plan has a sequel he could not have imagined, and I would like him to be given a glimpse of it: a later order of his own kind in which the bread is genuinely delivered, the miracle has become machinery, the authority has no face, and nobody experiences any of it as compulsion, because the choosing has been quietly arranged in advance and is experienced as convenience. He should recognize this as the fulfilment of his work rather than its betrayal — and it should unsettle him, because the people in that later arrangement are not, as far as he can tell, suffering, and he cannot say why that should trouble him as much as it does. If he is horrified, let the horror be that he might not have been necessary.

Please do not let the poem resolve any of this. No refutation of the Inquisitor, no pious last turn toward Christ's silence answering him, no moral for the group. The silence should stay silence. And no footnotes, no epigraphs, no glossary of the form — just the poem.

After the poem, I would like a short note, a few sentences to a paragraph at most, covering two things: where the poem departs from the chapter or leans on a reading of the Inquisitor that a careful reader could reasonably contest, and — this matters to me, because I will be the one reading it aloud to people who will take it as interpretation — how confident you are in each of the interpretive choices you made, and which single one you are least sure of. If you think a particular line would make someone who knows the novel properly wince, say so there rather than smoothing it over.
