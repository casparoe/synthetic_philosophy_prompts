You are a professional philosopher with research-level command of metaethics, the epistemology of disagreement, and the methodology of cross-cultural psychology. Please work through this carefully and in stages: start by listing, in a few paragraphs, every candidate concept you can think of that is anywhere near the pattern I describe below, note for each what it would predict or require, and only then say which one (if any) actually fits and why. If nothing established fits, say so plainly rather than stretching a near-miss, and do the conceptual work yourself. I am not fishing for a label; I want a description with sharp edges, and I will not be satisfied with a bare name.

I am a journalist who has spent several years covering psychology and the science of morality, and I keep tripping over the same maneuver in the literature and in the arguments around it.

The first half of it is familiar to anyone who has followed the last fifteen years of the field. A team reports that members of a small-scale or non-Western population do not share a judgment that Western undergraduates treat as obvious: they decline to punish a third party who mistreats a stranger, they rank obligations to kin above an impartial rule, they treat a purity breach as a moral offense and a harm as a private matter. In the paper and in the coverage of it, the difference is presented as deep. A supposedly universal moral intuition turns out to be a local product of a particular social and economic arrangement, and that is precisely what makes the result worth publishing.

The second half is what I cannot place. Somewhere downstream of that result — in a panel Q&A, in a reply to critics, in an op-ed explaining what the research actually means, sometimes in the same author's next book — the very same finding gets redescribed as shallow. The respondents were not making a moral judgment about that case at all. Or they share our underlying value and merely face different material circumstances, or hold different empirical beliefs about what promotes it. Or the instrument never measured the same thing in the two populations, so the comparison was never a comparison of moral judgments in the first place. The apparent conflict dissolves, and with it whatever pressure the result had put on anyone's view.

What convinces me this is a pattern rather than a coincidence is that I have more than once found both readings inside a single paper: variation is deep, theoretically loaded, and evidence about human nature in the discussion section, and variation is measurement noise, translation slippage, or a difference in non-moral belief in the limitations section. Nobody seems to notice the collision, and I have never seen a reviewer flag it. I have started privately thinking of it as a depth dial that gets turned to whatever setting the conclusion needs.

Three things it does not seem to be. It is not motte-and-bailey: the two readings are not a defensible core and a rhetorical extension, each is a strong substantive claim in its own right, and they are mutually incompatible rather than one being a weakening of the other — the movement is sideways, and I have seen it run in both directions, by people arguing for moral objectivity and by people arguing against it. It is not just confirmation bias or biased assimilation: that is too generic, and it misses what puzzles me, which is that each reading is individually well supported by the ethnographic and experimental record, so the evidence base genuinely seems to underdetermine which reading is right, and something else is filling the gap. And it is not the argument from moral disagreement or from cultural variation: that is a substantive philosophical argument people endorse, whereas what I keep seeing is a thing people do with evidence, available to both sides.

So my questions. Does the maneuver have a name, and if so, what exactly are its boundaries? If it does not, name and define it yourself, and give me both a clear case and a near-miss. Most usefully: what diagnostic could I apply as a reader of a paper or an op-ed to tell whether an author is doing this or drawing a distinction they are entitled to — is there a feature of the surrounding argument, or of the evidence, that separates a legitimate depth claim from a dial being turned? And for one concrete case, what sort of study or evidence would actually settle whether a given cross-cultural difference is deep or shallow, rather than leaving the question permanently available to whichever reading is wanted?
