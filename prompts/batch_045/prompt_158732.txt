Compare the following two kinds of choice-preserving intervention.

- A nudge: an intervention in choice architecture that forbids no option and changes no material incentive, and that works by harnessing a predictable quirk of human cognition — default effects, framing, salience, status-quo bias — in order to steer people toward the option the architect believes serves their own ends.
- A neutralizer: an intervention of the same formal kind — same options, same incentives, no coercion — that instead works by cancelling such a quirk. It symmetrises or strips out the cues on which a known bias feeds (for instance, presenting rival options in strictly parallel terms so that framing cannot do any work), so that the outcome reflects the chooser's own judgement rather than the architect's.

Both leave the menu of options intact, so the standard libertarian-paternalist defence — that nothing has been forbidden and every option remains available — appears to cover them equally. Address the following: (a) which of the two, if either, is the more paternalistic, and on what grounds; (b) whether the neutralizer is coherent in the first place, given that every presentation is itself some frame, and that muffling one bias may simply hand the outcome over to another one, or to whoever designs the supposedly neutral format; (c) whether, if neutralizers are feasible, an autonomy-minded libertarian paternalist should prefer them to nudges, and what follows for the justification of nudging if they are not.

Conclude by defending a verdict on whether the nudge/neutralizer contrast marks a morally significant difference, or is merely a difference in technique wearing moral language.
