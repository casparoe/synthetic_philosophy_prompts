I'm a 4th-year PhD student in history and my advisor is handing me one 90-minute session of her 200-level course on catastrophe in the long nineteenth century — about 22 students, mostly sophomores, mix of majors, and I can't count on them having done the reading. I want to spend it on whether germs, weather systems and volcanoes can be actors in a historical account at all, or whether treating them as actors just quietly collapses into environmental determinism.

The distinction I keep coming back to in my own work is between what I've started calling shock-causes and tempo-causes. A shock-cause acts at a datable moment and leaves something you can point at, so it slots into the ordinary grammar of X did Y — an eruption, a flood, an outbreak with a traceable index case. A tempo-cause never acts at any particular moment: it just sets the tempo and the range of what's possible over years, like a growing season that shortens by a couple of weeks, or a disease that goes endemic and quietly raises the baseline. My working claim is that narrative history has a comfortable slot for shock-causes and basically no slot for tempo-causes, so it either drops them or inflates them into a master explanation — and that inflation is the determinism I want them suspicious of.

Can you design me a runnable activity that makes them feel that? Constraints and things I've already got:

- A simulation or role-play with real choices and small stakes, not a discussion. I've built a synthetic case so nobody has to get local history right: a made-up grain-growing valley district, 1815-1818, with a big volcanic eruption somewhere far away that I'm going to keep unnamed. Materials: index cards, a big sheet of paper for a resource board, poker chips, a seperate stack of envelopes.
- I want two students to draw non-human roles (say the Mountain and the Rain). Rules I'm imagining: they don't speak, they can't be questioned, they act only by passing me a card that I read aloud at the top of a round, and some of their cards are dated with a lag so the visible effect lands two or three rounds after the card that produced it. Everyone else sits on the district council and makes choices each round — what to plant, whether to store or sell grain, whether to ration or let people leave — with consequences in chips.
- The trap I want them to walk into: early on, the payoff structure should nudge the council into a decision that is genuinely theirs (switching to a higher-yield but frost-vulnerable seed, or selling off seed grain for cash), and then a couple of rounds later the harvest fails and it looks like the Mountain did it. I want to see whether the official account blames the Mountain. Three or four students are chroniclers who write the valley's history at the end, and I'm thinking of scoring it by number of distinct causes named — which rewards naming non-human causes but also rewards over-attribution, which is sort of the point.
- No mini-lecture up front, and I don't want anyone to hear the words agency, determinism, shock or tempo until the debrief.
