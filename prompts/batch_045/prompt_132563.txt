My wife's uncle Ray is retiring at seventy-four. For about thirty years he ran a small sports-betting syndicate out of a strip-mall office — he told his mother he was in logistics, and one of the grandkids only found out last year. He is a genuine Kelly man: he bet half-Kelly his whole career because he never trusted his own edge estimates, and he never put more than a couple of percent of bankroll on anything, ever. I'm giving the toast at the dinner, roughly four minutes. Half the room are finance and quant types who will catch every reference to log utility, geometric growth, absorbing barriers, ruin; the other half are family — his eighty-eight-year-old sister who thinks gambling is a sin, his daughter, four grandkids. I need the idea doing structural work, not decoration, and I need it to land as affection rather than as a lecture.

Two things that constrain what can be said. First: it cannot console anyone with an ensemble-average platitude. The whole point of what Ray actually believes is that "it comes out ahead in the long run" is precisely the mistake, so no "it all evens out in the end", no "the odds were on his side", no growth-toward-the-sunrise line about a career compounding. Whatever comfort the toast offers has to be the kind that survives taking time averages seriously. Second: this last year he's been going on about something he calls the floor rule — you name the state you refuse to fall below, and then you maximize the growth rate of your distance above that floor rather than the growth rate of wealth itself. He has never written it down. I want the toast to use it, because "he thought about survival as a floor and not as an average" is honestly the most loving true thing anyone can say about him — but I also want it to be straight about the part he never solved, which is what the rule tells you when you have two floors you can't trade off against each other. He had two: the syndicate's bankroll and his marriage, and he knows which one he got right.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered beats, two or three sentences each, in the order I'll say them, no intro and no sign-off, no stage directions or notes — just the lines, and if a gambling metaphor shows up it has to work for the people in the room who will hear it literally. Beat five is the actual raise-your-glasses line.
