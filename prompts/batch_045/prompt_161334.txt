I am writing a paper on Moore's paradox and would like a consistency check on my own position before I circulate it to my supervisor and to two people in the department whose instincts differ from mine. I have a suspicion that at least one item in the package is doing less work than I think, or is in conflict with something else I want, but I cannot locate the problem and I would rather find it now than in a seminar. Here is where I have landed, stated as flatly as I can manage.

(1) Semantic innocence for both forms. Moorean sentences are consistent, they can be true, and their oddity has to be explained at the level of assertion or at the level of the attitudes expressed — not by anything in the truth-conditions, the semantics of the attitude verb, or the semantics of the first person.

(2) Assertion is governed by a knowledge norm: one may assert that p only if one knows that p. This is what makes the omissive form aberrant — asserting the first conjunct commits the speaker to knowing that p, and the second conjunct denies precisely that.

(3) The commissive form inherits its status from the same norm, via the fact that knowledge entails belief. So it is not a separate puzzle about self-knowledge, and the explanation should not need any special first-personal access to one's own beliefs; it needs only factivity plus the general norm.

(4) About self-knowledge I hold something deflationary: there is no first-person authority worth the name. Knowing whether one believes that p is an ordinary empirical achievement — fallible, evidence-based, drawing on behaviour, on what one finds oneself saying and doing, on introspection of varying reliability — and there is no constitutive tie between believing that p and being in a position to know that one believes it. What is called the transparency of belief in one's own case seems to me an overstatement of a statistical regularity.

(5) A case verdict I want to keep. There are actual subjects of whom the commissive conjunction is true and who are not thereby criticizable: the bereaved person who says, months later, that she knew and did not believe it; the phobic passenger who knows the aircraft is safe; the person who accepts an unwelcome conclusion about herself and sincerely denies that she believes it. I want both that the conjunction is true of these subjects and that they are not at fault.

(6) An interpretive claim I am attached to: the Moorean combination marks a genuine rational defect and not merely an impropriety of utterance — a failure of coherence in the subject's total doxastic state, which would be there even if she never said anything aloud. The paradox, on this reading, is a window onto a requirement of self-intimation, roughly that rationality requires you to believe that you believe what you believe.

(7) The part I think of as my own contribution, and the part I care most about. Some Moorean-adjacent sentences are fine and some are not, and the boundary is not where a static norm predicts it should be. Hedges are acceptable. But the reversed conjunction — denying the belief first and asserting the proposition second — strikes me as markedly less aberrant than the canonical order, and I want to explain this dynamically: the first conjunct incurs a commitment that the second then retracts or overrides, so part of what is wrong with the canonical version is a matter of the order in which commitments are incurred, not merely of what gets asserted. I am fairly confident about the datum and less confident about the explanation.

What I am asking for is an audit, not a survey of the literature and not a tutorial. Take these commitments pairwise — or in larger groups where pairs are the wrong unit — and tell me which combinations are genuinely in tension and which only look as though they are. Rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally serious, and for each one say precisely what would have to be given up, what else in the list that would cost me, and what the cheapest available revision is. Where an apparent conflict dissolves on inspection, say what the dissolving distinction is; I would rather be told that two of my commitments are comfortably compatible than be handed a list of invented problems. If a single revision would settle several tensions at once, identify it. If any item in the list is idle and could be dropped for free, say so. And tell me plainly whether this survives with a cheap fix, or whether one of the things I most want has to go.
