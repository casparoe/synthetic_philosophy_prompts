I'm working on a magazine piece about governing by prediction markets — Robin Hanson's futarchy setup, where you vote on the values and let a market on conditional outcomes pick the policy — and I want one properly worked case rather then another think-piece about how it would work in principle. So: take the 2016 Brexit referendum and run it through futarchy.

What I want is a real mapping, not vibes. Set out what a futarchy over Brexit would actually have had to look like: who sets the welfare metric, what the metric even is (GDP per capita? some wellbeing index?), when it gets measured, what the conditional contracts are, and what happens to the branch of the market that never gets adopted. Then say what the theory predicts would have come out as the decision, and what it would have predicted about the run-up.

Facts I'm going from memory on, so take them as given rather than litigating them: the referendum was a straight binary, leave or remain; the bookies had Remain as a heavy favourite right through polling day, implied something like 75 to 90 percent, and were wrong; there were large liquid markets on the vote itself but essentially nothing on conditional outcomes like GDP-five-years-on; Leave won narrowly, turnout around 72 percent.

Then the part I actually care about, where it breaks in both directions:

1. The 2016 markets were not futarchic markets. They paid off on the vote, not on a welfare metric; they were thin-ish offshore books with favourite-longshot bias, sentiment money and no conditional-on-adoption contracts. So how much can this episode even tell us? Is the fact that the markets got Brexit wrong a genuine data point against futarchy, or a category error?

2. The self-referential bit. In futarchy the market is supposed to be a thermometer, but its price is public and it *is* the decision. The odds were in the newspapers for months, and there is a plausible story that a confident Remain price depressed Remain turnout or energised Leave. Call it the performative prediction problem: the forecast feeds back into the thing forecast. Does the theory have any resources here, or does it simply assume the market is causally inert?

3. The metric-and-horizon problem. Pick GDP per capita measured in 2021 and you get one answer; measure it in 2030 and you may get another, because the transition shock is immediate and the trade effects are slow. Whoever writes the metric and picks the date has already made most of the decision. How much of futarchy's content survives once you notice that?

4. A wrinkle I have not seen handled and that I want taken seriously: distinguish price-side manipulation, where you trade to move the price, eat the loss on those trades and profit on the outcome, from what I would call outcome-side manipulation, where a trader can partly *cause* the variable being measured. In the Brexit case the obvious candidates are big financial institutions and the campaigns themselves. If a market is deep enough to be informative, is it not also deep enough for actors who can move GDP to profit by moving it? What, if anything, does the conditional-market design do about that?

Close by saying whether the episode is an argument for futarchy, against it, or for a patched version — and give me the strongest version of the pro-futarchy reply to your own critique, because I do not want a piece where the theory is a strawman. Also be explicit about what futarchy simply has no opinion on here, such as whether to hold a referendum at all. Around 900 words if you can, and flag anything where you are filling in a fact I should check myself.
