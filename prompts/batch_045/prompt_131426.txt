You are a professional philosopher who also writes formal verse — someone who cares as much about whether a poem's end-words rotate correctly as about whether its epistemology would survive a question from the floor. I'm the teaching assistant for a fourth-year seminar on epistemic paradoxes, and for our last session I'd like to hand out a poem instead of another problem set. The session covers the two paradoxes of conjunction and closure that my students insist on treating as the same puzzle in different costumes: the lottery paradox, where for each ticket in a large fair lottery you are justified in believing that it will lose, and closure then seems to entitle you to believe the conjunction of all those claims, which you know to be false; and the preface paradox, where an author is justified in each claim of her book and is also justified in believing that somewhere in the book there is a mistake. I want the poem to bring out that these are structurally different cases — but without ever saying so in so many words.

Please write a sestina: six stanzas of six lines with the usual rotating end-words, plus a three-line tornada in which all six words return a final time, paired within the lines. Use these six end-words — TICKET, ODDS, SURE, CLOSURE, ERRATA, FALLIBLE — though if one of them proves genuinely unusable you may substitute a near neighbour and note the substitution. The philosophical work should be done by the machinery of the form rather than by exposition. Stanzas one and two are the lottery, and the rotation itself should do the accumulating: each return of TICKET and ODDS should feel more reassuring, while CLOSURE keeps arriving at line's end whether or not it was invited, until the reader notices that the reassurance has quietly summed to something false. Stanzas three and four are the preface, in the author's own voice as she writes the apology page, and the contrast they should carry is that nobody has told her anything — there is no announcement, no published odds, only her own track record, the length of the book, and the fact that books like this one, by people like her, always contain at least one error. The turn comes in stanza five, and I want it to be a case I invented for the handout, one the seminar has not seen: suppose the author receives a copy-editor's note saying that at least one claim in the manuscript is false, without saying which. Call it what you like. It is a lottery whose tickets are her own sentences, and I want the poem to place it — to make the reader feel that it goes with the lottery rather than with the preface, even though it concerns her own book — and thereby to suggest where the real difference lies: in the lottery the second premise is handed to you from outside, whereas in the preface it comes from the very fallibility that was supposed to underwrite everything else, so the pressure has to land somewhere else. Stanzas six and the tornada must resolve nothing: the six words come round one last time with both claims still standing, and the final line names no culprit. No stanza should tell the reader what to conclude.

Before drafting, spend a few lines brainstorming — say which end-word you intend to lean on for which paradox, where exactly the turn lands, and what the tornada is withholding — and then give me the poem. It should be performable aloud in about two minutes. Please name no philosophers, papers, or dates, add no epigraph, footnotes, or glossary, and avoid the two lines my students have already heard a hundred times (that certainty is an illusion, and that the only thing one knows is that one knows nothing). After the poem, give me three or four lines of answer key I can use while marking: which stanza is which paradox, what the copy-editor's note is meant to show, and where you think the honest answer would put the burden — on closure, on the justification of the individual claims, or somewhere I haven't thought of.
