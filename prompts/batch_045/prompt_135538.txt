After forty-odd years in geophysics — most of them spent on inverse problems, where one acquires the habit of asking whether two formulations are really the same problem written in different coordinates — I took early retirement, began auditing philosophy courses at the university, and was eventually pulled into a reading group at the local library consisting of four retirees and two graduate students. We spent two evenings on the pair of decision-theoretic cases that go by the names counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail, and got nowhere, largely because everyone kept asserting what the correct theory says instead of doing the arithmetic. The instructor of the Master's-level option in rational choice has now asked me to set one question on her take-home paper and to supply the model answer and marking scheme myself, precisely because she wants something that cannot be passed simply by reciting labels; I am glad to do it, but I would like the question drafted properly rather than improvised from my notes.

Here is what I have in mind. The question must be entirely self-contained — the students have no library access and no reading should be required or creditable — so it should set both cases out with explicit numbers and then identify the three competing approaches only by the procedure each prescribes: intervene on your own action and ask what would causally follow; condition on your action and ask what it tells you; and ask what would follow if your decision procedure returned this output, holding fixed whatever else in the situation depends on that procedure (the approach has a name, but I would rather the question referred to it by that prescription, and allowed students to name it only if they define it). Use a fair coin, $1,000 and $1,000,000 in the coin case: the predictor simulates you beforehand, and if the coin lands heads you are asked to hand over $1,000 and receive nothing, whereas if it lands tails you are never approached but are handed $1,000,000 if and only if the predictor predicted you would have paid had the coin landed heads; the coin has in fact landed heads and you are being asked. In the second case your house is worth $1,000,000 to you and will be destroyed by a natural flood with probability p (take p = 1/100), independently of anything you do; a blackmailer with a predictor of your deliberative dispositions posts a letter demanding $1,000 and threatening to destroy the house — which he can do at no cost to himself — if you refuse; the letter is posted if and only if exactly one of these is true, that you would pay if it arrived, and that the natural flood occurs; the letter has arrived. I want three parts. The first asks for what each of the three approaches recommends in each case, with the expected values written out rather than asserted. The second is the crux: the third approach appears to recommend paying in the first case and refusing in the second, and I want candidates to explain why that is not a contradiction and to diagnose the tempting argument — one member of my reading group pressed it at length — that the two cases are formally identical up to a relabelling of variables, so that any coherent theory must answer them the same way. The third part is a variant I have not seen anywhere and would like to include: suppose the predictor's prediction of what you would do is correct with probability q, independently of everything else, while its flood prediction remains perfect and p is negligible; derive how reliable the predictor must be for the third approach's recommendation to survive in each case, since my own suspicion is that the two thresholds are wildly different, though I have not done the algebra carefully and would like the model answer to settle it; then ask what the asymmetry, if there is one, is supposed to show — whether it tells in favour of the third approach or against these examples as evidence — and what further information one would need in order to decide. It is one of three questions on a ninety-minute take-home paper, with a limit of 1,200 words and 25 marks.

Please give me three things. First, the question exactly as it should appear on the paper. Second, a model answer of roughly 800 words that works the arithmetic honestly and does not smuggle in the conclusion. Third, a marking scheme organised around five or six must-hit points totalling 25 marks, giving for each point the marks it carries, what an A answer must contain in order to earn them, what a B answer typically does instead, and a one-line note on how candidates usually get it wrong — including, if it is indeed a mistake, the manoeuvre of comparing the payer's $1,000 in the non-flood worlds against the refuser's $1,000,000 in the flood worlds while overlooking the worlds in which no letter arrives and the house is lost anyway, since I made that slip myself and want to know whether it changes the verdict. I would also be grateful for a short note on how to credit candidates who say something defensible but different about what the evidential approach recommends in the second case, as my reading group split three ways on that and I do not want the scheme to penalise a candidate for disagreeing with me on a point where I may simply be confused. No bibliography, reading list or references of any kind, please: everything must follow from the set-up I have given. And although I write British English myself, as you will have noticed, the papers are second-marked by a colleague at an American university and the department standardises on American spelling and usage, so the question, the model answer and the marking scheme should all be written in American English.
