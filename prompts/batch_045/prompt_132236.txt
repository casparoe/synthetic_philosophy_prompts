I build auction and pricing systems for a living — adtech, most recently — which probably explains my instinctive pro-market reflexes, and over the past few months, after a friend at a health startup that wants to broker compensated living kidney donation pushed me to defend my gut reactions, I've been trying to write down where I actually stand on markets that people find repulsive. Here is the list, as honestly as I can state it.

(1) Selling a kidney should stay illegal even with fully informed consent, no fraud, and decent medical follow-up — and I don't think the reimbursement distinction is load-bearing, since a generous enough expense allowance is just a price with extra steps. (2) Paid surrogacy contracts should be legal and enforceable, and paying egg donors, sperm donors, and people who enroll in clinical trials is fine and should stay fine. (3) Laws against emergency price gouging are a mistake: a hardware store doubling the price of generators and bottled water the day before a hurricane makes landfall, or a ride-hailing app surging during the evacuation, is legitimate and probably the least bad way to ration scarce stuff. (4) Buying and selling votes should remain a crime, and paying people to register or to turn out is corrupt, even though I can't point to anyone who is harmed in the ordinary sense. (5) I don't think the familiar explanations of what's wrong with the bad markets do any real work: not exploitation or the fact that the sellers would mostly be people with few options (roofing and night-shift meatpacking are also dangerous and also mostly done by people with few options, and I don't want those banned), and not inequality in who ends up with the good (I tolerate wildly unequal access to tutoring, housing, and healthcare). What I think is doing the work is something expressive: some markets price a good whose value isn't the kind a price can track, and treating it as a commodity is corrupting whether or not anyone was coerced. (6) Underlying most of this, I think informed consent between adults, absent force or fraud, is doing the heavy lifting and the state should usually stay out.

When people push me on (1) against (2) I end up mumbling something about kidneys not growing back while gestation is closer to labor than to a sale, but I don't find it convincing, and I put the reimbursement clause in (1) because I noticed it was the usual escape hatch and I don't want to rely on it.

What I want from you is an audit rather than an essay, and please don't send me to papers or reading lists — I'm not going to read them, I want the argument. Go through the set pairwise: which commitments genuinely pull against each other and which only look like they do, with the real tensions ranked by severity rather than all flagged as equally bad; for each real tension, what has to give, what the least costly revision would be, and whether there is one adjustment that cleans up more than one of them at once; and for each pair you clear, what the difference is that makes it fine. I'd also like you to say how confident you are in each of your calls as you make them, and to single out the specific judgment you're least confident about and why.
