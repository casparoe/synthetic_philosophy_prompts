Suppose a large philanthropic-advice organisation launches a standing "moral-trade clearinghouse." Its clients are people who disagree, often sharply, about which interventions do the most good: one thinks a dollar spent on cause X does a hundred times more good than the other thinks it does, and vice versa for cause Y. The clearinghouse matches such people into reciprocal commitments — I fund the intervention you think is best, conditional on your funding the intervention I think is best — holds both contributions in escrow, and releases them only once both sides have performed. Assume it verifies payments reliably and charges no fee.

I want a number, not a survey of considerations. Give me your credence in the following proposition, and show me how you got there.

**M:** Five years after launch, the median participant is better off *by their own moral lights* than they would have been had they simply spent the same money on their own favoured intervention. Imagine an audit that reconstructs, for each participant, the counterfactual bundle they would have funded alone, and asks whether the bundle actually funded is preferred under that participant's own values. M is true if more than half say yes.

Decompose M. The terms I would like priced, with a number and one line of justification each, are:

1. **Detachability.** That the value people assign to an intervention is largely detachable — it accrues to the outcome regardless of who funds it, why, or whether the funding forms part of a life that expresses commitment to that value — rather than endorsement-dependent, in the way that integrity-based, aretaic, or expressive views suggest: on such views an outcome bought by an outsider as part of a trade may be worth much less, or nothing, or less than nothing.
2. **Non-hostility of the value profile.** That matched parties regard each other's favoured interventions as merely *lower-value* by their own lights, not as positively harmful, forbidden, or side-constraint-violating. If they regard them as harmful, the trade destroys value for them rather than merely costing them little.
3. **Exchange rates without a common metric.** That budget-balanced reciprocal commitments can be found at scale without anyone having to compare incommensurable values — that the multilateral matching problem has a solution leaving every participant better off by their own lights, given that bilateral trade needs only each side's own verdict but a clearinghouse with many clients and a balanced budget may need something more.
4. **Enforcement and moral hazard.** That escrow solves defection, and that the scheme does not drift into moral-hazard dynamics: being paid to refrain, becoming the sort of person who is willing to do the thing one is paid not to do, threats disguised as offers.
5. **Offsetting hazard.** The net effect on resources: whether the scheme crowds in new money, or crowds out participants' own direct giving through something like moral licensing — the sense that one's obligation is discharged because someone else is funding one's cause.
6. **Thin margins versus transaction costs.** That the gains per trade, which are only the difference between what each side thinks the other's cause is worth, survive the costs of identifying partners, specifying terms, and establishing the counterfactual.

Add, drop, or reshape terms as you think best, but keep a number attached to each. Then show how they combine into your final credence, tell me which single term M is most sensitive to, which term you are least confident in, and what one piece of evidence or argument would move that term most.

For what it is worth, I am at about 0.35 on M and a colleague whose judgement I trust is at about 0.75. Tell me which of your terms our disagreement most likely lives in, and which of us should move if you had to pick one.
