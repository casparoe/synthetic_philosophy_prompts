I am to lead a single ninety-minute seminar on procreative beneficence and the case for liberal eugenics, for a mixed group of about twenty people: first-year genetic counselling trainees plus a handful of clinicians. Most of them have solid biology, almost no moral philosophy, and an occupational habit of thinking about every intervention as a treatment offered to a patient who is already there. About half will arrive suspecting that whatever I describe is eugenics with the serial numbers filed off; the other half will arrive thinking it is obviously fine precisely because it is only parents choosing. I do not need the view explained to me. I need a plan for explaining it to them.

Give me the sequence, with the dependency structure explicit: which distinction has to be in place before the principle can even be stated without sounding either tautological or menacing, and what goes wrong if I state it first. In particular, tell me where the point about selecting among possible children rather than treating a child who will exist anyway has to appear, how much of it I should actually give them, and why handing it to them too late turns it into sophistry rather than a hinge. Then the order in which the scope claim (that the reason is not confined to disease), the liberal qualifier (no state, no coercion, only the parents' own reasons), and the maximizing-versus-enough question should each be opened, and what each one needs already on the table.

Give me the one case to carry the whole session, with an argument for what it must contain and what it must avoid. My current instinct is that it needs: a fixed set of candidate children rather than an open-ended one; a trait that is not a disease on any clinical nosology, so their clinical reflex cannot do the work; a trait whose expected effect on the child's life runs partly through a contingent and partly unjust feature of the social world, so the bad-in-itself versus bad-because-of-how-we-arranged-things fork opens when I choose rather than by accident; and a trait that is environmentally modulated or conditionally expressed, so that when someone inevitably says that we should fix the world instead, I can separate selecting a different child from changing the conditions under which this child develops. It must, I think, have no state involvement and no effect on anyone outside the family, so that the liberal/authoritarian contrast stays visible and so that the aggregate-effects worry — many small private selections shifting a population and thereby changing what the trait means — can arrive later as a genuinely new step rather than being smuggled in. And the options must not be unanimously ranked: two defensible conceptions of a good life should order them differently. Tell me what I have got wrong in that specification and what the case should be.

Then the misconception map, in the order they will actually form, with the moment in the sequence where each should be defused rather than merely answered: that beneficence here means beneficence to a patient; that the principle is about preventing suffering and that its extension to non-disease traits is therefore a reductio rather than its content; that the embryo is the patient; that calling it eugenics requires a state somewhere, together with the mirror error that the word liberal dissolves the objection; that the reason must be maximizing along a single scale; that the disability-rights objection is simply the claim that discrimination is wrong, rather than a question about what a selection expresses and whether the wrong lies in the act, the motive, or the aggregate pattern; and that procreative autonomy is a side-constraint that settles the matter, when in the argument it is what sets the permissive scope but does not rebut the reason.

Finally, the check: one case at the end — I have in mind a variant where the trait's contribution to the child's flourishing depends partly on the parents' own response to it, so that the ranking seems to move with facts about the family rather than the genome — that will tell me whether they have located a premise they can reject or are only registering discomfort, plus what I should explicitly refuse to settle in ninety minutes and say out loud that I am refusing: the full metaphysics of the non-identity claim, how heritable any of this is or how well screening ranks embryos, and whether the principle yields a duty or only a permission.
