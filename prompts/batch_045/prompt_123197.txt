I am a novelist, three drafts into a book I did not intend to write about metaphysics. Its central scene: a woman who has served a minor desert deity faithfully for thirty years is commanded by that deity to do a thing she is certain is wrong; she does it; and the narration never settles whether she was right. Early readers keep telling me the book is *about* the question Plato gives Socrates to ask about the pious and the gods — whether the god wills an act because it is good, or whether it is good because the god wills it. I would like to be able to think about that question properly. But I would rather think about it in the vocabulary I already have than in the vocabulary of the philosophy seminar, and I want an honest account of what my vocabulary fails to carry.

The vocabulary I actually work in: authors and characters; canon and retcon; what is *true of a character* as against what the author has so far written down; a character surprising its author, or coming alive, or refusing a scene; the constraint that an action be in character, and the author's experience of that constraint as discovered rather than chosen; the reader's revolt when a narrator asserts a virtue the page has not shown; and two different modalities I am constantly confusing in my own drafts — what might happen next *given the book so far* (possibility inside the world) versus what the author could have written instead (possibility for the book, in some unwritten draft).

What I want, in this order.

First, a faithful restatement. Give me both horns of the dilemma, and the standard escapes from it — the move on which goodness turns out to name the deity's own character, and the move on which the deity's will is only one value-maker among several — in the terms above, as precisely as the terms allow.

Second, the residue report. Which claims translate cleanly? Which translate only after being bent — and bent how? Which have no expression at all in a novelist's vocabulary?

Third, and this is what I most want, press on four places where I suspect the translation is doing work it shouldn't:

1. **The two modalities.** The dilemma asks whether the deity could have commanded otherwise. My vocabulary has two notions of this, and I cannot tell which one the dilemma needs: could-it-have-been-otherwise-*in-the-story*, given the character and everything written so far; and could-it-have-been-otherwise-*for-the-author*, a different book she never wrote. Say what each horn requires, and whether collapsing them is what makes the dilemma feel forced.
2. **The experience of discovery.** Novelists really do report that a character will not do the thing they had planned, and we treat this as a mark that the character is alive rather than as evidence about the author's psychology. There is an obvious deflation of it — the author has internalized constraints she cannot fully introspect and is surprised by her own output. Tell me whether the deflation succeeds, and whether, if it does, it has any analogue in the theological case: whether a theist's sense that value is *encountered* rather than stipulated is exposed by the same move, or whether it is differently situated.
3. **The in-character constraint as a genuine third thing.** In my practice, the constraint is both authored and binding: I made it, I did not find it, and I cannot now disregard it. Is that a third option the dilemma has no room for, or is it just the divine-character move wearing a novelist's coat — the same answer with different furniture? I want to know whether the residue here is a discovery about the dilemma or an artifact of fiction's peculiar ontology, in which the author stands outside a world she nonetheless enters.
4. **Show, don't tell, as an argument.** My readers will not accept a goodness that is merely decreed; they demand that it be demonstrated on the page, and they are right to, aesthetically. Does that demand carry any weight outside aesthetics, or is it exactly the kind of local, craft-relative norm that should not be allowed to legislate about value?

Finally: which horn, if either, does my own practice as a novelist commit me to — and is that commitment a reason to believe anything, or only a fact about what readers will sit still for? And tell me, before I go back to chapter nine, what the seminar-room vocabulary would have caught that mine cannot see, and whether that means my vocabulary is missing something real or that the dilemma is in part a quarrel over words.
