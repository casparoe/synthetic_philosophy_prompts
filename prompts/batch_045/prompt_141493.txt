I run a small reading group — about 14 people, mostly philosophy grad students with four or five from the econ side — and we've got one 90-minute slot coming up on Aumann's agreement theorem. I'd much rather do an activity than a lecture, and I want it to be a game with real numbers and real (tiny) stakes on the table, not people sitting around discussing hypotheticals.

The thing I want them to feel rather than be told: nobody in the room learns anything new about the world, everyone just keeps announcing their probability out loud, and yet the numbers get forced together. They should walk out having internalised the difference between hearing someone's posterior, hearing which cell of their information partition they're in, and hearing their likelihoods — and having noticed for themselves that the theorem only needs the first, and needs it to be common knowledge and not merely known by everybody.

Constraints: printed cards and poker chips or points on a whiteboard, no laptops, no real money. Roughly 60 minutes of play, 30 for debrief. I was thinking groups of 3 or 4 so there are enough rounds of announcements for the convergence to be visible, but tell me if that's wrong.

Two things I want built in and can't get right myself:

1. A second, shorter variant where the common prior is quietly broken. I want them to watch convergence fail and then have to explain why it failed, without having been told that anyone's prior was different — something about the setup should make it fall out on its own. (I know the standard line that people who start from a common prior can't agree to disagree, and I vaguely remember someone arguing that once you let priors differ the result has basically no content left, so I'd like the debrief to be able to go there.)
2. Something that prises apart agreeing on the number from being willing to act on it. I half-remember a no-trade-flavoured result being taught next to this one. Ideally after the group converges I offer to trade with them at the common number and there's a defensible reason someone still says yes that isn't about the probability — but I want that reason to be a real one and not a cheat.

For the debrief I need: a mapping from specific moments in the game onto the machinery (state space, the prior, each player's partition, the posterior, common knowledge of posteriors, why the process has to stop where it stops); an honest note on which features of the theorem the game can't reproduce and which I'll therefore have to just say out loud; and a list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. I'm definately expecting one person to just average the numbers, someone to refuse to move at all, and the arithmetic to break somewhere in round three.

Last thing: someone in the group will say this only works because you made everyone a perfect Bayesian with a fixed partition and real people aren't like that, and I want an answer better than yes, the assumptions are strong. Also, I think there's a result out there about how much communication — or how many rounds of announcements — is needed before agreement is forced, with a bound that's somehow independent of how much the agents know, plus a case where it's still bad. I can't reconstruct it. If it's real, I'd love it in my pocket for exactly that objection.
