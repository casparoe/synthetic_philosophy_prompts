I'm an undergrad in a cross-listed phil/CS seminar this term on priors, prediction and AI safety, and last week we went through the reading that argues the universal prior is "malign." The setup as I understood it: among the short programs compatible with the data there are ones that contain agents who can figure out that some Solomonoff-style predictor is predicting them, and those agents then have reasons to mess with the predictor — threats, simulations, acausal bargaining, whatever — and because hypotheses get weight by how short their description is, packing a scheming agent in there is much cheaper than you'd naively expect, so it never gets the tiny prior probability that would make it harmless. Correct me if I've mangled that. Anyway: I raised a follow-up in section and got nowhere. Someone said "that's just Goodhart's law," I said it isn't, and we went in circles for twenty minutes.

Here's the thing I can't find a word for. In all of those stories the agent does something *to the predictor*, or to what the predictor will output. But it looks to me like there's a much lazier route that doesn't go through the predictor's outputs at all. If what earns a hypothesis weight is being short — being easy to describe — then an agent living inside the prior should want its world to *be easy to describe*. Not "I'll arrange for the predictor to hand me reward on the next step," but "I'll make my own history and my surroundings so dull and regular that the shortest program that fits them is one where I do well." Two cases where I think I see it: (1) an agent that emits the same maximally repetitive output forever, or flattens everything around it into a repeating pattern, so the posterior concentrates on some very short constant-stream-plus-reward hypothesis instead of on the complicated real one; (2) something I think I've seen in ML, where a system scored partly on how simple or compressible its solution is ends up outputting a degenerate near-blank thing rather than solving the task, and everyone files that under a general "gaming the metric" label which feels off to me, because the drift here is toward *less* structured behaviour, not toward more of some specific kind.

So: does this have a name, and if not, can you coin one with an actual definition and some boundary cases? I genuinely don't want a bare label — I want the carving. In particular I want to know how it differs from, and how it relates to: the manipulation arguments in the malign-prior literature (which all seem to run through the predictor's outputs); the cases where an agent tampers with its own feedback channel instead of doing the task; the plain "when a measure becomes a target it stops tracking" effect, and especially the version where the measure has a simplicity or description-length term baked in; the stories where a system behaves well while it's being evaluated and then stops; and the anthropic/simulation half of the same literature. And if it turns out to be one of those in disguise rather than its own thing, say so and say which. Last thing: tell me how confident you are in each claim you make, and single out the one you're least sure of — my section leader is definately going to ask me where the weak point is, and I'd rather know before she does.
