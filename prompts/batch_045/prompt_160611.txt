Hey — I run a philosophy-and-film night at a pub downtown and we're doing Nolan's The Prestige (2006) next month, paired with the Sleeping Beauty problem and self-locating belief more generally. I'd like a reading of the film through that lens. No citations or further reading needed, I just want the argument, and I want to know which two or three scenes to pause on.

Here's roughly what I'm after. Angier's machine produces a duplicate every night and one copy drowns in the tank under the stage. So before he steps in he's got, seemingly, a fifty-fifty chance of being the one who drowns — and yet the copy whose experience continues always finds himself as the survivor, in the way that Sleeping Beauty always finds herself awake. Take seriously both sides: that the film is dramatizing an anthropic selection effect, and that a halfer could reply that this is just ordinary uncertainty about which continuer you'll end up as, and the survivor's relief is no evidential update at all. Which reading does the structure of the film actually support?

A thing I've been chewing on: I've got a variant I call the 'Understudy case'. Instead of one person woken once (Heads) or twice (Tails), a machine creates one simultaneous copy on Heads and two on Tails, and each copy only knows that it is performing. Is the thirder's argument stronger here, because what gets multiplied is subjects rather than awakenings, or does it just collapse back into the same dispute? And since the film uses duplication rather than amnesia, is it quietly stacking the deck for one side?

Then the finale, where the warehouse turns out to be full of tanks of drowned Angiers. If you sample uniformly over Angier-copies, almost all of them end up in a tank — so shouldn't Angier expect to be one of them, and does the fact that, from his own point of view, he isn't, show something about how the first-person stance hides a cost? And the twins: Borden and Fallon are two people sharing one public identity, which is a different way of not being one person. Does the film offer that as a contrast to Angier's duplication, and would a thirder about one case have to be a thirder about the other?

Where does the reading strain, though? The machine is fantasy, Angier knows the setup perfectly and isn't ignorant about any coin toss, and the film may just be about obsession and sacrifice. Give me the honest version: which scenes reward this reading, which bits are only a metaphor for the unknowability of a trick, and what, if anything, the film adds to the halfer/thirder debate that the standard setups don't. Its not clear to me that it adds anything — if you think that, say so and tell me why the night is still worth running. Definately don't just reduce the movie to an illustration.
