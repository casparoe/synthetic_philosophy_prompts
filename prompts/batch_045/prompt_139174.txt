I teach moral philosophy, and I am rebuilding the last third of a graduate seminar on responsibility, where I spend two weeks on Arendt's report on the Eichmann trial and on what she called the banality of evil. Every time I teach it the discussion collapses into the slogan that ordinary people with ordinary motives can do extraordinary harm, and I have come to think the slogan is worse than useless: it is equally true of the embezzling accountant and of the man who drives home drunk, and neither of those is what I am trying to point at. So over several iterations of the seminar I have been trying to write down the condition that actually does the work, and I would rather have it tested against cases than explicated. My provisional gloss is this: an instance of wrongdoing is banal to the degree that the agent's operative reasons would have moved him to exactly the same action had the people affected been different people — the victims are substitutable without any loss in the agent's reason for acting — whereas non-banal evil is victim-involving, in that the agent's reasons essentially advert to who is being hurt, or to the hurting itself. Here are the verdicts I find myself giving on a set of invented cases, which I have deliberately not adjusted to fit the gloss.

First, the transport officer who never sees a camp, whose day consists of matching rolling stock to quotas, who takes genuine professional pleasure in solving a scheduling bottleneck, and who says at trial that he wished nobody ill and would have done the same for any cargo: banal, and the paradigm. Second, the guard who volunteers for the night shift because it lets him beat prisoners, whose cruelty is specific and sexualized: not banal, and indeed the contrast case. Third, the convinced ideologue who takes the killing to be a world-historical necessity, acts from conviction, and keeps a diary of self-exhortation: not banal either, but for the opposite reason, and I would like to know whether depth is the right axis for that verdict. Fourth, the man who takes part in a killing operation because the alternative is to be marked unreliable, who understands perfectly well what he is doing, loathes himself for it, and drinks himself to sleep afterwards: I call this banal, but it is the verdict I am least confident in, because he has a perfectly audible inner auditor, and Arendt's emphasis, as I read her, fell on the failure of that inner dialogue rather than on the ordinariness of the motives. Fifth, the clerk who processes deportation paperwork impeccably, and stays late to correct a colleague's errors, because she cannot bear to do any job badly: banal, and the most banal of the set, since here the harm runs through a virtue. Sixth, a present-day caseworker in my own city who loses a family's housing file through pure procedural drifting and thereby ruins their life — no malice, no hatred, victims entirely substitutable: thoughtless, harmful, appalling, but I resist calling it evil.

That sixth case is my worry, and the reason I am writing this out at length: cases five and six look indistinguishable under my gloss, and yet I want to call one evil and the other merely a disaster, so either my gloss is missing a structural condition — scale, or the character of the enterprise being served, or the difference between harm that is aimed at and harm that is merely foreseen and then not looked at — or one of my verdicts has to go. A colleague in German studies who sat in on the seminar last year kept pressing me on whether the concept is a property of the agent, of the act, or of the wrong taken as a whole, and I could not give her a clean answer. So: give me the principle that captures as many of these six verdicts as you can, state it in one sentence, tell me plainly which verdicts it forces me to abandon and which of them you think is least defensible, say whether substitutability is repairable or should be scrapped, and say whether the notion sits in tension with the vocabulary of radical evil that Arendt had used in her earlier book on totalitarianism and, if so, how that tension resolves. Then run the principle on two cases I have no settled verdict about: a product-safety committee of eleven, each of whom votes to ship a device known to cause a small number of deaths, no single vote decisive, each acting for ordinary career reasons, the eventual victims wholly interchangeable; and an official of the first case's kind who on two occasions quietly signed release papers for people he happened to like, so that the same agent seems banal in one compartment and not in another. Finally, give me one further case of your own construction that your principle handles badly. I am not asking for a survey of who has said what; I want the carving. Close with a last line of the form \answer{...} containing a single phrase of no more than ten words naming the feature your principle treats as doing the decisive work.
