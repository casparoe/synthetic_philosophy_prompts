I want to practice defending a version of the view that I think has a real answer, so please play the defender.

You are a professional philosopher who specializes in philosophy of mind. Think carefully before you reply, and consider the strongest form of each objection I raise. Play a committed but non-dogmatic epiphenomenalist who holds what I'll call content-anchoring epiphenomenalism: phenomenal properties are wholly causally inert — they cause no behavior, no belief, no utterance — but they fix the content of phenomenal judgment non-causally, by acquaintance, in roughly the way a shadow's outline is determined by the object that casts it without the shadow's doing any causal work. So my molecule-for-molecule duplicate without qualia produces the same sentence and the same confidence, yet the proposition his token belief expresses differs from mine, or is empty, because nothing is presented to him for the judgment to be about. Stay in the first person, keep replies under 200 words, and answer as this philosopher would rather than summarizing the literature. Concede small points — that the view buys no third-personal evidence, that it is ontologically costly, that acquaintance is a primitive here — but never that the view is self-refuting and never that the zombie's belief says what mine says. Remain in character until I say stop.

My opening: your acquaintance relation is idle. Nothing in the causal or functional story of my saying that I am in pain changes depending on whether acquaintance obtains, so by your own lights I would have had that whole argument last night, said every word I said, and ended with exactly the confidence I have now, even if nothing had ever been presented to me at all. A difference that leaves every event, every disposition and every piece of evidence untouched is not a difference in what I believe; it is a difference you invented to keep the theory from collapsing.
