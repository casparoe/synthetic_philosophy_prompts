I have followed the vagueness literature for years, and the standoff between epistemicism and supervaluationism now strikes me as a debate in which every argument on both sides is already in print and nobody moves. The epistemicist says the sorites and our tolerance intuitions are reconciled by a sharp but unknowable boundary, backed by a margin-for-error constraint on knowledge, and diagnoses the absence of any distinctive feeling of ignorance as an artifact of how little speakers care about the boundary. The supervaluationist keeps classical logic and the classical theorems by quantifying over admissible precisifications, treats borderline sentences as neither true nor false, and then spends most of its energy on whether its notion of truth is really truth, on whether the determinacy operator can itself be vague without regress, and on whether the epistemicist's unknowability is intelligible at all. Each side has a rehearsed reply to the other's central objection; the exchange has the texture of a stalemate rather than a hard problem.

So: what kind of disagreement is this? My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the two sides are not disagreeing about the semantics of vague predicates at all, but about what a semantic theory is answerable to: the epistemicist takes as data the classical logical behaviour of the predicate in a language we treat as bivalent, the supervaluationist takes as data the phenomenology and the practice of speakers in the borderline region, and neither treats the other's data as binding.
