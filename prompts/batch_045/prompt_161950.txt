I clerk for a constitutional drafting commission in a small, religiously mixed polity — roughly two-thirds of the electorate belongs to two long-established churches, the rest to newer immigrant faiths, the unaffiliated, and a couple of strands of comprehensive secularism — and the clause we keep failing to agree on is a public-reasons clause attached to the legislature's standing orders. Our two expert advisers have been circling each other for a year. One is a restrainingist: there is a duty of civility, such that a citizen or official should not advocate for, or vote for, a coercive law unless she also has and is prepared to offer reasons she takes to be accessible to her fellow citizens apart from her own comprehensive doctrine. The other is an inclusivist: citizens and officials should speak and vote from the convictions that actually move them, religious ones included, and a duty to translate or substitute for those convictions is demeaning to the devout, epistemically naive about how moral reasoning actually works, and imposes an unjust asymmetry on precisely those citizens whose deepest commitments happen to be religious. They respect each other and I am fairly sure they have been talking past each other, so instead of another hearing I want the document an honest adversarial collaboration between them would have produced.

Concretely, I want three things. First, a numbered section of claims both could sign: about what coercion is and why it stands in need of justification at all; about what the restraint is for (legitimacy, stability for the right reasons, respect for persons, better deliberation — with priorities stated rather than listed); about what is plainly not at issue, since nobody is proposing that religious people be silenced in public debate, that churches be disestablished, or that religious premises be barred from identifying a social problem in the first place; and about what each side would count as its own view going wrong. Second, a disagreements table, one row per crux, with these columns: the crux in neutral wording; the restrainingist's own formulation of her position on it, worded so she would sign it; the inclusivist's own formulation of his, worded so he would sign it; a concrete case, institutional device, or piece of evidence both agree would bear on that crux whether or not it has ever been run; and each side's prediction of what it would show, written down in advance. Third, a short closing section marking which of the residual disputes are verbal or definitional rather than substantive — say which and why — and what, if anything, both would accept as actually settling one of them.

For seeding, here is where I suspect the real work is, though if you think the cruxes lie elsewhere put them where you think they lie. One: is the duty about the reasons available for a law, or about the reasons the agent actually acted on in voting for it? I keep coming back to a case where a religious argument and a secular argument converge on the same coercive law, and the officials who voted for it were moved only by the religious one. Two: is accessibility a property of a reason's content, or a fact about what these particular citizens in fact accept? Test it against the unanimous-believer jurisdiction next door, where everyone does in fact accept the same scripture as authoritative, and against the natural-law theist who thinks the secular reason is itself part of her religion, so that translation is trivially available to her and possibly insulting. Three: is the asymmetry objection real? The restraint is normally said to apply to comprehensive doctrines generally, but the secular comprehensive doctrines circulating in our own drafting notes — autonomy-based perfectionism, one or two strands of secular humanism — appear to be doing the same kind of work as the churches' doctrines, and I want to know whether both advisers think those are equally restrained and what follows if not. Four: compliance and legibility. I have floated to the Commission a device under which each legislator files, with every vote on a coercive law, a sealed one-paragraph statement of the reasons he actually acted on, opened after thirty years; everyone dislikes it, but both advisers agree the archive would tell us something. Where does that device sit in the table, what does each predict the archive would show, and would either accept it on its merits? Please write the whole thing as a text both advisers could put their names to, with formulations that bite rather than ones nobody could dissent from, and without citations or a reading list — the Commission has no library and wants argument only.
