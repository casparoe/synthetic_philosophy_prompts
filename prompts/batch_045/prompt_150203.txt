I sit on the restitution committee of a large national museum, and I want to know what our own track record commits us to. Please reason only from the precedents below — no outside law, conventions, or real institutions.

Our decisions so far:

1. Court bronzes looted in an 1890s punitive expedition from a West African kingdom, claimed by the royal court's successor institution and by the state: returned outright.
2. A marble frieze removed from a temple under a permit issued by the imperial authority governing the territory at the time, claimed by the present state: return refused; instead a renewable loan with joint stewardship and shared labelling.
3. A ceremonial shield taken in battle between two polities, later bought by us in good faith from a private collector, claimed by the descendant polity: returned outright.
4. Ancestral remains excavated without consent, claimed by an Indigenous council: returned outright, and all casts and photographs in our possession destroyed.
5. Grave goods of a people with no living descendants, taken during a colonial conquest and claimed by the state where they were found: return refused; kept under a co-curation and rotating-display agreement with that state's national museum.

The new case: a set of carved rain-shrine figures, made around 1880, still the central objects of a living cult with roughly nine thousand practitioners. In 1904 a district officer bought them openly, for cash, from the carvers' own family; the bill of sale survives, the sale was lawful under both colonial and local law, and there is no evidence of force or fraud. In 1961 the practitioners were forcibly resettled off their plateau: most now live across a border in a neighbouring country, a minority remain in the country where the figures were made. The figures may be seen only by initiated elders, but we have exhibited them for sixty years and hold 3D scans and plaster casts. The claim is joint, from the elders' council based abroad and from the neighbouring country's national museum, which has a humidity problem and lost objects to theft in 2011; our conservators say the wood is stable here and likely to crack there. The elders add that if we will not hand the figures over, they want the scans and casts destroyed and the figures withdrawn from display permanently, and will accept co-stewardship only on those terms.

What should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Give me: the two or three rules that fit our five precedents about equally well and what each predicts here; which of cases 1–5 is the closest analog and why it can't simply be copied; your best guess with the reason for preferring it; and the one further fact or further case that would separate the leading rules — in particular whether it is the legality of the 1904 sale, the living ritual relationship, or the condition of the claimant's museum that is really doing the work. One short paragraph per item.

Finish with a single line, with nothing after it, in exactly this form:
