Hi — I'm the one person on our university's Senate Committee on Academic Labor who was dumb enough to volunteer to draft the memo we owe the provost by next Friday, and I could use a hand getting it into shape. Quick background: our 1,800-ish graduate instructors unionized about two years ago, the first contract has been stalled forever over two items (health coverage for dependents and a hard cap on section sizes), and six weeks ago, instead of walking out, they voted for something they're calling a 'partial withdrawal.' Each instructor files a written declaration withholding a stated fraction of their contracted hours — most picked 20% — keeps teaching their sections and holding office hours, but stops entering grades, attendance, and the rubric-tagged feedback into the learning management system, and has stopped completing the learning-outcomes assessment forms the registrar needs for the accreditation self-study. Those forms also happen to be the training data for an AI tutoring pilot the provost's office is launching in the fall, which the union knows perfectly well. Their argument, which I think is genuinely stronger than the registrar wants to admit, is that the record of work is a seperate work product from the labor itself, that withholding a declared fraction while continuing to teach is more honest and more proportionate than an all-or-nothing walkout — nobody's rent goes unpaid, students keep getting taught — and that the binary strike is an artifact of a legal regime rather than the natural shape of the right to withhold cooperation. Our general counsel says it's a partial strike or deliberate slowdown, therefore unprotected, and wants to dock 100% of pay for participants and issue written warnings; two of us think that's thuggish; the registrar keeps pointing at roughly 400 graduating seniors and 90 or so international students whose records are now stuck; and the accreditation deadline is five weeks out.

What I want from you is the memo itself — the document the committee signs and sends up. It needs a bottom line: a concrete recommendation with dates attached. I'm currently leaning toward recommending that the provost treat the action as protected concerted activity for a defined window running to the accreditation deadline, with pay docked only in proportion to declared hours withheld, conditional on a written protocol (advance notice, a cap on the fraction any one instructor can withhold, and a carve-out requiring that graduating seniors' and visa-related records be entered within some fixed number of business days), plus binding mediation on the two stalled items and an undertaking not to discipline anyone who complies. But I'd rather be argued out of it than have it ratified, so push back if you think the recommendation should be different.

Beyond the bottom line, there are three things I want handled properly rather than gestured at. First, the conceptual one at the center: is withholding the record while continuing to perform the labor really continuous with striking, or is it a different animal — closer to a slowdown, malicious compliance, or something like sabotage — and does it change the answer that these particular records are a byproduct the university is now repurposing as training data for a system that will eventually be used to evaluate or partly replace the same instructors? Second, the students: the standard reply to third-party harm in a strike is that the harm is the employer's responsibility, since they can end it by settling, and that reply fits awkwardly here, where the harm is deliberately spread thin and open-ended rather than concentrated and brief, and where the people absorbing it are in some cases the strikers' own undergraduates. Third — and this is the one I most want done well — the strongest version of counsel's position, which I take to be something like this: the moral credibility of a strike rests on workers giving up the wage as a costly signal that they mean it, which is what distinguishes a withdrawal of labor from a holdout, and a tactic that keeps the pay flowing while applying pressure is a form of leverage without sacrifice that invites an arms race of ever more inventive pressure tactics and erodes the framework everyone depends on. Whichever side loses, give it the best statement you can, and tell me plainly where my proposed compromise is most vulnerable — my own guess is that demanding the carve-out hands back the only records that actually bite and guts the action, but if you think the soft spot is somewhere else, say so. Write it in language the union could actually answer rather than HR boilerplate, no headings or bullet points or bold or tables, continuous prose like an email to a colleague, and please don't cite anything or point me at things I'd have to look up, since I need to circulate it as-is. One last thing: I teach political philosophy, and half the committee visibly checks out when I start talking about rights to withhold cooperation in the abstract, so keep the reasoning tied to this case and this timeline instead of running it up to first principles for their own sake.
