I hold the following package in the philosophy of mind, and I want it stress-tested rather than summarised.

A mental representation has the content it does in virtue of a selection history: some downstream consumer mechanism was selected for responding to states of that type because they covaried with condition C, and so the state carries the content C (or whatever finer-grained content the consumer's actual job fixes). Because the etiological story plainly runs out somewhere, I bolt on a fallback clause: where there is no selection history to appeal to — a spontaneously assembled duplicate of a creature, the first member of a lineage, a representation type never selected for at all, such as a thought about electrons — content is supplied instead by a broadly interpretive, holistic account of the whole system's dispositions. I added the fallback because I find it incredible that such systems have no content at all, and I'd rather give up the etiological core's monopoly than accept that.

Tell me what would count against this package, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and say for each whether a committed teleosemanticist would concede it as counting against them or dismiss it. I'm especially interested in three exposures. First, indeterminacy: what does my detector-content have to be for a consumer selected for tracking moving dark specks — flies, food, prey, moving things — and if the view cannot deliver a determinate answer, is that fatal or merely a cost everyone pays? Second, drift: take a mechanism whose original function has contributed nothing to fitness for many generations because the lineage now lives in conditions where the mechanism tracks something quite different, and where that new tracking is what does the work. Which clause of mine decides that case, and does it decide it correctly? Third, the fallback: does it do so much work that the etiological core becomes decoration, and does the package thereby collapse into the interpretivist view it was supposed to replace?

Also say whether the position risks anything at all, once equipped with the fallback: what it predicts that its rivals don't, and whether it has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if it has, whether that is a problem for a view of this kind. I would personally find most persuasive a defeater that arises from inside my own commitments, such as a case where the two clauses give conflicting verdicts, rather than an intuition I have already discounted. Please answer in British English.
