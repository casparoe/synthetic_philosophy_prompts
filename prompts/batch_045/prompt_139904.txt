I work in operations at a regional freight company and I edit our staff newsletter. I have no academic background — no philosophy, no economics — but for about three years now I have been reading on my own about working hours: the old Protestant idea that diligence is a virtue in itself and idleness a fault; the thirty-five-hour laws and the long argument over whether they helped or hurt; and the word one country has for dying from overwork. What I keep noticing in all of it is not the disagreement but the agreement underneath it. Almost everybody now argues about hours in terms of measured output. Those who want shorter hours say output per hour goes up. Those who defend long hours say output per hour goes down. When the numbers come in the wrong way, the case for rest quietly collapses, and the man defending a sixty-hour week and the woman opposing one are weighing the same life on the same scale.

I would like a piece of satire that presses on exactly that, and I would be grateful if you would write it.

The form I have in mind is a management-consulting discussion paper: memo header, dated, from an invented firm to an invented corporate client, with a title in the register of a consultancy deliverable. Its proposal should be that the industry stop measuring hours altogether and adopt instead a single auditable, tradeable unit — something like a certified unit of fatigue or expended effort, banked by the employee, audited by the employer, and recognised somewhere in the accounts — on the grounds that this is what the debate has really been about all along. Invent the unit and its name yourself.

What I would ask you to observe:

Keep it deadpan all the way through. No puns, no winking, no paragraph in which the narrator admits the scheme is terrible. The recoil belongs to the reader, not to the memo.

Let the humour come from the mechanics, not from asides: how the unit is defined, how it is audited, what happens at the edges where it stops working.

Three of the sections must handle, without ever naming them as doctrines or as countries: a jurisdiction where the working week is capped by law, and how the cap is to be reconciled with the new unit; employees who volunteer for more than their allocation, who need a clause of their own; and a death attributed to overwork, booked in the flat language of an accounts manual, with no gore and no commentary.

The piece should let a reader reach three objections without ever stating them: that once rest is wanted only as an input to output, the defender of long hours has already won the argument about what work is for; that a thing which is counted and settled for stops being a thing anyone tries to prevent; and that a worker's own sense of whether their days are their own is not the sort of thing an instrument like this can register. No character should say any of these. They should be available and unasserted.

Do not make the joke about any country or national character; the joke is about the accounting. No real firms, no real people, nothing quoted as from a real writer, no academic vocabulary — a depot supervisor with no training should be able to read it and get most of it.

Close with an implementation timeline in three phases, the last of which is the point at which the reader understands what has been agreed to.

Hard limits: seven numbered sections at most, and seven hundred words at most, not counting the header.
