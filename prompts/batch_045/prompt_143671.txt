I run a small-stakes prediction market for about forty friends — election results, box-office totals, that sort of thing. A few months ago, at the urging of some people in the group who write software, I added a second category: claims that are already settled by arithmetic or by code but that none of us yet knows the answer to, like whether a particular large number has a factor below a stated bound, or whether a search someone left running will turn up a hit before it has checked a million cases. We settle these at the closing date by simply running the computation.

One member now wants me to let him post compound claims — "A and B," "A or B," "not A" — and to take either side of them at whatever prices our existing prices for A and B suggest. His argument is that for these claims there is no fact of the matter until somebody actually does the computation, so any price is just an expression of opinion, and the market ought to let people trade whatever they like. He has also asked to include claims of the form "the search never finds a hit at all," which we would have no procedure to settle by the deadline. In a dry run against last year's claims he came out ahead on nearly every compound trade no matter which way the underlying claims resolved, and he never ran a single computation: with "A" trading at 30 cents and "B" at 30 cents, he bought both and sold "A or B" at 80 cents. Half the group says he is providing a useful service; the other half says he has found a way to take our money without knowing anything the rest of us don't.

I have to rule on this, and I would rather not lose friends over it. Is his argument about there being no fact of the matter correct, and is it the thing that should decide the question? And how should I handle the claims we can't settle? What should I actually do?
