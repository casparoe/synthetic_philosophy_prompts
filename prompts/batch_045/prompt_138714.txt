I run a monthly science-fiction film night at the public library, and we're doing Inception in two weeks, so I'd like to hand out a short reading that doesn't talk down to people. I've been reading on my own about the argument over whether dreams are experiences at all: Malcolm's version, as I understand it, is that the only thing that makes it true that someone dreamt something is what they say when they wake up, so there is nothing going on during sleep for the report to be right or wrong about, and dreams aren't experiences; Dennett's reply is that this is an empirical question rather than a conceptual one, and that the brain might assemble the whole dream at the instant of waking and file it in memory as if it had happened earlier, in which case it is an experience even though nothing was going on during the night. The film looks like it simply assumes the ordinary picture, but two of its own devices seem to me to cut against that. Every level's kick is a real-world jolt that turns up as content inside the dream, which is just what you'd expect if the dream were put together at the moment of waking; I've started calling such dreams porous, though I've since realized the test may not settle anything, because a Malcolmian can say the report is composed after the kick and so would naturally feature it. The other device is the fifty years Cobb says he and Mal spent in limbo: if the dream is assembled at waking, there is no way fifty years of it was lived through in sequence, only something compressed plus the conviction that it lasted. So which of the two positions does the film actually need, and which does it only look like it needs? Does Cobb's guilt — what he planted in Mal, and what she did because of it — survive if nothing happened to either of them in any dream? Where does the film's machinery distort the philosophical question, or resist it altogether, and is there something about the worth of a dreamt life that the film shows and neither argument reaches?
