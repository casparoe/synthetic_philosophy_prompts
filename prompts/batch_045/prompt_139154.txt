I programme a film-and-philosophy series at a community cinema, and our next short season is on public memory: who gets put in bronze, whose name goes on the street sign, and what is owed to the people who have to walk past either. I want to close the season on Spike Lee's Do the Right Thing, which I think is a much better text for this than the usual statue-row arguments, because its central dispute is not about a monument at all: it is about a wall inside a private business, on a block in Bed–Stuy that (I looked this up) sits on Stuyvesant Avenue, carrying the name of Peter Stuyvesant, the last Dutch colonial governor of New Netherland. In my own notes I have been working with a distinction I have not seen made cleanly anywhere, between three things commemoration can be doing at once: *honour*, an assertion that the person or event was worthy; *remembrance*, an assertion that it matters to us and should not be forgotten; and *address*, a claim about who the public of this place is, and to whom the space is answerable. I have also been using two rough devices: *ambient commemoration*, for the kind that nobody is supposed to attend to — street names, neighbourhood names, the lettering on a building — where the point is precisely that the name has become part of the furniture, which is what makes it hard to challenge and, I suspect, what makes it powerful; and *the ratchet*, the fact that names and statues are typically installed by a determined faction and can only be removed by something close to a supermajority, so that "leave it alone, the status quo is neutral" is never a neutral position.

What I would like is a reading of the film through that frame, faithful in both directions. Concretely: take the Wall of Fame in Sal's pizzeria and Buggin' Out's campaign to get Black faces on it. What sort of claim is he actually making — honour, address, or something the three-way distinction does not capture? Does Sal's reply (that it is his wall, in his business, and that his heroes are his heroes) answer it, and does the film think it does? Then take the end: Radio Raheem's death at the hands of the police, the burning of the pizzeria, and the next morning's reckoning between Mookie and Sal, with the closing pair of quotations, one from Martin Luther King Jr. and one from Malcolm X, on the legitimacy of violence, followed by the image of the two of them shaking hands — the same photograph the character Smiley has been trying to sell on the street all day, as I remember it. Is the destruction of the pizzeria intelligible as a kind of counter-commemoration, and does the film present it as justified, tragic, or both? And is Lee's own framing device — setting those two quotations against each other and then leaving us with the handshake — itself a commemorative act of exactly the sort the season has been about, and, if so, is it doing it well or badly? Tell me too where the reading breaks down. My worry is that the Wall of Fame is not commemoration at all but decoration and fandom, so that my framework is a category mistake when applied to it; but then again the neighbourhood plainly does treat the wall as though it speaks for the block, which is the sense of *address* I was trying to isolate, and I think the film's real subject may be the contested publicness of a surface rather than any disagreement about the past — a question the philosophical literature mostly skips, because it assumes the monument already stands on public land. If you think that is a misapplication, say so plainly; I would rather have that in the notes than a forced fit.

The deliverable is about eight hundred words for the programme notes, in British English, plus two or three moments in the film you would pause on for the post-screening discussion, and one paragraph on what the film shows about commemoration that the arguments on their own do not — my hunch being that it shows these disputes as being about who has standing now, conducted among people who still have to live and trade with each other the next morning.
