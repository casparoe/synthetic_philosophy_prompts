I have no formal training in philosophy, so I hope you will forgive the homemade vocabulary. Reading on my own about which computations matter morally, I keep coming up against two pictures that I cannot get to touch each other.

Call the first the what-it-is-actually-doing picture: whether a system matters morally, and how much, is settled by the causal organization of the physical process going on inside it — which states depend on which, how information is integrated, whether there is a single place where competing goals get settled and a self-model that gets updated. How we find it useful to describe the system, and what we choose to call its states, adds nothing and takes nothing away.

Call the second the relative-to-a-reading picture: a physical system implements a computation only relative to a way of reading its states as computational states, and which readings are admissible is constrained by standards like simplicity, systematicity and usefulness. But within those constraints, which computations a system contains — and how many instances of them — is not settled by the physics alone; two admissible readings can disagree, and there is no further physical fact that decides between them.

I would like you to build me a thought experiment to the following specification. What I want it for: I keep going back and forth on whether the amount of moral weight in a box is something you discover or something you settle in choosing a description, and I want a case where that choice changes what somebody should actually do, so that I can see which answer I would have to live with.

Specifications:

1. One setup, describable in a single paragraph, that an engineer could really build now or in the near future — off-the-shelf hardware, no brain emulations, no exotic physics. Batched or shared computation, deduplicated execution, redundant controllers and the like are all fair game.
2. Both pictures should accept that there is some moral weight here, and in particular that a single instance of the relevant architecture, running alone on its own hardware, would be one subject that matters. The disagreement should be about how much is in the box, not about whether anything is.
3. The setup should make the what-it-is-actually-doing picture the inflationary one: it should have to count more weight than the other picture does — more subjects, or the same stretch of processing counted more than once — and it should get there from a principle a sensible person could hold, rather than from something silly I have tricked it into.
4. The relative-to-a-reading picture should come out with a determinate, smaller number, and should reach it through its own constraints rather than by saying that we may decide, so let us decide on one.
5. Whatever feature produces the gap should also make a practical difference: there should be something an operator could actually do — add an instance, delete one, perturb one until it diverges, start computing the shared part only once — that the two pictures rank differently.
6. Show me a nearby variant of the setup on which the two pictures agree, so I can see that the gap is not just a general standoff.

Please give me: the scenario in one paragraph; what each picture says about it and the reasoning that gets it there; which feature is doing the work, and what each picture says once you take that feature out, in both directions if there are two; the best reply open to whichever picture comes out worse, whether it is principled or ad hoc, and what it costs that picture; and an honest note on whether the case really generates both verdicts or whether I have built them into the setup, flagging anything you had to stipulate along the way. I do not need names, labels or references — I cannot check them, and I would rather have the machinery than a bibliography.
