I teach an upper-level seminar on Indian Buddhist philosophy, and I would like to open next week's session on Nāgārjuna's catuṣkoṭi with a joke that will land for students who have just worked through the opening chapter of the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā. Could you write me one?

Two constraints. First, the humor should turn on the actual mechanics of the fourfold negation rather than on Buddhist exoticism or on hearing "emptiness" as "nothing is real": the four cases — the thing arises, it does not arise, it both does and does not, it neither does nor does not — are each run through and rejected, and the point is not that a fifth, ineffable answer hides behind them, but that no account of a thing's arising, whether from itself, from another, from both, or from no cause at all, can be made to stand. Second, I would like the joke to double as a small lesson about prasaṅga, so that it also makes sense to someone who has read the dispute between Buddhapālita's defenders and their Svātantrika critics: on the reading I teach, the Madhyamaka speaker is advancing no thesis of his own, so the punchline should not let him assert one. If he must say something positive, let him say it as a reductio of his interlocutor's own commitments, or let him borrow it as conventionally true while declining to own it as ultimately true.

Please avoid the two cheap failures: a punchline that merely trades on "emptiness" meaning "the void" (or "no self, so no one is there to order a drink"), and a punchline that treats the emptiness of emptiness as a paradox to be admired rather than as the reminder that śūnyatā is a designation for dependent origination — a medicine against views, not one more view to defend.
