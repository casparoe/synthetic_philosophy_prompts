Two people in my reading group have been going at each other for about six months over whether an ant colony can have a mind, and I'm pretty sure that at this point they're mostly talking past one another, so I want to force out the document an adversarial collaboration between them should have produced months ago.

Camp one (I'll call her the colonist) holds something like Hofstadter's ant-colony thought experiment in Gödel, Escher, Bach, where he befriends a colony he calls Aunt Hillary and "talks" to her — you ask a question, wait, and read the answer off the pattern of her foraging trails — and she takes that at something close to face value: the colony itself is a candidate subject, something that thinks, notices things, has wants, maybe has a point of view, even though no single ant has any of the thoughts. Camp two (the skeptic) holds that the colony is a spectacular distributed control system and that holding a conversation with Aunt Hillary is closer to reading a Rorschach blot: the intelligence is in the ants and in us the interpreters, and there's nothing that it is like to be the colony.

What I want is the joint statement, not a verdict on who's right and not a dramatized back-and-forth. Concretely:

- A numbered agreed-claims section. Make the claims non-vacuous — things a thoughtful outsider would actually deny or be surprised by, not "ants are interesting". I'd expect them both to be able to sign something real about what no individual ant can do on its own, about the trail network carrying information and feeding back into behaviour, about the queen not being in charge, about why "solves problems no single ant can solve" is not by itself enough, and about what the conversation test can and can't establish even on the colonist's own terms.
- A disagreements table with the issue, the wording the colonist would accept for her own view, the wording the skeptic would accept for his, and a column saying whether you think that row is a substantive disagreement or just a fight over "mind" / "thinks" / "conscious" / "agent" / "subject". Where a formulation would be vacuous or slanted ("the colony is obviously just a machine"), rewrite it until a hostile reader from the other camp would sign it.
- The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them, and for each one the case or argument both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance — including a number where a number is meaningful.
- At least one thing each side would count as evidence against their own view.
- A short note on which parts of the dispute are verbal and why.

Please make them work through these five cases specifically; they're the ones I think actually seperate them:

1. The ablation ladder. Swap ants out one at a time for "filler" ants that walk the same routes and shift the same amount of dirt but neither deposit nor follow pheromone, so the colony's traffic looks identical to a camera while the signalling is slowly gutted. Does Aunt Hillary fade out smoothly or is there a point where she stops being there at all? At roughly what fraction does each side expect the conversation to break down, and do they read the breakdown the same way — a performance failure versus the disappearance of a subject?

2. Fission and re-merge. Split the colony into two viable halves, each with a queen and enough workers, keep both foraging for a week until they've diverged and both are readable as conversational partners, then put them back together. One mind, two, none? Did the original survive? And does each of them think there is a determinate answer here, or is the determinacy itself part of what they're arguing about?

3. Gradual robot replacement. Swap in, one at a time, tiny robots that reproduce each ant's signalling behaviour exactly, so the colony's behaviour is indistinguishable throughout. Does Aunt Hillary survive? Does either of them's answer change if the robots are behaviourally identical but get there by a completely different internal mechanism — i.e. does the colonist's view commit her to the colony's mind being mechanism-independent, and is the skeptic's objection really about the biological parts or about the colony-level claim?

4. Overlapping candidate systems. Two neighbouring nests whose foraging trails merge into a single connected chemical system that reads as one conversational partner, while each nest also reads perfectly well on its own. How many minds are there? Does the colonist's view commit her to an absurd count (if she bites the bullet, say so plainly), and does the skeptic have any principled way of picking which of the overlapping systems is the real candidate, or does he just get to say none of them?

5. The unread colony. Same colony, same causal structure, except nobody ever reads its trails — no Hofstadter figure around — and in the nastier variant some other species reads them systematically wrong. Is being a mind the kind of thing that depends on there being a competent interpreter around, or is that just an epistemic point about how we'd ever find out? My hunch is that this is where they're definately disagreeing about observer-relativity without realising it.

No citations or reading lists, and please don't turn it into a dialogue or give me a verdict — I want the document both of them could put their names to, with what's left over stated exactly.
