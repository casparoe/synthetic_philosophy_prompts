I teach a final-year seminar on the epistemology of assertion, and an argument I originally wrote as a teaching example has turned round on me. It appears to saddle the knowledge norm with a conclusion that no one should accept, and I cannot locate the fault.

Let me say at the outset what I want to keep, so that you can price the options against it. I want to keep the knowledge norm — the thesis that a speaker may assert that p only if she knows that p — and I want to keep it as a norm speakers can actually apply, not merely as a free-floating standard of correctness that no one is ever in a position to consult. I want to keep the platitude that permissible assertion is closed under conjunction: if I may assert p and I may assert q, then I may assert p and q. And I want to keep the denial of the sceptical conclusion below. I do not want a survey of the rival norms — truth, reasonable belief, credal threshold — and I do not want a bibliography; I know the options. What I want is the faulty step named, an account of why that step looked safe, and a price for each of the ways out.

The case. Vera certifies sealed units in batches of five thousand. Her test on any one unit is 99.9% reliable, and the tests are independent of one another. She also knows, from a manufacturer's guarantee that she has excellent reason to trust, that exactly one unit in each batch is faulty, and nothing in her results singles out which. Her grounds for any particular "unit i is sound" are, taken one at a time, as good as grounds ever are outside a philosophy seminar: a test she has run herself, on an item in front of her, with a known and very low error rate.

The argument.

(1) A speaker may assert that p only if she knows that p.

(2) Permissible assertion is closed under conjunction: if a speaker may assert p and may assert q, then she may assert p and q.

(3) Vera's evidence for each "unit i is sound" is of a kind and quality that yields knowledge whenever merely fallible evidence yields knowledge at all. Nothing about her situation is exotic apart from the guarantee that one fault is somewhere in the batch.

(4) So, by (1) and (3), for each unit i, Vera may assert that unit i is sound.

(5) So, by (2) applied across the batch, Vera may assert that every unit in the batch is sound.

(6) But Vera knows, from the guarantee, that not every unit is sound. By (1), she may not assert that every unit is sound.

(7) Given (2) and (6), (4) must fail for at least one unit; and since her position is exactly symmetrical across units, it fails for every one. So there is no unit of which Vera may assert that it is sound.

(8) Her grounds for each conjunct are no worse than yours or mine for saying that the train leaves at 9.10, that there is milk in the fridge, or that a signature is her grandmother's handwriting. So if (1), (2) and (3) are all in order, the knowledge norm forbids essentially all ordinary assertion, and forbids it on the strength of grounds that all of us rely on all the time.

(9) That is not acceptable. A norm that prohibits nearly every assertion anyone ever makes is not a norm we have, and I would give up almost anything rather than accept it.

What makes the result harder for me to shrug off is the way the prohibition distributes across time and hearers. Suppose Vera spends the day telling each of five thousand inspectors, separately, that his unit is sound, and suppose we grant (4) and say that each of those utterances is perfectly in order. By the evening she has signed off the batch; every inspector knows his own unit is sound; and the inspectors between them possess everything Vera has told any of them. Asked in front of all of them whether every unit is sound, she must say that she does not know, and on the knowledge norm she must refuse the assertion — even though she has just asserted each of its conjuncts to the very people in the room, and even though those people can work out for themselves that one unit is faulty. It looks as though what Vera may say depends on how the content is packaged and on what her hearers already have in hand.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?

If you intervene at (2), I take it you are committed to saying that assertion is not aggregative: there are cases in which each of a set of claims may be asserted but their conjunction may not. I want to know what that does to the practice. Can a speaker convey a content piece by piece that she may not convey as a whole, and if so is that a defect in the norm or a discovery about assertion?

If you intervene at (3), you need to say what is missing from Vera's grounds for each conjunct, and — this is the part I cannot do — why the missing ingredient is not also missing from the ordinary perceptual and testimonial grounds we count every day. I would like the comparison carried out rather than asserted.

If you think the fault is not in any numbered premise but in something the argument assumes without stating — about what sort of thing a norm of assertion is, about the difference between a claim's being epistemically in order and its being permissible all things considered, or about the relation between knowing something and being able to tell that one knows it — then say what that assumption is and what happens to the knowledge norm once it is withdrawn.

Two constraints on your answer, if you will. First, it is no use appealing to what is at stake: run the case with novelty keyrings and nothing hanging on the outcome, and the structure is unchanged. Second, whatever you propose should leave the knowledge norm able to do the work that made it attractive in the first place — in particular, explaining why it sounds wrong to say that p but one does not know that p, and why an assertion invites the challenge "how do you know?" in a way that saying what one believes does not. If your honest conclusion is that the argument is sound and that I should simply pay one of these costs, tell me which cost is cheapest and why.
