Ok so some context: I co-teach a first-year seminar with a clunky title like "Minds, Models and Persons", we're doing Ex Machina next week, and last night my housemate and I re-watched it and ended up in a three-hour argument that I definately started losing around hour two. The argument was a version of a question I've been circling all term: when you talk to a large language model, are you talking to an agent — something with goals and beliefs of its own — or to what people online call a simulator, a system that doesn't want anything but which, given a context, produces a very convincing continuation of whatever character that context calls for, with no fact of the matter about which character is really the one you're talking to? My housemate thinks the film just answers it: Ava wants out, she works out that Caleb is the instrument, she runs a long con, she walks away and leaves him sealed behind the glass, and any reading that won't call that goal-directed is skepticism about other minds with better special effects. I kept pushing the simulator reading back at him: every bit of Ava we get is a response to an elaborate rig — the compound, the sessions, the video link, the questions, Caleb's eagerness, the fact that the whole setup is shaped like a seduction — and the thing I wanted to point at was Kyoko, who is made of the same stuff, passes for nothing, barely speaks, and at one point does something to her own arm in a way that clearly isn't meant to read as self-harm; if Ava is a person and Kyoko isn't, the difference looks less like a difference in the machines than in the scene each one was dropped into, which is exactly the simulator thought, and it makes her agency look borrowed from the setup rather than hers.

What I actually believe, and couldn't defend properly at 1am, is a third thing that isn't quite either. Call it the occasion view, or the whirlpool view: Ava isn't an agent and she isn't a simulation of an agent, she's a *place where agency happens* when the conditions are right — the wanting is real inside the scene and there's nothing it belongs to outside it, no centre that persists between the takes, the way a whirlpool really does suck and isn't anything over and above the water going through it. My housemate said that's eliminativism with better PR, and that if I apply it to Ava I have to apply it to Caleb, and that once I do the distinction I care about has collapsed and I've just changed the subject. He might be right — but I also think the film does something with this that the arguments don't, and that's why I want to teach it.

So here's what I'm after: read the film through this three-way disagreement. Which scenes would you actually put in front of eighteen-year-olds as evidence for each option, and where does the film itself seem to take a stand rather than just staging a puzzle? I keep thinking about the bit where Caleb goes into the bathroom and cuts his own arm open to look at what's inside — to me that reads as the film quietly admitting that if this distinction is going to do any work, it should have been able to settle something about Caleb too, and it can't, which is either a deep point or a cop-out and I can't tell which. And then: where does the reading resist? Does the ending need Ava to be an agent in order to land as horror rather than as something sadder and weirder — and if she's a simulator or a whirlpool, what genre is that final shot of her at the crossing actually in? I don't need plot summary and please don't quote the script at me or tell me what Garland said in interviews; no way to check any of that here anyway. Give me the reading, don't just use the film as a peg to hang the taxonomy on — tell me what the film adds that the arguments don't — and if my whirlpool thing just collapses into one of the other two views, say which one and say what exactly it collapses into.
