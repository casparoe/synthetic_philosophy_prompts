I did numerical weather prediction for about thirty years, and now that I'm retired I've started reading my way into philosophy of history — big change of scene, and I cant always tell whether I'm reading it right.

What I want from you is a number. Give me your credence that within roughly the next fifty years a cliodynamics-style research program — the quantitative, structural-demographic sort that looks for slow secular cycles in state stability rather than for great men and contingent accidents — will be able to forecast macroscopic political instability with real out-of-sample skill. By real skill I mean it beats a flat baseline in which instability just occurs at some roughly constant background rate; I don't mean that it fits the cases we already knew about, which is what most of these models seem to do.

And I want it decomposed: a probability and one line of justification for each of roughly these, then how they combine — that there are quantitative macro-historical regularities at all rather than each episode being sui generis; that the variables these models need can be reconstructed well enough from proxies; that the dynamics aren't so chaotic or path dependent that nothing beyond very short horizons is ever available; that genuinely novel things keep entering the system and breaking the cycles; and that the field itself survives long enough, with enough data, to find out. Then tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to.

Two things on top. My own background makes me think the chaos objection gets overstated: sensitive dependence kills forecasting the orbit, but not the claim that the system is drifting toward a different regime, and I'd weight that distinction heavily — tell me if you wouldnt. And my number is around 0.25 while my brother, who read one popular book about this, is at 0.6; I suspect we aren't disagreeing about history at all. Where does that gap most likely live, and what single piece of evidence would move you most?
