I'm a novelist and next month I'm giving the annual Ethics and Public Life lecture at a university I won't name — forty minutes, then twenty of questions, and the chair has already told me he'll cut me off at forty-five. My new novel is about a woman who monitors Japanese shortwave broadcasts for the US censors in San Francisco in 1945 while her brother flies B-29s out of Tinian, so I've been living inside this material for three years, and I've got a thesis I want to put on the table rather than just read an extract and take softball questions.

Here's roughly what I'm arguing, so you know what they'll be aiming at.

One, Anscombe was right, in the fuss she made when Oxford voted Truman an honorary degree in the mid-fifties, that the bombing was murder, and right about *why*: the whole "it shortened the war / it was the least bad option" framing is a way of declining to let the action be described truly. It answers a different question than the one asked. I call this the grammar of exemption in the book — the first-person plural ("we had no choice"), the counterfactual body count, the passive voice, all doing the work that a justification should be doing.

Two, my test, which I want to be the engine of the talk: the empty city. If the target were an arsenal or a troop concentration, and the people in it were miraculously gone, the mission would still have succeeded — better, in fact. Now ask the same question about Hiroshima: the city evacuated overnight, the same bomb, the same blast, the same rubble, nobody in it. Does the mission still count as what it was for? I say no, it fails, and that tells you the deaths were what was aimed at and not an unfortunate side effect of hitting a real target.

Three — and this is where I go past her, and the part I most need help defending — I don't think the intended/foreseen distinction actually has room for the wrong that's in play, especially on the second bomb, three days later. Those deaths weren't only a means to a surrender. They were also an utterance. Someone was being said something to: the Emperor, the Supreme Council, and, I'd argue, Moscow, which had declared war on Japan the day before. So I want a third category alongside intended and merely foreseen, which I've been calling addressive killing: killing a person partly in order to say something to somebody else, using a death as a sentence in a language the intended audience can read. My claim is that this is worse than plain murder in one respect — the victim isn't even being removed because of what she's doing, she's being used as a medium, as a kind of sign — and that a framework built only on intention either misdescribes it as a side effect or flattens it into ordinary murder and loses what's distinctive about it.

Now the room. I need to be ready for *this* room, not for a book festival audience:

- Two or three analytical Thomists / neo-Aristotelians who work on Anscombe and her circle. They'll be glad someone is defending her and they will hate my third category. I expect them to say that double effect already handles everything I'm pointing at, and that adding categories is what happens when a novelist reads *Intention* once.
- A bigger block of revisionist just-war philosophers, the liability-and-individual-responsibility crowd, some of them fairly utilitarian. Their instinct will be that intention talk is pre-revisionist machinery, that the only real question is whether those civilians were liable to be killed, and that my empty-city test is a clever way of asking a question that doesn't matter.
- Two historians of the Pacific War. One works on the Japanese side and will correct my chronology and my targeting details — I think I have right that the first bomb and the second were treated as a continuing operation rather than two fresh decisions, and that the Soviet entry into the war matters as much as either bomb, but I'm not confident and I'd rather be told now than from the floor. The other is hostile to the whole "atomic diplomacy" reading — that the bombs were aimed at Moscow as much as at Japan — and I gather thinks it's a tired 1960s argument that the archives don't support. If my whole third category rests on that reading, I need to know how much of it I can drop and still have a category.
- One or two international-law types who will say that deliberately attacking a city is already a war crime and that "addressive killing" is unusable in law, so what exactly am I adding.
- And then the public who turn up to these things. There's always someone whose father was in the invasion force and who will want to know whether I'm calling him a murderer.

I did one term of moral philosophy as an undergrad and dropped it, so they will smell blood the second I misuse a term.

What I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of my position. Give me the objections I'll actually get, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it — I want the Thomist objection to sound like a Thomist and the historian's to sound like a historian, not a generic list of "some might object." For each one: a two-minute answer I could actually give standing up, and an honest note on where that answer is soft. Tell me what I should concede in the first five minutes to take the wind out of them, and what I must not concede because the talk collapses without it. Tell me which single reply has to land. And flag anywhere I'm likely to get a term wrong or overstate a historical claim, because I definately will.

The question I'm hoping nobody asks, by the way, isn't from the panel. It's from the floor: if Nagasaki is addressive killing, then what is the firebombing of Tokyo, and why did nobody — including Anscombe — call that murder at the time? I don't have a good answer to that one and I'd like to.
