I run a monthly reading group out of the back room of a pub — about 14 people, mostly programmers and ex-academics, plus one actual historian who I have to bribe not to say anything until the end. We get 90 minutes, a whiteboard and one long table. Once a quarter I do a session on how we get the past wrong, and I want to build one around the habit of writing history as a road that leads to (or away from) the present.

What I want is that people *do* the thing and only afterwards notice they've done it. No mini-lecture up front, and nobody in the room should hear the phrase whig history, or any synonym for it, until the debrief.

The specific thing I want them to feel is that this isn't just triumphalism about winners. As I see it there are three separable moves, and the interesting one is the third:

- reading with the ending in hand, so whatever happened looks like it had been on the cards all along and the alternatives look like they were never really live (the hindsight / creeping-determinism bit);
- building the story only out of the people and ideas that turned out to be ancestors of something present, so the losers — including the ones who were the absolute centre of attention at the time — quietly vanish from the account;
- and then scoring the past by its distance from us, either how close it got or how far we've fallen from what it had.

The bit I think nobody has properly internalised is that the third move runs backwards just as happily. A decline story that selects ancestors of the present badness and treats them as having been obviously doomed is structurally the same vice, and half my group holds exactly that kind of story about something and thinks of it as the critical, anti-progress one. I want them to catch themselves running the same trick twice, in opposite directions, inside one session.

So: design the session. Invent the archive — I definately want it made up rather than real, both because nobody should be able to win by already knowing the answer and because I don't want to get fact-checked by the historian. Give me a fake controversy from roughly the 1890s to 1910s (a municipal policy fight, a professional society split, a technical standard, whatever), a set of short documents, and a real outcome that's genuinely mixed: the side that eventually "won" won for reasons that are not in the documents at all, and the thing everyone at the time treated as the whole point turns out not to matter.

There should be something at stake, small but real — points, wagers, or an explicit prediction record — so that at the end I can hold their early selves up against their later selves. That receipt is the whole point of the evening. No real primary sources and don't quote anything real; make it all up.

Sections please, with rough lengths:

- Prep (150 words): what to print, what to keep hidden, how to seat people.
- The archive (400 words): the documents themselves, or enough of them that I can fill in the rest.
- Run sheet (400 words): minute by minute, including what I actually say out loud.
- Debrief (400 words): which moment is supposed to produce which realization, and how I name the three moves without it collapsing into a lecture.
- Failure modes (250 words): what predictably goes wrong — especially the historian, the guy who smells the trap and writes a deliberately contingency-flavoured narrative, and the person who says "but isn't all history written backwards anyway."
- What this can't teach (150 words): the part of the idea the exercise structurally can't reach and I'll just have to say out loud.

Commit to a design: pick one, give me your reasons, and flag the one or two places where you're guessing about how it'll actually land and I should have a fallback ready. Don't hand me three options to choose between.
