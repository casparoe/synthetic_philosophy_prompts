I'm retired — thirty-odd years in experimental atmospheric physics, aerosol work mostly, nothing anyone's heard of — and for a bout three years I've been working through philosophy in a slightly systematic way, which has turned into a small podcast I record in the back bedroom for other retired science types. Next month's guest is Dr. Miriam Ostrow, a philosopher with a new book arguing that some minimal experience goes all the way down, and that the unpleasantness of a system's experience is set by a physical quantity you can actually measure: roughly, the rate at which the system degrades free energy, how fast it burns through available work on the way to equilibrium. Stars and accretion disks, on this picture, are where nearly all the experience in the universe is; brains are a local curiosity; a rock sitting at equilibrium is experientially blank. She's careful to say this doesn't make her a panpsychist of the naive sort, and she thinks it has real ethical consequences, which is why I booked her.

What I'd like is eight to ten questions, in language I can say out loud to an audience of retired engineers, each with a one-line note on the answer she's most likely to give and a follow-up keyed to that answer. I've read enough of the book to know how the conversation will go and I don't want her to get through it on charm.

Things I want covered:

- The dominance problem. If the quantity she cares about is dissipation rate, then nearly everything that matters morally is happening in stars and accretion disks, and everything anyone has ever worried about on Earth is a rounding error. She'll probably say that only experience bound into a single subject counts, and that most astrophysical dissipation isn't. The follow-up has to be: what's the physical criterion for that binding, in terms I could in principle compute, and does the criterion itself scale with dissipation or with something else entirely.

- When the deflection comes. Whenever the combination problem comes up she says that the science of which physical systems form unified subjects is young, and that this is a research program, not an objection. I want a short sequence — two or three questions in a row — that makes that reply progressively harder to lean on, ending with a single question about what evidence or result would count against her view, or about what she'd have to give up if the criterion never arrives.

- Whether the consciousness is doing any work. If the bad thing is fast degradation of free energy, why isn't the honest position just that dissipation is bad, with the experiential bit bolted on afterwards? And separately: if each fundamental event carries only an unimaginably tiny amount of unpleasantness, how do you ever add up to anything that matters? My instinct from physics is that you can't get a large finite value out of a sum of near-nothings unless the sum diverges, and I want to know whether her addition rule is really an addition rule or is quietly importing a threshold.

- The hardware question, which is the one I care about most. Two systems running what I'd call the same computation, one implemented efficiently and close to reversibly, one leaky and wasteful. On her account the second has more experience and more of whatever is bad about it. If she says yes, I want the follow-up about whether pain depends on how badly your substrate is engineered; if she says no, I want to know what the physical quantity was for in the first place.

- A question from my own patch: which formulation of physics her bookkeeping is supposed to run on. In statistical mechanics I can count transitions once I've fixed how I'm coarse-graining, but the count changes with the coarse-graining, and I don't see how to count events at all in the field-theory version, or why the count would come out the same for two observers. If the amount of experience in a region depends on how I choose to describe the region, or on who's watching, that looks like a problem for the view rather than for my arithmetic, and I want a question that puts that to her in a way she can't wave off as a technicality.

- The sign of the thing. Why degradation of free energy should come out as unpleasant rather than pleasant or neutral. Is there anything in the physics that could settle the direction, or is the direction an extra postulate she's brought in from somewhere else? And if the direction did turn out to be the other way, would the view have any ethical upshot left?

Finally: she's said in other interviews that there are practical upshots. I want one question near the end that pins her to something concrete — what a research funder, or someone deciding whether to build a large datacenter, should do differently if she's right — with a follow-up ready for the case where the honest answer is that nothing changes, because that's definately where I think she's headed and I'd like to hear her say it.
