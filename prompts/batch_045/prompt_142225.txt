I'm writing the second paper for my upper-level seminar on Arendt, and I want to use Jonathan Glazer's 2023 film The Zone of Interest as my central case. As you'll recall, the film keeps the camp almost entirely off-screen — the wall, the gunshots, the trains and furnaces are heard from the garden while the frame stays with the Höss family's domestic routines — and it ends with Höss retching on a Berlin stairwell and a cut to present-day janitors cleaning the Auschwitz-Birkenau museum.

My thesis is that the film's formal strategy dramatises a variety of the banality of evil that Arendt's account needs but does not separately name. Distinguish three familiar versions: motivational banality (the agent's aims are ordinary — career, comfort, family), deliberative banality (his reasoning is slogan-governed and shallow), and distributed banality (the division of labour means no one performs the whole deed). I want to add a fourth, perceptual banality: the suffering lies outside the agent's field of attention, so that he can look at the wall and see a garden. Three questions, please, in under 600 words and with no plot summary:

1. Is perceptual banality genuinely a fourth notion, or does it collapse into one of the other three — or into Arendt's own idea of thoughtlessness, understood as the failure to take up the standpoint of another?
2. Which moments would you use as evidence for the reading, and which actively resist it? I am especially unsure about the Berlin party scene, where Höss spends the evening working out the most efficient way to gas the people in the room, and about the retching.
3. What is the strongest objection a sympathetic Arendtian would press? My worry is that she locates the failure in judgment and imagination rather than in perception, and that an agent can perceive perfectly well while nothing he sees counts as a reason for him — in which case my notion is at best a cause of the condition, not the condition itself. Sketch the reply I should give.
