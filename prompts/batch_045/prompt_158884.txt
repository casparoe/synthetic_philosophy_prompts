Imagine Condorcet shown a modern engineering practice rather than a political one. A lab has built a jury for binary factual questions: it instantiates a thousand copies of the same base language model, gives each copy a different random seed and a different system-prompt persona, has each vote yes or no, and takes the majority. Individual copies are right about 62% of the time on the question set; the majority of a thousand copies is right about 71%, and adding copies beyond a few hundred changes nothing.

Write his reply to the lab, in the first person, about 500 words. The substance should be the gap between what his jury theorem predicts and what the engineers measure, so engage it directly: are these copies voters in his sense at all, given that they share weights, training data and fine-tuning and therefore fail the independence condition; would he read the plateau as a refutation of the theorem or as a measurement of how badly its assumptions fail; and what would he actually prescribe, given that he wanted both to raise each voter's competence and to get the aggregation rule right? Have him also confront two wrinkles: letting the copies deliberate before voting reportedly raises individual accuracy while lowering the accuracy of the majority, and new voters can be instantiated for a few cents, which makes the theorem's headline prescription — add voters — trivially cheap and apparently worthless. If the jury is asked to choose among five candidate answers instead of two, majority cycles appear; let him notice that this is his own paradox returning. Where his political commitments — suspicion of entrenched authority, faith that instruction raises competence — pull him toward a reading the mathematics alone would not give, say so.

End with a brief note, separate from the voice portion, stating how confident you are in the reconstruction and isolating the single claim in it you are least sure of.
