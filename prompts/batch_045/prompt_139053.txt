I'm a staff engineer on the platform team at a mid-sized software company. I have no philosophy background — one freshman course I remember badly — and I've been volunteered to give a forty-five-minute internal brown-bag next month on "worst-case versus average-case guarantees in AI safety," a title someone else picked and that I'm free to change. The room will be roughly twenty-five people: mostly backend and infrastructure engineers, four or five ML people who actually train and evaluate models, three PMs, and one lawyer from the policy team who asked to sit in. Almost everyone will walk in with two things already in hand: the intuition that this is a temperament difference (careful people want worst-case bounds, pragmatic people ship on averages), and the reflex example of quicksort's quadratic worst case against randomized expected linearithmic behavior. I ran the talk past my partner, who's a statistician, and she compressed the whole thing into "it's just a question of your reference class" in about five minutes — correct, I think, but useless for this room, and I'd rather not build on the sorting example even as a thirty-second on-ramp, because in sorting a bad case costs you time and I need them to feel that here the bad cases can be rare, individually cheap-looking, and correlated with one another.

So don't explain the distinction to me — plan how I should explain it. What I most need is the dependency structure: what has to be in place before "we have a worst-case guarantee" or "we have an average-case guarantee" is even a well-formed sentence. My own guess is that a guarantee is three-place rather than one-place — a bound on some named metric, over some named set, for some named system-in-a-context — and that people collapse three different axes into the phrase "worst case": worst or average over inputs including an adversary with a stated budget; over which model you happened to get out of training, given seed and run-to-run variance; and over the future deployment distribution. But I can't sequence it, and the two points I care most about are ones I keep failing to place: first, that an average-case claim quietly assumes failures are independent across components and across time, when the interesting failures are precisely the ones where a single input breaks three things at once, and that if we only ship systems that pass evaluation then the population we measured is a selected subset of the population we deploy, so the average we estimated isn't the average we get; second, that a worst-case claim is trivially satisfiable by a system that does nothing, so the real object of interest is never a bound on its own but a bound paired with a usefulness floor. Tell me where each of those has to land and what has to be said first for them not to sound like word games.

Beyond the sequencing I need three more things. One: the single example that should carry the whole forty-five minutes, with an account of what it must have and what it must lack — my candidate is our internal code-migration agent, which has write access to a subset of repos, but I'll take something better if that's the wrong shape; my constraints are that the bad outcome has to be discrete and easy to picture, the tail has to be fat and correlated rather than a clean independent coin flip, and it must not smuggle in anything that reads as science fiction. Two: the misconceptions in the order this particular audience will form them, and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off — I'd guess they'll include "worst case means jailbreaks and average case means normal users," "with N components the risk just divides so averages are fine," "if we've bounded the worst case we've bounded the risk," and "the guarantee is a property of the model rather than of the model plus its scaffolding and permissions" — and I'd like a closing check that would show it landed, not just that people were interested; I was considering handing out a one-line invented spec of a guarantee and asking which axis it quantifies over and what single edit would render it vacuous, but tell me if that's a bad test. Three: what I should openly decline to cover at this level, and a sentence I can use to decline it that doesn't sound like evasion — my instinct is to rule out whether future systems will be agentic or on what timescale, interpretability internals, formal verification and probabilistic certificates, and expected-value reasoning under deep uncertainty, on the grounds that they're real but are a different talk, and I'd rather say that explicitly than have the lawyer or one of the ML people drag us into it for twenty minutes.
