ok so I've been reading this stuff since the old SSC days and I basically accept the standard arithmetic: most wild lives are probably net-negative by a wide margin, the numbers dwarf everything we do to farmed animals, and the fact that nobody is causing it doesn't obviously let us off the hook. But my gut verdicts on actual interventions are all over the map and I want to know whether there's a principle behind them or whether I'm just rationalizing a yuck reaction. Please assume for the sake of argument that we finally have a decent welfare metric — allostatic load plus time-budgeting plus stress hormones, validated well enough to rank populations — so that "we can't measure it" isn't doing any work.

My verdicts:

- Aerial oral rabies vaccine baits for foxes and wild dogs: fine, obviously, no hesitation.
- Culling an invasive species that humans introduced, when the alternative is watching it eat a naive endemic fauna: fine. I'll kill a lot of animals here and not feel bad.
- Immunocontraception for overabundant deer instead of culling: fine, and I mildly prefer it.
- Restoring a drained wetland, reforesting an upland: fine, I'm enthusiastic. Which is weird, because I know it increases wild-animal-years and by my own arithmetic that's bad.
- A gene drive spreading a heritable allele that turns down pain sensitivity in a widespread rodent: not fine. Feels like a violation of something I can't name.
- A drone-and-camera "welfare warden" system that continuously scans a reserve and humanely euthanizes whatever it finds injured, starving or diseased: not fine, actively dystopian — even though per dollar it's plausibly the best intervention on this list.
- An injured deer I find on a hike: I help it, obviously.
- Eradicating a species whose typical life is net-negative by the metric: not fine. I balk harder then at the culling case, even though the culling case kills more animals for worse reasons.
- Feeding predators through a hard winter so they take fewer prey: I'd rather not, and I don't love that I can't say why.
- The one I actually keep going back and forth on: a gene drive that doesn't change anyone's experience at all, only life history — mice have three big pups a year instead of twelve mostly-dying ones, same total population, far fewer miserable deaths. Some days that reads to me as just better culling; other days it's obviously the same overreach as the pain-dulling drive.

My tentative rule, which I don't trust: it's fine to change the flows — how many animals there are, how and when they die, which diseases and predators are in the picture — but not the forms: what the animals are like, what they're capable of, what a species-typical life consists in. Flows not forms. It handles 4, 5, 7 and maybe 8. I don't think it handles 6 (pure flow, still hate it) or 9 (also flow). And I have no idea what it says about 10, which is the case I care about.

So: is there one principle that captures most of this, or am I defending three unrelated rules with one mouth? What does it say about 6, 9 and 10, and is flows-not-forms a real moral distinction or just a fancy name for "gene drives are icky, give to bednets instead"? (Related embarrassing datum: I believe the arithmetic and I still don't give to wild-animal stuff.)

Answer as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, no preamble and no wrap-up: (1) the principle, stated sharply enough that I could apply it cold; (2) which of my cases forces which clause; (3) its verdicts on 6, 9 and 10, plus one new case of your own that I'd actually learn something from; (4) which of my verdicts it forces me to give up; (5) the strongest reason to think the whole pattern is aesthetic discomfort with a moral story bolted on afterward. Skip citations, I know the literature.
