Hey — I'm on the incident review board at the company I work for. We run a couple hundred autonomous sidewalk delivery robots, plus a dispatcher model that reassigns jobs between them, plus a small remote-ops team that can step in. When something goes wrong — a robot clips a cyclist, blocks a wheelchair ramp for forty minutes, a dog gets a paw caught — we meet within 48 hours and have to come out with three things: whether anyone gets a written warning, retraining, or gets pulled off remote ops; what goes in the report that a regulator may read; and what we say to the person who was harmed. Right now we basically wing it, and it collapses into one of two failure modes: we blame the remote operator because they're the only human visibly in the loop, or we shrug and say the system did it.

I've read a fair amount of the philosophy on responsibility gaps and I don't need a literature overview and I don't want any citations or reading suggestions. What I need is a procedure.

Give me a decision procedure we can actually run in a 90-minute meeting with people who have never done philosophy, ending in one of a small fixed set of verdicts. I want the questions in a fixed order: cheap factual questions first that knock out the easy cases fast, the expensive and contested ones later. For each question tell me what counts as a sufficient answer, what evidence we'd need to have to answer it at all, and where the procedure should just stop rather than push on.

The distinctions I want built into it — I've got my own names for some, rename them if you have better ones:

- causal vs epistemic vs attributability gap: no human was suitably connected to the outcome / we can't establish who was / someone was connected but blaming them would be unfair. Keep these apart, because they call for totally different responses, and I suspect we currently collapse the middle one into the first because it's less awkward to write down.
- the swap test: if we dropped a different, reasonably trained person into that role on that day, would they have done the same thing? If yes, whatever we're doing isn't giving anyone an incentive — it's doing something else. Name what it's doing and give me a non-blame response instead.
- the update obligation: after the first weak signals, did anyone have a duty to pull the behaviour or retrain the model? I think this is where most of our "someone is responsible after all" verdicts secretly come from, and I want it as its own node rather than smuggled into "was it foreseeable."
- the crumple check: is the person we're about to blame actually the one who could have prevented this, or just the most visible one sitting nearest the failure? I want a concrete test here, not a vibe.
- the retribution check: seperately from what blame is warranted, how much will the person harmed and the public want to hand out? If those come apart, that's a design and communications problem, not a blaming problem, and the procedure should route it somewhere else entirely.
- a no-blame-but-not-no-response verdict, with what it actually obliges us to do — repair, apology, design change, audit — so it doesn't decay into the shrug.

Also: mark clearly where the procedure can't settle something and a human just has to make a values call. I'd rather those be flagged than hidden inside a test that looks objective. And give me a section on how we ourselves could game this: I can already see one version where "no suitable agent" becomes the default finding and everybody quietly walks, and another where we find a scapegoat in ten minutes because the first question on the sheet is "who was nearest the system."

Then run it on three cases:

1. A remote operator had three robots in the attention queue, ignored a low-confidence obstacle alert for about ninety seconds, and the robot mounted a kerb and fractured a pedestrian's ankle. Alerts of exactly this type had fired forty times before and been dismissed every single time without review.
2. After a firmware rollout, a couple dozen robots started hugging the building wall on one particular street. Nobody on the team had seen this failure mode in simulation or in the wild. A blind pedestrian got pinned between a robot and a wall for roughly a minute. There were three earlier customer complaints about robots acting weird near that corner, closed as duplicates.
3. A dog got its paw caught in a wheel well. Nobody foresaw it, nobody plausibly could have, and there's no design change that obviously prevents it without making the robot useless. The owner is furious and wants someone fired. My guess is this one is a genuine gap — I want to see what your procedure says we owe them and, just as much, what it says we don't.

Constraints: ninety minutes, ops lead running it off a checklist, and no philosophy vocabulary in the output itself, because the rationale goes into a document a regulator might read. Every verdict needs a one-paragraph written rationale we could defend out loud. Assume nothing about what our lawyers will say — I want the moral version first and we can bolt the legal layer on later.
