I've been going back and forth on a made-up case and I want someone to just settle it for me. Here it is.

Nell and Otto are two philosophers arguing about whether there are any composite material objects — tables, chairs, people, that sort of thing. They've got a complete physics and they agree on every non-philosophical fact, including exactly which particles are arranged tablewise and which aren't. Otto says: strictly speaking there are no tables, there are just particles arranged tablewise (plus maybe the particles themselves). Nell says: of course there are tables.

Nell is a quantifier variantist: she thinks "there are" can mean lots of different things, differing in what falls in the domain, and that no one of those meanings is the uniquely correct one. So she's happy to say: under my meaning of "there are", yes, tables; under yours, no; nothing further to fight about; I'm still going to keep talking my way. Otto holds the opposite. He thinks some candidate meanings carve nature at the joints better than others, that this is what makes one quantification privileged over another, and that "there are" should be assessed by whether it tracks that structure — so Nell's sentence is true-in-her-idiolect, but her idiolect distorts the world.

Now the twist. Suppose both of them come to accept something I'll call the Tie: it turns out that the two quantifier meanings in play — the one ranging only over particles, and the one ranging over particles plus all their fusions — are exactly equally joint-carving. Nature has no preference. No privileging structure singles out either. Both Nell and Otto fully accept this.

And yet Nell keeps saying "there are tables" and Otto keeps saying "there are no tables," and neither of them thinks they are squabbling about words.

So: is this dispute merely verbal? Write me an essay that works through it and actually lands somewhere.

Some things I want you to do:

- Run whatever the right test is. I have in mind the paraphrase / elimination move: get both parties to restate their claim in neutral terms, disambiguate the contested expression, and see whether any disagreement about the world survives. So actually write down the neutral sentence or sentences that Nell and Otto should both be able to assent to, and then tell me whether their both assenting to it really does end the argument. If it doesn't end it, say what's left over, and say whether that leftover is the kind of thing that makes a dispute non-verbal.

- Take a stand on the leftover. Suppose what remains is a disagreement about which quantifier to *use* — about norms of assertion, or about which language is more useful, or about which framework to do science in. Is that enough to make the original dispute substantive? Or should we say the ontological dispute is verbal and there's a separate genuinely normative dispute travelling alongside it? Dont just say "it depends what you mean by verbal" — pick a reading, argue for it, and then say in a sentence or two what your verdict would be on the other reading.

- Deal with the overgeneralization worry. Whatever account you use to get your verdict on Nell and Otto, try it on a case where I think the answer is clearly that the dispute is substantive: a theist and an atheist arguing about whether there is a God, in a world where they agree on all the historical and physical facts. If your account spits out "merely verbal" there, that's a strike against it, and I want to see you deal with that.

- One more: is "verbal" a property of a dispute at all, or only of a pair of sentences relative to a meaning? Nell thinks the whole thing dissolves; Otto doesn't. One of them might be in a better position to dissolve it than the other. Say whether that asymmetry matters for your verdict, and why or why not.

Reach a conclusion and defend it. No fence-sitting ending, and I dont want citations or a literature review. What I do want: be clear about which of your claims are definately settled among people who work on this stuff and which are live controversies that a well-informed person could deny — don't dress a contested claim up as the consensus. Then flag the single premise your verdict leans on hardest, and say what happens to the verdict if that premise is false.
