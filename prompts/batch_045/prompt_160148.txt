I'm putting together a three-year research programme — myself plus two postdocs, conceptual work only, no empirical component — on whether conscious experience could be causally inert and on what follows if it is. We've decided to concentrate on the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if phenomenal states make no causal difference to what we say, think, or write, then our judgments about our own experience are not explained by their subject matter, and the worry is that this strips those judgments of warrant — including the judgment that epiphenomenalism deserves to be taken seriously.

What I need is a defended agenda, not a survey. Assume we can pursue three lines of work properly and must drop everything else. Rank the candidate lines and defend the ranking on how much else depends on their answer, on tractability in three years with conceptual tools alone, and on how much difference the answer makes downstream — to the case for dualism, to illusionist and other eliminativist options, and to whether the standard anti-physicalist arguments can be trusted at all.

To make this concrete, here is the map I'm working with, including some distinctions I've invented to organise it. Tell me where the map is wrong as well as what to prioritise.

1. The tracking reply. Phenomenal states are causally inert, but a non-phenomenal introspective mechanism reliably co-varies with them, so phenomenal judgments are reliably produced and qualify as knowledge. My worry here is what I'll call the surrogate problem: a co-varying surrogate explains why judgments are accurate only if something explains why they co-vary at all — a bare correlation looks like a second coincidence stacked on top of the first.
2. The constitutive or acquaintance reply. The judgment that one is now in pain is not merely caused by the experience but partly constituted by it, or the phenomenal concept deployed is such that undergoing the experience suffices for being acquainted with it. The dividing question, as I see it, is whether acquaintance is meant to be a causal relation, a merely modal one, or something sui generis — and I suspect the answer to that does more work than people admit.
3. The common-cause reply. Experience and judgment are joint effects of a single physical cause, so the co-variation holds as a matter of natural necessity within the actual nomological order. This is the option I find least discussed. It predicts that the correlation is contingent in a strong sense — nothing underwrites it under perturbation — and it forces a question I want either prioritised or dismissed: whether knowledge requires its reliability to be modally robust, or whether reliability within the actual laws is enough.
4. The scope question. Does the paradox stop at first-person judgments about current experience, or does it generalise? Two generalisations worry me and I can't tell which matters more. Diachronically: if our judgments about experience are not explained by experience, what explains the long-run convergence — or failure to converge — of an entire research literature on consciousness? Argumentatively: the conceivability and knowledge arguments that motivate epiphenomenalism in the first place are themselves judgments about the relation between physical and phenomenal facts, and if they are not causally explained by the phenomenal facts they concern, the paradox may consume the very arguments that generate it. I suspect this last point is the most upstream item on my list, and I want you to press hard on whether that's right or whether I am overweighting a dialectical flourish.
5. The dissolution options: illusionism and other eliminativisms about the phenomenal, or the retreat to dual-aspect or neutral monism, on which the causal question is redescribed rather than answered.

Two tests I've been using to sort these, which you should feel free to reject. The grain test: does a proposal predict that judgments discriminate fine-grained phenomenal differences — nearby shades, the precise character of an afterimage — or only coarse ones such as pain versus itch or seeing versus hearing? A view that secures only coarse reliability secures at best coarse knowledge, and I want to know how much that matters. The perturbation test: does the proposal let us say anything about what would happen if the correlation between experience and judgment broke down, and if it does not, is that a cost or a feature?

What I want in the response: a ranking of at most three lines of work with the reasoning explicit, including the criteria you actually applied and the trade-offs between them, saying where and why you weight upstreamness against tractability; a statement of which options on my map you take to be dead ends, and which option I've omitted that you'd add; a dependency map in prose, specifying what must be settled before what and which pairs of questions can be worked on in parallel without loss; and some account of what, appearing over the next three years, would move an item up or down — a conceptual programme gets no experimental results, but there must be something, a formal result or a shift in the dialectic, that could function as a checkpoint.

Throughout, mark clearly what is settled or near-settled among people who work on this, what is genuinely contested, and where you are going out on a limb or extrapolating past anything with a real literature behind it. This is going into a case for support, and I don't want to discover afterwards that something I presented as the state of the art was one side of a live dispute, or my own invention. If any of the distinctions above already have names in the literature, or collapse into distinctions that do, tell me which — but don't pad the answer with citations; I can find those myself.
