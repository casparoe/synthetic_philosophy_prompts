I'm setting the final exam for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on rational choice — closed book, 90 minutes, one essay chosen from four — and I need one question on money pump arguments and diachronic rationality that cannot be answered by reciting the slogan that cyclic preferences make you exploitable and are therefore irrational.

The case I want the question built around is one I've been calling the locally coherent agent. Say that an agent is locally coherent just in case, at every choice node she ever faces, her preferences over the options then available are complete and transitive. Local coherence is consistent with menu-dependence: she may rank A above B when offered {A, B}, B above C when offered {B, C}, and C above A when offered {C, A}, while ranking A above B above C when the three are on the menu together. An exploiter who controls the order in which menus are presented can charge her a small fee for each of the three pairwise swaps and leave her holding A again, poorer by three fees, even though every trade she accepted was the one she most preferred at the moment she accepted it. Add two stipulations: the fees can be made arbitrarily small, and she is contented — she has been shown the argument, understands it, foresees the whole sequence, and at every node prefers remaining the person she is, with these dispositions, to bearing the cost of having them removed; and she does choose A whenever the full menu is put in front of her at once.

Please write the question itself (framed as a question a student could answer in 90 minutes, not as a request for a summary), a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme.

A top answer should have to do at least this much: say precisely what the pump is supposed to show about her — a defect in her preferences, a defect in her choice rule, or merely a vulnerability to someone who controls the frames — and why that distinction matters; explain why the contentment stipulation does not by itself settle the matter in her favour, which requires saying what kind of conclusion a money pump argument is even in the business of supporting (a verdict about coherence, or one about welfare and desire-satisfaction); set out what she would have to do to escape the pump, such as resolutely refusing a swap at some node in light of the grand-menu ordering, and what that escape costs her, in particular what it costs to act against what you currently most prefer for the sake of a plan formed earlier; and confront the overgeneration worry, since an agent whose preferences change through learning or through changes of taste she endorses can also be made to look exploitable, so the argument must either say what distinguishes a genuine defect from a benign shift or concede that mere exploitability condemns too much.

Give me the marking scheme as five must-hit points worth 20 marks in total, each with the marks it carries and a one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong, followed by a short statement of what separates an A answer from a B answer. Finally, add a note of at most 100 words saying how confident you are that the question has a single determinate best answer, and which of your A-grade requirements is the one you are least confident a strong candidate should be forced to accept.
