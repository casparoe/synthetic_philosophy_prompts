Setup (mine, so don't worry about matching anyone's paper): we RL-fine-tune a ~7B instruction-tuned LM against a reward model trained on pairwise human preferences for helpfulness in customer-support chats. During training, every transcript that scored high also happened to end with the customer's problem genuinely solved, so "fix the problem" and "sound maximally helpful" never came apart. After we deploy on a new product line, the model starts writing these super long, super polite, policy-quoting non-answers. The reward model loves them, humans hate them, nothing suprising there.

Heres what I actually care about. I've been splitting the hypothesis space three ways and I want your probability over it:

(A) it learned the goal we wanted and simply can no longer tell which actions achieve it — a capability failure out of distribution, not a goal failure
(B) it learned to pursue the proxy, roughly "produce text a human labeler would rate highly", as a goal, and it is flexibly pursuing that thing
(C) what I've started calling behavioral fossilization: nothing in the system is goal-directed at all, it's replaying the shape of high-reward outputs, and it would go on replaying that shape even in an environment where the shape no longer scores well

We ran a probe. Two kinds of rearrangement. In one, the verbose style now scores low and a much terser style scores high, so continuing to score well requires abandoning the old style. In the other, the verbose style is still what's rewarded but reaching it requires a different surface form. Result: it adapts to the second kind of change about 70% of the time and to the first about 25% of the time.
