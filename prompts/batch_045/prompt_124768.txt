You are a professional philosopher who works on both epistemology and aesthetics. Before you rule on anything, spend a moment laying out the distinct claims you will need to keep apart here — it seems to me that "knowing that a work is good," "knowing that you will like it," and "knowing what makes it good" may come apart, and I want to see whether you agree.

Two friends of mine argued about this over dinner and I couldn't referee it. Marcus had been told by a critic he trusts that a new film was a masterpiece. He hadn't seen it, but said he now knew it was good. Dana said that's impossible: aesthetic judgments have to be made first-hand, and no one else's word can put you in a position to make one. The exchange, roughly:

(1) Dana: Aesthetic value isn't like being shot in French or being released last Friday. It is a matter of how the work strikes a suitably situated viewer, so you can know about it only by occupying that position yourself. What testimony gives you is excellent evidence that you will enjoy it — but that is a prediction about your own future reaction, not knowledge of the work.

(2) Marcus: Then take the best possible source. I have compared my reactions with this critic's on a couple of hundred films and we agree about 94% of the time; our sensibilities are demonstrably aligned. Whatever worry there is about our tastes diverging, it has been answered — his saying so is as reliable a signal as anything you get in science.

(3) Dana: Reliability was never the issue. Even if he were a perfect indicator, believing it because he said so is the wrong kind of basis. You would have no sense of what makes the film good, could not point to a single feature, and could not defend the verdict if challenged. You would have a true belief unanchored to any appreciation of the thing.

(4) Marcus: That confuses whether I know with what knowing is for. Plenty of knowledge — the result from a lab you never visited, the theorem whose proof you cannot reconstruct — comes with no ability to defend it and is knowledge nonetheless. Mine may be shallow or second-hand, but calling it not knowledge looks like moving the goalposts.

(5) Dana: Then change one thing in your own case. Suppose the recommendation came not from the critic but from your own diary, in your own hand, written twelve years ago, about an album you have since completely forgotten. You are as reliable an indicator of your past self as anyone could be, and the belief would arrive with the feel of memory. I would still say you do not know the album is good until you put it on. If that is right, the obstacle is not the interpersonal source at all; it is that no basis which skips the present experience can get you there.

Marcus had to leave and never answered the diary case.

Please adjudicate: rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, justify each rating briefly, and mark how confident you are in each. Then give an overall verdict on who is more right, tell me the strongest reply available to Marcus against move (5) and whether it works, and say what, if anything, both of them are missing. If the exchange rests on a confused assumption — if "knowing that it is good" is not one thing, or if the diary case does not test what Dana thinks it does, or if the 94% figure is doing less work than Marcus supposes — say so plainly rather than answering the question as posed.
