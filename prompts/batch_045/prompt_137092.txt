My philosophy of logic reading group has been going in circles for two weeks, and I'd like an outside diagnosis. One member built the argument below, and its conclusion is stronger than anything any of us is prepared to accept, yet none of us can locate the break.

(1) In Lewis Carroll's dialogue, the Tortoise accepts A and also accepts the conditional 'if A then Z', and still declines to accept Z; what he asks for is a further premise to the effect that if A and if-A-then-Z are both true, then Z is true.

(2) Supply that premise and he can ask for another licensing the new three-premise inference, and another after that; no finite stock of premises ever gets him to Z.

(3) The moral generalizes: a rule of inference can move a reasoner only if she already grants, as a further premise, something that encodes that rule.

(4) But treating a premise as a reason for anything is itself an inference, so by (3) it stands in need of its own licensing premise.

(5) Hence no rule of inference is ever what makes anyone infer anything: deduction cannot be justified to someone who is not already playing by its rules, and no one's coming to accept a conclusion is ever explained by a rule she accepts.

(6) Nothing in (3)–(5) depends on the rule's being deductive. The same structure arises for induction, for testimony, for the reliability of perception, for any principle whatever: a consideration can justify you in believing something only if you already accept the principle linking that consideration to that conclusion.

(7) Therefore no argument has ever justified any belief; every justification is circular, regressive, or arbitrary. And since (1)–(7) is itself an argument, it cannot justify its own conclusion either.

(8) Yet (7) cannot be asserted: it is offered as the conclusion of an argument, and if it were true we would have no reason to believe it. So something above is false.

Here is the variant that makes it harder for me. Call him the Cooperative Tortoise. He grants you modus ponens itself, and he will grant you every conditional you ask for; what he withholds is any connection between granting a rule and being governed by it. He will happily add 'Z follows from A together with if-A-then-Z' to his list as one more entry alongside the others, and then ask what is supposed to make him believe Z rather than merely write it down. The three replies I keep being given — that a rule is not a premise, that you cannot justify to someone a rule he does not already use, and that the regress only shows you cannot coerce a reasoner by argument alone — all seem to me to be replies to the original Tortoise, and I cannot tell whether any of them reaches the Cooperative one.

Two of us would most like to keep (5), three of us would most like to keep (3), and none of us will accept (7). Name the step you would reject, and price the alternatives.
