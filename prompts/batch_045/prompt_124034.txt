I'm a journalist who covers social science and ideas for a national magazine, and I've been awarded a nine-month fellowship at a policy institute on the understanding that it produces two things: one long feature for a general audience, and one working paper that people who actually draft electoral rules could use. The selection committee — two political scientists and a philosopher — wants a two-page proposal next week, and it will fund the project only if the proposal shows that the interesting part is not reporting: it wants the question stated so that it has a determinate answer, an argument that the existing answers leave it open, a method, and an honest account of what would count as the project failing. My editor thinks the magazine piece should be about turnout decline; I think that is the boring version of the story, and I'd rather the proposal not go there. Please don't include a literature review or citations — I'll add those myself — but the conceptual architecture does need to be sharp enough that the working paper says something a drafter of election law couldn't have arrived at unaided.

Here is where I've got to, and I'm aware it's half-formed. The three franchise reforms that come up again and again in the places I report on — compulsory voting; lowering the voting age, usually to sixteen; and extending the vote to resident non-citizens, at least in local and school-board elections, as a few jurisdictions have tried and some have had reversed in court — are almost always argued about separately, by different people, on different grounds. Compulsory voting is defended in the language of civic duty and the fair sharing of a collective burden, roughly on the model of jury service. Lowering the age is defended with developmental evidence about adolescent decision-making capacity, plus the observation that sixteen-year-olds work, pay tax and are subject to the law. Non-citizen voting is defended by pointing to stake rather than status: someone who lives under a city's ordinances, pays into it, sends children to its schools and can be deported for breaking its rules has an interest in its decisions that formal membership does not capture. My suspicion is that these grounds do not cohere, and specifically that the duty framing and the stake framing carry opposite implications about what the vote is. If the vote is something you owe your fellow members, it is odd to extend it to non-members, and odd to treat it as a shield against coercion that you are entitled to. If the vote is a protection you are owed because the state coerces you, it is hard to see why you may be compelled to exercise it — you don't owe anyone the exercise of a protection — and hard to see why the coercion of a fourteen-year-old generates less standing than that of a sixteen-year-old, unless there is quiet competence-based reasoning doing the work. So the left-ish package that bundles compulsory voting with franchise expansion may be the least stable combination available, while compulsory voting plus a competence-restricted franchise, and voluntary voting plus a stake-based expansion, each hang together. I keep losing versions of this argument at dinner parties with friends who work on campaigns, which is not evidence, so I'd like the proposal to state it as one question rather than three and to give a principled reason to think the tensions are genuine rather than a reflection of who happens to like which reform.

What I want the proposal to supply, beyond the precise question: why the existing positions leave it open, given that the literatures barely talk to each other and that where they do they argue about turnout or about the abstract problem of who counts as affected by a decision rather than about concrete packages of reform; a method that is genuinely philosophical, since I can't do original survey work and don't want to fund it — my own idea is a handful of test cases at the intersection of the three reforms, used as a diagnostic for which justification yields which verdict: the sixteen-year-old permanent resident who has lived in the same city since she was three, works part-time, will be eligible to naturalise at eighteen and hasn't got round to it; the middle-aged citizen who says he has a conscientious objection to voting and would rather pay the fine; the long-term resident with no legal status whose children are citizens; a statement of the possible conclusions and which considerations would decide between them; and a description of the deliverable I have in mind, which is something like a compatibility map — a table of the standard justifications, the reforms each one supports, and the packages that can and cannot be defended together — that someone writing a city charter or an electoral code could actually consult. Then say plainly what would count as failure: the duty and stake framings turn out to be complements rather than rivals; the map merely reproduces the bundling that political actors already exhibit; the competence threshold turns out to be the only real crux and the rest is decoration; or the whole thing collapses into the general boundary problem and I have no way to stop it expanding to future generations, prisoners and corporations. Finally, be realistic about scope: tell me what a nine-month, one-person, non-specialist project cannot do, and which part you would cut first.
