Consider the following two candidate norms governing the speech act of assertion. The first is a familiar position; the second is a proposal of mine that I want you to take seriously as a rival to it. Do not consult or cite any sources, and do not treat the first norm as authoritative simply because it is the one that gets discussed more often — argue for your verdict on the cases.

**(KN) Knowledge norm.** A speaker may assert that *p* only if the speaker knows that *p*.

**(GN) Grounded-answerability norm.** A speaker may assert that *p* only if (i) the speaker's asserting is appropriately guided by considerations the speaker takes to support *p*, and (ii) the speaker is disposed and able, without first gathering new evidence, to offer at least one such consideration if challenged — where a consideration "counts" only if communicating it would tend to raise the credence of a reasonable addressee in *p*.

Note that (GN) is deliberately two-part and deliberately audience-involving: it constrains both the causal-psychological history of the assertion and the speaker's standing to answer for it to the person addressed.

Please compare the two norms in detail, addressing at least the following.

1. **Divergence cases.** Work through (a) a journalist who asserts that a minister took a bribe, knowing it on the basis of a source she is ethically bound not to name and cannot otherwise corroborate; (b) a speaker who knowingly retains a true belief but has wholly forgotten where it came from and could not reconstruct any ground for it; (c) a speaker who sees what is (unbeknownst to her, and without any her-visible difference) a cleverly constructed fake — the standard barn-facade environment; (d) a speaker who infers from published base rates alone that a particular lottery ticket lost; (e) a spokesperson who asserts, on a committee's behalf, that the funds were misallocated, where the evidence is distributed across members and no single member individually satisfies either norm. For each case say what (KN) and (GN) predict, whether the prediction matches your sense of the case, and whether the mismatch should be charged against the norm or against the case's description.

2. **The Moore-paradoxicality test.** One standard argument for (KN) appeals to the oddity of asserting something of the form *p, but I don't know that p*, and contrasts it with the lack of comparable oddity in *p, but I don't believe that p*. Run the parallel test for (GN): is *p, but I can't give you any grounds for p* objectionable in the same way, or in a different way, or not at all? What does the result show about whether (GN) is a candidate *constitutive* norm of assertion rather than a norm of conduct in its vicinity?

3. **The challenge-and-retraction data.** Ordinary practice licenses the challenge "how do you know?", permits a speaker to answer it by citing a source rather than by producing an argument, and treats certain assertions as calling for retraction rather than mere excuse. Which norm explains this pattern better, and is the explanation available to (GN) also available to (KN) in some form, or does it depend on (GN)'s specific architecture?

4. **Audience-relativity and zero-audience cases.** (GN) makes permissibility depend on the addressee. Is that a defect? Test it against assertion in a private diary, assertion addressed to a child or to an expert, assertion to someone who already knows, and assertion to an audience the speaker wrongly takes to be hostile. If audience-relativity is a defect, can (GN) be reformulated indexically — an assertion is answerable *to whoever is addressed* — without collapsing into (KN)?

5. **What kind of norm would (GN) be?** Suppose you conclude that (GN) is false as an account of when assertion is *permissible*. Say whether it might nonetheless be true as an account of something adjacent: of when an assertion is *blameworthy*, of when a hearer is entitled to resentment or to discount the speaker's testimony, or of what it is to *tell someone something* as opposed to merely *assert* in their presence. Specify what diagnostic would distinguish a constitutive norm of assertion from a norm of these neighbouring acts, and apply it.

Close by giving a verdict on which norm, or which combination of the two, you would defend, and state the single strongest objection to your own position in the form you find hardest to answer.
