I've been reading the rationalist blogs for something like ten years — long enough to have internalized the usual moves about calibration, base rates, and noticing that you're inside-viewing — but not long enough to know any of the actual academic vocabulary, which is why I'm asking this here rather than looking it up. The pattern I keep running into goes like this. At a meetup last month someone argued that our forecasts in some domain should be discounted, because the people who reason most carefully about that domain have historically done worst on it. I said that the claim itself had been produced by exactly the kind of careful reasoning it was impugning, and he said that was just a debating trick, and I said no, I meant it structurally, and we went in circles. Then last week I had a genuinely bad reaction to a medication — foggy, slow, I could feel it — and while foggy I judged that I was too foggy to trust any judgment I formed, and then noticed that this judgment was itself one of the foggy ones, and I couldn't tell whether that made it self-undermining, self-confirming, or neither. A third case, from work: a colleague says she doesn't trust her own assessment of her team because she knows she's biased in its favor; when I asked how she knew, it turned out she knew by way of the same assessment.

I think there are at least three things tangled together here, and I want them separated if they really are different. In the medication case the evidence that the faculty is broken arrives *through* the broken faculty. In the colleague case that may not be so — she might have perfectly good independent evidence, like a track record — but applying it still requires her to use the faculty she's been told to distrust, in order to work out how far to discount and in which direction. And there's a version I keep hitting in arguments: two people disagree, and whether the other one counts as an epistemic peer in this dispute is itself one of the things they disagree about, adjudicated by the very faculty in dispute, so each side's case for not deferring is exactly the sort of claim the other side says they're unreliable about. What I want is not a label but the carving: whether what I've gestured at is one thing or several, how close the nearest established concepts get, and whether the loop bottoms out at all — whether "this evidence tells me my reasoning is unreliable, and the evidence is mine" has a stable resting point at some definite confidence, or whether the honest answer involves oscillating, or suspending in a way that is itself unstable. It also feels different when the defect is total ("your judgment is worthless right now") versus partial ("there's a small chance it's badly broken, and telling which case you're in requires using it"), and I can't tell whether that's a difference in degree or in kind. Please don't send me to a reading list or tell me which papers to look up; I want the distinctions in your own words, and I'll go find the literature later if there turns out to be any.

Could you answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no preamble and no closing summary? Something along these lines: (1) the nearest established concept or concepts to what I've described, stated precisely enough that I can check whether it's my thing; (2) exactly what in my description fails to fit it; (3) the next-nearest candidate, and whether it fits better or worse and why; (4) if nothing established covers it, a name you'd coin plus a one-sentence definition that would let me recognize an instance; (5) what happens at the bottom of the loop — fixed point, oscillation, or suspension — including one boundary case on either side of which the phenomenon stops being what it is. For what it's worth, the word I half-remember from practical reason, about acting against your own better judgment, has never felt like the same thing, so if the nearest concept is named by some extension of that word I'd like to know why the extension is apt rather than a pun.
