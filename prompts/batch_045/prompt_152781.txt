I've been working through Kripke's skeptical paradox about rule-following on my own for a few months now — no classes, just secondary material online and one book — and I've run into a worry I can't settle. I'd like a case built to order so I can see whether the worry is real.

Where I've got to: every presentation I find runs the argument on the arithmetical example. A speaker has added numbers up to some point; a rival rule is cooked up that agrees with ordinary addition on all the small pairs and then delivers something different on a big one; and then we're told that nothing about the speaker — no disposition, no inner state, no fact about his past behavior — settles which of the two he meant. My suspicion is that the force of that example comes from things arithmetic happens to have: an endless supply of cases, almost none of which the speaker has ever touched, and rival rules that can be manufactured to order by stapling an arbitrary cutoff onto any rule you like. If that is what is doing the work, the paradox is a local curiosity about indefinitely extendable rules and not a general worry about meaning at all.

But I've also noticed something that cuts the other way, and I can't tell which of the two is the real engine. Two rules can agree on every case — actual, counterfactual, all of them — and still be different rules. "Add two" and "write the next even number" never come apart in what they produce. If that is enough to generate the indeterminacy, then finitude was never needed, and the paradox is general after all; but then I also don't see why anyone should care, since nothing differently would happen on either hypothesis. I'd like that tension addressed rather than smoothed over.

So: build me a case that generates the skeptical conclusion without the arithmetical machinery, and see what it takes.

Specifications:

1. An ordinary realistic setting — a workplace, a shop, a clinic, a workshop, a family. One competent adult speaker, one expression or practice, one paragraph, intelligible to a bright fifteen-year-old. No science fiction, no philosophical apparatus: no brains in vats, no deceiving demons, no thought experiments nested inside the thought experiment.
2. There must be a live question about which rule the speaker has been following, with at least two candidate rules on the table.
3. Both candidates must be rules a normal person could self-consciously adopt and state in a sentence, and neither may be gerrymandered: no arbitrary cutoffs, no "and after that, do something else" clauses, no rivals reverse-engineered to fit the data. Both should be rules that someone in that setting would describe as the natural thing to be doing.
4. The indeterminacy must be about which rule the speaker is following, not about where the boundary of a term lies — no vagueness, no sorites, no borderline cases — and not about a word's having two senses.
5. I want to know whether the case can be made without the speaker simply never having met the relevant kind of case. If you can build it so that the speaker has faced the hard case and is still genuinely unsettled, do that. If you can't, build it the other way and tell me honestly which premise you had to lean on and whether it is the finitude premise wearing a disguise.
6. The intuitive datum has to be that there is a fact. A reader should come away thinking "obviously she meant one of these," and the skeptical verdict should be unwelcome rather than easy. A case whose natural reaction is "sure, no fact there" tests nothing: the paradox is only a paradox if the conclusion collides with what we ordinarily take for granted.

After the scenario, what I want:

- Run the skeptic's argument on the case. Say what the skeptic points at, why the speaker's dispositions and past behavior fail to settle it, why the wider community's practice fails to settle it either, and what exactly the conclusion is meant to be — that there is no fact of the matter, or that facts about meaning get replaced by something weaker?
- Say what the two standard replies do with your case: the realist reply that treats meaning as primitive and not reducible to anyone's dispositions, and Kripke's own replacement of truth conditions with assertibility conditions grounded in what the community will accept. For each: does it give back the ordinary conviction, or only explain why we have it?
- Name the single feature of your case that is carrying the weight, and say what happens if it is removed — if one candidate rule is made unnatural, or the speaker is made to answer promptly and without doubt, does the paradox still bite?
- Give the strongest attack available to whichever of those positions comes out of your case looking worst.
- Then answer my actual question: does the paradox generalize past the arithmetical case, or did you have to smuggle back in something equivalent to the finitude of the speaker's evidence? And if you built it instead from rules that never diverge in what they produce, tell me whether the indeterminacy you get is one anyone has reason to care about.

On confidence, and where I want you to push back: please mark plainly where you are reporting a received position from the literature and where you are reconstructing, improvising, or drawing your own inference — I have no way to check, and I would rather know which is which. If my specifications cannot all be satisfied together, or if the question rests on a misunderstanding — if the finitude point is not doing what I think it is doing, if I have mislocated which premise does the work, or if the worry I raised about co-extensive rules is a confusion — say that directly instead of producing a case that quietly violates a constraint I'm not in a position to notice. And if the honest answer to "does this generalize" is that there is no settled answer and people argue about it, tell me that rather than picking a side so the answer can end.
