I'm a philosophy professor (early modern stuff, nothing to do with any of this) and last spring I got voluntold onto the university's committee on historical commemoration — the body that handles petitions about the names on buildings, the plaques, the portraits in the faculty club, and who gets a named chair. We get five or six cases a year, we meet for ninety minutes once a month, we have one history grad student who can give us maybe six hours of research per case, and whatever we decide has to be defensible in a two-page recommendation that goes to the board of trustees, who can overrule us and have. Nobody else on the committee is a historian and I'm the only philosopher, so nothing you give me can lean on anybody knowing the literature — and for the same reason, if a step needs evidence, tell me what kind of document would settle it, not which book to read; I can't check citations and neither can they. What we keep going in circles on is presentism. Half the committee says you can't judge a nineteenth-century mill owner by twenty-first century standards, the other half says that's just an elegant way of never judging anybody, and both sides are convinced they are the reasonable ones. The old charge of Whig history gets thrown around, usually by whoever is losing.

I've been toying with a few distinctions that I think ought to survive into whatever procedure you write, but I don't trust my own version. First, there's what I'd call conceptual presentism — attributing to an agent concepts or categories he had no access to (calling someone a racist when the category didn't exist for him) — and that feels different from the availability question: whether people in his own time and milieu did in fact articulate the objection in terms he could have understood. If they did, then he couldn't have known looks to me like a false claim about the world rather than a value judgment, and I want it treated as a matter of fact the RA can in principle check. Second, there's a kind of presentism that makes no false historical claim at all: every sentence on the plaque is true, but the facts selected for mention were selected because they grate on us now. I don't know what to do with that one, and it's the version we're most often actually guilty of. Third, I've started to think a name on a building isn't a verdict on a whole life but something narrower and weirder — an ongoing act of holding someone up as exemplary in some respect — in which case was he a bad person is the wrong question and a lot of our arguments have been about the wrong thing. Which raises the worry I like least: honoring is present tense. A wrong can have been excusable in 1840 and yet our honoring him in 2026 might still be something we shouldn't be doing, and I can't see how to build that into a checklist without smuggling in my own politics as if it were a step. Finally, our reflexive move is always he was no worse than his peers, or better than his peers, and I've come to think that move is rotten: it makes the verdict depend on how bad we later discover the era's average was, so the more awful we find his contemporaries, the more exonerated he becomes. If your procedure ends up killing that question, kill it, and tell me what replaces it.

So: give me a decision procedure the committee can actually run, in the order the questions get asked, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, where it hands the decision to the trustees because the question is one of values and not facts, and what to do when the record is too thin to answer (that's most cases). Mark clearly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure and which one I personally will be tempted to fudge. Then run it on three cases. (1) A science building named in 1922 for an 1840s textile manufacturer who employed children in his mills, legally and routinely, with no complaint from anyone in his town in any record we can find, and who also endowed scholarships for local kids. (2) A dormitory named in 1958, by people who knew exactly what they were doing, for a professor who published eugenic and anti-immigration views in the 1910s and 20s while colleagues at this very university said publicly that he was wrong. (3) The one I expect not to like the answer on: an atrium named in the 1990s for a donor whose firm marketed infant formula in ways that were ordinary industry practice here and are now regarded as predatory, and who dismissed a contemporary boycott and the clinicians behind it — his daughter is on the board, the family still gives, and the gift agreement says the name stays. If the procedure's answer on (3) turns out to be structurally the same as its answer on (1) or (2), say so explicitly, because that is the result that will get us yelled at and I'd rather hear it from you first.

Format: after the procedure and the applications, put the case applications in a table with columns Case | The step the case turns on | What the record would have to show to flip the answer | Verdict or hand-off | Who signs off, and then give me a single JSON object with these fields: steps (each with step_number, question, sufficient_answer, type: fact or value, stop, handoff), hard_stops, judgment_calls_dressed_as_procedure, cases (each with case_name, outcome, step_it_turns_on, flip_condition, unwelcome: true or false), what_the_procedure_cannot_decide, and predictable_misuses_by_a_committee. Please write the whole thing in American English, and keep it in language I can hand to a chemist and a trustee without translating.
