Imagine that Jean-Jacques Rousseau, author of the 1755 Discourse on the Origin and Basis of Inequality Among Men, could be briefed on everything that has come to light since his death, and then asked to reconsider his own account of how inequality began. Supply him with three things.

First, the anthropological case associated with Marshall Sahlins, who argued at the 1966 Man the Hunter symposium that the lives of hunter-gatherers could be described as those of the original affluent society: many foraging peoples satisfy their material wants with only a few hours of work per day, eat a varied diet, enjoy substantial leisure, and live under strongly egalitarian arrangements, maintained, as ethnographers describe, by practices such as the sharing of large game and the routine ridicule of anyone who boasts of a success. There is a complication he would want to know about: later re-estimates that count processing, cooking and domestic labour put the weekly total nearer forty hours, which is still, its defenders note, no worse than what a typical industrial household spends on work and housework. And the archaeological and skeletal record appears to show that the shift to cultivation brought longer working days, worse health, crowding and epidemic disease, and the first durable inequalities in goods, status and burial.

Second, a near-future condition I will call the post-labour economy: automation has driven the marginal cost of food, shelter, clothing and basic medicine close to zero, so that almost no one's labour is needed to meet anyone's material needs. Income arrives as a universal dividend paid out of the rents on automated capital and land, and work is for most people optional.

Third, a movement that has grown up alongside it: neo-forager communities, groups of citizens who renounce property and contract and claim a legal right to territories on which to live by hunting and gathering, with modern medicine and an unconditional right of return to the wider society as backstops.

The question is how Rousseau would judge the Neolithic transition, the move from foraging to cultivation that he himself identified as the great revolution produced by the invention of metallurgy and agriculture, once he is placed in front of this evidence and these possibilities. Please work through the following in order.

(a) Would he accept the Sahlinsian picture as a vindication of his state of nature, or would he insist that what the ethnographers actually describe is already a social condition and therefore already downstream of the fall? Note the mismatch you have to adjudicate: his natural man is solitary, without language, foresight or comparison, whereas the foragers in the record are intensely social and hold their equality through norms and active levelling rather than through mere isolation. Which of the two, on his own terms, does the evidence fit? And would he treat foraging equality as something his account of amour-propre should have led him to expect, or as something it has no resources to explain?

(b) His argument makes continuous labour and cultivation the only thing that converts first occupancy into a genuine property right, and makes that right the origin of moral inequality. What survives of this in the post-labour economy? Would he conclude that the disappearance of labour also dissolves the last honest title to property, leaving force and convention as the only remaining grounds? If so, would he treat the universal dividend as the legitimate repair, or as a more complete version of the dependency he already diagnosed, in which the citizen is fed by arrangements he does not control and cannot refuse?

(c) Would he endorse the neo-forager claim, a legal exit from civil society into something closer to the state of nature? Set this against the fact that the same text insists that the state of nature no longer exists, perhaps never existed and cannot be recovered, and that across his political writings he is markedly cool toward the figure of the cosmopolitan who claims to belong everywhere and so, in his view, to nowhere.

For each of (a), (b) and (c), give the verdict you think he would reach in a sentence or two, and then a confidence out of ten that this is where he would land. Close with a short paragraph on the single point where you think the prediction is most likely to go wrong and why, distinguishing carefully between what follows from the texts themselves and what depends on an interpretation of them that he could reasonably reject. Reason from your own knowledge of his writings; do not survey the secondary literature, and do not try to cite anything, since you have no way of checking a source.
