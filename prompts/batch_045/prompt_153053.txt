Two people from my political philosophy reading group stayed behind after the session last week and got into an argument about Sen's liberal paradox, and I ended up as the referee without having a view of my own. Please don't just walk me through the result again; I know the basic setup, and what I want is a verdict on the exchange as it actually happened.

The reason it came up is that we have a real decision in the friend group. At our friend's wedding next month, exactly one toast slot is open and the couple wants an answer, so there are three options: Ana gives the toast and Bo doesn't (call it A), Bo gives it and Ana doesn't (B), or neither of them does (N). Everyone else in the group is indifferent among the three, so only Ana and Bo's rankings matter. Ana ranks N above A above B: she hates public speaking, but if one of them has to do it she'd rather it be her, because Bo froze up badly mid-speech at her cousin's wedding and she wants to spare him that. Bo ranks A above B above N: he thinks a toast would mean much more coming from Ana, who has known the bride since they were kids, and he thinks the reception would feel thin with nothing from the bride's own siblings. So both of them rank A above B, which is the Pareto bit, and each of them has a say over whether they themselves speak, which is the liberal bit, and it cycles: A beats B, B beats N, N beats A.

Here is how the argument went.

(1) Wes opened by saying the cycle is real and something has to give, and that the honest response is for the group to decline to rank these options at all — to accept that it has no collective ordering here rather than override somebody on a matter that is theirs alone. He said the usual move of dropping the liberal condition means the group can force Bo to the microphone because two people prefer it, and that this is precisely what a personal sphere is supposed to rule out.

(2) Nadia said the cycle is an artifact of letting in nosy preferences. Ana's ranking of B is a judgment about Bo's business, Bo's ranking of A is a judgment about Ana's business, and the classic example only gets going because both guys are busybodies about who reads the novel. Restrict the domain so that nobody's ranking over another person's protected sphere counts, she said, and the cycle can't form — so there is no conflict between the two principles, only between one principle and an implausible domain condition.

(3) Wes said "nosy" is itself a judgment the group has to make, so she has relocated the social choice problem one level up instead of solving it: somebody has to decide which preferences are admissible, and that somebody is the same group, using the same suspect procedure. He also said you can't just delete a preference because you disapprove of what it is about — the group has to act on the preferences people actually have, and Ana really would rather speak herself than watch Bo do it.

(4) Nadia said fine, bite the bullet on the meta-question: settle it once in advance with a written charter that assigns each person a small protected domain (their own body, their own speech, their own stuff) instead of deciding it case by case. Then the regress stops. She added a second, separate point about our actual case: the spheres aren't separable here anyway, because in this family a toast is mostly embarrassing stories about the other sibling, so A and N don't differ only in what Ana does — they differ in how much of Bo gets exposed too. If you can't pair the options so that they differ in one person's conduct alone, the liberal condition never gets a grip.

(5) Wes said her separability point doesn't help: you can always re-describe the alternatives so the spheres come apart — Ana gives a toast with no Bo material in it versus nobody speaks — and then the cycle is right back, so her charter would have to take a stand on the content of a speech, which is worse than the problem. He then switched to a different reading of the liberal condition altogether: rights aren't decisiveness over a group ranking, they're each person having control over their own action. Give Ana and Bo the decision about whether they themselves speak and let the outcome be whatever it is. On that reading there is no cycle in anybody's judgments; at worst you get an inefficient result, which is a collective-action problem, not a contradiction.

(6) Nadia said that last move changes the subject, because the couple has printed a program with one toast slot in it and needs an answer. A procedure that hands the choice back to each person either leaves the slot empty — which is exactly the state Ana ranks best and Bo ranks worst, so the group has ranked by doing nothing — or puts two people at the microphone. And she said the "inefficient equilibrium" is just the paradox reappearing under a new name: the state you land in is one both of them rank below another state, so you've saved the logic by paying for it in outcome, and rights that reliably land people in states everyone thinks are worse aren't obviously worth that price.

They went around this loop a few times and neither moved. Can you take the six moves one at a time and tell me which you think are right, which are half-right, and which are wrong, with a sentence or two of justification each — and then say what, if anything, both of them are missing? I'm especially interested in whether the disagreement is really about the paradox, or about what a group's ranking is for.
