I build evals for semi-autonomous agents (things that file tickets, book meetings, move small amounts of money). Our annotation schema has a field where human raters judge whether the agent brought about some side effect "intentionally". We kept noticing this pattern: when the side effect is bad — the agent burns through the API budget to hit its deadline — almost every rater ticks intentional; when the outcome is good — it quietly saves money by dropping a step nobody told it to keep — far fewer tick intentional, even though in both cases the raters can see the trace, the agent predicted the side effect and went ahead anyway. Rewording the question to "on purpose" or "did it do X as a way of getting Y" shrank the gap but didn't remove it. Someone told me this is basically the Knobe effect and pointed me at experimental philosophy.

What are the most important implications for someone in my position? Mainly these: is "intentional" doing two different jobs at once (one about foresight and control, one about blame), and should I split it into two fields, or is the moral part load-bearing and I should just drop the label and ask about the trace instead? Does it matter for practical purposes whether the effect shows the ordinary concept is itself a bit moralized, versus people just answering a different question than the one I asked? And does any of this change if my raters are engineers who've read the code versus non-technical raters who only see what happened?

Give me the implications that would actually change what I build — not the history of how the debate went.
