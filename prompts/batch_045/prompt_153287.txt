I'm a software engineer by training, with no formal philosophy background, and I sit on our company's consent review panel: four people who sign off on whether a product flow genuinely obtains valid consent from users before we collect or process their data. We have never written down a doctrine. What we have is a stack of past rulings and a habit of reasoning from whichever earlier ruling feels most similar. I want to know what rule we have been implicitly running, and what we should do about a proposal now in front of us.

Our rulings so far, all unanimous:

- Rejected: a telemetry gate with "Accept all" as a large green button and "Manage" as grey 8-pixel text below the fold, with the categories pre-ticked. Recorded reason: "the user isn't really choosing."
- Rejected: a telemetry prompt saying the data was collected to fix crashes, when the same data also fed a third-party scoring vendor. Recorded reason: "they agreed to something other than what we told them."
- Rejected: thirteen-year-olds opting into a location-sharing feature, with parents merely notified. Recorded reason: "they can't consent to this yet."
- Rejected: consent demanded as the condition of using the only working client for filing a government benefit, with no offline alternative. Recorded reason: "there's no real alternative."
- Approved: an unticked checkbox, a two-sentence plain-language summary of the data uses, a "Decline" control of equal prominence, and no loss of functionality beyond the optional feature itself.
- Approved: a small in-app credit offered to users who opt into analytics. Recorded reason: "an offer isn't a threat; nobody is worse off for saying no."
- Approved with conditions: an annual re-consent prompt that honestly restates current uses and requires an affirmative click, conditional on the summary staying under three sentences.

We have never had to rule on a flow where the disclosure was accurate and reachable but almost certainly unread; every flow we have approved so far put the key terms on the consent screen itself.

The proposal now is to replace our static consent screen with a per-user screen rendered by a model trained to maximise opt-in rate. For each user it selects from roughly forty pre-approved combinations of layout, framing, and illustration style — all factually accurate, none concealing or misstating a data use, all with a "Decline" control of equal prominence and no penalty for declining — choosing the variant predicted to be most persuasive for that particular user, on the basis of signals such as time of day, device, and prior in-session behaviour, none of which have anything to do with the product. In A/B testing the optimised flow lifts opt-in by nineteen percentage points. It does not depress comprehension: users who see an optimised variant score slightly higher on our three-question quiz about what they just agreed to than users who see the static screen. Adults only, no deception we can identify, and declining costs the user nothing beyond the optional feature.

What should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves — approve, reject, or approve with conditions? Which of our precedents is the closest analogue, and which is the most misleading one to reason from? The rule we would be committing to matters more to me than the bottom line, so please set out the two or three rules that fit our track record about equally well, say what each predicts here, give me your best guess with the reasons for preferring it, and state what principle we would be adopting under each of the alternatives.

I would also like verdicts on two variants, because I suspect they pull the candidate rules apart in different directions. First: the same optimised flow, except that the accurate summary of data uses sits in a settings page three taps away rather than on the screen, which says only that by continuing you agree to the uses described in Privacy Settings. Second: a flow that misdescribes one secondary use, where a post-hoc survey finds that ninety-four percent of users, once told the truth, say they would have consented anyway.

Finally, we can instrument any follow-up we like, so name the single further case — ideally one we could actually A/B test — that would best tell the surviving rules apart. And please be explicit about your confidence: how much of your recommendation follows from our precedents rather than from a substantive view you are bringing in yourself, where your reasoning is most likely to break down, and which argument or piece of evidence would most change your mind.
