I am writing the second chapter of a thesis on the boundary problem and I want an audit of my own position before I commit to it in print. Below are the commitments I currently hold, plus two half-formed ideas I have been toying with as repairs. I do not know which of them is doing the damage, and I would rather not be handed the standard menu of positions: tell me which of these actually pull against each other, which only look as though they do, and what the cheapest repair is and what it costs. Argue from the commitments themselves; a literature survey is unnecessary.

My commitments:

C1 — Affected interests as the basic test. For any decision, membership in the demos should be settled by whether a person's interests are significantly affected by it. Citizenship, residence, species and date of birth are at best proxies for affectedness and never the ground of inclusion.

C2 — No coercion anchor. The all-subjected principle is the wrong rival: being subject to the coercion of the law is neither necessary (think of people abroad whose prospects are set by our emissions policy) nor sufficient (think of the merely passing visitor).

C3 — Standing constituency. The demos must be fixed in advance by standing rules. I reject decision-by-decision demoi, because whoever draws the boundary per issue would effectively rig the outcome in advance.

C4 — Actualism about future people. Future persons count as affected only if they will actually exist; merely possible people get no membership, even though policy partly determines which people come to exist.

C5 — Case verdict: long-term resident non-citizens. They should be able to vote in municipal elections but not in national ones.

C6 — Case verdict: children. Children are members of the demos, and their membership is exercised by a proxy (parent or guardian) at full equal weight.

C7 — No epistocracy. No weighting of votes or of members by competence, expertise or stake, ever.

C8 — Binary inclusion, equal weight. Membership is all-or-nothing and, among members, weight is equal; I reject graded stakes such as fractional votes for the lightly affected.

C9 — Scope discipline. I do not think C1 can be applied to decisions about the franchise itself, so I currently exempt boundary decisions from its scope.

Two ideas I have not decided about:

(i) The anchoring move. Rather than let C1 compose the demos, let a standing rule compose it, and give groups that are significantly affected but excluded a weaker second-order power: a right to be consulted on the record and a suspensive veto that forces reconsideration, but no vote. This looks like a way of keeping both C1 and C3, but I cannot tell whether it is a genuine reconciliation or a way of changing the subject, since the veto itself is a power over outcomes and so seems to reintroduce the very question it was meant to settle.

(ii) The reflexive case. C1 seems either to generate a regress or to have fixed points, depending on how it is read. If the demos for a decision about the franchise is composed by appeal to who is affected by that decision, and who is affected depends on who gets to decide, then either the composition never settles or some sets are self-stabilising and others are not. I would like to know which it is, and whether C9 is a principled answer to this or an evasion of it.
