hi — I'm running a one-off seminar on authorial intention next month and instead of handing round a sheet of positions I want to hand round a poem and let the argument happen in the discussion afterwards. So: I need a villanelle.

The case I want built into it (it's invented, so don't worry about whether it really happened): a man buries his wife and has four words cut into the stone — 'She was always going.' He meant them about her restlessness: she never stayed anywhere more than a year, she was always on the road. What anyone reading those words on a gravestone takes them to mean is that she was always dying, always on her way out. There's also a third thing they can mean that he probably didn't intend either — always on the point of leaving and never quite leaving.

What the form has to do:

- The refrain is those four words, unchanged every time it comes back, and the whole poem works by making it not be the same claim each time. By the end the reader should have heard at least three distinct things in a line whose letters never altered once.
- The a-rhymes — the ones the refrain rhymes with — should all be -ing words in the travelling/departing family. The b-rhymes should all be things physically around the inscription: stone, plot, date, moss, the weather. That way the rhyme scheme itself carries the point that what fixes the reading is what the words are standing next to.
- Halfway or so, and again at the end, disclose that her letters and passport stamps turn up years later and confirm the restlessness reading. The poem has to sit with the fact that this changes what we know about what he meant without seeming to change what the stone says. Don't turn it into a riddle with a solution.
- It should be readable both ways: someone who thinks what a text says is fixed by the author's successful intention, and someone who thinks it's fixed by what a competent reader in that context would take it to say, should each feel the poem is on their side. But by the final tercet it should be clear that the poem thinks the second one is right about what the stone says, and that the first one is right about something else — about what he was doing in having those words cut, not about what they now mean. If you know that debate as a distinction between what something means and what it comes to matter as, put the weight there without naming it.

Constraints, because otherwise it'll turn into a lecture:

- No vocabulary from the seminar. No 'intention', 'meaning', 'interpret', 'text', 'reader', 'ambiguity'. The words have to do it.
- Not in the widower's voice. Give it to someone standing at the grave later, someone who's the one who finds out about the letters, so the discovery is inside the poem's present rather than explained around it.
- No slogans about dead authors, no theory names, no winks at the seminar. Someone who has never heard of the debate should be able to read it as a poem about a woman and a stone and still be moved; someone who has should be able to say exactly where in the nineteen lines the disagreement gets settled.
- Under a minute read aloud.

I mostly want to know whether a villanelle can definately hold a disagreement like that without collapsing into either a shrug or a verdict — if you think the form fights the idea, say so and tell me what you'd use instead, but write the villanelle anyway so I can see.
