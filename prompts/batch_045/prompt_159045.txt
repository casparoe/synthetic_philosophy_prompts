You are both a professional philosopher of art — someone who works on the ontology of artifacts and on what, if anything, makes an original worth more than an indistinguishable copy — and a writer of short literary fiction in the tradition where a story is built to do the work of a thought experiment. Your task is to write a short story of 900 to 1,200 words that isolates and tests one specific, under-explored wrinkle in that territory.

The background. For roughly forty years, museums have used what its partisans call relay reconstitution. A scanner records a painting at molecular resolution; a printer rebuilds it, layer by layer, from generic feedstock; the source object is consumed in the process. Nothing but form survives, so there has always been a minority view that this is not preservation at all but destruction followed by replacement with an authorized counterfeit. The practice is nonetheless routine, and the institutionally recognized continuation of the work is whatever comes out of the printer.

The case your story must stage. In the mid-1980s a museum put a fragile triptych by a painter who had died the previous year through relay reconstitution. It has now come out that the pipeline was run twice for this work: a technician, facing a watchdog timeout on a partial build, restarted it, and then — out of panic, curiosity, or something less nameable — allowed the first run to finish as well. Two objects emerged. For decades one has hung in the gallery and one has sat in a crate. During a basement flood the crates were moved twice, and the run logs were later overwritten. It is now unknowable which object came from which run. The two are not strictly molecule-for-molecule identical: the build is subject to thermal noise, and they differ in a few thousand molecular placements. But the differences are distributed in such a way that no available or foreseeable test performed on a sealed object can determine which object is which.

There is a third possibility that nobody in the institution wants to say out loud — that the scan did not end the work but divided it, so that each object now stands in the same relation to the painter's act that it would have stood in had it been the only one. If that is right, the question is not which object is the original but whether "the original" was the sort of thing that could have two equally good claimants.

Around this sits a knot of live interests. The painter's heir has filed a restitution claim demanding the original back, on the argument that the original was destroyed in the scan and that both survivors are forgeries, so the museum's continued possession of either is unlawful. The museum's director has scheduled a public rededication of the triptych for three days from now, and wants one object quietly destroyed before then and the other restored to the wall. The conservator who discovered the duplication — mid-career, the person who has spent eleven years writing condition reports on the object in the gallery, and the person who must sign the next one — is your protagonist.

Requirements.

First, begin with a short section labeled Plan. Brainstorm three or four genuinely different ways of staging this material — different protagonists, different endings, different degrees of public disclosure — say in a few sentences which one isolates the wrinkle best and why, and commit to it. Then write the story.

Keep the technology in the background. No speculation about how the scanner works, no futuristic spectacle, no twist in which the machine turns out to be sentient or the archive turns out to be incomplete in a convenient way. The interest is in what people do with an object when the story of how it got here has become indeterminate.

Do not resolve the plot by any device that makes the historical question answerable: no forensic discovery, no hidden signature, no confession, no recovered log. The indeterminacy has to survive to the last line. Equally, do not resolve it by showing the historical question to be worthless — some character must want the original back for a reason the reader cannot simply wave away, and the heir's claim should be made at least as strong as the museum's.

The story should dramatize rather than lecture. No character gives a disinterested seminar on authenticity, and there is no narrator's summary of the issues at the end. What the reader should be left holding is both of these at once: that it matters enormously which object is which, and that there may be nothing left for "which" to mean.
