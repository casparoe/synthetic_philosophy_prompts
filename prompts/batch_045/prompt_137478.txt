I'm writing a paper for a graduate seminar on whether "better than" is transitive, and I've reached the point where I trust a stated number more than I trust a paragraph of hedging, so I want yours. Fix the relation as all-things-considered prudential betterness for a person over complete lives — not moral betterness, not "better for", not choiceworthiness — though tell me separately whether your figure moves if the relation is one of those instead. The claim I want a credence on is that betterness so understood is not transitive: there are specifiable lives A, B, C such that A is better than B, B is better than C, and C is better than A. Take it as resolved in favor of intransitivity if some cycle is generated by pairwise judgments each of which survives reflective scrutiny; take it as resolved against if the best treatment of the cases locates the trouble elsewhere — in vagueness or imprecision, in parity or incomparability, or in a false judgment about the individual steps. I don't want "it depends on which theory of value you accept": I want a number, and the decomposition that produces it.

The case I keep being handed is the familiar single continuum: a long string of lives, each a hair better than the one before, running from a life I rank high to one I rank far lower, which appears to force a choice between transitivity, the small-improvement premise, and the completeness of the relation. I find that case easy to wave away, because it has a privileged endpoint — you can always say the end-of-chain comparison is the reliable one and the damage happens somewhere in the steps. So I built a version without that feature, and it has been bothering me for a week. Call it the carousel: lives vary along three dimensions — intensity of pleasure, duration of life, and variety of distinct pleasures — and I arrange three long ladders in a loop. The first ladder climbs by trading a day of duration for a very small gain in intensity; the second climbs by trading a little intensity for a little variety; the third climbs by trading a little variety for a little duration; the loop closes back on the life you started from. At every rung the trade is small enough, and favorable enough on net, that the next life looks better than the current one, and yet a full lap leaves you somewhere very different — and no rung on the loop is privileged as the place where you are allowed to discard the chain judgment. Two further wrinkles I can't settle: granularity, since I can build the carousel with three large rungs or with ten million imperceptible ones, and I genuinely don't know whether making the steps smaller should raise or lower the probability of intransitivity; and the diachronic version, where at each rung you are offered the next life for a dollar, repeatedly, and may also pay to step back — does the money-pump shape of that case add any pressure beyond the synchronic one, or does it merely redescribe it?

So give me your credence that betterness is intransitive in the sense above, decomposed. At a minimum I want rough numbers on: whether the step judgments in a carousel-style case really are all correct, and whether that probability depends on the steps being imperceptible, perceptible but given no weight, or perceptible and weighted but tiny; whether betterness is chain-closed at all — whether each-link-better entails first-better-than-last, or whether transitivity is only a local constraint on neighboring comparisons; whether betterness admits any real-valued representation (or any representation by an order-preserving numerical scale), since representability would settle the question on its own; whether the correct diagnosis of these cases is imprecision or vagueness rather than intransitivity; whether it is parity or incomparability instead; and, conditional on intransitivity being possible in the abstract, whether it arises among lives of a kind a person could actually lead rather than only in contrived, gerrymandered, or limit cases. Attach a number and a one-line justification to each term, show how they combine into your headline figure, and then tell me which single term that figure is most sensitive to and what argument or evidence would move it most. For calibration: I sit at roughly 0.4 on intransitivity and my supervisor sits at roughly 0.85, and I would like your view on which of those sub-questions our disagreement most likely reduces to — I suspect it is not the step judgments, but I cannot say what it is instead. Finally, since I may end up reproducing this structure in the paper, please be explicit about epistemic status throughout: separate what is settled convention or close to unanimous among people who work on these questions from what is genuinely contested, do not present a contested diagnosis as the received view, and flag any point where your number is doing more work than your reasons are.
