I write durability and safety claims into specifications for reusable medical-device packaging and delivery hardware. Roughly two dozen such claims a year pass across my desk; each one costs about a week of engineer time, has to be signed off by QA and Regulatory, and — because my team are engineers with no philosophy training and I can't justify more than an hour of conceptual work per claim — I need something they can run cold, in ordinary language, with the reasoning stated in your own words. Please don't point me to papers or name-drop authors; my team can't use them and I'd rather have the argument than the bibliography.

The claims are almost always dispositional: "stays closed under shock," "maintains a sterile barrier for 36 months," "cannot deliver a harmful bolus," "resists ingress after repeated flexing." The naive way to cash these out — the item would do X if condition C obtained — keeps failing us in three distinguishable ways, and I want a procedure that separates them:

1. Cases where the test itself destroys or removes the very capacity being certified, so the item passes for the wrong reason or is consumed by the test (single-use test on a reusable part).
2. Cases where something merely blocks the manifestation without removing the capacity, and where the blocking factors are open-ended — a finite checklist can never enumerate them all.
3. Cases where the observed behaviour is produced by something other than the capacity we're claiming, including behaviour produced by damage the test itself inflicted.

There is also a fourth worry I can't get straight: a pattern of passing is not the same as a capacity. We once shipped a claim that held for two years only because our test rig was miscalibrated, and the "disposition" was really an accident of our procedure. So the procedure has to say what distinguishes a genuine capacity from a mere run of good results, in terms an engineer can actually check.

A colleague tells me the philosophical literature has tags for some of this — something like finks, masks and mimics — and that there is a live argument about whether a capacity that never manifests is still there. I don't know the details and don't need them. If that framing earns its keep, use it; if it's a distraction from the shop-floor problem, say so and drop it.

What I want from you:

An ordered decision procedure my team runs on any proposed dispositional claim before it goes into a spec. For each step: the question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, what to record, and what to do when the honest answer is "we don't know." Mark explicitly (a) the steps where the procedure stops with "do not write this claim, or write it in this weaker scoped form," and (b) the steps where it must hand the decision to a named person because the remaining question is about tolerable risk rather than about fact. Be blunt about which distinctions survive being simplified for technicians and which ones I am losing by simplifying, and about where the procedure will misfire.

Then run it on three real-shaped cases:

A. A new claim: "the tray latch stays closed under a 30 g shock," tested destructively on sampled units, where the shock visibly deforms the latch — and the deformation is plausibly what keeps it shut.

B. A legacy claim I'd be sorry to lose: "the pouch maintains a sterile barrier for 36 months on the shelf," where the "manifestation" is a continuing absence rather than an event, and the only evidence available is accelerated aging on a surrogate.

C. A claim Regulatory and Sales both want in absolute form: "the pump cannot deliver a harmful bolus." It holds because an occlusion sensor triggers a shutdown that removes the pressure build-up before it becomes dangerous — and in one rare failure mode the sensor is itself what hides a real occlusion.

If the procedure tells me a claim has to come out of the spec, or can only be written in a weaker form than Sales wants, say that plainly rather than softening it.

Finally: for each step you flag as irreducibly a judgment call, tell me what would change your mind about the procedure itself — what argument or what kind of evidence would show that step is mis-specified, or that I've kept the wrong distinction and dropped the one that mattered. And name the class of dispositional claim on which you'd expect this procedure to fail silently rather than visibly, plus the one step you'd expect to become a rubber stamp within six months of real use.
