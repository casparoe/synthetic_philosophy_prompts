So my mate Sam and I had a massive argument last night after our reading group on reasons, and it ended with us practically shouting over a hypothetical about a guy who genuinely doesn't care about anything past next Tuesday, and I've realised we were using "reason" in two completely different ways and just talking past each other the whole time. What I want is an actual diagnostic instrument, not another overview: a decision tree with about nine branch questions that takes someone from "here is a person, here is an action, does this person have a reason to do it?" down to a named position. Every branch has to be a concrete case, never a label question like "do you think reasons depend on desires?" — I want people sorted by what they say about cases, not by which -ism they've heard of. Please use roughly these cases (rename the characters if you like): Marla, who cares nothing about her future self beyond next Tuesday, and saving money; someone whose only route to being motivated involves being drugged, hypnotised, or told a false story; someone who'd be motivated only after learning a true fact they don't currently know; someone who desires to hurt kittens for fun; someone torn between a desire for revenge and a steadier desire to be the sort of person who forgives; a thoroughgoing egoist faced with a moral demand they'd never be moved by even after ideal reflection; and a case where the agent has no existing motivation at all but does have a bare capacity to acquire one, like feeling guilt. Every node should be phrased as a yes/no or two-to-three-option question a first-year could answer without knowing any of the literature, and each branch should be labelled with the claim that actually splits it — something like: does a reason require any motivational purchase on this agent at all; does "could be motivated" include motivation produced by non-rational means; may deliberation add true beliefs, and how much; must the surviving motivation survive rational scrutiny, and whose scrutiny; can a reason come from the conditions of agency itself rather than from what this agent happens to want; is the thing doing the work the agent's psychology or a fact about value.

At the leaves I want the actual named positions located in that space: Williams-style internalism with the sound-deliberative-route condition, the looser Humean/desire-based view with no soundness filter, ideal-advisor or fully-informed-desire views, Korsgaard-ish constitutivism about reasons from the conditions of agency, Parfit-ish/value-based externalism, and the line that external reason claims aren't really reason claims at all but advice or a kind of bluff. If you think a hybrid belongs there (external reasons that exist but only bind agents capable of responding, say), add it. Crucially, I want the tree built from the logical space first — the questions and the combinations they allow — and then the real positions placed in it, with the unoccupied combinations listed separately and given a one-line diagnosis each: is nobody there because the combination is incoherent, because it's coherent but unattractive, or because nobody's bothered to defend it yet? And please flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree can't actually separate rather than pretending the cases do more work than they do — I'd rather know where the instrument bottoms out. It's for a handout for first-years who've done one week on this, so keep the cases short and concrete, avoid footnotes, and put any technical term inside the leaf description rather than in the question. Oh, and please write it all in British English — cheers.
