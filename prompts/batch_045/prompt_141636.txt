My mum got a recall letter after a routine screening scan a couple of months ago and the whole family spent about nine days being completely unable to keep two sentences apart. The doctor said at one point, "if there were anything there, the scan would probably have picked it up," and my brother walked out of that appointment thinking she had a 90-something percent chance of having the thing, because he'd read the leaflet in the waiting room that said the scan picks up 9 out of 10 cases. It turned out to be nothing. But now I'm the person in the family who gets asked to translate medical sentences, and I somehow ended up on this volunteer thing where a handful of us rewrite patient information leaflets, and I've basically become the annoying one who argues about whether a sentence should say "if you have this, the test will find it" or "if you had this, the test would find it".

Last week someone in the group got fed up with me and said: look, the probability of a conditional just is the conditional probability, so it literally doesn't matter which way you phrase it — the number the patient walks away with is P(finds it | you have it) either way — and if you're worried about people inverting the conditional, that's a separate psychological problem, not something the wording can fix.

Everyone in the thread seemed to nod at that. I didn't. It sounded like one of those lines that's either a truism or straightforwardly false depending on how you unpack it, and I also have a feeling it's only true (if it's true) of indicatives, and that the counterfactual phrasing is doing something extra the slogan doesn't cover. Then someone muttered that there's a famous triviality result — Lewis's, I think? — that shows you can't even read the slogan literally, and the thread moved on before anyone explained it.

- the one where conditionals don't have truth conditions at all, and the slogan is really a claim about how confident you're entitled to be in one
- the literal reading, where "if A then B" picks out a proposition and the probability of that proposition equals P(B|A) — plus whatever Lewis's result actually does to that reading
