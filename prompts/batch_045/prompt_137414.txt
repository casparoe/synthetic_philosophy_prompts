My friend and I got into a three-hour argument last night about eulogies and I want someone to tell me what the real disagreement underneath it is, because we kept going in circles and I think we were both right about the facts and still disagreeing.

So here's the practice I can't stop thinking about. A eulogy doesn't work like a résumé. Nobody gets up and lists the promotions, the publications, the years on the school board. Whoever speaks tells two or three tiny stories: how he always took the worst shift on holidays, how she remembered everyone's kid's name, the dumb thing he said every single Sunday. And the room is supposed to come away not just sad but, like, quietly resolved to be a little more like that. It's the most widely practiced bit of moral instruction from a life that we have, and almost nobody thinks of it as moral instruction.

My friend did classics and he said that's straight-up Plutarch. As far as I can remember what he said: Plutarch writes books of paired Lives, a Greek and a Roman, with a comparison at the end, and he says right at the front that he isn't writing history but lives, and that some chance remark or a joke tells you more about a man than his most famous siege — the small unguarded stuff is where the character actually shows, not the big achievements. Which is exactly the eulogy's method. Fine, we agreed on that much.

Then we split completely, because Plutarch also puts in the bad parts. His heroes get drunk, take bribes, lose their tempers, do cowardly things, and as far as my friend remembers he says somewhere that he's not going to leave that out just because the man was great. A eulogy does the exact opposite. There's a hard norm about speaking nothing but good of the dead, and people obey it even when everyone in the room knows the guy was difficult or worse. My friend says that makes eulogies hagiography, full stop: the verdict is fixed in advance and then the evidence gets picked to fit it, so the genre can't teach anybody anything about character, it can only flatter the family and comfort the room. I said that's too fast, the stories are true, they're only selected, and anyway maybe you need the good version to want to imitate anybody at all — you don't learn to write by studying bad sentences first.

And then there's the part that actually weirded me out, which is the inconsistency in the other direction. The same culture that won't let you say one critical sentence at a funeral will happily treat a single bad thing someone did as the whole truth about them: the pile-on, the "we're not giving this person a platform," the 600-page biography that comes out ten years after the flawless obituary and is entirely about the cruelty and the affairs. Same method, opposite filter. So either there's one theory of character underneath both and I'm not seeing it, or we're just seperately inconsistent and neither practice is thinking about character at all.

The bit I kept throwing at him (and I think it's the only genuinely new idea either of us had) is that maybe "learning from a life" is two different jobs: copying an exemplar, versus finding out what human beings are actually like. Maybe the eulogy only ever signed up for the first one, and my friend is grading it on the second. He said that's a dodge.

So what I want is this: what does the practice of the eulogy presuppose? About what character is, about how you could possibly know someone's character from the outside, about what a life is supposed to teach the people left listening, and about whether selecting which details to mention is a form of dishonesty or just what any narrative has to do? Is the praise norm doing real philosophical work — idealized models motivate in a way messy ones don't, grief makes accuracy cruel, character isn't one score so praise can be true even when incomplete — or is it pure etiquette that happens to wreck whatever teaching job the genre otherwise has? Be specific about which parts of the practice strictly require which theory and which parts several different theories would license equally. And then tell me what a eulogy would actually look like if it were built on the theory you think my friend is committed to, if that's different from the one we've got. Please don't just tell me we both have a point.

Last thing: I want to know where your answer is weakest. What argument or consideration would actually change your mind here — what would make you say the praise norm is load-bearing after all, or that it's just manners? And where is your reconstruction most likely to be wrong? He's coming over Friday and I'd rather show up with the soft spots already found.
