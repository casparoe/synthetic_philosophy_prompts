I've been reading LessWrong, Astral Codex Ten and the old Overcoming Bias archive for something like a decade, and I run a small reading group in Bristol that works slowly through one book a term — last term Parfit's *Reasons and Persons*, this term Hofstadter's *I Am a Strange Loop*. On the group's mailing list we have more or less settled the questions that this crowd usually settles: we are patternists about teleportation, we treat the destroy-and-recreate case as survival and the duplicate case as a branching problem rather than a mystery, and we are entirely used to saying that identity is not what matters. What we cannot agree on is whether Hofstadter is adding anything to that picture at all, or merely supplying better emotional prose for it.

So: please compare Parfit's reductionism about personal identity with Hofstadter's claim that an 'I' can be distributed across brains and go on existing in other people — his central case being his wife surviving in him, and, more prosaically, in their children, students and colleagues. Is Hofstadter's view reductionism with sentiment bolted on, the same metaphysics plus permission to speak of the dead as still around, or does he make a distinct claim about what matters? I would like the comparison run through two constructions I have been using to try to prise the two apart. Call the first the abstract-pattern reading: a self is a pattern, a pattern can be instantiated in one brain or in several or in none, and so persistence in others is simply re-instantiation and ought to behave exactly like the copying cases my group already thinks it understands. Call the second the causal-load-bearing reading: a self is an ongoing causal process, and what persists in another person is a piece of the actual causal machinery she set running in you and that keeps running — the borrowed phrases, the habits of inference, the reflex reactions — in which case a mere copy of her would not be her surviving in anything at all.

Run both readings, and both philosophers, through three tests: (i) the asymmetry test — Hofstadter's case looks one-directional and unequal, more of her in him than of him in her, whilst Parfitian connectedness is normally presented as a quantity comparable in degrees in either direction, so does either framework have the resources to say that the distribution is lopsided, and does anything break if it cannot; (ii) the dilution test — if what survives is spread across five people at thirty per cent fidelity each, is that one person surviving to degree 0.3, five partial survivals, or one and a half units of her, and which answer is each view obliged to give versus able to give; (iii) the cut-off test — at what point, in fidelity or causal dependence or elapsed time or the survivors' subsequent changes, does the distributed self stop being that self and become merely an influence, and if Hofstadter needs a threshold here, is he smuggling back some version of the deep further fact that he and Parfit both insist is not there?

Please keep the entire answer to no more than 600 words, and state plainly at the end which of my two readings you think Hofstadter is actually committed to — or whether, as I half suspect, he is quietly running them together and the view has to pick one.
