hey, I need a piece of satire for a little zine I put together, and I want the target to be that move where someone dissolves a hard question by saying "well, pick one at random then" — you know the old Bertrand chord business: ask for the probability that a randomly drawn chord of a circle is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, and you get 1/3, 1/2, or 1/4 depending on whether you randomize over two points on the circumference, over a point on a randomly chosen radius, or over an interior midpoint. (you know all this already, just so were on the same page.)

Write it as a parody of a federal Notice of Proposed Rulemaking — docket number, SUMMARY, SUPPLEMENTARY INFORMATION, numbered sections, and a closing set of "questions for comment" — issued by a fictional Office for Randomness Assurance that certifies random-chord-generating devices for use in regulated gaming, audit sampling, and so on. 700–900 words, deadpan bureaucratic register the whole way through, no winking at the reader.

things I'd like in there:

- The Office's actual ruling: since no method is more random than any other, conformance will be assessed relative to each device's own declared measure — a machine counts as properly random as long as its output matches whatever parametrization it declared on form RA-7. Then follow that logic straight-faced out to where it turns ugly: if no probability statement means anything until a measure is declared, then every agency, poll, or risk assessment that reports a figure without saying what it was uniform over has reported nothing at all — so the Office proposes that, absent a declaration, the default measure shall be uniform in whatever quantity the reporting form happens to ask about. A reader should finish that section and realize you can get any number between 0 and 1 by redesigning the form.

- The three classical setups described accurately, geometry and all (threshold chord length √3 times the radius, etc.) — someone who knows the paradox should be able to tell I actually did it, and shouldn't wince.

- A comment on the docket from someone arguing that demanding invariance under translation, rotation, and rescaling of the circle settles the matter; the Office noting that it has no jurisdiction over transformations.

- A fourth method you invent, "under validation" for three years: the chord is fixed by two points drawn uniformly from the square circumscribing the circle, keeping the line if it crosses the circle. The Office loves it because it is grid-based and neutral like everything else in the standard, but the validation study keeps returning a different figure depending on which way the square is laid over the circle, and the Office has provisionally concluded that this is a fact about reality rather than about the method. Don't put a number on it.

- One quote from an interested party — a device manufacturer, a concerned textbook author, whoever works.

Keep the target as the argument and the practice, not any real person, and keep it readable for someone who has never heard of the paradox.
