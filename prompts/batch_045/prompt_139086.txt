I'm teaching *To Kill a Mockingbird* in a first-year seminar on moral cultures and want to read it through the honor/dignity/victimhood framework: the claim that a culture's style of handling conflict falls into one of three patterns — answering an insult directly to defend reputation, absorbing it and handing the dispute to an impartial authority, or publicizing one's injury to recruit third-party sympathy and moral standing. Maycomb looks like a town caught mid-transition: Bob Ewell lets no slight pass, Atticus answers provocation with restraint and a courtroom, and Mayella's accusation against Tom Robinson reads, on the framework's own terms, as a textbook victimhood claim — an injury reported upward to an authority in the hope of vindication — while Atticus's forbearance is a luxury Tom plainly cannot afford.

Give me a reading that works in both directions. Name the two or three scenes carrying the most weight for you — I'd want the jailhouse scene and Heck Tate's handling of Ewell's death at the end addressed rather than skipped, and I'd want Atticus's marksmanship, which the book quietly respects, put in the picture — say what the novel shows about what each culture costs, and then say where the reading resists you: the framework is officially neutral on whether a reported injury is genuine, while the novel's moral weight turns entirely on one reported injury being a lie and a real one going unheard, so does the taxonomy have any way to mark that difference, or does the novel expose a blind spot in it?

Commit to a verdict rather than surveying options: in the novel's own moral economy, is any one of the three cultures ranked above the others, on what grounds, and at what point does the framework stop being a useful lens on Maycomb — and flag which of your claims you'd defend in seminar and which are speculative.
