Build me a disagreement case set somewhere realistic — a hospital team, an engineering team, whatever — with no oracles, no implanted beliefs, noone drunk or sleep-deprived, and both parties people you'd actually call epistemic peers. They share all their evidence and all their reasoning, they both know they've shared all of it, and they still come out at genuinely different credences: call them 0.8 and 0.3 on whether the rare thing is going on. Then I want three verdicts out of the case, and they need to fall out of each view's own rule rather than you just announcing them:

- plain equal-weight conciliationism has to land near the midpoint;

- and a third view I've been calling difficulty-first conciliationism in my head, which says the bare fact that an equally-informed peer still disagrees is evidence about how much the shared evidence was ever worth, so both of you should shrink toward the base rate for cases in this reference class — that one has to put the 0.8 person *below* the 0.3 person, with both of them ending up in roughly the same place under 0.3, converging instead of splitting.

Constraints: the divergence itself has to carry no new first-order information about the world, so don't cheat by having one of them quietly pick up a new symptom or test result from the other — the only new thing is that they disagree. The base rate for that reference class is low, well under 0.3, and that has to be part of the setup rather than a number you invent at the end. Don't make any of the three verdicts look stupid; I want to see what each view is committed to, not which one wins. And don't build it so that the 0.8 person should already have expected to move before hearing anything, because that breaks the martingale/reflection thing and my friend will jump on it in about four seconds.

Give me exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no intro and no closing paragraph: 1. the scenario, 2. what equal-weight conciliationism says and why, 3. what steadfastness says and why, 4. what the difficulty-first view says and why, with the numbers, 5. which feature of the case is actually load-bearing, what happens to the verdicts if you remove it, and the best thing a defender of whichever view comes off worst can say against the setup.
