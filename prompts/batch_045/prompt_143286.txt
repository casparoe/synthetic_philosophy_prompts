I have been trying to repair the demarcation criterion rather than abandon it, and I would like your assessment of whether the repair preserves what was worth preserving in the first place.

The two standard objections to falsificationism seem to me to be these. First, that falsifiability was never actually measured: nobody says how much empirical risk a theory is running, so the criterion does no sorting work in practice. Second, the holist point that any theory can be shielded from a bad result by adjusting something else, so no disconfirmation ever compels a retraction and the criterion has no teeth. Both, I think, are answered by making demarcation a backward-looking statistical property of a research field rather than a modal property of a theory.

Define, for a field F over a fixed twenty-year window, its refutation rate r(F): the proportion of F's tested novel predictions that were disconfirmed. Count a prediction as novel only if the data it anticipates played no role in constructing the hypothesis that yields it. Add a compliance condition: F counts as scientific only if, when one of its novel predictions fails, it retracts or substantially modifies the generating hypothesis within five years rather than excusing the failure by appeal to auxiliary assumptions. Demarcation then becomes: F is a science if and only if r(F) is at or above some threshold and the compliance condition is met. This is measurable from publication records, it makes no assumption about which theories are true, and it appears to give the right answer in the stock cases: the fields everyone wants to exclude generate few risky novel predictions and almost never withdraw anything, so their rates sit near zero.

I regard one further consequence as a virtue of the proposal rather than a defect. On this criterion a field with a flawless record, in which every novel prediction has been confirmed for decades and r(F) is zero, is not scientific, or at any rate not scientific on that evidence. That strikes me as exactly right: a theory that never loses a test is a theory that is being protected, and the modal talk of falsifiability in principle was only ever a way of gesturing at this without having to count. The rate criterion also lets us say something the older literature could not, namely that a field can lose scientific status by becoming skilled at absorbing anomalies, and regain it by making and then abandoning risky predictions.

Before I write this up I want to know whether you think it is genuinely a new proposal or simply falsificationism with the modality removed and a frequency put in its place. If it is in substance the same proposal, what work was the in-principle clause doing that the rate cannot do? If it is a different proposal, is it a better one, and what does it give up? Two worries of my own, which I would like you to adjudicate rather than merely grant: whether the rate can distinguish a field that is bold and error-correcting from one that churns out throwaway risky claims and abandons them, and whether a threshold fixed by stipulation is any less arbitrary than the logical criterion it replaces, or whether that arbitrariness tells against the whole statistical turn. Where, if anywhere, does the proposal go wrong?
