hey — I need a thought experiment built to order. My own attempts keep collapsing into something boring, so I'm outsourcing.

What it's for: I'm writing a short internal doc for my team about when it's OK to delete old stuff that nobody remembers adding. If I write it about code, everyone reads it as "this is about our monorepo" and tunes out, so I want the same argument in a non-software setting. Pick a hospital ward, a university timetable office, a city bus network, a court clerk's office, whatever — one paragraph, realistic, ordinary people, no sci-fi, no villains, no conspiracies.

The split I want engineered. There are two arguments I keep lumping together and I want them pulled apart:

1. Chesterton's fence — the bit I mean is the rule that you're not allowed to tear down a fence until you can say what it was put up for. Let's call it the *historical-rationale test*: go do the research, come back when you can state the original purpose.
2. The other one, which I've seen called Burkean: the idea that an inherited arrangement carries more than any one person can see, so there's a presumption in favor of leaving it alone. Call it the *presumption*.

Here's the case I want, and the direction is the annoying one. The reformer does everything right. She digs through the archives, finds the origin, and the origin is definitively a mistake — a clerical typo, a door hung on the wrong frame, a misprinted schedule, whatever, documented, no hidden wisdom, nobody was ever clever. So she *passes* the historical-rationale test on its own terms: she can say what it was for, and the answer is nothing. Best-available-evidence cost-benefit says remove. She runs a pilot, staged, with a rollback plan. And she's still wrong: removing it makes things worse, and the presumption was right.

Constraints, because this is where my drafts die:

- The Burkean win cannot come from a hidden purpose. Don't let there be a secret reason the founders were right — that just restates the conclusion. The origin has to be a known accident.
- The Burkean win also can't come from the reformer being sloppy, arrogant, or rushed. She has to be the kind of person I'd want making this call, and I should still end up agreeing with the person who said keep.
- The reason keeping is right has to be concrete and nameable: I want at least three specific other practices, habits, or workarounds that have grown around the accident and that are load-bearing now. Not "the system adapted" in the abstract.
- Non-negotiable: the reformer's evidence has to be *complete* with respect to test 1 and *necessarily incomplete* with respect to the presumption. That gap is the whole point. What I think is going on is that the original purpose is the kind of thing you can look up, while the current function is the kind of thing you can only find out by removing the thing and watching what breaks. Build it so that's true rather than asserted.

After the scenario, give me:

- The verdict each of the three delivers — historical-rationale test, best-evidence cost-benefit, Burkean presumption — and why, in a couple of sentences each.
- The three (or more) adaptations, named.
- Which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens if you flip it: if the origin were undocumented, or if the pilot could have revealed the downstream mess in advance, does the case collapse back into everybody agreeing? Say which one is the load-bearing bit.
- How a defender of the view that comes out worst would attack the case. I mean the reformer's side here: what's the strongest thing she says to avoid counting this as a loss.

And if two of these specs are jointly unsatisfiable, say so plainly and tell me which one you dropped.
