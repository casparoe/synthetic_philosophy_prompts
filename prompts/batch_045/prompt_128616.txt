I've been arguing with my roommate about norms of assertion for like a week now (she's in a philosophy PhD program, I'm just a med student who got nerdsniped), and we ended up on the early-2020 US mask guidance, and I genuinely can't tell whether the case is doing any real work or whether we're just using it as a proxy for our politics. So here's the ask. Take the February–March 2020 statements by senior US public health officials -- the Surgeon General's late-February tweet telling people to stop buying masks, and Fauci saying on camera that masks weren't especially effective for the general public -- and analyze them as assertions governed by a norm. Stipulated facts, and please don't relitigate the history or turn this into a take on covid politics: (a) at the time they said it, they did not believe the literal proposition the public took away, i.e. that masks offer the wearer no protection worth caring about; (b) they said it substantially because they were worried about the public buying up the supply that hospitals needed, and they expected the literal claim to work better at stopping that than an honest explanation about allocation would have; (c) within a few weeks the guidance reversed, recommending cloth face coverings, and by then there was real accumulating evidence about asymptomatic transmission -- though whether that amounted to knowledge at the moment of the reversal is exactly the sort of thing I want you to be careful about.

What I want is a disciplined mapping, not a vibe. What exactly is being asserted: a factual claim about mask efficacy, a directive about what to do, or a claim about what the audience has reason to do -- and does the answer change which norm even applies? Who is the audience in the sense the theories need, given that these are broadcasts to millions of people with wildly different epistemic situations and different things they'll go do with what they hear, rather than utterances to one addressee in a conversation? Whose epistemic state is the one that's supposed to matter: the individual's, or the institution's, since the person speaking is simultaneously a private believer and the voice of an evidence base he only partly has in his head? Then the part I actually care about. My worry is that the case is a dud: if he didn't believe what he said, then the knowledge norm, the truth norm and the reasonable-belief norm all say the assertion was impermissible, and the episode doesn't discriminate between them at all. Tell me whether that's right, and if it is, what you'd have to change -- or which other moment in the same episode, maybe the April reversal where he did believe it -- to turn it into a case that actually separates the theories.

Then run it the other direction: what does the episode do to the theories anyway, even granting that it doesn't separate them? Does it show they're too focused on the speaker's belief, too individualistic, in need of a blame norm stacked on top of a correctness norm? Or does it expose a whole class of utterance -- call them guardrail statements, where you say what you don't believe in order to head off a downstream harm you can clearly foresee -- that epistemic norms were never built to cover, so that they aren't violated so much as simply overridden by something outside the theory? My roommate's fix was a reliance norm: you may assert p only if it's reasonable for your actual audience to rely on p as a premise in the deliberations you can foresee them making. The obvious objection I see is that it lets you lie to people who won't rely on you; give me the strongest objection I haven't thought of, and tell me whether her norm just collapses into the belief norm once you spell out what "reasonable" is doing. Also say plainly where all of this goes silent -- I'd much rather you tell me the theory has nothing to say about some piece of this than watch you stretch it to cover everything. No reading lists or citations, I can't get the papers anyway. And at the end, put a confidence marker on each of your main claims rather than hedging all of them equally -- give me the one you'd actually bet on -- and then single out the single point you're least sure of, and say what would change your mind about it.
