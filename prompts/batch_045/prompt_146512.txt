The question (600 word limit) was:

(a) In Freedom and Resentment Strawson distinguishes two ways in which a reactive attitude towards someone can be given up or modified: what is involved in the plea of excuse, and what is involved in the plea of exemption? Explain the difference, and explain why Strawson thinks getting it right matters for the debate about determinism and moral responsibility.

(b) A 54-year-old man with no history of dishonesty or gambling is prescribed a dopamine agonist for restless legs syndrome. Over the following fourteen months, while on the drug, he secretly gambles away money belonging to his sister, for whom he is a signatory, and lies to his family about it on many separate occasions. The drug is then stopped; the behaviour ceases within weeks and he is horrified by what he did, though he reports remembering the acts and remembering thinking at the time that he would soon stop and that things would be all right. (i) On Strawson's account, is this best understood as a case of excuse, a case of exemption, or does it not fit the distinction? (ii) His sister has fully accepted the medical explanation and still cannot stop feeling resentful towards him, and feels guilty about feeling resentful. Does Strawson's picture give us anything to say about whether that resentment is irrational or misplaced?

Here is one answer.

---

Strawson's starting point is that the reactive attitudes — resentment, gratitude, hurt feelings, indignation, guilt, love — are not optional extras bolted onto our dealings with each other. To be in what he calls the participant stance is just to be involved with others in a way that makes these attitudes possible. The alternative is the objective attitude: to see someone as, in his words, something to be managed or handled or cured or trained, an object of social policy or treatment. The question the paper is really answering is whether determinism would force us out of the participant stance and into the objective one, and Strawson's answer is that it would not and could not.

Part of why people misread him is that they assume he's either an optimist or a pessimist about the practice of blaming. The optimist says the practice survives because it has good consequences; the pessimist says that if determinism is true it collapses. Strawson's real move is to reject what both of them take for granted, namely that the whole practice stands or falls with some external rationale we could in principle supply or fail to supply. His point is that no such rationale is owed. And he also notes (this is the bit I think most people skip) that there are self-reactive and vicarious versions of the attitudes, not just the ones directed at others, so the practice isn't just about blaming.

On the two pleas. An excuse says: the agent is a fully responsible agent, the standing demand on them remains in place, the relationship continues on the same footing — it's just that this particular action doesn't manifest the ill will or disregard that resentment is a response to. So he didn't mean it, or he didn't know, or he was pushed into it. The resentment is withdrawn but the participant stance isn't touched. An exemption is the same kind of thing only bigger: it's an excuse stretched over a period of someone's life rather than a single act. That is all Strawson means when he says the exempted agent is not held responsible at all; the difference between the two pleas is just one of how long the participant stance is suspended for, a single act versus a stretch of time.

This matters for the determinism debate because Strawson's whole reply is that these attitudes are natural human reactions, so no question about whether they are justified can even arise. There is nothing to be said for or against them. The practice is simply beyond rational assessment, and that's why the determinist's challenge never gets going.

Applied to the case: because the man's behaviour was produced by a neurochemical cause rather than by his own take on what he had reason to do, this is a case of exemption rather than excuse. He couldn't help it, and Strawson treats that as the paradigm plea for exemption. Once we accept the causal story we are committed to the objective attitude towards him for that period, and the objective attitude and resentment cannot be held at the same time. So the sister's lingering resentment is, by Strawson's own lights, just an irrational residue: an emotion that hasn't caught up with what she now believes, and the guilt she feels about it is guilt about failing to update her beliefs properly. The right thing is for her to work on getting rid of it.
