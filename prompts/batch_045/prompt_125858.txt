I have been trying to work out what I actually believe about guilt that outlives the people who incurred it, and I would rather start from the verdicts I find myself making than from a theory. To keep the structure rather than the history in view, I'll use one invented running example: a Central European republic, call it Vardania, whose mid-century regime deported and killed a minority population, seized their property, and was then defeated and reconstituted.

1. An ordinary citizen who voted for the regime, knew roughly what it was doing, and did well out of the seizures. I say without hesitation: guilty, in some sense.
2. A citizen who resisted at real cost, was imprisoned, and gained nothing. Not guilty in any moral sense — and yet I think this person, and this person's children, owe something to the survivors and their descendants. Political rather than moral, but real, and enforceable.
3. A descendant born forty years after the fact, who has repudiated the national identity, emigrated, and taken another citizenship. I say: no guilt. But I also think there is something wrong with the descendant who is merely *indifferent* — who knows the history, feels nothing about it, says it is ancient history, and goes on enjoying the family house and the unearned good name. I want to deny that this person is guilty, and I also want to distinguish them sharply from the descendant who has reckoned with it. If neither is guilty, what am I criticizing?
4. The descendant who actively endorses it — says it should have been done, marches under the old flag. Guilty. But I take this to be new guilt incurred by a new act, not inherited guilt, so I suspect this case is easy and shouldn't count as evidence.
5. An industrial firm that used forced labour under the old regime, the same legal person today, with entirely new shareholders, managers and workforce, none of whom derived any benefit from the forced labour. I think the firm owes reparations, and this is the strongest intuition on my whole list.
6. Vardania itself, whose population has turned over twice since, many current citizens descending from people who were not citizens at the time and some from the victim group. Here my confidence drains away. I want to say the liability *fades* — but fades into what, on what schedule, and by what measure, I cannot say. Whatever is happening in 6 feels like a different phenomenon from whatever is happening in 5, and I can't tell whether that is a real distinction or an inconsistency.
7. A successor state: the polity that did it was dissolved; the successor's founding document explicitly disclaims legal continuity; but the successor kept the anthem, the national museum, the archives, and the claim to the same historic territory. My verdict is "somewhere between 5 and 6," which is not a verdict.
8. The looted paintings hanging in the national gallery. Every current citizen's enjoyment of them traces back to the seizure, but nobody now living chose that. Is enjoyment of this sort the kind of benefit that grounds a liability, or is the benefit clause doing no work here?
9. The other side: descendants of the victims press a claim against descendants of the perpetrators. I think the claim survives the original victims, and that it changes character as it travels, but I also think it survives the descendants of the victims having committed comparable wrongs of their own. I am fairly confident the two don't cancel. I can't say why I'm confident.
10. And then, to my embarrassment: Vardania was itself invaded a generation later and its people killed in large numbers, and I notice this makes me *less* willing to press the claim in 9 — even though I think it shouldn't, and even though 9 says these things don't offset.

The principle I keep trying and failing to make work is: guilt tracks agency, liability tracks benefit, obligation tracks capacity. It handles 1, 2 and 4; it falls apart on 5, where the benefit-recipients are gone but the liability isn't, and on 3, where the benefit is intact and I still withhold guilt.

What I want is a principle, or a small set of competing candidates, stated precisely enough to apply to cases you haven't been shown. For each clause, say which of my cases forces it. Give me its verdicts on: whether the entity in 5 or 6 can apologize at all, and who could accept the apology — can the descendants of the victims forgive on their behalf, or is that nobody's to give? Whether the liability ever lapses, and whether it lapses outright or first turns into something else. Whether 5 and 6 really differ or whether I am being inconsistent. And whether the four-way distinction I keep being handed — criminal, political, moral, metaphysical — does any work here, or merely renames the problem. Tell me honestly which of my verdicts your account cannot accommodate and which one you would overturn. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give me the reasoning; no preamble, and don't restate my question back to me.
