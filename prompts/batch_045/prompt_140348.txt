I want a number, and I want the reasoning that generates it.

The proposition: Hume's argument in the first part of his essay on miracles has genuine in-principle force — that is, it establishes something stronger than the uncontroversial point that an event's prior improbability must be offset by proportionally strong evidence, and in particular it shows that no possible body of human testimony could ever make belief in a miracle rationally permissible. Take "miracle" in Hume's own sense: a violation of a law of nature, not merely an unexplained anomaly. Take his maxim, in paraphrase, to be that no testimony suffices to establish a miracle unless the falsehood of that testimony would itself be more miraculous than the event reported. My own credence that the argument has that stronger force is about 0.3; a colleague whose judgment I trust puts it at 0.75, and I want to locate the disagreement.

To keep the question from floating, fix one evidentiary profile and vary the event along a severity ladder. The record, in every case, is "saturated": forty independent adult observers present throughout, with no prior contact with one another and no detectable communication during the event, each with an independently estimated 1-in-100 chance of spuriously reporting an event of that kind; continuous video from twelve synchronized viewpoints with verified chain of custody; concurrent readings from three independent instrument types; and, where noted, repetition on demand before adversarial observers over thirty days. The events: (i) a mechanically isolated, publicly audited die rolled sixty times, six every time; (ii) a machine-shuffled deck emerging in an order sealed in an envelope beforehand; (iii) instantaneous, complete regression of a biopsy-confirmed terminal tumour in a continuously monitored patient, with tissue retained; (iv) a person certified dead by three independent clinical teams after forty-eight hours of documented asystole and core cooling, continuously monitored, regaining full consciousness and function within seconds.

Give me your credence in the proposition, decomposed. The terms I think it has to route through — add, merge, or substitute as you see fit, but mark any substitution explicitly:

1. Whether uniform past experience can amount to a "proof" against a miracle, given that on Hume's own account of causation there is no necessity in the objects — i.e. whether laws have the modal strength the argument needs.
2. Whether a probabilistic reconstruction is even the right frame for the argument, or whether the evidential relation here resists a prior-probability treatment (because the prior on a violation is not a well-defined probability, or for some other reason).
3. The dependence structure of witness reports: whether correlations, shared causes, rumour propagation, and the unenumerated "natural but extraordinary" explanations (hoax, collective misperception, fabricated recordings) impose a ceiling below rational belief no matter how many witnesses are added.
4. The unknown-law branch: whether, in any realistic case, most of the probability mass that looks like it is going to "violation" is in fact going to "lawful process we have not yet described" — and what repeatability does to that split.
5. Whether the first part of the argument is self-sufficient, or whether it quietly depends on the later considerations about rival miracle traditions cancelling one another's credit.
6. Whether the argument's plausibility varies systematically along the severity ladder, and if it does, what that variation reveals about an unstated premise.

For each term give a rough probability and one line of justification; then show how they combine, and say whether multiplying or weighting them is even legitimate here. Then tell me how much the total would move if each term were set to 0 or to 1, and which single term the number is hostage to. Then: at which rung of the ladder does the maxim first become implausible, and does that rung differ between the strongest and the weakest reading of the argument? Finally, which of the two of us is most likely wrong about which term, and what one piece of evidence or argument — a formal result, an actual case, a conceptual distinction — would move your number the most.

Please reason from the structure of the argument rather than surveying what has been written about it; I do not want citations or a map of the literature.
