I'm in a reading group that's slowly grinding through Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments — nobody in it is a philosopher, we're mostly history and econ people plus one retired lawyer — and we got completely sidetracked onto something and I've been chewing on it ever since, so I figured I'd ask here.

Here's the thing that set us off. As I understand american criminal procedure (and I'm happy to be corrected if I've got the details wrong, I think it varies a lot by state), courts do three things at once that don't obviously fit together. At the sentencing stage, and especially in capital cases, they let the prosecution put on what's called victim impact evidence: family members get up and describe who the dead person was, what the loss has been like, the kids left behind. Then the judge turns around and instructs the jury that it has to decide on the evidence and the law and must not be swayed by sympathy, passion, or prejudice. And on the other side of the case there are whole categories of evidence about the defendant's own background and about what a sentence will do to his family that mostly get kept out. So the institution deliberately makes one person's suffering vivid to the decision-makers, then tells them that the vivid fellow-feeling it just produced must not do any work, and meanwhile blocks the symmetrical attempt to make the other side's situation vivid.

Our retired lawyer says this is just incoherent lawyering and doesn't reflect any view of anything. But the rest of us got into an argument about whether it actually presupposes something about sympathy. One friend's view: it's Smith's impartial spectator turned into law by people who half understood it, because for Smith moral judgment just is what you get when you imagine someone else's situation and then check that feeling against the standpoint of a well-informed observer — so an instruction to discount sympathy is like an instruction to stop perceiving, and the court can't really believe both halves of what it's doing. My view, which I couldn't defend very well: it's not incoherent, because Smith also thinks uncorrected sympathy is systematically miscalibrated — he says somewhere that a man in Europe would lose more sleep over his own little finger than over news of a catastrophe that killed millions in China, even though he wouldn't trade the one for the other, and he makes a lot of the fact that we feel with the rich and the great much more readily than with the poor. So you can think fellow-feeling is the mechanism of moral judgment and still think some particular bit of it is noise, and maybe that's all the court is doing.

So: what does this pattern of rules presuppose about sympathy? Does it commit anyone to thinking fellow-feeling is a contaminant of judgment rather than the raw material of it, or could someone with a Smithian view — or just someone who thinks moral judgment needs emotional input — endorse the whole package without cheating? Is there a principled reason, on any view of sympathy, for letting the victim's side in and keeping the defendant's out, or is that asymmetry just an accident of who has standing? And the bit I actually care about most: if a court took Smith's own remedy seriously — the internal standpoint of a well-informed, impartial observer rather than a blunt instruction to discount feeling — what would concretely have to change in the courtroom? Would victim impact evidence come in differently, would more of the defendant's story come in, would the instruction be reworded in a way that matters, or would it all just collapse back into what we already do? I'd also like to know where the practice is simply underdetermined, i.e. where several different theories of sympathy would all license exactly the same rules and there's no fact of the matter to recover.
