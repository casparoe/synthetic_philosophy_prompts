ok so we read The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas in english last week (the really short Le Guin one) and I got stuck leading the discussion on it on tuesday, 20 minutes, and half the class won't have read it. Basicly the setup is: there's a city where everything is beautiful and everyone is happy, and all of it depends on one kid being locked in a dark cellar in total misery, and nobody is allowed to help the kid, because if they do the whole city's happiness collapses. Everyone gets shown the kid once when they're old enough to get it, and most people are horrified at first and then just... accept it. But a few people walk out of the city alone and never come back, and nobody knows where they go.

My teacher said the story gets used against utilitarianism, and when I googled I found this thing called the "demandingness objection" — the complaint that consequentialism asks way too much of you, that you can never just have a normal life. But that seems backwards for this story? Like, the utilitarian answer here looks like "stay, be happy, enjoy the festival, because one kid's suffering is tiny next to a whole city's joy." So it's not demanding anything, or it's demanding the opposite of what the walkers-away do — and the walkers-away give up their entire lives to do something that doesn't help the kid at all. So I'm not sure demandingness is even the right lens, and I might be using the term wrong.

I made up two versions to test it and I want you to run the lens on them:

1. Spread-around Omelas: no single kid. The exact same total amount of misery is split into a million tiny pieces and each citizen carries one — a permanent low-grade sadness or a mild headache they can never shake. Now nobody is being asked to give up anything big. Does the demandingness objection get weaker, or stronger, or just not apply? Does it matter that each person's own share is too small to matter if they opt out?

2. Lottery Omelas: same one-kid setup, but every few years it's a different kid picked at random, and everyone knows it could be them or their little sister. Does knowing it might be you change what you're required to do, or is that just a feeling and not a reason?

Also my friend says walking away is just self-indulgent — the kid stays just as miserable whether you leave or not, so you're paying a huge cost to feel clean. Is that fair, or does it miss what the objection is even about?
