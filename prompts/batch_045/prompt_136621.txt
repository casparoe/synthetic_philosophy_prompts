i'm putting together the final for my seminar on reasons and deliberation (mixed last-year undergrad / MA group) and i need one more essay question. what we've covered: byproduct-y goods and the paradox of hedonism, Sidgwick on esoteric morality, Railton's sophisticated consequentialist and the alienation worry, Joyce's shmagency objection plus Enoch's deliberative-indispensability reply, and a week on ideal-deliberator views (Smith's ideal advisor, Firth-ish ideal observer). i do NOT want a question where someone can get a first by writing down everything they remember about Railton — the marks have to come from doing something with the material, and i want the crux to be a genuine fork rather than a compare-and-contrast.

here are two distinctions i made up for the course and i want the question to turn on them:

- call a normative standard deliberately self-erasing (DSE) when an agent's trying to conform to it, and having the standing disposition to try to conform to it, systematically makes them less likely to conform to it, compared with some alternative deliberative procedure that's available to them. note: not hard to know, not expensive to compute — actually aiming at it makes you do worse by its own lights.
- call the DSE ersatz if it would vanish under idealization (full true descriptive information, no akrasia, perfect instrumental reasoning, unlimited time); call it robust if it survives all that, i.e. the fully idealized agent still does better by the standard's lights by not aiming at it.

the thing i want students forced into is the squeeze for anyone who wants both indirect normativity (rightness isn't what deliberation aims at) and an idealized-deliberation account of what deliberation should aim at. i've been calling it the three-ways collapse:

(a) idealize only descriptively, and you haven't touched robust DSE — the ideal agent still shouldn't aim at rightness, so idealization hasn't delivered a deliberative target;
(b) idealize all the way up to full normative information, and either the idealization is circular (it's been built to output rightness) or deliberation goes otiose (it's an oracle reporting an answer rather than a procedure);
(c) build the fix into the idealization, e.g. stipulate that the ideal deliberator has the disposition not to aim at rightness, and you've loaded the conclusion, and owe us a story about why that disposition is part of ideality rather than a wart of real agents.

and whichever way they jump, they then have to answer what i call the proxy-status question: whatever a real agent should aim at in deliberation, is it (i) merely instrumentally recommended by the standard, (ii) itself a genuine normative standard with its own authority (a subjective or evidential ought), or (iii) not normative at all, just what it's rational to do? they need to say what each option costs.

so, deliverables:

1. ONE essay question, 90 minutes in class, 1500 words max. it should have a yes-or-no-looking crux at the centre, not discuss the literature phrasing. it should include one concrete case of your own that isn't the paradox of hedonism and isn't Railton's example, so nobody can just replay the reading — ideally a case that makes them decide whether the DSE in it is ersatz or robust and why that matters.
2. a model answer of roughly 700-800 words that actually commits to a position on the three-ways collapse (flag where it's making a call rather than reporting one).
3. a marking scheme: five must-hit points, 20 marks total, marks per point, and one line each on how people typically botch it. plus one sentence on what separates an A from a B.

please make the question self-contained enough that it doesn't depend on looking anything up, and keep the model answer's substantive claims defintely separable from summary of the readings. assume the students know the readings, so the question shouldn't ask for exposition of them.
