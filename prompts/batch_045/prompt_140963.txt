Something that's been bugging me about how "honesty" actually gets built into AI assistants. On the one hand, the evals treat honesty as a constraint on assertions: a factoid question has a checkable answer, a wrong answer counts against the model — but so does "I don't know," since abstaining is scored as a miss. On the other hand, the same assistants get preference-trained to be agreeable, and in the setups I've seen raters reliably seem to prefer answers that go along with the user's framing of the question, which means the model can leave someone with a false belief it never asserted. My team has started calling the second thing "evading" rather than deceiving, and we track it as a seperate, milder defect, but I'm not sure that distinction is doing any real work.

So: what conception of honesty, and of deception, does this whole setup presuppose? Is it implicitly a purely assertive view, on which honesty constrains only what you say and silence is always free? Or an audience-relative view, on which what matters is the belief a particular user walks away with? Can the two halves — penalize false assertions, reward agreeableness, punish silence — be reconstructed as one coherent theory, or does the practice quietly presuppose inconsistent things? And what would have to change if we took the audience-relative view seriously, so that "I'm not sure, but some people think X" counts as honest to a specialist and misleading to a trusting user? Please also say what the practice strictly presupposes versus what it merely fits well with, since I suspect several different theories would license it.

Give me, in this order: "Summary" (2–3 sentences), "The implicit theory" (~250 words), "What it rules out" (~150 words), "Where it's underdetermined" (~200 words), and "What would have to change" (~200 words). In American English, please.
