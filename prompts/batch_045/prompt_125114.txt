Committee, since that matters a lot here. Chair is an empirical ML person who does interpretability — probing classifiers, sparse autoencoders, the usual — and privately thinks the whole "eliciting latent knowledge" framing is unfalsifiable safety hand-wringing; she will want baselines, ablations, and a number for everything. Second member is a Bayesian/statistics ML guy whose instinct on any proposal like mine is identifiability: he'll want to know whether the thing I'm estimating is even determined by the data or whether I'm picking one point out of a big equivalence class and calling it the truth. Third is a philosopher of science who works on underdetermination and scientific realism; she's the only one who's read any AI safety material, she's sympathetic to the problem, but she'll push on whether "what the model believes" is well-defined at all and whether I'm helping myself to a naive correspondence story about truth. Nobody else in the department works on this stuff, and two of them think the framing is weird.

The setup, briefly. There's a big predictive model trained on a high-bandwidth sensor stream (video of a vault) to predict future frames. I want a reporter head on its activations that answers questions in language: is the diamond still there, or has someone tampered with the camera feed? The trouble is I can only supervise the reporter on cases where I can check the answer myself, and the model's edge shows up exactly in the cases I can't check, and the known failure mode is that the reporter just becomes a simulator of what a human would say given the visible evidence, which fits every training example and tells me nothing.

My move is to not train the reporter on the real model at all. Step one: hand-build a generative simulator of the same kind of domain, a game-engine-ish world model where I stipulate the true latent state by construction (diamond moved at t=37, feed swapped at t=52), and train the reporter on the simulator's internal state to report those latents in language — full ground-truth supervision, no ambiguity about what "really happened" means. Step two: learn a mapping from the real model's activations into the simulator's state space, using unlabeled paired data (both systems fed the same stream) plus the requirement that the mapped state predicts the real model's own future predictions. Step three: run the simulator-trained reporter on the mapped activations. The pitch is that I buy real supervision for what it means to know what's going on without ever checking the real model's answers, and what gets transferred is supposed to be a causal variable rather than a correlation, because the reporter was trained on a system where I did the interventions myself.

What I want from you: the eight-ish objections I'll actually get, tagged with who raises them, phrased the way that person would put it, ordered by how much damage they'd do to the proposal, each with a two-minute-ish reply and a blunt note on where that reply is soft or hand-wavy. Tell me what I should concede in the first five minutes rather than get forced into, which single reply has to land for the thing to survive at all, and the question I'm praying nobody asks. Also, put a rough confidence on each objection's force and on your suggested reply, and single out the one you're least sure about — the bit where you'd want me to go check something rather than repeat it to my committee.
