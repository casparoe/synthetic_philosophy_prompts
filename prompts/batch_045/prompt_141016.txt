I'm in a graduate seminar this term on AI alignment and moral philosophy, and my term paper argues a narrow thesis: that coherent extrapolated volition — Yudkowsky's 2004 proposal that a system should aim at what we would want if we knew more, thought faster, were more the people we wished we were, and had grown up farther together — is not simply an idealized-preference theory in a different idiom. The informed-desire and ideal-advisor accounts in metaethics look like they do the same work, and I want to test whether they actually do.

What I need from you is a constructed case rather than a survey: one ordinary human decision in which CEV and an idealized-preference account give opposite verdicts for the same person, with as little else varying as possible. Specifications:

1. One person, one decision, in circumstances a normal adult could face — a career, a relationship, a medical choice, a move, a commitment. No science fiction: no uploads, no emulation, no future technology, no AI system in the story itself.
2. The divergence must not come from the usual suspects. The person's current preferences are coherent; they are not misinformed about any fact that matters; they are notakratic, depressed, or operating under a diagnosed pathology; and there is no second person whose preferences have to be aggregated. Both frameworks are looking at the same individual with the same information, so the disagreement can't be a factual or an interpersonal one in disguise.
3. The case must turn on what I've started calling an asymmetric transformation: a change someone can undergo but cannot, from where they now stand, rationally choose to undergo — even though once they have undergone it they would not reverse it, would endorse the change, and would judge the years before it as worse than they could then have known. The existing literature on transformative experience captures part of this, but I want the asymmetry to be motivational and not only phenomenal: the point is that the agent can know what they would become and still have no reason, from here, to become it.
4. CEV has to recommend bringing the transformation about. The idealized-preference account has to recommend against it. And both verdicts have to be generated by the machinery of the two views rather than stipulated: CEV because extrapolation runs the person forward through a process of becoming, the idealized-preference account because it evaluates a counterfactual state without simulating the route to it.
5. One paragraph for the scenario, and I'll be presenting this to people who could take either side, so realism matters more than dialectical tidiness.

My own hypothesis about which feature does the work is that the fault line is temporal — that CEV is the limit of a dynamic process while idealization is an atemporal fixed-point evaluation, so the two come apart exactly where the endpoint is reachable only by a route the agent cannot now have a reason to take. Treat that as a hypothesis: if you think the real difference is elsewhere, or that no case can meet all four constraints at once, say so and argue for it.

Then show me the design working: the verdict each framework gives, the reasoning that gets you there from that framework's own setup, which single feature of the case is doing the work and what happens to the divergence if you remove it, and the strongest objection available to a defender of whichever view comes out worse. I'd also like your own committed judgment on whether the case shows a genuine divergence or an artifact of how I've formulated one of the two views — and if it's the latter, which constraint should be dropped and why. Please take a definite position throughout and defend it; I don't need a menu of options with the choice left to me, and I'd rather have one real case with its flaws named than three hedged ones.
