Hi — I'm a PhD student in philosophy of physics (working on emergence and effective field theories) and I want to use a metaphysical premise as a load-bearing step in a paper I'm drafting. Can you sanity-check how much of it is safe before I build on it?

The premise: there is a fundamental level of reality, and — this is the bit I actually need — the search for a "final" theory in physics isn't just a methodological preference of physicists, it's tracking something metaphysically mandatory. Here's the argument I've got:

1. Grounding is asymetric, irreflexive and transitive.
2. Every non-fundamental fact is grounded in some other fact(s).
3. So if there were no ungrounded facts, you'd get either a cycle or an infinite descending chain. Cycles are out immediately — by transitivity a cycle gives you a fact that grounds itself, contradicting irreflexivity. And an infinite chain is just a vicious regress: you never reach anything that does the explaining, everything floats, nothing gets grounded all the way down.
4. So there are ungrounded facts, and "fundamental" just means ungrounded, so there is a fundamental level.
5. And grounding tracks composition: wholes are grounded in their parts, bigger things in smaller things. So the order of being lines up with the order of physical scale, and whatever a completed fundamental physics ends up positing is the fundamental furniture of the world. Which is why physics has to bottom out, and why the EFT story about higher-level autonomy is epistemological rather than metaphysical.

Three things I want from you. First, I want to state the regress half of step 3 as a clean lemma rather than hand-waving about viciousness — what's the tightest way of doing that, and is there a standard label for the no-infinite-chains assumption I can just cite? I've been writing it as "well-foundedness." Second, my supervisor's office-mate is some kind of priority monist and keeps saying the fundamental thing could be the whole cosmos with the particles as derivative, which sounds to me like getting the compositional arrow backwards — the whole is made of the parts, not the other way round — but tell me whether beating him on the arrow is enough for step 5, or whether I also need a physicalist premise, or a premise that the basis "covers" everything else (I've been calling this the coverage condition: the ungrounded things have to be such that everything else is grounded in them, rather than some things just happening to be ungrounded and idle). Third: is there any room in the framework for termination without a single unified basis — chains that bottom out but bottom out in lots of disconnected terminuses?

And please be explicit about confidence and status: separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who actually work on grounding, tell me which of my steps someone in that literature would just deny rather than grant-and-argue, and don't hand me a contested view dressed up as the consensus. If the problem is with my framing rather than with a step, say that plainly — I'd much rather find out now than get it in review.
