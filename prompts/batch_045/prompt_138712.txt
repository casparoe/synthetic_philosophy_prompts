I'm rebuilding the first session of my graduate seminar on free will, and I want to replace the usual survey handout with something diagnostic. I always open with Strawson's Basic Argument: you do what you do because of the way you are; to be truly morally responsible for what you do you must be truly responsible for the way you are; that in turn requires a self-origination no finite agent could achieve; and of the three apparent exits — an infinite regress of self-forming acts, an origination of one's character out of nothing at all, and the denial that anyone is truly morally responsible — only the last is genuinely available. The problem is that my students' papers consistently defend positions under labels that don't match what they actually say about cases, so I want a decision tree rather than a summary: something that starts them at a single node, *I grant that no agent can be the ultimate, self-originating source of her own character*, and walks them to a named position through eight to ten internal nodes, each phrased as a question about a concrete case that they can answer without knowing which view it leads to. (Students who reject the ultimacy requirement should be routed at the very first branch and never see it again; that disagreement is where most of the seminar is actually lost.) Each leaf should carry the name of the position — or *unnamed*, if nobody holds it — and a single sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Three cases must appear as nodes, because they are where I find my students least self-aware. First, what I call the closed loop of endorsement: an agent whose deepest values were formed by a process she had no hand in, but whose subsequent reflective endorsement of them is total, unconditional, causally effective, and itself a product of those very values. Second, the authored agent: a system whose dispositions were deliberately engineered by other people — I use a deployed planning system, but a child raised inside a cult works the same way — which now acts from those dispositions coherently and reasons-responsively. Third, an agent held responsible not for an action but for an unchosen attitude, say a contemptuous spontaneous belief, on the grounds that it expresses something about her. Near the end of the tree I want a node for the generalization worry: whether the argument bites only on desert-involving responsibility, or equally on authorship, epistemic credit, and the practice of demanding reasons from one another at all, so that some students exit through a *proves too much* reductio rather than through any positive theory.

Among the leaves, these must all be reachable: the eliminativist who keeps forward-looking practices and drops desert; Strawson's own combination, on which there is no true moral responsibility but the reactive attitudes and the self-expressive sense of responsibility are ineliminable; views that locate responsibility in guidance control or in the mesh between an agent's deep self and her action; quality-of-will or answerability views that reduce being responsible to being answerable to demands for reasons; and a revisionist view on which the practice is retained under a justification it did not previously have. I also want one cell reserved for a view I've been toying with in my notes and can't place: call it the legibility conception, on which holding someone responsible is the activity of making her will intelligible to her community, and which is supposed to need neither desert nor any suspension of the practice. Tell me whether that cell collapses into one of the others or is genuinely unoccupied. Please also mark two or three combinations of answers that no one occupies and say briefly why — incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored — and flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot in fact separate. I have the reading list covered, so no bibliography, please; assume the reader is a first-year graduate student working through the handout alone in about ten minutes.
