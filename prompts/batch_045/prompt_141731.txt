I've been trying to work out where I actually stand on the extended mind thing by going case by case, because the abstract arguments just bounce off me. Here's where I end up, and I want to know whether there's one principle behind it or whether I'm just being inconsistent.

- Notes app on my phone: fifteen years of notes, synced everywhere, full-text search, I check it without deciding to. Yes, part of how I think.
- The same notes in a paper notebook in my bag that I physically flip through: yes, just as much. I don't think speed or searchability is what's doing the work.
- A "second brain" app that auto-generates links and surfaces notes I'd forgotten, including connections I never made and half of which I wouldn't endorse: yes, but this is the one where the thing seems to be contributing structure I never put in, and that worries me.
- A notebook from 2011 full of notes in a substitution cipher I invented and then lost the key to: no. It's mine, its in my drawer, and I can't get anything out of it anymore.
- Voice memos where I talk a problem out loud, feel much clearer, and never listen back: I want to say no, but it bugs me. The talking itself did real cognitive work at the time. No storage, but very tight coupling in the moment.
- My hearing aids. They do their own AI noise filtering and directionality, suppressing stuff I never notice was there. I say no, that's equipment, like glasses. But I can't say why that's different from the notebook, which also shapes what I end up with without me noticing.
- My wife. Knows my passwords, finishes my sentences, remembers where I put things, I trust her completely and consult her automatically. No, obviously not — she's a separate person.
- My LLM assistant at work. It has basically everything I've written, drafts in something close to my voice, and I genuinely think with it: I leave sentences half-finished and let it complete them. When I'm honest I want to say this one is closer to yes than my wife is, which seems insane, because she actually knows me and this thing is a next-token machine that doesn't know anything. But it's the case I keep going back and forth on.
- A shared grocery list app with my wife, half the entries hers, some added by both of us at different times, none of it decided by me: no idea at all.
- A hypothetical implanted memory prosthesis: yes, obviously. Which I know I'm not supposed to care about, since the skull isn't meant to be a magic boundary.

The rule I'd been using was roughly: it's part of my mind if I consult it automatically and reliably, trust it without checking, and the information is available when I need it. That gets the first four right, but it says yes to my wife, which I reject, and I don't think it says anything about the hearing aid.

So: give me a principle, or two or three candidates, that captures these verdicts, with the case that motivates each clause. I especially want its verdict on two cases I haven't put on the list: (a) my phone battery dies for six hours and I feel like I've lost part of my memory — does that mean it was part of my mind and I temporarily lost it, or does your principle predict I'm just confused about what's happening to me? and (b) my notes app contains addresses and opinions I wrote years ago and now disagree with, and I act on them anyway. And be straight with me about which of my verdicts I'd have to give up — particularly the wife-versus-assistant pair, since those two are structurally similar and I'm calling them differently.
