Right, so me and my mate got into a massive argument last night about pest control on farms and I've been stewing on it since. He's one of those people who'll go on about insect sentience, and his position was basically that every spray is a mass-killing of beings that might be having experiences, so the only honest thing is to stop. I pushed back: a field of oilseed rape isn't a nature reserve, the pests mostly wouldn't exist without the crop, and not acting can cause plenty of misery too (starvation, or a bigger hit later). We went round in circles for two hours. We did end up agreeing on one thing though, which is that the interesting question isn't "is it alive" but what the death actually consists in and what it prevents — a neurotoxin that leaves something twitching for hours seemed worse to both of us than a bird eating it, and neither of us could say why in a way that didn't collapse.

Here's the thing: this is literally my job. I'm the agronomist for a group of about forty arable and horticultural holdings, and several times a week I have to advise on a pest control decision — spray or don't, which product, what threshold, whether a non-chemical option is actually better. It's a half hour conversation standing in a field, on my phone, with the grower wanting an answer now because the crop is moving fast. My team have pesticide training but no philosophy. Whatever we decide has to be defensible to the organic/assurance scheme inspector and to the farm's own audit trail, and some of it has to be signed off by the grower because it's his money.

So I want a decision procedure out of this argument, not an essay. Something like: the questions in order, what actually counts as a good enough answer to each one when I don't have a lab and never will (say roughly what evidence standard you want — "within a factor of ten", "grower's own records", whatever), where the procedure just stops and says no, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because the question is about values not facts. A one-page version I could laminate and stick in the van would be ideal.

Some things it has to cope with, which is where me and my mate got stuck:

- The big-number problem. His argument was: there are millions of aphids, so on any expected-suffering calculation the insects win and you have to stop farming. That proves too much, but I don't know what to say instead. Does the arithmetic run away or not, and what do I do at 7am if it does?
- Sentience uncertainty. Nociception, pain, whatever the actual distinction is — I can't resolve it, so how do I act under it without either ignoring it or being paralysed by it?
- Displaced versus added deaths. Loads of these things would be dead in days anyway from weather, predators or starvation, and populations often bounce back. Does a death that would have happened soon anyway count the same? I half-think it doesn't, and I half-think that's a convenient thing for me to think.
- Methods that don't kill and might be worse. Pheromone confusion, sterile-insect releases, repellents — my mate says those are obviously better and I'm not sure: don't they just move the suffering somewhere else, or create offspring that starve? Same with biological control, which everyone treats as the natural, clean option — a parasitoid wasp larva eating a caterpillar from the inside over several days sounds pretty grim to me, and I want the procedure to say plainly whether outsourcing the killing to a predator changes anything morally or only changes who feels bad about it.
- Knock-on effects I do care about: non-target insects, secondary poisoning of the things that eat them, resistance meaning I'm back for a second application, and partial doses that maim rather than kill.
- And the awkward ones where the only real complaint is aesthetic — greenfly on roses in a hotel car park, wasps near a picnic area. Those come up more than you'd think and I currently just wave them through.

Then run it on three: flea beetle in oilseed rape (pyrethroid versus trap crop versus doing nothing and reseeding); slugs in a market garden (ferric phosphate pellets, beer traps, hand-picking at night into soapy water, or just reseeding); and the roses one. I want at least one of those to come out with an answer I won't like — I have a feeling it'll be the beer traps, which everyone round here thinks of as the kind option, but tell me honestly.

Please also be straight about which of my questions are moral disagreements dressed up as technical ones, and what the procedure simply cannot settle. No citations or reading lists, I can't be doing with those. And write it in British English, ta.
