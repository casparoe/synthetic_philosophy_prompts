On wednesday I'm giving the talk at CAM — "Control, Agency, and Mechanism", a joint philosophy/psych/robotics reading group that meets in a basement room with terrible acoustics and a projector that only takes VGA, so realistically I get 35 minutes and whatever's left for questions. I'd normally not stress about it, but this group eats speakers: last month someone reduced a visiting speaker to "so you've just redescribed the thing we already had a name for" in about four minutes flat, and I really don't want that to be me.

The thesis is that sphexishness — Hofstadter's word for the digger wasp business where you move the paralyzed cricket a few inches while she's inside inspecting the nest and she drags it back to the threshold and then has to go inspect the nest all over again, apparently indefinitely — isn't fundamentally about behavioral rigidity at all; it's about what I've been calling re-entry. The distinction I want to hang the talk on is this: first-order flexibility is doing a different action when the world changes, and that's cheap, any controller with a couple of set points has it; the interesting property is what happens when an ongoing routine gets interrupted partway through — does the agent resume it at the stage where it was interrupted, or does it have to fall back to the top and run the whole sequence again? The wasp restarts; a flexible agent re-enters. Then three moves: (1) the real target isn't action selection but the stopping rule — a genuinely non-sphexish agent can revise not just which action it performs but the conditions under which the routine it's running counts as finished, and this comes in degrees rather than being a binary; (2) the free will literature has been groping at this architectural property and misdescribing it as a metaphysical one, so "is the will free?" should be replaced by "how much meta-level revisability does this agent have, at what grain and at what cost?" — which has the consequence I'm fond of, that some paradigmatically human behavior (the completion urge that keeps you grinding on a task whose point evaporated an hour ago, going back to check the stove you already saw was off) is sphexish in the strict sense while some non-human behavior isn't; (3) the regress worry — what makes the reviser itself non-sphexish? — I want to bite the bullet on rather than dodge: there is no top level, there are only levels that get progressively more expensive to revise, and the philosophical demand for a final self-certifying level is itself a sphexish demand for closure.

Who'll be there, as best I can tell: Marion, the department compatibilist, reasons-responsiveness and guidance control, who will think my "architecture" talk is a redescription of what she already believes with extra engineering vocabulary bolted on. Two people from the animal behavior side who regard the whole Sphex anecdote as a philosophers' fairytale — one of them has already grumbled to me in the corridor that re-inspecting a disturbed nest might just be adaptive, and both of them are going to want to know what the dependent variable is in my "operational test" for re-entry. And a scattering of agnostics plus at least one grad student who will bring up addiction or checking compulsions — the case where the agent clearly sees that its stopping rule is wrong and still can't revise it.
