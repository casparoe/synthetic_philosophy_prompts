Some background before the ask: I spent about thirty years as a lab scientist (microbial ecology, later a lot of data wrangling), retired four years ago, and since then I've been going to a philosophy reading group at the local university's continuing-ed program — my first real exposure to the stuff, so please don't assume I know the vocabulary. I got hooked on 20th-century philosophy of science and recently stalled on that 1951 paper of Quine's attacking the analytic-synthetic distinction. As I understood it: he argues that "analytic" never gets explained without circularity (you try to cash it out as synonymy, or necessity, or semantic rule, and each of those leans on the very notion you were defining), and then adds the holism point that experience tests our claims in blocks, so nothing is in principle immune to revision — which is why he says the boundary was a dogma rather than a discovery. Someone in my group mentioned a reply by Grice and Strawson that our intern insists Quine never really answered. I mention all this only because the abstract question has turned into a very concrete fight on a committee I sit on, and I need to write something for it by next week.

The committee: I'm one of nine people on the data-standards committee of a mid-sized international scientific union (let's just call it the Union). We maintain a controlled vocabulary and data schema that a few thousand labs submit to, and once a year we vote on revisions. Right now we're fighting over the "core definitions" section. The chair — a senior taxonomist, very practical — has circulated a draft rule that would let us tag certain clauses DEFINITIONAL, glossed as "true in virtue of the meaning of the term, not to be revised on empirical grounds," and she wants those tags to require a supermajority plus a harmonization review before they can change. A subgroup of younger data-science people says any such tag is superstition: they want every clause, definitional ones included, to carry a "last revised" date and an evidence-basis field, revisable by ordinary majority whenever the evidence shifts. The fight is real because of the actual terms on the table: our current entries for gene and for species (we use something like the interbreeding/reproductive-isolation criterion) have both been scrambled by things nobody anticipated a few decades ago — overlapping and RNA-only genes, prokaryotes swapping DNA sideways, organisms that don't have sexes in the relevant sense. I keep thinking of the Pluto reclassification, where a stipulation got voted away after new objects turned up, and I can't tell whether that's a case of a definition being falsified or of a convention being sensibly retired. To try to get us unstuck I sketched a three-way taxonomy for clauses — S-clauses (pure stipulation: we simply fix how a word is used inside the schema), B-clauses (bridge clauses tying a term to a measurement or operational test), and E-clauses (substantive claims about the world that could turn out false) — plus two tests for assigning a clause to a bucket. The "recalcitrance test": imagine an observation the clause handles badly, and ask which the community would actually do — revise the clause, or reinterpret/exclude the case and keep the clause. The "breakage test": count how much downstream machinery (mappings, legacy datasets, crosswalks to other standards) would have to be rebuilt if the clause changed, and score that as a revision cost. I've floated both and got two opposite reactions, which is the problem.

Our intern, a philosophy PhD student, says the whole taxonomy is Quine's point restated and that recalcitrance is just a sociological fact about how stubborn a community currently is, not a semantic fact about what the clause means — so a "definitional" tag is only ever a claim about who has power to refuse a revision, dressed up as metaphysics. The chair says that's exactly backwards for a standard like ours: a controlled vocabulary is a stipulated framework, the stipulations are what make data from two labs comparable at all, and she has a Carnap-flavored line about meaning postulates being conventions chosen relative to a framework, conventions that are then as "necessary" as you like inside it. My own instinct, as a working scientist, is that the chair is right about practice and the intern is right about principle, and I don't know if that's a genuine distinction or just me ducking the question. So: please draft the decision memo I'll circulate to the committee before we meet on the 14th. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame (approve the chair's tagging scheme as amended, adopt the subgroup's scheme as amended, or something you think is better — e.g. pilot the S/B/E tagging on the two or three contested terms and report back at a named milestone), say how the tag should actually be worded in the standard so it doesn't smuggle in a philosophical claim we can't defend, and tell us whether my three-way taxonomy and my two tests are doing any real work or just relabeling the disagreement. I especially want the strongest version of whichever side you advise against — if you side with the chair, give the intern's best shot at the "nothing is immune" point; if you side with the intern, say honestly what we lose for data interoperability — because both of them will be in the room and I'd rather hand them something they can argue with than something they can dismiss.
