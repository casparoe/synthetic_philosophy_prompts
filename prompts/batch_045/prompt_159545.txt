I am to give the eight-minute opening address at a two-day meeting in Weimar — a German foundation's gathering on memory cultures in comparison — that brings together people who work on the memory of state violence in Germany, Japan, Turkey, and the American South. In the room will be survivors' families, schoolteachers, museum staff, two professional historians, a Turkish novelist who has been tried at home for what he writes about 1915, a Japanese teacher who has spent years contesting the textbook his prefecture adopts, an Alabaman who campaigned for a marker at the site of a lynching, and a man from the same county whose family helped pay for the Confederate monument still standing on the courthouse square. Part of the audience will hear German, part English, with whisper translation.

The difficulty is that both available frames are unusable. I cannot present the German case as the endpoint of a sequence the others are somewhere behind on: it flatters my hosts with a story about themselves that is partly false — the decades in which the Wehrmacht's role and the non-Jewish victims went undiscussed — and it insults the people in the room who have paid for their work with prosecutions. Nor can I retreat to the thought that each society must find its own way to remember, which here would be heard as cover for the position of the man from the same county.

The device I want the speech hung on is my own hobby-horse: that Vergangenheitsbewältigung is built on a verb of finishing — one bewältigt a workload or a crisis — so the very name of the German achievement smuggles in the thought that there is a state of being done, whereas what the people in that room share is a practice with no endpoint. Make that land, in under a minute, for listeners with no German and no taste for philology, and let it do the work of refusing both frames.

Write the address, giving these people an actual reason to be in the same room for two days. Take a definite position on what the German example does and does not license the others to do, and defend it rather than canvassing the options; then add a paragraph on where the speech leans on claims you would not defend in print.
