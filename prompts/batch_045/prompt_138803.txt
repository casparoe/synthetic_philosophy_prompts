I finished a doctorate in philosophy some years ago and now read the metaethics literature on the side, in the evenings, and there is one move I keep running into in the exchanges about evolutionary explanations of moral judgment. Every time I meet it I have to re-derive from scratch what I think about it, which makes me suspect it has been identified and named somewhere and I simply missed the memo. If it has not, I would like it named and defined properly, because I think I need it as a tool.

The move is this. One side offers an explanation, in terms of selection pressures on small bands of kin and repeated partners, of why we are disposed to make a certain family of moral judgments: favour kin, reward cooperators, punish cheats, guard local reputation, and so on. The other side grants the explanation and then points at a judgment it does not cover — that the suffering of a stranger on the other side of the world counts for exactly as much as the suffering of my sister, say, or that creatures who will never reciprocate can nonetheless be wronged. And the reply is not the modest one, that this judgment is untouched and so survives. It is the strong one: that this judgment is now in *better* epistemic shape than it was before the explanation was offered. The debunking story functioned as a sieve; the material that passed through has had its rivals screened out; it has no known source that is not truth-tracking, and so the truth-tracking source is left standing by default. On this way of arguing, a debunking explanation that succeeds across eighty per cent of our moral beliefs does not merely spare the remaining twenty per cent — it promotes them, and the more thorough the explanation becomes, the smaller and better certified the residue gets.

What makes me think there is a genuine phenomenon here, rather than just a fallacy with a familiar name, is that I have seen the very same feature of a belief run in the opposite direction. In other exchanges, a judgment that the debunking story conspicuously fails to predict — the equal standing of all persons, for instance — is treated as the most suspect item in the whole set, on the ground that nothing in our history would have pushed us toward it, so far as anyone can tell, and that a conviction with no explanation at all behind it is precisely the kind of thing we should distrust. There, unexplainedness discredits; in the first case it certifies. I would like to know whether these are one phenomenon with a hidden parameter or two, and what principled consideration is supposed to fix the direction of the inference.

So: does this have an established name and a literature? If it does, I do not want the label alone — I want the phenomenon carved: what exactly it is, what it is not, and where its edges fall. If it does not, then coin a term, give a definition tight enough to classify cases, and tell me which nearby notions do the nearest work and how the thing I am describing escapes each of them. I would guess the neighbourhood includes inference to the best explanation, the requirement that a debunking explanation be complete or exhaustive in some sense before it can debunk anything, the family of coincidence and third-factor replies, and the more general habit of arguing from gaps in an explanation to a favoured remainder; but I have never seen any of them distinguished from the move above with the comparative, graded, more-thorough-the-explanation-the-cleaner-the-residue structure that I am interested in, and that structure is the part I cannot place.

Three cases I want any definition to rule on. First: suppose the debunker could in principle extend the explanation to the residual judgment tomorrow, with more empirical work. Does the residue's elevated status then turn out to have been provisional all along, and what does that do to the move? Second: consider a moral judgment that is old in content but that only became near-universal very recently, or a judgment whose content depends on technologies and institutions that did not exist when the relevant selection pressures operated. Does recency of uptake help the sieve or hurt it? Third: run the same move outside morality. Imagine an equally ambitious causal-explanatory story about the evolution of our dispositions to reason deductively or to accept simple arithmetic, and then the parallel claim that whatever such a story leaves over — our grip on modus ponens, perhaps — is thereby certified. Does the move look better or worse there, and does the answer tell us anything about the moral case?

Finally, and separately from the naming question: tell me whether the move is any good as carved, where it breaks, and what would have to be true for the sieve inference to be sound. Please do not give me a bibliography or a reading list; refer to positions and arguments rather than to authors and papers, and I will supply the references myself. And please mark how confident you are in what you are claiming overall, and single out the one claim in your answer you are least sure of.
