I am writing a novel in which a minor character is a decision theorist of the updateless persuasion, and I want her to sound like someone who has read the literature rather than a magazine profile of it. Unfortunately, a magazine profile is what I have. A Sunday-supplement piece on rationalist culture last month compressed the whole view into five sentences, and before I let my character say anything in this key I would like to know how much of it is load-bearing. Here it is, as I copied it out:

(1) Updateless decision theory — these days it usually answers to functional decision theory, which is the same idea with the formalism tidied up — says that you should never ask what will happen if you do this rather than that; you should ask which policy, meaning which complete mapping from every observation you might make to the action you would then take, has the greatest expected value, and you should score the candidate policies with your original prior probabilities rather than with probabilities you arrived at by conditioning on what you have since seen. (2) That is why it takes only the one box in Newcomb's problem: it treats the choice of one box as the thing that makes the predictor's earlier prediction come out as "one box," since a version of you that was always going to take both boxes is a version the predictor would have described as a two-boxer. (3) And since it declines to update, it also declines to learn from experience: an updateless agent who had assigned low probability to rain would walk out under a sky that had already opened without an umbrella, because it insists on scoring its policies by the prior. (4) The same feature makes it hand over the hundred dollars in the counterfactual mugging, in the branch where the coin has already come up tails and the ten thousand is lost for good, on the grounds that a policy of paying was worth roughly four thousand nine hundred and fifty in expectation before the toss. (5) Which is the fatal flaw the piece closes on: a theory that pays real money in the world it is actually in for the sake of a branch it knows did not happen is exploitable by anyone who knows its policy, and so cannot be a serious account of rational choice.

My ear tells me something is off in the second sentence, and the fifth has the ring of a journalist's summary of an objection rather than of an objection — but I could not defend either instinct, and I have no idea whether the third sentence is a fair description or a caricature.

Take those five sentences in order and give me exactly five numbered items, one per sentence, with nothing before the first item and nothing after the last, each item no more than two sentences long: for each, say whether it is accurate, a simplification a defender would accept with a wince, or wrong, and give the one correction or qualification a defender would insist on; in the fifth item, say whether that closing verdict survives once the earlier sentences are corrected.
