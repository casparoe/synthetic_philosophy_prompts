A magazine piece on experimental philosophy summarizes the so-called Knobe effect roughly as follows:

"In the classic study, a company chairman is told that a new program will harm the environment; he replies that he cares only about profit; the program is launched, profits rise, and the environment is harmed — and most people then say he intentionally harmed it. Swap 'harm' for 'help' and most people say he did not intentionally help it, even though in both versions he says outright that he doesn't care about the side effect either way. So people claim he meant to bring the bad outcome about while denying he meant to bring the good one about: judgments of intentionality are just moral disapproval in disguise, with the verdict on the action fixed first and the factual judgment falling into line afterwards. The writer adds that the same asymmetry has been reported in young children, which he takes to show that it is an emotional reflex rather than anything like a conceptual judgment. He then describes a follow-up in which the side effect was produced by an automated system the chairman had signed off on; there the asymmetry disappeared, which he says settles the matter: the effect is really just blame-seeking, and once there is no human in the loop people apply 'intentionally' in its plain factual sense. The lesson, he concludes, is that armchair appeals to intuition — the method of cases analytic philosophers rely on — tell us nothing about how our concepts actually work."
