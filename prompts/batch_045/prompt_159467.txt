I've come away from a long, unresolved argument with a friend — she's a historian of the early modern Mediterranean with a strong particularist streak, and I'm the one who keeps reaching for equations — holding a position I want stress-tested rather than defended. The position is this: structural-demographic cliodynamics is, or is on its way to becoming, a genuinely predictive science of macrohistory. By that I mean something specific: that models built from a small number of state variables — population pressure on resources feeding popular immiseration, elite overproduction turning into intra-elite competition, and fiscal strain on the state — can put real, checkable risk on the *timing* of large-scale political instability, not merely redescribe each case after the fact with a well-chosen vocabulary. I take the research program to have put itself on the record in advance: Turchin's 2010 letter in Nature warned of growing instability in the United States and western Europe in the following decade, and the theory carries the further commitment that the relevant dynamics are oscillatory rather than monotonic — secular cycles of roughly two to three centuries in agrarian states, and a shorter roughly two-generation rhythm in the modern data — so that instability arrives in phase, not just eventually. My friend's反击 was that this is curve-fitting on short, noisy, badly dated proxy series, and that every miss gets excused by an invasion, a plague, or a climate anomaly. I didn't have a good answer.

So: what would count against the position, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and — importantly — which of these would a committed cliodynamicist *concede* as counting against it, as opposed to dismissing as a misunderstanding of what the models claim? I'd like the defeaters sorted by how decisive they'd be for me, including results that have arguably already arrived. I have some candidate tests in mind and want to know both whether they're fair and what result would be damning. First, an out-of-sample test: fit the model on one set of polities and pre-register predicted windows for disintegrative phases in held-out ones, with the window width fixed in advance — say, correct onset within a quarter century. Second, a matched-noise benchmark: run an autocorrelated red-noise process with no cyclical structure through the *identical* detrending and smoothing pipeline and see how often it yields apparent periodicity of the same length, since I suspect much of the visual evidence for cycles is manufactured by the preprocessing. Third, and the one I find most troubling: a deliberately cheap rival heuristic — assume instability recurs roughly two generations after the last major upheaval, i.e., reset the clock at each crisis and use nothing else — and require the real model to beat it, not merely to beat chance. Fourth, the proxy-shopping worry: because onset is operationalized differently in different cases (real wages, construction activity, elite numbers, fiscal distress), and each series has substantial dating uncertainty, the theory may have enough slack to fit anything; would a defender accept pre-committing to a single proxy per polity before fitting, and would failing that test be decisive or merely embarrassing? Fifth, the escape hatch: bound the use of exogenous-shock excuses, so that the theory is in trouble if, say, more than some stated fraction of the sample needs one.

Two further things I want addressed honestly. The first is the tautology worry: every polity in a historical sample eventually breaks down, so predicting breakdown is nearly free, and the only content at risk is the phase, the amplitude, and the contrast with rivals — I want to know precisely how much of the theory's apparent success survives once you subtract that baseline. The second is whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so whether that's actually a problem here: the effective sample is a few dozen polities, there is only one genuine future to have predicted so far, and maybe no strong test is statistically available yet. Is that a defect of the theory, or just the epistemic situation of a young science with sparse data — and how should I, as someone deciding how much credence to give it, treat a claim that cannot yet be strongly refuted? For what it's worth, and so you can weight your answer: I would be most moved by a pre-registered prediction about a case that has not yet been coded into the databases, and least moved by general methodological argument about whether history can have laws at all. Please reason from the structure of the theory rather than pointing me to papers or reading lists.
