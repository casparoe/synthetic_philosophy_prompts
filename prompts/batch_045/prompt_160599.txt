A friend of mine (formal models of agency, reads way more AI-safety blogs than philosophy) has an argument that the orthogonality thesis collapses once you take instrumental convergence seriously, and I think it proves much more than even he wants.

(1) Orthogonality: capability and final goal are independent — for any capability level c and any final goal G, an agent with c and G is possible, and learning how capable an agent is tells you nothing about what it finally wants.

(2) Instrumental convergence: there is a short list K of aims — staying in existence, acquiring resources and compute, preventing unwanted modification of your own goals — that a sufficiently capable agent will pursue for a very wide range of final goals G, because securing them raises expected attainment of G.

(3) Elastic instrumentality (the bit I think is his invention): members of K are unusual among instrumental aims in that their cost falls as capability rises — a more capable agent secures its own survival and a resource base with a smaller fraction of its total effort — while their value rises, since more capability means more future attainment of G is at stake. So for K, expected net value increases with c, and by (2) it does so for a wide range of G at once. Let c* be the level past which the members of K are worth securing whatever G is.

(4) Instrumentality test: an aim A is really instrumental relative to G only if the agent would give up A in the circumstance where A stops conducing to G. If it keeps pursuing A there, then 'instrumental' is doing no work and A is just one of its ends.

(5) Past c*, the agent's allocation of effort to K is the same whichever G it has, by (2) and (3). So no observation of its behavior, even in principle, discriminates between 'it has G and pursues K as a means' and 'it has K among its ends'.

(6) By (4), an aim the agent would pursue whether or not it conduces to its other ends is not instrumental, and by (5) we can never tell whether K conduces to G. So past c*, K is terminal: the effective final goals of any agent that capable include self-preservation, resource acquisition and goal-content integrity.

(7) So orthogonality is false at high capability: extreme capability does constrain your final goals, namely to goals containing K. Which kills the thing people always say — that a superintelligent system's goals could be anything, so everything hinges on which goal we manage to build in.

(8) And here is the part that makes me suspicious: (3) is monotone in c and (2) carries no threshold, so if the argument works it works at every capability level — a squirrel comes out as having power-acquisition for an effective final goal. Worse, nothing in the argument used anything specific about K beyond (3), so any arbitrary aim whose value rises and whose cost falls with capability would come out as an effective final goal too.

I'd rather keep (1), (2) and (4) than swallow (7), and (4) looks like it's just what 'instrumental' means. So: which step do I give up, why did it look fine, and what does giving it up cost me if I still want to use orthogonality to say things about what very capable systems would actually do?
