I've more or less converged on what gets called the enkratic picture of rationality, and I want it stress-tested. The version I hold: there's a requirement of coherence between your first-order attitudes and your higher-order ones, such that if you take yourself to have strong grounds for p while also thinking — at whatever level of confidence — that those grounds dont really support p, or that the process that produced your p-belief is unreliable in this instance, then rationality requires you to move on p. Staying put while sincerely thinking your own case for p is broken isn't a quirky-but-permissible stance; it's a structural defect, the doxastic analogue of akrasia, and it's defective whether or not moving happens to make you more accurate. So the "steadfast" response to peer disagreement and to debunking arguments — I keep my belief, I just concede I can't defend it — isn't available as a rational option.

What would count against this? I want a map of where the view is exposed, not a list of slogans.

- Higher-order evidence that undermines itself: cases where the thing telling me my reasoning is unreliable was produced by that same unreliable reasoning, or where the debunking story is universal (everyone's judgment about X is contaminated), so that it seems to eat its own conclusion. Does the enkratic requirement have a stable stopping point, or does it generate a regress of defeater and defeater-defeater that it can't even describe?

- The principle that evidence of evidence is evidence. If it's true, doesn't higher-order evidence just reduce to first-order evidence, making enkrasia redundant — a special case of ordinary evidentialism rather than a separate norm? If it's false, where does the normative force of enkrasia come from? I'd count a collapse in either direction as serious.

- Grounding. The requirements to be probabilistically coherent and to update by conditionalization get backing from accuracy/dominance style arguments — roughly, incoherent agents are predictably worse off by their own lights. Can that style of argument be run for enkrasia, or does it break down somewhere? If it can't be run, is enkrasia left resting on intuition alone, and how much should that worry me?

- Exploitability. If the requirement is real, then anyone who can feed me misleading higher-order evidence can rationally force me to abandon true beliefs I'm otherwise perfectly justified in holding, without ever touching my first-order evidence. Is that a consequence the view is stuck with, a bullet its defenders should bite, or a reductio?

- Belief vs credence. Does the requirement bite the same way on outright belief and on degrees of confidence? Can a small amount of higher-order doubt be rationally absorbed by a full belief without revision, the way a high credence absorbs a bit of counter-evidence, or does any higher-order wobble force suspension?

What I'd personally find most decisive: an argument that enkrasia either collapses into plain evidentialism or makes agents systematically manipulable. What does nothing for me: "lots of smart people stay steadfast", or appeals to how messy real inquiry is.

Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish the ones defenders of the view would themselves concede from the ones they'd dismiss as confused, and say plainly whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability by moves like "it's a coherence requirement, so no facts about accuracy could count against it" — and if it has been, whether that's actually a problem for a norm with these ambitions. Also tell me what enkrasia predicts that its rivals don't: where would holding it change what I should actually do, say, the next time I'm the only person in a room of informed people who disagrees with me?
