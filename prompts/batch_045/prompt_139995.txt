I'm a journalist working on a long piece about how institutions decide what caused what — aviation boards, hospital safety panels, the teams that now do event attribution for extreme weather — and I've been given anonymized access to the determinations of the causation review panel of a large academic health system. The panel is three people: a retired appellate judge who chairs it and is fond of remarking that they are not in the metaphysics business, a patient-safety officer, and a clinical epidemiologist. It issues two kinds of finding about an episode of care: an omission was a cause of the outcome, or it was a background circumstance. Here is their track record, as it was given to me. (1) A night nurse did not administer a scheduled anticoagulant; the patient died of a pulmonary embolism; the omission was a cause. (2) An off-duty paramedic, walking through a hospital courtyard, did not wade into an ornamental pond to pull out a toddler who drowned in sixty centimetres of water; the review explicitly accepted that she would almost certainly have saved him, and the panel still found the omission a background circumstance, on the grounds that she had no relationship to the child and no assigned role that night. (3) A maintenance contractor, contractually obliged to replace the fire suppressant in a server room within a day of a scheduled test, did not replace it; a fire destroyed the imaging archive; cause. (4) A resident omitted an overnight review of a flagged head CT that her role plainly required her to perform, but the neuroradiologist had already read the scan and the outcome would have been the same; background circumstance. (5) An emergency physician did not perform a bedside scan that the hospital's own red-flag protocol required for that presentation, because the only machine was in use elsewhere and he had been told it would be freed within ten minutes; the scan would in all likelihood have revealed a tamponade; the panel found the omission a cause and added, in as many words, that a finding of causation is not a finding of fault.

The case coming before them next is one I've been allowed to sit in on. Six months earlier, the hospital migrated to a new records system. A machine-learning early-warning score for sepsis, live in the old system, was never re-enabled. The migration contract required the vendor to preserve and re-validate all decision-support functionality, and the hospital's clinical informatics lead signed a migration checklist listing the score — but no clinician was told, and in six months no one on any ward knew the score existed or had ever seen it fire. A sixty-eight-year-old patient's vitals crossed the model's threshold at 02:14; a retrospective run shows the score would have fired and paged the rapid-response team, whose median response at that hour was six minutes, and the panel's clinical reviewer believes the patient would in all likelihood have survived. Nobody omitted anything at 02:14. No nurse skipped an observation, no doctor skipped a review, and the ward ran exactly as it had run every night for six months. The vendor says the hospital never asked for the score to be ported; the hospital says the contract schedule covers it; the informatics lead says sign-off was a formality.

I have two questions and I'd like both answered, but the reasoning is the part I'm actually writing about. First, what will they say — cause, background circumstance, or something in between — and what should they say if they want to be consistent with the five determinations above? Second, what rule or rules are their earlier verdicts compatible with? I've tried to reverse-engineer them and I keep producing two readings that fit every earlier case but that I cannot prise apart until this one: one that reads the baseline off what the rules and roles required, and one that reads it off what actually and normally happened in that setting. Say what each predicts here, give me your best guess with the reasons for preferring it, identify which of the five earlier verdicts is the closest analogue, and tell me what principle the panel would be committing itself to with each choice — including, if they call this a cause, whose omission it is, given that no one failed to act that night. I'd also like you to take seriously the skeptical hypothesis that the panel is not doing causality at all and that its causal language is just a responsibility verdict in disguise: tell me what that predicts here, how it fares against the earlier cases, and what it would take to tell it apart from the others. And please give me the single further case you'd put in front of them to settle which rule they're running. One constraint: don't give me names, dates, or a survey of the literature on causation by omission — I have no way to check them and they'd be useless to my readers. Argue from the cases and from the structure of the problem.
