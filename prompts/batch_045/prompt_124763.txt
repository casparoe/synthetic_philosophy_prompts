Hey — I need help designing one session of my upper-level undergrad ethics seminar. 22 students, 80 minutes, one whiteboard plus a projector, and I can bring in a couple of bags of those tiny chocolate bars to use as actual stakes. We'll have already done a couple of weeks on distributive ethics generally, but no lecture up front on this topic and I don't want them hearing the name of the view or of the objection until after the thing is over.

Here's what I want them to walk out having felt, not having been told. First, that "extra benefits matter more the worse off the recipient is" has a strong grip on them — I want them to discover that they themselves keep voting that way. Second, that this same move, followed out, commits them to approving a change where you take from the better off and give the benefit to nobody at all, and that this isn't a weird extra bullet they can bite separately but the very same feature of the view doing the work it was hired to do. Third, and this is the bit I care most about: I've got a half-formed distinction I want the activity to test, between what I've been calling benefit-priority and level-priority. Benefit-priority is the claim that a given increment of benefit counts for more in the overall evaluation when it goes to someone worse off; level-priority is the claim about how much someone's final level contributes, the thing that keeps getting smaller as the level rises. I think the first one on its own is compatible with never preferring a change that makes nobody better off, and the second one isn't, and that most people run them together. If that distinction is fake or collapses under pressure, say so and give me something better to build on.

So: design me a runnable thing. My rough idea is a sequence of anonymous pairwise votes over distributions of chocolate among anonymous recipients, run by show of hands or slips of paper, where each individual vote looks obviously right to someone with priority-friendly instincts, and where the set of votes, chained together, adds up to a verdict in the final round that nobody saw coming — the levelling-down one. And I want that final round to be genuinely executed: if the class majority votes to level down, chocolate is really taken from the better-off pile and given to no one (I'll eat it, or it goes back in the bag, whatever is more obnoxious). That's the moment where the idea shows up in what happens instead of in what I say afterwards, and I think the real stakes are what make it land. Tell me which pairings to put up, in what order, and why each one does the work you think it does.

Two things I'd like folded in if they fit. One: an extra round where the levelling down is arranged so that the person who loses is picked by a random draw only after the vote, so that at the moment of voting there's no identifiable loser. I want to know whether their discomfort survives that, since I suspect their objection is secretly a person-affecting one and they don't know it. Two: something that forces the question of whether what bothers them is that the levelled-down outcome would be *better*, or only that there'd be no reason to bring it about — I'd rather stage that as a choice they have to make than as a distinction I announce.

Then the debrief: map specific moments in the activity onto the parts of the argument, say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm guessing at least: people voting self-interestedly because they might be a recipient, someone spotting the chain early and spoiling it, the class's majority votes not adding up to any consistent ranking at all, and the sheer silliness of destroying chocolate in front of adults), and tell me how to handle each. Also be honest about which part of this you can't get them to feel: my guess is that the activity can show them the entailment but can't make them feel why anyone would take the entailment to be a *reduction* of the view rather than a cost worth paying, and that I'll have to supply that myself. Say whether you agree and what else is missing.
