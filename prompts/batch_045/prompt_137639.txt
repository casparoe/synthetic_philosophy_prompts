Me and two friends have been arguing on and off for months about whether the world is actually getting less violent, and I've ended up as the one defending the "yes it is, and that's a real fact about the world, not just a trick of how we choose to count" side — roughly the position made popular by that big book a while back arguing that violence has declined over history, though I haven't actually read it cover to cover, just summaries and arguments about it. I've got no academic background, I'm just working through this on my own, and I don't have access to a university library or journal logins or anything, so please don't tell me which books or papers to go look up — I want the reasoning itself, not pointers. What I want to know is how exposed my position is, and specifically what would count against it.

Here's roughly how I've been arguing, so you can see which bits are load-bearing. On the per-capita versus absolute thing: my friend points out that the mid-century world wars killed so many people in such a short window that any decline claim has to be per capita, and once you're dividing by population you've already made a choice that's doing a lot of work. My reply has been a denominator I made up myself: possible encounters. The number of pairs of people who could in principle come into conflict grows something like the square of the population, not linearly with it, so if you measure violence per potential encounter rather than per head, then a flat per-capita rate would actually represent a big rise and a falling per-capita rate is a much steeper fall than it looks. I like it because it's like counting traffic deaths per mile driven instead of per person — but I also suspect its doing something illegitimate, since nearly all my "encounters" in a city of millions are anonymous passings-on-the-street, nothing like the encounters inside a small band of foragers, so I'm not sure the denominator means the same thing on both sides of the comparison, and I don't know whether that ruins the move or just means it needs a fancier weighting (say, by hours of exposure, or by how much is at stake in a given encounter).

The other two legs: first, maybe violence hasn't declined but been converted into credible threat — the state's monopoly on force, nuclear deterrence, prisons, policing, borders — so that counting only realized violence counts the money in circulation but not the reserves, and a threat that works does the same practical job as a blow that lands. Second, maybe "violence" is more like "the cost of living" than like "the temperature": something that only has a value once you've picked a basket of items and weights for them, in which case asking whether it went down is like asking whether prices went up without saying whose basket, and there may be no fact of the matter beyond "relative to this weighting, it fell." So: what would actually count against my position, ranked from fatal to merely awkward? Which of my legs is the weakest, and which of these objections would someone on my own side concede as a real problem rather than wave off?
