I'm in a philosophy/econ class on future generations and I have to turn in an essay (about 1500 words) on pure time preference, and here are my rough notes (sorry if they're messy). Teh reading said pure time preference is discounting a future good just because it's later, after you've controlled for uncertainty, inflation/opportunity cost, and so on, and that this is a kind of irrational bias: the bare fact that a pleasure is at t+50 instead of t now can't make it less valuable from an impartial standpoint.

I definately agree with the first part, but I want to make a distinction and then test it, and I'm not sure it holds up. The distinction is between:

- pure time preference: discounting a good solely because of its date, even from an impartial/atemporal perspective; and
- temporal-position partiality: the permission an agent has, just in virtue of acting now, to weight her own present projects and relationships more heavily, the way many views let you favor your own kids or your own plans without thinking they are worth more from the point of view of the universe.

My worry is that people run these together, so either they say all discounting is bias, or they say any agent-centered weighting is fine and then smuggle in pure time preference again.

To seperate them I made up the Future Twin case. You are at t0 choosing between a smallish pleasure for yourself right now and a bigger but otherwise identical pleasure for a perfect psychological twin who will only exist at t+50, has no overlap with you, and whom you care about exactly as much as you care about yourself. There's no risk the twin won't exist, no opportunity cost, and no identity-based reason to prefer your own pleasure, because the twin is just as much you in the relevant sense. If you still pick the smaller present pleasure, the only remaining difference is that one is now and one is later. So, I argue, the case isolates pure time preference as opposed to ordinary self-interest or risk.

But I see a possible objection I call the availability objection: maybe you take the now-pleasure not because later is worse, but because the present pleasure is the one that is available to the perspective from which you're actually deliberating, while the twin's pleasure is not yet reachable. That sounds like it could be a different, non-arbitrary form of discounting: you're responding to your temporal location as an agent, not to the date as such. My reply is that this either collapses back into pure time preference (if available just means occurs at the time of action) or it's really temporal-position partiality, in which case it's question-begging to use it to defend discounting from the impartial standpoint the reading cares about.

So my tentative thesis is: pure time preference is coherent as a kind of agent-centered temporal partiality, but it's not thereby justified for an impartial social planner; the Future Twin case shows the two are genuinely different, and the availability objection doesn't save the defender of pure time preference.

I'd love comments on: (1) whether the Future Twin case really isolates pure time preference, or whether it just builds the answer into the description of the twin; (2) whether my distinction between pure time preference and temporal-position partiality is coherent or just wordplay; (3) whether the availability objection is a real objection or just a redescription; and (4) whether this is interesting enough for a 1500-word essay or if I'm overcomplicating a simple point. Also feel free to tell me where the reasoning is weakest, I can take it. I'm not looking for sources or anything, just whether the argument works.
