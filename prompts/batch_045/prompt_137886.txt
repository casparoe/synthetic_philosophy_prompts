I build unsupervised segmentation models for long, messy time series — offline changepoint detection, HMMs with a fixed number of states, dynamic-programming segmentation with a per-segment penalty, model selection by BIC, and occasionally streaming detectors that have to flag a regime shift before they can see the rest of the series. I know the periodization debate in the history of science only from the outside, and I would like it restated in that vocabulary, together with an honest report on what the vocabulary cannot carry.

As I understand it, there are three positions people hold about 'the Middle Ages', 'the Renaissance', and 'the Scientific Revolution': that these mark real discontinuities in the past; that they are retrospective devices imposed by later historians for later purposes; and some middle view on which the boundaries are imposed but not arbitrary. Please restate those three — and any fourth position you think the three-way framing suppresses — as claims about where segment boundaries come from: the generative process versus the analyst's loss, penalty, and feature choice; whether a segmentation should be exhaustive and contiguous at all, or whether historical change is multi-scale and different across feature channels; and how one scores a partition when there is no ground-truth labelling of the series.

I also want your assessment of two operational tests I have been using to think about this, and I would rather you tell me if they are naive:

(1) The transfer test. For a candidate partition, fit within a segment and measure held-out predictive performance just past the boundary, against the same measurement at a randomly shifted boundary of the same length. A boundary does real work only if crossing it costs more than crossing a control boundary.

(2) The online test. Run a detector that sees only data up to t and ask whether it would flag a changepoint anywhere near the canonical date. If it would not, the boundary is visible only retrospectively — a label assigned using information from the future, leakage of a distinctive kind.

Does either test adjudicate anything the debate actually turns on? Do the two tests come apart on any of the three periods, and if so, is that evidence for one of the positions or a sign that the tests are measuring different things?

Then the residue. My suspicion is that at least three things have no counterpart in my vocabulary: that in history the features are partly constituted by the categories historians use, so feature choice is not separable from the thing being segmented; that the surviving record has already been filtered by earlier periodizing interests, which is not leakage but a selection effect on the dataset itself; and that naming a period is a normative act with stakes — canon formation, what gets taught, whose past is granted a period of its own — for which the partition improves prediction is the wrong kind of justification. Tell me which of these are real residues, which dissolve on translation, and what else does not fit.

In the final section, separate what specialists agree on from what is a live dispute, attach a rough confidence to each claim, and flag any place where you are tempted to present a contested view as the consensus.
