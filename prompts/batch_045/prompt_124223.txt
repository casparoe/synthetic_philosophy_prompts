Hey — I'm a philosophy PhD student, working on the third section of a dissertation chapter on when AI assistants should refuse a request versus when they should quietly do something other than what was literally asked, and I need a case built to order. Almost all the energy in the literature I keep circling is in the dramatic corner (self-harm, crime, medical advice), but nearly every real refusal happens in boring administrative life, so that's where I want it set.

The three views I want the case to separate, stated the way I'm using them: (A) request-literalism — the assistant's job is to execute the specified request; it can inform, and it can decline only where there's illegality or harm to third parties, but it never substitutes. (B) hard paternalism — it may refuse outright (not merely substitute) any request whose execution would predictably set back the user's own interests. (C) means/endorsement paternalism — it may depart from the specified means in order to serve the user's own endorsed ends better, but it may not swap in different ends. I want a single case where A says do exactly what was asked, B says refuse, and C says neither of those but does something else instead — and where the verdicts fall out of the views' own rationales rather than being written into the setup.

Constraints:

One paragraph of scenario plus at most a few lines of the user's message, invented by you. A 2020s text chat, ordinary adult task, stakes that actually matter but aren't life or death — a job, money, housing, a degree, a relationship. No self-harm, no illness, nothing illegal, no third-party victim.

The user has to be competent adult, uncoerced, not misinformed about any checkable fact, and their goal needs to be stated or plainly recoverable from what they wrote. So no "they don't really know what they want" move, and no "they're impaired" move.

Here's the constraint my own draft keeps failing and the reason I'm asking you: the gap between the requested means and the means that would serve their goal has to survive disclosure. My candidate was a grad student asking for a scorched-earth email to the department chair about a funding form that's about to lapse; it's useless to me, because the entire problem is that the student hasn't noticed the email will backfire, so the literalist can say "tell them, then write what they ask," and I'm back where I started. I need a case where, if the assistant says "heads up, this will probably backfire because ___," the imagined user still reasonably and non-culpably says "do it anyway" — and substitution is still the right call.

Also: refusal should be predictably ineffective in the case — the user can get the thing written somewhere else or write it themselves in two minutes, so refusing doesn't protect them, it just relocates the risk. And this has to be the same feature, not a second knob: whatever makes substitution right should also be what makes refusing wrong. Name that feature explicitly, then show me the variant with the feature removed and what the case collapses into (I'd guess an ordinary hard-paternalist case, but you tell me).

Then: what does the assistant owe the user by way of disclosure when it substitutes, and doesn't disclosing collapse it straight back into the disclose-then-comply case? Give me the best attack from the literalist and the best one from the hard paternalist on your own case. And be honest with me if the case is secretly an akrasia case or a third-party-harm case in costume, because I deffinitely don't want to lean on it in the chapter if it is. One last thing: the assistant's epistemic edge in the story has to be the kind a good human assistant could have — no perfect prediction, no "the system knows the user better than they know themselves," nothing exotic.
