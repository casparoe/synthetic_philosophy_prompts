Help me figure out whether I've actually got an argument here or whether I've just spent three days rederiving something trivial. I'm writing the second section of a paper on uncertain evidence and I tried to write down, as cleanly as I could, the thing that's been bugging me about Jeffrey conditionalization, and the conclusion I got is obviously false, which usually means I broke something.

Heres the argument, finite case, everything with positive probability:

(1) The rule for learning from uncertain evidence is Jeffrey conditionalization. Your experience shifts your credences across some partition E1...En without making you certain of any cell; rigidity holds, i.e. your conditional credences P(H|Ei) stay fixed; and your new credence in H is P_new(H) = sum over i of P(H|Ei) P_new(Ei).

(2) So a change of opinion is rationally fine just in case there is some partition and some new assignment of credences to its cells such that your new credence function is what you get by Jeffrey conditionalizing on that partition with those new cell-credences as input.

(3) Take any finite space of possibilities and let the partition be the finest one, the atoms a1...ak. Then rigidity is automatic: both P(H|ai) and P_new(H|ai) are 1 if ai entails H and 0 otherwise. And the sum over i of P(B|ai) P_new(ai) just is P_new(B), since each atom either sits inside B or is disjoint from it. So for any prior and any posterior you like, there is a partition on which the posterior comes from the prior by Jeffrey conditionalization with rigidity satisfied.

(4) Therefore every belief change whatsoever is rationally permissible. Reading a horoscope and dropping to 0.02 on some well-supported scientific theory. Becoming certain of a conspiracy theory because the light in the room felt strange. Flipping your whole political worldview on a coin flip and then describing the coin as your evidence. Whatever posterior you end up with, (3) hands you a partition that represents it as a Jeffrey update.

(5) That's absurd. Some belief changes are irrational. So one of (1) through (4) is false or the moves between them don't hold.

I really want to keep (1) — the whole point of my section is that Jeffrey conditionalization is the right generalization of conditionalization for cases where your evidence doesn't come in as a certainty, the bad-light cloth case and so on — and I want to keep rigidity as a real constraint rather than a tautology. So I guess I have to attack (2) or (3). But (3) is a two-line bit of arithmetic and (2) looks like just what the rule says.
