I am putting together a short piece for a conference reception, and I want one joke aimed at people who actually work on the metaphysics of emotion — the three-way dispute over whether an emotion is best understood as a kind of judgment or appraisal, as a perception-like state that presents its object as bearing some evaluative property, or as, fundamentally, a feeling or bodily disturbance. Write that joke.

Constraints on it:

1. The punchline must turn on the actual content of the disagreement — on what each of the three accounts is committed to saying about a case — not on philosophers being pedantic, on the topic being abstruse, or on the general comedy of academics talking past one another. Someone who knows the dispute should laugh because the joke found something true about the structure of the positions.

2. Build it around a case I will stipulate in ordinary language rather than in the vocabulary of the debate. Take the familiar situation in which someone's fear of a particular harmless thing — the decorative fountain in their building's lobby, say — survives everything: they have checked that it cannot hurt them, they have said as much out loud, they have watched other people put their hands in it, and the fear is entirely unchanged. Now add the reverse of that situation. Their physician puts them on something that removes every trace of the unpleasant somatic side: no racing heart, no tightness, no urge to pull back. What remains is that they still, calmly and in comfort, take the long way around the lobby, and when asked they say they're still afraid of the thing while being wholly untroubled by it. The joke should get its leverage from the fact that the three accounts are each forced into a different description of this person: one owes us a story about a judgment the subject somehow does not endorse, one about a seeming that has not been revised by what the subject has learned, and one about whether what remains counts as emotion at all.

3. No in-group vocabulary inside the joke. The names of the three families of view, and any philosopher's name, should not appear in the setup or the punchline; someone who only knows that there is a disagreement about what emotions are should be able to follow it and get it.

4. Form: a short dialogue or a setup of a couple of sentences with a punchline. Not a sketch, not a list of one-liners, and no puns on the word "feeling," "moving," or "sense" — I have seen those and they are not what I want.

After the joke, add a short closing note, a few sentences and no more:

- State which reading of the three families of view you had to lean on to make the joke land, and where you had to simplify or caricature to get the laugh.
- In that note, keep separate what is broadly agreed among people who work on these questions from what is live controversy. I would rather be told that something is contested than be handed a contested claim as if it were the consensus. In particular, if your joke assumes that the persistence of an emotion after the subject has judged otherwise tells against the judgment view, or that it makes sense to ask whether an emotion is correct or incorrect in the first place, mark that as a place where you took a side rather than a place where everyone agrees.
