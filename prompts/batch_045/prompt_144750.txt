Area: sortition, lottocracy, citizens assemblies, randomly selected political bodies generally. The worry I can't yet state sharply enough to put in an application is this: the case for sortition seems to run on two arguments that pull in opposite directions, and nobody in the literature seems bothered by it.

Argument one, call it the mirroring argument: a randomly selected body statistically reproduces the population's composition, and therefore (somehow) its preferences, and so we get decisions that track what the population would want without the familiar distortions of electoral incentive, campaign finance, careerism, party discipline. Argument two, the transformation argument: put ordinary people in a room with balanced briefing materials, expert testimony, a decent facilitator and enough time, and they'll actually change their minds, reason together, land somewhere better than the aggregate of what they walked in with.

If the second is right, the first falls apart. A body that has genuinely deliberated is no longer a mirror of the population it was drawn from, because the population hasn't had that experience and the body has. The more the transformation succeeds, the less the mirroring holds at the moment that matters. So "it's representative" and "it deliberates well" can't both be doing the load-bearing work, and I want to know which one defenders are actually committed to — and what's left of the case for lottocracy once you give up the other. In my notes I've been calling the two things entry-mirroring and exit-mirroring, which is probbaly a bad pair of names, but the distinction is the one I want.

Some questions I'm circling, and I only want to commit to one or two in the final version:

Is the conflict real, or is it an artifact of describing mirroring in terms of preferences when it should be described in terms of interests, or social position, or being subject to the same laws? If mirroring only has to hold at entry — the value is just that everyone had an equal shot at being in the room, full stop, and who cares whether the output matches anything — then sortition collapses into a fairness-of-procedure view, which has competitors (rotation, compulsory jury-style service with no exemptions, sortition plus a veto, whatever), and I'd want to know whether it beats them or ties with them. If instead substantive representativeness of the *output* is what matters, then the lottocrat owes an account of why transformed preferences are authoritative for the untransformed majority, and that looks to me like an epistemic argument wearing descriptive clothing. In which case the honest version of lottocracy is the cognitive-diversity argument and the mirroring stuff is marketing. Is there a fourth option I'm not seeing?

Also — and this may turn out to be the more interesting half, or may turn out to be a separate paper — there's an empirical wrinkle that I think does conceptual work. Take-up rates for these invitations are low and skewed, so the equal chance isn't equal either, and the standard fix is stratified sampling from whoever said yes. But then the pool is self-selected for a certain civic disposition, and I suspect the transformation story is partly a story about who self-selects. If so, then scaling up from "advisory mini-public that met for four weekends" to "lottocracy as a constitution" isn't a scale-up, it's a change of mechanism: you can't opt out of a legislature the way you opt out of an invitation, and compulsory service plausibly changes the deliberative dynamics in both directions. I don't know yet whether that belongs in this paper or is a sequel.
