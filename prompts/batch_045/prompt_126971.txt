I'm writing the final paper for my seminar on decision theory and the philosophy of economics, and the section I keep failing to write is the one about what preferences and utility *are*. The austere reading we were given is that a person's preferences are nothing over and above the relation that rationalizes their observed choices — the thing tested by axioms like the weak one, which says roughly that if you pick x when y is on offer, there had better be no menu on which you pick y while x sits there unpicked — that 'utility' is merely a numerically convenient way of encoding that relation, and that talk of what someone really wants adds nothing we could ever test. I can recite the case for it, but I don't know whether I believe it, so I want to start from my own verdicts on cases and see what principle, if any, they add up to.

Amir picks the apple over the banana, the banana over the pear, and the apple over the pear, reliably, in ordinary circumstances: yes, his ranking really is apple above banana above pear. Nia picks the cheap laptop when only two are on offer, but switches to the mid-priced one once a third machine is added that nobody would ever choose; I say the three-option choice reveals no preference of hers at all, while the two-option one does. Kai takes the train even though driving is faster and he dislikes trains, because he expects the highway to be jammed; I say his choice reveals nothing about his ranking until you hold his beliefs fixed, and even then I am unsure whether what it reveals is a preference for trains or only a conditional one. Ines takes the higher-paying job with the long commute, stably and with full information, though she sincerely reports that she would be happier in the other one and would rather have taken it; here I want to say the choice wins and the report is about something else. Rhea, during a six-week depressive episode, declines every invitation, stops applying for jobs, and says nothing matters; after treatment she reverses all of it and is horrified by the letters she sent: I want to say those choices did not reveal her preferences, even though at the time she would have endorsed them. Jonah, after the birth of his daughter, gives away money, changes his diet, stops travelling, and holds the new pattern for years, resisting any suggestion of going back: I want to say his preferences genuinely changed, and that the psychological cause of the change does not discredit the new ones. Priya deletes a distracting app at midnight and wants it back at seven the next morning, and also pays for a savings account that imposes a twenty-four-hour delay on withdrawals; I want to say both of her choices reveal something real, but not the same thing, and I don't know whether one utility function can cover both. Deshi hands his whole portfolio to a robo-advisor and never overrides it, and cannot explain a single holding when asked; on some days I say the resulting pattern is his revealed preference, on others that it is the algorithm's. And a friend of mine pays more to eat at a restaurant with only three dishes and would pay to have the dessert menu taken away altogether, which strikes me as revealing a preference over menus rather than over dishes.

The two verdicts I can't reconcile are Rhea and Jonah: a shift in psychological state produces a shift in choice, and in one case I withhold the attribution while in the other I grant it; and notice that Ines pulls the other way from Rhea, since there I let the choice beat the agent's own report. My tentative principle is that a choice reveals a preference only if it is stable across small and irrelevant menu changes, only if it survives the correction of false beliefs about what the options are, and only if the agent would still make it under calm, vivid, informed reflection and would not pay to have it undone. The third clause is the one doing the work on Rhea and Jonah, but it also seems to turn preference into something partly forward-looking and partly evaluative rather than something present in the choice behaviour — which is exactly what the austere reading denies.

So: test that principle against my nine cases, repair it where it mispredicts, and tell me which clause is load-bearing and which is decoration. State the best candidates precisely, clause by clause, each tied to the case that forces it. Tell me honestly which of my verdicts no single principle can accommodate, and which I would have to give up, and at what cost, if I want to keep the austere reading. Say what utility turns out to be on each candidate — ordinal or cardinal, a measure of how well the agent's life goes or only an index of what she'd pick, and whether the revealed-preference axioms come out as empirical claims or as definitions. And say what the account predicts in the cases I haven't listed: a committee whose aggregate choices satisfy the axioms while its individual members' choices cycle; the same choices made under a two-second deadline; and an algorithm trained on someone's past choices doing the choosing for her from now on. The last one is the case I actually care about, since I think whatever I say about Deshi is what I really think about preference.
