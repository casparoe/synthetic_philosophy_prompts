So, context on me: I'm a self-taught programmer, never took an econ class in my life, and a while back I got completely nerd-sniped by that old socialist calculation debate. Here's the version of it I've pieced together from reading around online, and please correct me if I've got it wrong:

- Mises: without real market prices for the means of production, there's no way to do rational economic calculation at all, so a socialist economy isn't just impractical, it's flying blind.
- Hayek: related but kind of a different argument — the relevant knowledge is scattered across millions of people and a lot of it is local, tacit, temporary (a machinist noticing a machine is acting up, a shopkeeper noticing what the neighbourhood wants this month), so the problem isn't that planning is hard, it's that the information never exists in collectable form in the first place.
- Oskar Lange: no, it's fine — the central planning board can run a kind of simulated market. Tell firms to set price equal to marginal cost and produce until price equals marginal cost, then the board nudges prices up where there's a shortage and down where there's a surplus, and iterates until things roughly clear. My impression is Lange also sort of treated the whole thing as a big computation problem and thought electronic computers might eventually do the number-crunching. (Not sure if he really said that, it's just what I took away.)

OK. Now fast-forward, and here's the part I want you to engage with seriously. Set it in 2035, in a mid-sized country that actually tries to build Lange's board for real. The specific design is my own invention, so don't go looking for it in a book — I made it up:

1. Every firm, warehouse, farm, vehicle and household appliance emits a live telemetry stream — stock levels, throughput, idle time, energy draw, spoilage, queue lengths. Nobody fills in a form or reports anything on purpose; it's all just scraped.
2. The board builds a big simulated copy of the whole economy (a "digital twin") and, inside that simulation, trains one reinforcement-learning agent per firm. Each agent is rewarded for hitting its output targets while economising on inputs.
3. Nobody sets prices. Prices fall out of the simulation as the scarcity weights — the shadow prices — of a constrained optimisation: if the sim keeps running out of lithium, the lithium weight climbs. Those same weights are then used both to score the RL agents inside the sim and as the actual prices real firms pay.
4. The twin is retrained every night on the previous day's telemetry, so it's supposed to learn demand curves and production possibilities instead of being handed them. And nobody writes down a utility function; the objective is something vague like: maximise a moving average of a basket of welfare-ish indicators plus self-reported wellbeing surveys.

The question: if you could drop Oskar Lange into that country in 2035, what would he actually say? And if he thought there was still a problem, where exactly would he put it: in the data, in that vague objective function, in the fact that the RL agents have incentives to game or lie to the simulator, in the fact that it's trained on yesterday's behaviour and can therefore only ever interpolate, or somewhere else?
