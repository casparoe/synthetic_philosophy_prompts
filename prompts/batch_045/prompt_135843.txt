I teach a graduate seminar on induction and confirmation, and I want to present Goodman's new riddle of induction to students who think in statistical learning theory rather than in predicates and projectibility. My own grasp of that vocabulary is solid but second-hand: hypothesis classes and VC dimension, uniform convergence and PAC bounds, priors and minimum-description-length penalties, overfitting, the i.i.d. assumption, and domain adaptation under covariate shift.

Please restate, as faithfully as you can, four items in that vocabulary: first, the construction of grue, defined as green when examined before some future time t and blue otherwise, and why it yields a pair of hypotheses that fit all evidence so far and diverge thereafter; second, the claim that the two hypotheses are evidentially indistinguishable, and why that is a puzzle rather than a stand-off; third, Goodman's own answer in terms of entrenchment, the thought that predicates differ in how often they have actually been projected, together with its relativization to a language; fourth, the skeptical moral about lawlikeness and natural kinds, if there is one.

Then give me the residue report: what translates cleanly, what survives only after quietly adding assumptions absent from the original (I suspect the fixed-distribution assumption is the offender, since on a fixed test support the grue hypothesis is either identical to green or not in the class at all), and what has no counterpart. Two specific questions. Can entrenchment be rendered as a hierarchical or empirical-Bayes prior estimated from past environments, a case of meta-learning the inductive bias, or does its language-relativity make it inexpressible as a prior over hypotheses? And when the riddle is recast as unidentifiability under distribution shift, does it retain the force Goodman claimed, or has it become a different and more tractable problem? One constraint: you may not dismiss grue by appeal to a simplicity prior without first saying what that prior is a prior over and why the resulting ordering is not itself language-relative. Where translation fails, say whether the fault lies in the learning theorist's vocabulary or in the original notions.
