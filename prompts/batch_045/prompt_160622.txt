I am a retired physiologist who has taken up philosophy since leaving the lab, and I recently attended a public lecture on Maimonides' treatment of animal sacrifice. The lecturer's account, as I noted it down, ran roughly as follows.

Maimonides holds that sacrificial worship was simply the universal religious practice of the ancient Near East, and that the Israelites, being unable to abandon suddenly what they were accustomed to, would have found an imageless, bloodless worship unthinkable. God therefore tolerated the practice and redirected it toward Himself rather than toward the idols, rather as a physician gradually reduces a dose instead of withdrawing it at once. The consequence is that the sacrificial laws are a concession to human weakness, with no rationale of their own and no value in themselves — a temporary scaffold, not part of the religion's real content. And from this a further conclusion is drawn: the rite has authority only for those who still need it, so a person who has outgrown the need for a cult of blood and smoke owes it nothing and should confine himself to intellectual contemplation. Finally, since the purpose of the whole institution was the cure of idolatry, the details of the ritual — which species of animal, which vessels, how the blood is disposed of — are explained by exactly the same purpose, and in the same way.

I have no Hebrew and have not read the Guide closely, so I cannot check this myself. What makes me uneasy is only the shape of the argument: in my own field I have often seen a mechanism correctly described and then a corollary extracted that the mechanism does not actually support, and the exemption for the philosopher looks to me like that kind of move. But I can locate my suspicion, not the errors.

Would you go through the summary claim by claim and say what a Maimonides scholar would accept, what is merely a simplification of the kind a lecturer might permissibly make, and what he would reject outright? I am especially interested in two points: whether the conclusion about a philosopher's exemption really follows from the explanation offered for the rite's origin, and whether the same explanation can be extended to the minute details of the ritual. And if the summary is unfair, is it unfair in a way that matters — that is, does the corrected version still support the lecturer's broader framing of Maimonides as, in effect, offering a debunking genealogy of the cult rather than a vindication of it?
