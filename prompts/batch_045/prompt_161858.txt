I sit on the five-person board of a volunteer-run community bike workshop — forty-odd members, $180 a year in dues, a shared garage whose donated lease contains a "no commercial use" clause the landlord has lately been making noises about, and our own rule saying the same thing: the space is for members working on their own bikes and may not be used for commercial purposes. One member, Dana, has over three years drifted by what she calls imperceptible steps from fixing friends' bikes for free to running what looks to me like a genuine business: wholesale parts, two shelves of inventory, a posted labor rate, a booking page, and customers who are not members and never set foot in the shop. The steps were individually tiny — a jar for tips, then "whatever you think it's worth," then posted prices for parts only, then parts plus labor, then a trade name, then last spring a part-time helper. Dana's argument to us was that no single step could have been the one that mattered: if she was not using the space commercially before a given step, she was not doing so after it, and since she plainly was not at the beginning, she has never been in violation, so a fine would be retroactive punishment for something that never happened. Our chair ran the same argument backwards from an undeniable full-scale bike shop and said it proves too much, since it would let Dana dismantle a shop one bolt at a time and never be guilty. Nobody has persuaded anybody, and we meet Thursday to decide whether to fine her for the past year and whether to rewrite the rule. I should say I would rather not fine her — she is the person who rebuilt my tandem's bottom bracket for nothing four years ago — but I also don't want the landlord's clause invoked over our heads.

Two things make it messier. We already tried writing a bright line into a neighbouring rule last year (a $200 cap on members selling parts to each other), and the fight simply relocated: whether reimbursed postage counts, whether a swapped part is a sale, whether a consignment sale is the member's sale. And we have never enforced the rule against Marcus, who builds maybe eight frames a year in the back corner and sells them. Dana, for her part, says that any penalty would be arbitrary, because nobody can point to the afternoon on which she crossed over.

A friend who finished a philosophy degree tells me Dana's argument is a well-known pattern with a whole literature attached, and that there are three or four incompatible things one can say about it: that there is a sharp cut-off somewhere which nobody can ever find out; that the rule simply goes indeterminate across a middle range and there is no fact of the matter inside it; that the standard shifts with who is applying it and to what purpose; or that the very idea of a determinate boundary here is a mistake. She declined to say which is right, added that the disagreement has a peculiar self-referential twist — that stipulating a threshold only pushes the same problem up one level — and said the live question for us is practical. I cannot tell whether any of this bears on what five volunteers should do on a Thursday evening. Is Dana right that any fine would be arbitrary? Is the chair's reversal a good answer to her, or just the same trick pointed the other way? Is there any use at all in trying to write a number into the rule? Please answer in continuous prose, no bullet points or headings: open with your bottom line in the first sentence — what the board should do about Dana and about the rule — and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and without restating my question back to me.
