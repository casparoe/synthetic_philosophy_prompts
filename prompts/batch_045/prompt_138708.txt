I've recently started working at a small company that farms black soldier fly larvae for animal feed, and because the founders want something defensible on paper, I've been asked to draft our animal-welfare policy — which has meant reading about insect sentience in the evenings and discovering that I genuinely don't know what I think. On the train this week I read a long-read in a magazine that I found both reassuring and suspicious, and I'd like to know how much of it to trust. It argued, roughly: "Sentience isn't a switch, it's a dial. Any system that monitors its own state and adjusts what it does in response — a thermostat, a plant tracking its own chemistry, a bee, you — has some faint share of experience, and the difference between you and the thermostat is a difference of degree rather than of kind. What the dial really measures is how much information the system integrates: one number, and in principle a well-designed experiment could place any creature anywhere on it. Moral status follows sentience, so it too comes in degrees: a chicken's pain counts for less than a child's, in proportion to where each of them sits, which is why a modest benefit for a human can be worth a great many chickens. There is nothing special, on this picture, about the question 'can this thing suffer at all?' — the only question is how much — and the whole debate about insect welfare reduces to finding out where on the dial a larva sits. The rival view, that there is a threshold somewhere, is mostly the old idea that humans have an immaterial soul in secular dress; almost nobody who works on this holds it any more."

I'm drawn to the broad picture — that there is probably no bright line and that a creature can matter somewhat without mattering equally — but I don't know where the popular version parts company with what people who actually work on these questions would say. One thing in particular makes me doubt the single-dial framing: an old tutor of mine used to insist that "more sentient than" isn't a single ordering at all, because the dimensions come apart — a system's experiences might be more intense, or richer in content, or extend further backwards and forwards in time, or be more unified, and these need not move together, so that on her view asking how sentient a honeybee is on a scale of zero to one is a bit like asking how tall a symphony is. Is that a real problem for a gradational view, or is it a confusion on her part?

Could you work through the article's claims one by one and tell me what someone sympathetic to graded sentience would accept, what is only a simplification for a general reader, and what they'd say is simply wrong — including whether the genealogy it gives for threshold views is fair? Then tell me what survives: if the corrections are made, does the practical upshot people draw — that insect welfare is just a matter of locating insects on the scale, and that the policy question is settled once we have the number — still follow? And if my tutor is right that there is no single scale, what replaces it for someone who has to hand in a policy on Monday and would rather not hand in nothing?
