I served on a criminal jury last month. We had the same evidence, the same instructions, and three days to talk, and we still deadlocked, I think nine to three. The judge called us back into court and read us an instruction urging those in the minority to reconsider whether their doubts were really reasonable, reminding us that a retrial would cost everyone time and money, and then discharged us; the case will now be tried again from scratch before a different jury, and could in principle come out the other way.

When I described this to a friend, she said it was a nice illustration of Aumann's agreement theorem. As I understand the result, it says that two people who start out with the same prior probabilities, who update properly on their own evidence, and who know each other's posterior probabilities must end up assigning the same probability to a claim, so that any disagreement that survives must come from somewhere else: different priors, evidence one side has not disclosed, or some failure of rationality.

What I want to know is what the practice I was part of actually presupposes about disagreement.

First: the whole apparatus seems designed to engineer those antecedents. Jurors are screened beforehand for fixed opinions and struck if they have them, they are confined to evidence presented in open court and told not to gather their own, they are made to deliberate, holdouts are instructed to reconsider, and failure to agree is treated as a breakdown of the process rather than as an outcome. Is the best reconstruction of this that the law is trying to manufacture a common prior and then force the exchange that would make posteriors common knowledge? Or does the practice need only something much weaker?

Second: I suspect the theorem may be about the wrong object. We were not asked to agree on a probability of guilt, but on whether guilt had been proved beyond reasonable doubt, and from where I sat the real disagreement was about where that threshold sits and how much weight to give a witness's demeanor. Does the unanimity requirement then presuppose that there is a single correct threshold, or can it be defended on grounds that never require agreement about probabilities at all?

Third: if my friend is right that the practice rests on a false picture of what shared evidence can accomplish, what would have to change — majority verdicts, verdicts on the balance of probabilities, individual reasons recorded from each juror, or something else? And is there anything in the way the institution is set up, such as retrying the identical case before a new jury, that shows it was never really committed to that picture in the first place?
