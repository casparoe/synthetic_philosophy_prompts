I'm retired now — forty years as a field ecologist, mostly freshwater invertebrates — and I've started going to a philosophy reading group at the local library, which is how I ended up reading about wild animal suffering. I'm trying to write down where I've got to, because it feels like the bits don't quite fit and I can't see where. Here's the set, roughly in order of how confident I am:

1. Welfare belongs to individuals only. A species, a population, an ecosystem — none of them has a welfare of its own, so nothing is literally good or bad *for* an ecosystem.
2. All the same, I use "ecosystem health" and "integrity" every day and I don't think I'm cheating. It tracks something real about a place.
3. Intact ecosystems run at a welfare loss, summed over time. Most of what's born dies in weeks or months and not nicely. My own lot were insect larvae and fish fry, and there are orders of magnitude more of them than of anything else — though I'll admit I don't actually know whether they feel anything at all.
4. When you compare what to do with a patch of ground, the sensible comparison is total welfare summed over the whole future, not the welfare of whatever happens to be breathing in it at the moment.
5. Habitat destruction is a serious moral wrong. I spent my working life arguing it and I'm not giving it up. I cash it out in terms of the animals already living there getting killed or pushed out, and how grim the transition is. I've never rested it on the headcount afterwards.
6. We should leave wild animals alone. No culling predators, no mass vaccination, no feeding programmes, no redesigning ecosystems so they hurt less. The models can't forecast second-order effects and every time we've tried we've made a mess of it.
7. But I don't accept "we're too uncertain to act" as a general excuse. When the numbers are that big, expected value has to bite somewhere — that's the same reasoning I use to say anyone should care about this in the first place.
8. I've also got a lot of sympathy with that thought (I only know it from summaries) that you can't complain on behalf of individuals who would never have existed anyway, which makes me unsure what I'm even saying in 4.

A bloke at the group says 2 is holism coming back in by the back door; someone on a forum says 6 is just cowardice given what I say in 3. I definately don't trust either of them, and I don't know which bit I'd drop if I had to.

So: audit this for me. I'd like a table with these columns:

- commitments: which numbered items are in play
- severity: rank, with 1 being the one that most needs fixing
- real_or_apparent: a genuine conflict, or only looks like one
- diagnosis: what exactly commits me to what, in two or three sentences
- cheapest_fix: the smallest change to a single commitment that clears it
- cost: what I lose elsewhere in the set if I make that change
- probe: one concrete field case that would force me to pick a side

Rank them rather than just listing, and don't treat all eight as equally guilty — I'd rather you told me one of them is fine than hedged. Two things I'd especially like sorted. First: whether the same uncertainty I lean on in 6 should also be making me doubt 3, given that 3 comes out of the same sort of demographic data as everything else I trust, and if not, what the difference is meant to be. Second: whether 8 quietly makes 4 unusable, or whether there's a way of holding both. Then, under the table, one line: the single revision that clears up the most.
