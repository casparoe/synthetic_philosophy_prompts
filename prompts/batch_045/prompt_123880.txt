I want a prediction, in your own voice rather than in character, of what Thomas Hobbes would have said about a puzzle in rational choice theory that was first posed roughly three centuries after *Leviathan* — argued from the machinery of his own theory, not from secondary literature, and with no citations.

The case is Derek Parfit's hitchhiker. A traveller stranded in the desert promises a passing motorist a sum of money if the motorist drives him to the city. Once in the city the traveller has nothing to gain and nothing to fear from paying: the motorist cannot compel him, will never see him again, and has no channel through which to damage his reputation. Yet a traveller who could not bring himself to pay would never have been rescued. The interest he is supposed to serve is therefore served only if, beforehand, he becomes the kind of person who will later do what at the moment of acting he has no reason to do — the phenomenon now labelled rational irrationality. The usual reply is that he should arrange to be the paying kind; the usual objection is that dispositions, and the beliefs that carry them, are not installed at will.

Hobbes is the right historical test case because he stages this argument himself. In *Leviathan* he puts the challenge in the mouth of a fool who holds that keeping a promise is not contrary to reason when breaking it serves one's benefit. On the reading I will assume, and which you should say if you reject, Hobbes's answer rests on two things: that a man known to break his word cannot reasonably expect to be received into any confederacy that might preserve him, and that the fool has overlooked a power he is unable to resist. Both moves depend on the hazard of detection and on the value of future cooperation. The stipulations of the hitchhiker case remove exactly those.

Work through three variants, stating for each what verdict you predict Hobbes would give and which of his doctrines he would reach for.

1. *The sealed desert.* Detection risk and future interaction are zero by stipulation, and the promise was not extorted by fear of the promisee — the traveller fears dying of thirst, not the motorist — so nothing turns on Hobbes's doctrine of promises made under coercion. Would he call the refusal to pay an injustice, given his account of injustice as non-performance of covenant? Or no injustice at all, on the ground that a covenant of mutual trust made where neither side can be sure the other will perform is void upon reasonable suspicion, so that the motorist who performs first has only betrayed himself and is himself at fault? If he falls back on the law of nature requiring gratitude to a benefactor, does that law bind the traveller inwardly while releasing him outwardly, given that the outward performance would expose him to no danger?

2. *The legible traveller.* Suppose now that the traveller is a thing whose decision procedure can be read off with certainty in advance — a mechanism whose source the motorist inspects before deciding whether to stop — so that what matters is not the act in the city but the disposition antecedent to it. Does Hobbes's psychology permit an agent to reshape its own appetites for the sake of such an inspection, given that he takes reason to be reckoning, the passions not to be chosen, and the sovereign to be the teacher of the laws of nature? Would he have to say the traveller must already have been trained into the disposition by education or by the sovereign, and would he even allow that a motive can be acquired self-consciously, for the reason that having it is useful?

3. *The sword arrives late.* The two reach a commonwealth in which the promise is enforceable. Is Hobbes's moral simply that the case is one more argument for the commonwealth, or is there an awkwardness he must handle: the covenant was made where there was no power to compel performance, so the sovereign's courts inherit an agreement that his own rules may already have made void? To keep the story coherent, would he have to say the courts should not enforce it?

Close with the general question. Once the hazard of detection is stipulated away, how much of Hobbes's reply to the fool is left standing, and what distinction or doctrine that he did not have would he have needed in order to answer the case as posed? Mark clearly where your prediction turns on a contested reading of Hobbes rather than on something his texts settle.
