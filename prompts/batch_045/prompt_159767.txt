I'm putting together a seminar session on the extended mind whose whole point is to unsettle a lazy assumption students arrive with: that the extended mind thesis is simply the permissive view about where cognition stops, and that its opponents are simply the restrictive ones. To make that point I need a case where a middle view grants extension while the extended mind thesis withholds it. Please build one.

Three positions, stated here so you don't have to reconstruct any literature:

(A) Extension by parity: an external resource counts as part of a subject's cognition when it plays the functional role an internal process would play and meets the usual integration conditions — it is there when needed, easy to access, reliable, and its deliverances are automatically endorsed.

(B) Wide computationalism: an external resource counts as part of a subject's cognition when the computation the coupled system performs cannot be individuated without citing that resource — the resource is a vehicle of the computation. On this view it does not matter whether the subject retrieves content from the resource, endorses anything, or could say what is "stored" there.

(C) Intracranialism: cognition is constituted by brain-bound states with non-derived content; environmental resources can cause, scaffold, and reshape cognition but never constitute it.

The case must yield these verdicts: (B) says yes, (A) says no, (C) says no — so that the view famous for extending the mind ends up agreeing with the view famous for confining it. The reversal has to be generated by the theories, not stipulated into the scenario.

Constraints on the scenario:

- One paragraph. Ordinary present-day human setting: no implants, no brain–computer interfaces, no science fiction.
- The resource must be inanimate and non-digital: no notebook, phone, GPS, written list, or search engine. It must not be a repackaging of the notebook case, the blind person's cane, or an arrangement of tiles or tokens used to solve a puzzle.
- No other person's cognition does the work: no colleagues, no social institutions, no division of labour across agents.
- The agent knows the resource is there and deliberately relies on it. The case may not work by making her unaware of it, deceived about it, or unusually forgetful or impaired.
- The resource should be something she did not design herself and could not simply carry away with her.
- At no point may you stipulate that the resource is, or is not, part of her mind; every verdict has to come from (A)–(C) applied to the facts of the case.

After the scenario, give me:

1. The verdict each of (A), (B), and (C) returns, with the reasoning that gets there — for (A), which of its conditions goes unmet and why; for (B), why the computation cannot be individuated without the resource; for (C), what it takes the resource to be merely doing.
2. The single feature of the case that is load-bearing, stated explicitly as a variable someone could toggle.
3. What happens when that variable is toggled: which verdicts flip, and why the case then collapses into a familiar instance that does no dialectical work.
4. The strongest reply available to the (A) theorist — the best way for her to argue that this is not a counterexample at all but a case of something else (mere scaffolding, embodiment, a coupled-but-not-constituted system, or whatever reclassification you think is strongest) — together with your honest assessment of whether that reply rescues (A) or merely relocates the real disagreement into the (B) versus (C) dispute.
5. One sentence on whether you think (B)'s verdict here is a cost or a benefit for that view.

Format: begin your reply with the scenario itself — its first sentence is the first sentence of your reply, with no heading, no preamble, and no restating of this request. In everything that follows, lead with each section's bottom line and then give the reasoning.
