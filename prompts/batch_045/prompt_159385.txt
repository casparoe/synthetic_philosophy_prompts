I convene a termly reading group for first-year philosophy students: no physics beyond school level, but several of them arrive having absorbed the popular claim that relativity shows the past and future to be equally real, and therefore that physics has already settled whether the things they will decide next week are in some sense settled now. I have one ninety-minute session on the Rietdijk–Putnam argument, and what I want from you is a plan for teaching it, not an explanation of it.

Please give me the following. First, the order in which the components must be introduced and the dependency behind that order: what has to be in place before the idea of a simultaneity slice can do any work, why the train of reasoning needs the disagreement to be about distant events rather than about signal delay, and where in the sequence the words now and already should first be permitted — I want to know what goes wrong if they are allowed in earlier, since I suspect most of the confusion in the room will be about which of those words is doing the real work.

Second, the one case that should carry the whole session, together with an account of what a carrier case must contain and must not contain. My candidate is the Andromeda-style case: two people strolling past each other in opposite directions on a street, whose simultaneity slices cut a very distant galaxy differently, so that on one of them a decision there has yet to be taken and on the other it has been taken. Tell me whether that is the right carrier, what it needs to make vivid, and what it must avoid — in particular it must not invite the thought that anyone is seeing or receiving anything — and, if I should use the train-and-lightning arrangement instead, why.

Third, tie the session to a distinction I want them to leave with, and tell me how to build it: on the one hand, the claim that there is no frame-invariant present and that reality is not tensed; on the other, the claim that future events are as determinate as past ones, so that what you will do is already fixed. I want them to see that these are separate theses, to see which one the argument is plausibly an argument for, and to see why the second is the one that would actually disturb them. Tell me at which step the distinction should be drawn so that it lands as a discovery rather than as a taxonomy I read out.

Fourth, the misconceptions to expect and the exact point in the sequence where each must be headed off. I can anticipate four: that the disagreement between the walkers is a disagreement about what they can observe; that the upshot is fatalism about deliberation; that calling simultaneity frame-relative makes the whole matter merely perspectival and therefore uninformative about what is real; and the under-reaction that nothing of metaphysical interest follows at all. Add any you think I have missed — I have a suspicion that someone will ask why, if the future is real, we do not remember it, and I do not know where that belongs. One of the sharper objections I expect from a better-prepared student is that being real relative to an observer is not transitive across frames, so the step that chains slices together into an all-at-once reality fails; tell me where in the sequence to raise this, and how to raise it so that it sharpens the argument rather than ending the session in the shrug that nothing follows.

Fifth, what I should openly decline to explain at this level, with a one-line reason I can give the room for each omission — I have in mind the Lorentz transformation, general-relativistic complications about whether a spacetime admits any global slicing at all, and the free-will literature proper.

Finally, a single closing question that would let me tell whether the idea has landed rather than merely alarmed them. My own candidate: suppose we keep every empirical prediction of special relativity intact but add to the world a physically undetectable preferred foliation, and ask which of the two theses above that addition would refute. Say whether this is the right discriminator, why, and what answers should count as success, as near-miss, and as failure.
