Looking for a consistency check on where I've landed while putting together a small benchmark of Bongard problems (twelve little pictures, six on the left that share some property, six on the right that lack it, and the job is to find the rule). Here's the set:

(1) Whether a system has got the concept is settled by whether it generalises to new figures; whether it can say the rule out loud in words is irrelevant.
(2) Each problem has one intended rule, and a system that locks onto some other rule that also fits all twelve pictures has got it wrong, not merely found an equally good answer.
(3) The general way to make which rule precise is a simplicity / minimum-description-length prior: prefer the shortest consistent description.
(4) The left-right structure is doing real work, and the right-hand figures should be produced by negating the intended rule rather than sampled independently.
(5) What I actually want to measure is compositionality: whether the system recombines primitives — counting, containment, symmetry, contact — in combinations that never showed up in training.
(6) Hand-written problems are curated and leak, so everything should be procedurally generated at scale.

The case that made me nervous: the way my generator builds the two classes, positives come out with two shapes and negatives with three, so there are exactly two shapes classifies all twelve correctly, is shorter than the intended rule (something like one shape is inside another), and agrees with it on every figure the generator can emit, but comes apart the moment a human draws three nested circles. I've started calling these shadow rules and I've been marking solvers that use them as wrong, but I'm not sure that's consistent with the rest.

Go through (1)-(6): which pairs genuinely collide, which only look like they do, and what's the cheapest thing to drop and what does dropping it cost me? Rank the real tensions by severity instead of flagging everything, and tell me which single commitment has to give.
