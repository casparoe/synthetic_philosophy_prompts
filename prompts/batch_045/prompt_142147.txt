My housemate is doing a PhD in something adjascent to institutional economics, and last night after a couple of drinks she backed me into an argument about races to the bottom that I haven't managed to get out of. We started from whether the AI-lab safety race is really a Moloch case or just an ordinary competitive market with negative externalities, and ended somewhere much worse. Her claim isn't that races to the bottom don't happen — she thinks they're everywhere — it's that once you take the standard story seriously it proves that no one inside one ever does anything wrong and that the standard fix is unmotivated too, which has got to be broken somewhere. As well as I can reconstruct it:

(1) A race to the bottom is just a many-person prisoner's dilemma: whatever the others do, each participant does better by defecting, and if everyone defects then everyone is worse off than if everyone had held the line.
(2) In any such situation big enough to be interesting — a fishery, an industry, a research field — no individual participant's choice makes a difference to whether the bad outcome arrives. Whether the stock collapses or the safety norm erodes doesn't hinge on what any single boat or lab does; if she sits out, someone else takes her place.
(3) If an act makes no difference to any outcome that anyone cares about, then there's nothing wrong with it and no reason not to do it — or at least no consequentialist reason, and it's hard to see where else one would come from, since blaming someone for something that changed nothing looks like pure theatre.
(4) So no participant in a race to the bottom does anything wrong by defecting, and none of them has any reason to restrain herself. Which already feels like too much: it lets the tenth lab to skip its safety review off the hook exactly as much as the first.
(5) The usual cure is meant to be: don't ask individuals to be saints, change the game — build the quota, the norm, the punishment, so that defecting stops paying.
(6) But getting an institution built is itself a collective action problem among the very same people, and in a group that size nobody's contribution makes a difference to whether it gets built.
(7) So by (3), nobody has a reason to help build it either, and the cure collapses for precisely the reason the disease was supposed to be tragic.
(8) So for any large-scale race to the bottom: nothing anyone does is wrong and nothing anyone could do is worth doing. Which is absurd, and also says something very strange about the people who invoke Moloch as a call to action.

I'd rather keep (1) and (5) than swallow (8) — I do think there's something real in the Canaanite-god framing, and I do think "fix the payoff structure, don't preach at people" is the right instinct — so if something has to go I'd want it to be (3) or (6), but both of those look like things I believe outside this argument: (3) looks like the plain thought that you can't be blamed for what made no difference, and (6) looks like the plain fact that large groups are hard to organise. My housemate also threw in two distinctions I don't know what to do with. First, she says real races aren't one-shot games at all: the whole point of a race is that each defection resets the baseline for everyone downstream, so the first lab to drop the review isn't interchangeable with the tenth — she has a name for those, something like 'hinge defections' — and if that's right then maybe (2) is just a bad model rather than a fact, though for all I know she made that up to win the argument. Second, she separates cases where the harm is threshold-shaped like an election from cases where it's continuous like a fish stock, and both of those from cases where the person who refuses to defect doesn't choose anything at all but simply gets selected out: the firm goes under, the paper doesn't get published. I think those distinctions are load-bearing somewhere but I can't tell which step they hit. So: which step do you reject, why did it look fine before you looked closely, and what does rejecting it cost you — what else do you have to give up, and which of the steps you kept gets weaker once you've paid?
