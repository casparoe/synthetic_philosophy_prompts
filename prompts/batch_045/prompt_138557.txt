Here's the question: what should count as philosophical progress? I want you to compare two quite different diagnostics, and I want it done in the weeds, not at the level of generalities.

Diagnostic one is the boring obvious one — call it closure. On this picture a subfield progresses to the extent that its first-order questions actually get retired: either answered to the field's satisfaction, or dissolved as confused or ill-posed. The crude proxy I have in mind: take a sample of the questions people in some area were visibly fighting about a century or two ago, and ask what fraction a working philosopher today would treat as closed rather than live. Those big surveys of what philosophers currently believe are sort of a shadow of this idea, except that agreement isn't really the same thing as closure, and I want you to say why that difference matters rather than assume it does.

Diagnostic two is one I half-invented after an argument with a friend, call it re-derivability. The thought: maybe philosophy's real product isn't answers at all but cognitive technology — distinctions, argument-forms, ways of building and killing counterexamples, moves that used to take a genius thirty years and now take a decent graduate student a week. On this view a field is progressing when its hard-won insights become cheap to re-acquire: you could delete the literature, hand a smart outsider the problems and the current conceptual toolkit, and they'd re-derive most of the good stuff fast, and reliably, and without needing to relive the field's confusions. Progress is compression of discovery cost, not accumulation of settled propositions.

So: compare them. Concretely, I want several things from you.

1. Where do they come apart? Give me cases — real ones you can reconstruct from memory, e.g. the justified-true-belief-style analysis of knowledge, the mind-body problem, induction, the sorites, or whatever you think makes the point better — where one diagnostic says big progress and the other says little or none. I especially want the failure modes of each: what would a subfield look like that racks up closure without progress (fashion? dogma? people just getting bored and drifting off?) and one that racks up re-derivability without progress (everything looks obvious in hindsight, we just keep rediscovering the same four positions more cheaply forever?). Which failure mode is more common in philosophy as you actually know it?

2. Which diagnostic is the better *description* of what philosophy has in fact managed to do, and which is the better *target* — the thing we should aim at? I don't think these have to be the same answer, and I want you to be explicit if they aren't.

My friend's objection, which you have to deal with: she says re-derivability isn't a separate idea at all, it's just compression of the disagreement space in disguise — the real claim being that progress consists in many surface disputes collapsing into a handful of deep cruxes (so you can span the field's whole pattern of disagreement with fewer and fewer independent questions), and that what I'm calling cheap re-derivation is just a side effect of that collapse. Is she right? Is there daylight between the two, e.g. a possible world where a field gets much better at re-deriving its own arguments without any compression of disagreement, or vice versa?
