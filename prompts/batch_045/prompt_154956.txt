I help out a guy who runs a tiny forecasting newsletter where he re-scores famous long-run doom predictions, and I'm the one who maintains the spreadsheet, so I keep hitting cases his labelling scheme doesn't obviously cover. His verdicts so far: Malthus's 1798 argument that population grows geometrically while food supply grows only arithmetically — falsified, no rescue, because the positive checks he predicted for Europe never showed up and per-head food went up instead. Ehrlich's Population Bomb, with its famines in the 1970s killing hundreds of millions and its opening claim that the battle to feed humanity is already lost — falsified on the same grounds, and he gets visibly annoyed when people say "right about the pressure, early about the date." Hubbert's 1956 call that US oil production would peak around 1970 — right, and on time, his one clean success. The late-nineties and 2000s calls that world oil would peak somewhere around 2005–2010 and then decline permanently — falsified, because what actually arrived was shale and then a price collapse rather than permanent scarcity. Limits to Growth's business-as-usual run — "right but early," which he defends by pointing at a 2008 paper (Turner? I always forget whether its Turner) that lined the standard run up against thirty years of actual data and found it hadn't diverged yet. Molina and Rowland on CFCs and the ozone layer — "self-defeating," his highest honour: the mechanism got independent confirmation from the Antarctic ozone hole and the catastrophe was then legislated away. Jevons on coal exhaustion — he splits it, falsified on exhaustion but right-but-early on the rebound effect. And fusion power, which has been thirty years away since the fifties — he refuses to call that early at all, on the grounds that each expired deadline is its own falsification and re-dating isn't a rescue.

The one I've just put in front of him is antimicrobial resistance: the mid-2010s projection that resistance will be killing something like ten million people a year by 2050, usually pitched as exponential growth of resistance against the slow, arithmetic pace of drug discovery, which is literally Malthus's ratio argument wearing a lab coat. I'm also told that more recent burden studies put current attributable deaths at more like one to two million a year, though I can never remember which study is which, so don't hold me to that — but if that's right, then hitting the 2050 number needs an enormous acceleration, not just a continuation.

What I actually want isn't the verdict, it's the rule. Can you set out two or three rules that fit all of the verdicts above about equally well — I can see at least a "has the test window closed yet, and was the miss caused by a response to the prediction" rule, a "was the escape valve already in view when he made the call" rule, and something structural about ratio arguments versus fixed-stock arguments — then say what each predicts about antimicrobial resistance, which one you think he is really running, and where they come apart? My own guess is that the window rule calls it right-but-early-but-unratable, the foreseeability rule says the pipeline problem was already visible in 2016 so a miss would be culpable rather than unlucky, and the structural rule says it's Malthus's mistake again and should be discounted hard — but I can't tell which of those he'd weight most, and I also suspect theres no box in his scheme for "the window hasn't closed and maybe can't close for another twenty-five years," which if true is a problem for the whole project. Last thing: if you could hand him exactly one more case to force the disagreement out into the open, what would it be? I keep coming back to the Green Revolution — whether the thing that falsified Ehrlich was itself a response to Malthusian alarm or was going to happen anyway — or to a prediction that shipped with a pre-registered falsification condition from the start, but I'd rather hear your pick and why it separates them better than mine does.
