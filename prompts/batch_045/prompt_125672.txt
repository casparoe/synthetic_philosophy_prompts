I am giving a paper at a joint ancient philosophy and moral psychology seminar in Cambridge next month, and I want a Q&A preparation sheet rather than a defence of the view. My rough sense of the room: about half specialists in early Chinese thought — one of them philologically-minded and suspicious of any reading that turns these texts into analytic moral psychology, one who reads the Xunzi's chapter on human nature chiefly as a polemic framed against Mencius and as political theory rather than as psychology, one who holds the deflationary view that Xunzi's claim is that our raw endowment is unruly rather than evil and that Mencius's claim is that we have the wherewithal to become good rather than that we are already good — and about half analytic moral psychologists and ethicists, including at least one person who works on nativism and prosocial behaviour in infancy and one who is broadly situationist about character.

The thesis I am defending is that the familiar framing of the dispute as a disagreement about whether human nature is good or bad is misleading, and that the genuine disagreement concerns what I call the deliberative transparency of our own motives. On my reading, Mencius holds that the sprouts are transparent to the agent: someone stirred by alarm and distress at the sight of a child about to fall into a well can, merely by reflecting on what moved her, recognise it as the beginning of something a Confucian would call benevolence, so cultivation is interpretive and extensional — attending to, and then extending outward, motives one already has. Xunzi denies this: the raw motivational endowment bears no intrinsic relation to the Confucian description of it, so the description must be supplied from outside, through ritual, teachers and exemplary models, and then made to stick through practice until it becomes a second nature; cultivation is therefore transformative, not interpretive. The supporting argument I am least confident about is methodological: I claim that the asymmetry is visible in the argumentative forms each thinker relies on, Mencius addressing the reader in the second person with first-personal thought experiments — imagine yourself at the well, imagine your own reaction to the ox being led to sacrifice — and Xunzi arguing entirely through third-personal craft analogies, crooked wood on the straightening frame, clay on the potter's wheel, a dull blade on the whetstone, and that this is evidence for the reading rather than a stylistic accident. I intend to concede the two deflationary points about the meanings of good and bad at the outset, so as to take the sting out of them.

What I would like from you: the eight or so objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased in the idiom of whichever subgroup would press it rather than in mine, and — where the objection belongs to a particular person in the room — a note on whose it is. I can anticipate the general shape of two of them, the nativist's worry that my reading of Mencius commits me to an empirical claim about innate moral content that the developmental literature does not support, and the specialists' worry that I am smuggling an anachronistic nature/nurture distinction and an analytic notion of a motive into texts that have neither, so please state those more sharply than I can state them myself rather than merely listing them. For each objection give me a reply I could deliver in about two minutes, and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Then tell me which objection I should concede in the body of the talk itself in order to defuse it in advance, which single reply has to land if the paper is to survive at all, and, finally, the question I am hoping nobody asks: my guess is that it concerns how a reading on which the two thinkers turn out not to be contradicting each other can survive Xunzi's explicitly naming Mencius and rejecting the claim that our nature is good, so tell me whether that really is the dangerous one and what I should say if it comes. Please write the whole thing in British English, since the handout will go out under a British series title, and please do not pad it with a bibliography or suggestions for further reading.
