I'm a novelist and I'm trying to write a dinner-table argument about healthcare that doesn't read like cable news, and I've realised I don't actually understand why the argument never goes anywhere. Two of my friends — an ER doctor who wants single payer, a health economist who wants more market in it — have been having basically the same exchange for a decade. They agree on a lot: the US spends far more for worse population health, emergency departments are a lousy way to deliver primary care, nobody should be bankrupted by getting sick, administrative billing is a parasite. And whenever a new study lands, each absorbs it without moving. The doctor's case is mostly about people skipping doses and dying with insurance paperwork in hand, and about care being something you're owed rather than something you shop for; the economist's is about queues, about prices doing work that rationing-by-waiting doesn't, about who pays for the next drug if nobody can charge for it, and about the option to buy above whatever floor exists.

My pet theory, which I want you to try to break, is that they're not describing the same object: each is picturing the other side's system as it would actually be implemented here, by legislators and insurers they don't trust, while picturing their own as it exists in the head of the person proposing it. I've started calling it the offstage-system problem. If that's right the hidden question is empirical but unanswerable — what this system would mutate into in our politics — and the stalemate is just the absence of the experiment. But maybe it's simpler than that: a values fight dressed as a predictions fight, or a fight about whether we're maximising a floor nobody falls below or the freedom to buy above it, and neither says which.

Diagnose it using what each side actually argues, tell me whether my offstage-system thing is doing real work or is just a fancy way of saying they distrust each other, and tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a kind of evidence, a different question — or whether I should accept it doesn't resolve and write the scene that way.
