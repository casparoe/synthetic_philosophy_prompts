I have been reading philosophy of physics for fun for about fifteen years, ever since a blog post about gauge freedom sent me down the rabbit hole, and I have ended up stuck on the hole argument — long enough that I now understand it well enough to be dangerous. I want a poem out of it, and I want it to be a real poem rather than a versified explainer.

Please write it as a sestina: six stanzas of six unrhymed lines, plus a three-line envoi, thirty-nine lines total, with six end-words permuted by the standard rotation. In case the rule is easier to apply than to recall, here it is: if a stanza ends 1 2 3 4 5 6, the next ends 6 1 5 2 4 3, then 3 6 4 1 2 5, then 5 3 2 6 1 4, then 4 5 1 3 6 2, then 2 4 6 5 3 1. In the envoi all six return, one inside each line and one at each line's end; the order is your call, but say which you picked. Pick the six words yourself — they should be short, unrhyming, and capable of genuinely changing their sense as they land in new company, since that is the point of the form.

The argument, so you are working from my framing rather than from memory: take a general-relativistic model as a manifold plus a metric plus matter fields, and assume manifold substantivalism — the manifold's points are individuals that exist independently of the fields laid over them, and their identity is not conferred by the fields. Pick a hole: an open region with no matter in it. Now apply a diffeomorphism that is the identity everywhere outside the hole but non-trivial inside it, and drag the fields along. You get two models that agree on everything outside the hole — every observation, every bit of matter — but disagree inside it about which point has which curvature. If both represent distinct physical possibilities, the theory is radically indeterministic: fix the fields up to the hole's boundary and nothing inside is determined. So the substantivalist either pays for primitive haecceities with indeterminism, or concedes that diffeomorphic models represent one and the same world, which is Leibniz equivalence, or tries the sophisticated move of keeping the points while denying that their identity carries across models.

Here is the wrinkle I actually want you to engage rather than nod at. A sestina is the one verse form defined by invariance under a non-trivial permutation: the rotation is a symmetry of the form, and if you apply it a seventh time you are back where you started, so the orbit closes. That makes a sestina inadvertently relationalist in a way a sonnet is not — its identity seems to be the orbit, not the representative. But then the uncomfortable corollary: a sestina with the same six words in any of the six orders would be the same poem, and the distinctness of the stanzas is as surplus as the identity of manifold points. I would like the poem to bite that bullet, or to say why it does not have to.

Structurally: let the first stanza build the naive substantivalist picture honestly, as something a smart person could believe. Let one middle stanza be the hole itself — an empty region where nothing happens except the words moving, so that within that stanza the two readings say exactly the same thing, and a reader who is tracking the argument can find the hole and see why it matters that the rearrangement is non-trivial there and trivial elsewhere. Put the argumentative turn in the fourth or fifth stanza, and make the turn the realization that the choice is not whether there are spacetime points but whether identity of points across models comes for free. Save the adjudication for the envoi, and give me one line there that only a substantivalist could love and one that only a relationalist could. Someone who knows the hole argument should recognize it rather than recognize the slogan, and should not wince; no prose summary of the argument inside the poem itself.

Take your time. Before drafting, sketch in a few sentences how you would state the argument technically, which end-word carries which burden, and where the turn lands; then write the poem.

Then add a short closing note, two or three paragraphs: which reading of the argument the poem is committed to; where it takes liberties or leans on a contested step (for instance, in sliding from isomorphism of models to identity of represented worlds, or in assuming Leibniz equivalence rather than arguing for it); and the part I care about most — what would change your mind. Name the single strongest consideration a substantivalist could offer that would lead you to write the poem the other way round, whether as sophisticated substantivalism or as the line of reply I half-remember which says the argument is weaker than it looks precisely because the two models are isomorphic, and say where the poem's own reasoning is most likely to fail. I am not asking for references, and please do not go looking for any — argue from the sketch.
