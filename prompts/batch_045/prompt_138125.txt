Write a pantoum of four quatrains — sixteen lines — to be read aloud at the close of the last session of a graduate seminar on non-classical logic. Some of the people in the room will know the material well; some will be attending out of politeness. The poem should work for both, and it should get the philosophy right rather than decorate it.

The mechanics of the form, in case you want them stated: the second and fourth lines of each stanza reappear as the first and third lines of the following stanza, so that every stanza carries two lines forward and contributes two new ones. In the final stanza, the first and third lines are the two carried from the stanza before, while its second and fourth lines should be the poem's own third and first lines, so that the poem closes on the words it opened with. Rhyme is optional; what must be audible is the return.

The situation. On a border, at dusk, there stands a boundary stone with one sentence cut into each face. The east face reads: "The sentence cut on the other face is true." The west face reads: "The sentence cut on the other face is not true." Follow it round. If the east sentence is true, then, exactly as it says, the west sentence is true; and the west sentence says that the east sentence is not true; so the east sentence is not true. And if the east sentence is not true, then the west sentence — which says precisely that — is true; and the east sentence says only that the west sentence is true; so the east sentence is true. There is no way to give each of the two sentences exactly one of the two classical values.

The poem is to be written from the dialetheist's side, and I want three commitments to be legible in it.

First, both sentences come out both true and false. Not meaningless, and not "neither true nor false": that is the gap theorist's answer, and it is the answer this poem declines. If the thought that they are neither gets a voice at all, let it be a temptation the speaker refuses rather than the resolution. The reason for holding out is, roughly, that the ordinary equivalence between a sentence and the claim that it is true is worth keeping even at the cost of a true contradiction, whereas the gap theorist has to restrict that equivalence to escape.

Second, the contradiction is local. Nothing else in the poem may be made to follow from it: the weather, the clerk's errand, the sheep, the treaty, whatever else the poem contains, should go on exactly as before, neither infected nor promoted. The poem must not end in a cascade of unrelated marvels, and it must not slide into saying that everything is true. The claim is that these sentences are true and false while nearly everything else is not.

Third, make the returning lines do the work. The east-face sentence should be the poem's first line and its last. The west-face sentence should be one of the lines that gets carried forward, and each time it comes back it should arrive in new company — as a plain report, as a denial, as a description of what is cut in stone, as something said to the walker — so that the same words are heard doing different work. The closing line should be the same words as the opening line, but by then unreadable as only one thing.

Give the speaker an errand that makes transcription matter: a surveyor's clerk sent to copy the wording into a boundary register that has a column for true, a column for false, and nothing else, who must nevertheless write something down.

Keep the technical vocabulary out of the verse itself — no "paradox," no "glut," no "dialetheia," no "logic." The argument should be carried by the stone, by the two faces, by the walking round, and by the way the lines keep coming back.
