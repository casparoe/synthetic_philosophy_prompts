Hey — I'm in a tiny second-year reading group on moral psychology (four of us, plus one faculty member who keeps saying "you're all asking different questions" and then refuses to say what the questions are), and we've now burned three sessions on whether an action has moral worth only when the agent does it for the right reasons. It is going nowhere and I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. Please don't give me a survey of the positions or a reading list — we've read the Kantian side, the one-thought-too-many complaints about the man who jumps in to pull his wife out of the water, the situationist worry that basically nobody deliberates the way the duty story seems to require, and the view that what matters is just acting from decent motives whether or not the agent thinks of them as reasons. Assume all of that; I want to know why the argument doesn't move.

Here's the shape of our deadlock, concretely. One of us holds that moral worth requires the right-making feature to be what the agent is actually guided by — help given because the person needed help, not because it was fun or prestigious or tax-deductible. Another holds that this is a fetish of self-consciousness, and that worth tracks quality of will: if you reliably want the right things and act on them, you have it, and demanding that you also think "because this is right" just adds a thought that would make you worse. A third has basically given up on moral worth as a notion and wants to replace talk of worth with talk of praise, blame, accountability and what we should encourage in each other. The case that broke us was one I invented: a hospital finance officer who spots a billing irregularity, flags it, and would definitely have flagged it either way — because it's fraudulent and because it would have blown up a budget target she cares a lot about. My attempt at a tiebreaker was a counterfactual-substitute test: strip away the self-interested reason, and ask whether she'd still have flagged it; if yes, she has moral worth, if no she doesn't, and the fact that the good reason wasn't the one doing the guiding work doesn't matter. Half the group said this is obviously right, half said it obviously changes the subject.

My own hypothesis, which nobody liked, is that "moral worth" is doing three different jobs and each camp has silently picked one: an appraisal job (does this agent deserve credit, is she praiseworthy), a guidance job (what should we tell people to aim at, what should we cultivate in children), and something closer to a claim about what makes actions right in the first place — and that the views look inconsistent only because a view that's plausible for one job is being read as an answer to all three. A weaker bit of evidence: at a conference dinner we read the same vignette to a handful of people and asked three questions — "did she deserve moral credit", "did she do it for the right reasons", "is she a good person" — and the verdicts came apart in a way that felt like it shouldn't have. So: is this a verbal dispute, a substantive one about which psychological states matter, a hidden empirical question about how people actually deliberate, a fight about which cases count as data (stylized vignettes vs. real agents vs. saints), or an ill-posed question wearing the costume of a hard one? Tell me what each side is actually committed to, where my hypothesis is wrong, and — given the diagnosis — what would count as real progress here: a distinction, an empirical finding, a change of question, or an honest argument that we should expect none. American English throughout, please.
