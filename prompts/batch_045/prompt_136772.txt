**What do pleasures have in common? A no-demand account**

Five claims I am relying on.

(1) Hedonism about welfare holds that a person's well-being consists in the balance of pleasant over unpleasant experience. The heterogeneity objection says the pleasant experiences themselves have nothing interesting in common: think of cool water on a hot day, a pun landing, finishing a hard proof, a warm bath, an orgasm, the last bar of a symphony resolving. Strip away what each of these feels like and, the objection runs, there is no residue left over that they share.

(2) The classical utilitarians largely assumed a single hedonic tone — a feeling varying in intensity and duration. Bentham's calculus appears to treat pleasures as differing only in quantity, and Mill's distinction between higher and lower pleasures, judged by those competent to compare them, is usually read as an attempt to accommodate quality within hedonism; critics reply that it quietly imports non-hedonic value.

(3) Contemporary replies divide roughly into functional accounts, which identify pleasure by the role it plays (what we desire, what reinforces learning, what we are disposed to repeat), and attitudinal accounts, which treat pleasure as a stance taken toward something rather than a sensation, so that the variety of feelings is no longer a problem.

(4) In affective neuroscience, hedonic impact and motivation can come apart: subjects can be driven to pursue a reward far more intensely while showing no corresponding increase in their enjoyment of it, and the reverse dissociation also seems possible. If so, the simplest functional account — pleasure is whatever you are disposed to repeat — cannot be right.

(5) The heterogeneity is asymmetric. Unpleasant states resemble one another far more than pleasant ones do: a toothache, grief, nausea, embarrassment, an itch and boredom all share an intrusive, demanding character. I'll call this the asymmetry of the negative.

§3. My proposal is that what pleasures share is not a quality but a directedness of their own phenomenology: a pleasure presents itself as something to be stayed in, a displeasure as something to be ended. The asymmetry then gets an explanation rather than a shrug. The negative presentation is thick: the urgency, the intrusion on attention, the way the state will not leave you alone — these are themselves felt, and they feel much the same whatever their source, which is why one pain resembles another. The positive presentation is thin: what it is to be presented as to-be-continued has to borrow its content from the activity it belongs to, so it wears a different face in every case. Absences and permissions can be realized in endlessly many ways; a single insistent demand cannot. The relief cases fit: the pleasure of scratching an itch or of taking off tight shoes is not a quality added to the situation but the felt cancellation of a demand, and it is partly constituted by the discomfort that preceded it. No inventory of pleasant feelings could have predicted that.

§4. The obvious objection is the neutral state. Most of waking life contains no pressure to change and is not thereby pleasant: the waiting room, the mildly boring walk. If pleasure is the absence of a demand, everything neutral becomes mildly pleasant, which is absurd. My reply is to distinguish a felt absence from a bare absence: the pleasant state does not merely lack a signal to stop, it positively presents its continuation as unproblematic, and that presentation is something you can attend to. I am aware this may be cheating — it looks uncomfortably like reintroducing a feeling tone under a more flattering description, in which case I have gained nothing over (2). I would rather know now than later. Relatedly, some pleasures look demanding rather than permissive: the itch that must be scratched, the rollercoaster, very spicy food, the frustrated absorption of a puzzle. My repair is that the demand in these cases is directed at continuation, not at cessation, so they are consistent with the account — but the repair may be too cheap.

§5. The dissociation in (4) is, I want to argue, evidence for a phenomenological account rather than against one, because a functional account has no way to explain why wanting and liking should ever diverge. My difficulty is the mirror image: if pleasure is one thing and pursuit another, the near-universal correlation between them becomes a coincidence to be explained, and my explanation — that evolution normally aligns them, so the correlation is contingent and not constitutive — is doing a lot of work. In the addiction cases I am prepared to bite the bullet: the person gets wanting, not enjoyment.

§6. The worry I have not solved is commensuration. Welfare hedonism needs pleasures to be summed against pains, which seems to require a common scalar quantity. An account on which pleasures share only a thin directedness may yield a yes/no rather than a scale, and then the view is fine as a theory of what pleasure is and useless for the purpose hedonists wanted it for. I don't know whether that is fatal or just a separate project.

Please end with a bottom-line sentence, then a final line in exactly this form, choosing one of the three labels: SOUND if the central move survives as stated, NARROW if it works only for a restricted range of pleasures or only as a partial account, FLAWED if the central move cannot be repaired.
