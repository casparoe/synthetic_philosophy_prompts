I'm a PhD student in philosophy — norms of assertion — and I've been having the same argument with a friend in sociolinguistics for about two years without either of us moving an inch, so I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict.

Her side, briefly: talk isn't mainly a pipe for shuttling true propositions between heads, most of it is relationship work, and saying "that might be difficult" when you mean no isn't a false assertion, it's a refusal with the edges sanded off — so the deception charge is a category mistake, and blunt people are just people who've decided their own comfort with the truth matters more than the other person's face. My side: assertion has a truth norm, you're getting me to believe something you know isn't so, and the fact that everyone in the room decodes it doesn't make it not a deception, it makes it a deception with plausible deniability installed. We agree on all the descriptive stuff — that directness norms and indirectness norms are each internally coherent, that outsiders systematically misread them as rudeness or as evasion, that both can be used kindly or cruelly — and we agree you shouldn't deceive people about things that matter to them. Still completely stuck.

My hypothesis is that it isn't a disagreement about lying at all: it's one about what the practice is for, information transfer versus the ongoing maintenance of a relationship, and each of us keeps reaching for cases where our own description of the practice obviously looks like the point of it. But there are two others I can't rule out — that it's verbal, turning on what counts as saying something versus merely getting someone to believe it; and a thing I've started calling the uptake question, about who bears the cost of a misunderstanding: the speaker, for what a reasonable hearer will actually take away, or the hearer, for not knowing the norms of there own speech community. Which of these is doing the work, or is it something else — a hidden empirical question about how indirectness is actually received, or bedrock intuitions neither of us can argue for? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress, and if the honest answer is that nothing would, say that. And please keep separate what's settled among people who work on this (the descriptive pragmatics, say) from what's genuinely contested, and don't hand me a contested position as the consensus — I got burned by that in a seminar last term.
