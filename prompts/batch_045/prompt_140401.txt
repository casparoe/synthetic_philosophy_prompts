I'm trying to work out what I actually think about when a gamble has a value, by trusting my gut on a family of coin-tossing games, and I want the principle that captures my verdicts, if there is one.

Setup: a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads, and if the first heads falls on toss n a payoff fixed in advance is made. Assume for the exercise that utility is linear in money: I know the diminishing-marginal-utility reply and I don't want it.

1. Payoff 2^n, the usual St Petersburg game: the value is infinite, it is worth more than any finite entry fee, and I don't think that verdict needs explaining away.
2. Payoff +2^n/n for odd n and −2^n/n for even n, the Pasadena game: no value at all. That adding the terms in their natural order yields ln 2 strikes me as an artifact of that ordering, since rearranging the same terms yields anything you like.
3. Payoff ±2^n/n² with the same alternating signs: a genuine value of about 82 cents, and I'd pay 80. Here the expected absolute payoff is finite, about 1.64, which is what I think is doing the work.
4. The Pasadena game truncated at 1000 tosses, with nothing paid if no heads has fallen by then: a genuine value, a shade under ln 2, and fine to buy.
5. The Pasadena game plus a sure dollar paid up front: I would pay 90 cents for it without hesitation, and I still want to say it has no value — the verdict I can't state coherently.
6. The Pasadena game blocked forever: the house runs the procedure twice and credits you the first run's payoff minus the second run's, then does the same again, endlessly, adding each block's net to a running account, and pays you the limit of that balance if it converges and nothing at all if it doesn't. Each block is a difference of two games I have already declared valueless, yet I want to say the whole arrangement is worth exactly 0.

My own stab: a gamble has a value just in case its expected absolute payoff is finite; where only the positive side diverges the value is +∞, where only the negative side diverges it is −∞, and otherwise there is no value and no betting price is licensed. That saves 1 through 4, but it says I am not entitled to pay 90 cents in case 5 and that case 6 has no value rather than the value 0, and both of those sit badly with me.

Two cases I haven't settled. A: the same alternating 2^n/n scheme run with a coin biased toward heads; my hunch is that enough bias toward heads cures the game while bias toward tails makes it worse than Pasadena, but I can't say why. B: the Pasadena game with an opt-out, where after the tosses are revealed I may decline the payoff and take nothing instead.

Is there a principle that gets all six verdicts, or a small set of candidates that differ only in which verdict they sacrifice? Test my principle against the cases, repair it or replace it, and tell me which of my verdicts I should give up.

Answer in exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion: (1) the principle, or the two or three candidates, stated precisely enough to apply to games I haven't described; (2) which of my six verdicts it saves and which it overturns, naming the clause that does the work in each case; (3) its verdicts on A and B, with the arithmetic spelled out far enough that I can check it; (4) which verdict you would abandon if forced to choose between a baroque principle that saves all six and a simple one that loses one; (5) what would change your mind, and where your reasoning is most likely to be wrong.
