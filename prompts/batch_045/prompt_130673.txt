Ok so I keep circling back to Singapore's population policy and I'd like someone to actually run it through the "is a larger population better?" machinery rather than just gesturing at the repugnant conclusion. Here's the capsule version, from memory — treat it as given, don't hinge anything on exact figures:

Mid-60s: Singapore sets up a Family Planning and Population Board and runs a hard "Stop at Two" campaign, with real disincentives for third and later children (no paid maternity leave after the second, higher hospital fees, school-choice preference for children of parents sterilised young, paid leave for sterilisation). By the early 80s fertility is below replacement. In 1983 the government pivots hard: a National Day speech warns that too few educated women are marrying and reproducing, and proposals follow — tax rebates, a state matchmaking outfit, and most controversially priority in primary-school placement for the children of graduate mothers. The backlash is enormous, especially from educated women; the ruling party's vote share drops sharply in the next general election; the graduate-mothers priority is gone or heavily watered down within a year or so. By 1987 the replacement slogan is "Have Three or More (if you can afford it)", aimed at families who are already financially stable. It never gets fertility back to replacement, and Singapore leans heavily on immigration instead.

What I want is a disciplined mapping in both directions. Who are the parties the theory can even see — the state and its fiscal institutions, existing workers and pensioners, would-be parents, graduate women specifically, the children who would or wouldn't exist, and the immigrants who are the alternative route to the same headcount? For each of the standard positions (totalism; average and critical-level views; person-affecting views with the asymmetry), what did the government face, and what should it have done — keep pronatalism at all, prefer immigration, target graduate mothers, or just stop? Then tell me which features of the episode each view explains and which it misdescribes or can't see.

Four things I want you to engage with rather than skip past:

1. The "how many" question and the "which ones" question come apart here in a way I don't think the standard views are built for. They compare worlds by total or average welfare; they have no slot for composition. And there's a nasty wrinkle: a pronatalist incentive only reaches parents who are responsive to that incentive, so the marginal child isn't the average child — the state can't in general buy more of the kind of people it says it wants. Is the compositional aim self-defeating, or is it just invisible to the theory?

2. The beneficiary mismatch. The reasons actually offered — dependency ratio, tax base, defence, the survival of a language or a political project — are reasons about the people already alive and the institutions they run. The "we" in "we need more babies" is not the "they" who'd be born. So does the asymmetry view have any resources to criticise pronatalism at all, given that the added people are plausibly not harmed and may have good lives? Or does it render the policy arational — no verdict either way — which feels like the wrong answer for a case that intuitively is objectionable? Where does the objection actually live: in population ethics, or entirely outside it (legitimacy, who decides, who bears the costs, which in this case was mostly women)?

3. The pitch seems to double count: more people means more contributors (innovation, agglomeration, division of labour) AND more consumers AND more payers per pensioner. Can one coherent aggregative story have all three, or does picking one quietly break the others? And note the pay-as-you-go arithmetic seems to imply that ever-more people is good forever, which smells like it's the wrong target variable. So: is "is bigger better?" really an index-selection problem in disguise, and is the population-ethics framework silent on which index to optimise?

4. Because the policy is identity-affecting, a successful pronatalist policy benefits nobody it adds (the non-identity structure), and a failed one harms nobody it failed to add — so all the evaluation has to be done from the standpoint of the already-existing. Does that mean the population-ethics framing dissolves the case rather than explaining it? Is there any population-ethics reason at all to prefer adding people by birth over admitting them by migration, or is that purely an institutional/nationalist reason the theory can't speak to?

Finish by saying what the episode does to the theory: a confirmation, an anomaly, or a kind of case it never anticipated — and be honest about where the theory is silent instead of forcing it to talk. If it helps you can say in a couple of sentences how the analysis changes for a coercive version of the same policy, but keep the object the Singapore case. Don't give me a literature survey and don't hedge your way to a non-answer on the four points.
