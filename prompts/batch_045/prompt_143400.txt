Next week I have a ninety-minute slot with a graduate reading group — six philosophy students, two cognitive scientists, one condensed-matter physicist — and nobody will have read anything beforehand. The topic I have been asked to run is panpsychism, but really the topic is the combination problem: I want them to leave able to say what would have to be true for many subjects to constitute one subject, and why that question is not the same question as how matter gives rise to consciousness. What I need from you is a plan for the session, not the explanation itself.

Start with the dependency structure, because my instinct is wrong somewhere and I want it corrected. My instinct is to give the motivation before I give the view: the trilemma of brute emergence, dualism, and an intrinsic nature for the micro-level; the point that physical description characterises the micro-level dispositionally or relationally and leaves its categorical character open; and the claim that experience is the one case where we know what an intrinsic nature is. My worry is that without this the view sounds like animism and the combination problem sounds like a technicality bolted onto a joke. But I cannot tell which of those three moves has to be in place before the problem can even be stated, and which can be withheld and supplied mid-session when the group demands them anyway, and it matters to me because I would rather the problem arrive as something they feel than as something I assert.

Then the example. I want one case to carry the whole hour, and it has to do double duty: a part-whole structure in which the whole has a property no part has, while that property is nonetheless nothing over and above the parts and their arrangement, so that the constitutive-versus-causal contrast is visible; and then an extension of the same case in which the property at the whole level is being a subject of experience, so that the exact point where the analogy fails is the point where the combination problem bites. It must not lean on spatial or aggregative imagery, because I do not want them picturing things glued together; it must not depend on any empirical claim about brains or on anything contested in the neuroscience of binding; and it must not invite the reading that the whole exists only relative to an observer. Say explicitly why not the obvious candidates — I keep coming back to a musical chord and rejecting it, because on that example the simultaneity-plus-a-single-auditor reading eats the whole point, but tell me if I am rejecting it for the wrong reason, and give me at least one other near-miss and what it would teach badly.

I have been organising the difficulty into three questions, and I want the plan to place them rather than treat them as three faces of one worry. The *arithmetic* question: if N subjects constitute one, where do the other N minus one go, and what is the cost of saying they never really were separate subjects in the first place. The *demarcation* question: what fixes which collection gets summed into this subject — the two people sitting beside me, my left hemisphere alone, the subjects in the chair I am sitting in — and note that I privately suspect this is the special composition question in a different hat, so I need one move that acknowledges the kinship and gets us back before the hour dissolves into mereology. The *symmetry* question: the parts are still there while I am conscious, so why is there not a larger plurality of subjects present right now, and why does the whole's experience not simply appear alongside the parts'. There is also the fact that whatever takes micro-subjects to a human subject looks as though it should take humans together with their surroundings to a larger one; I want that treated as a fork with priced horns rather than as a reductio, but placed late enough that it does not function as a way of dismissing the view before it has been understood.

Give me the misconception map with positions in the sequence. Ones I already expect: that panpsychism attributes pains, beliefs and desires to electrons; that it is vitalism or animism and has been refuted by science; that the combination problem is just the hard problem with extra steps, which I take to be a stronger objection than it looks and therefore one that needs to be met at a particular point rather than at the start; that combining is a causal interaction between experiences; that subject is a term of art doing hidden work; that a view with no distinctive empirical predictions is not a theory at all; and that once you say the relation is necessary and constitutive you have redescribed emergence and gained nothing. Give me also the two or three I have not listed, and in particular the ones my own framing will manufacture — I would rather be told that my trilemma will generate a specific confusion than discover it live. The physicists will ask whether any of this has to do with quantum mechanics; tell me where that goes, and I assume the answer is early, briefly, and firmly closed.

Finally: one question or short task at the end whose answer separates having the structure from having the vocabulary — not an invitation to restate the problem in their own words, since that is the one thing everyone will be able to do regardless — and an explicit list of what I should refuse to cover at this level, together with how to refuse it so that it sounds like a decision about ninety minutes rather than a dodge.
