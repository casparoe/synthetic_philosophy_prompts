I'm the TA for a graduate seminar on consciousness, and our instructor has been running an ongoing bit where she opens each session with a case, makes us commit to a verdict in writing before she rules, and then tells us what she thinks. She has been strikingly consistent, and my study group now runs a small prediction market on her verdicts, so I'd like help pricing a contract before Thursday. I keep trying to taboo "conscious" and operationalize everything as something like "available for report under a confidence rating," but every time I do that the interesting disagreements seem to relocate rather than dissolve, so I'd rather have the reasoning than a bottom line.

Her verdicts so far, as best I can tally them: (1) a classic blindsight patient who discriminates and guesses above chance in his blind field is not consciously seeing anything there — guessing well isn't experiencing; (2) in a Sperling-style partial-report setup with twelve letters flashed briefly and the cue coming after the display is gone, the subject was conscious of the cued row only, not of all twelve — she explicitly rejects the overflow reading; (3) in highway hypnosis, where the driver brakes and steers correctly while later having no recollection of the road, the visual states guiding the driving were not conscious at the time, and the driver's "I must have seen it" is retrospective reconstruction; (4) a long-dormant belief that the subject hasn't entertained for years is a genuine mental state but not a conscious one, and its being retrievable on demand does not make it conscious now; (5) human infants and macaques do have conscious experiences — she's emphatic that neither language nor the capacity to say so is required, which I read as ruling out any rule that demands conceptually articulated higher-order thoughts; (6) a deep-sea probe with a second-order representation of its own sensor states, which it uses to recalibrate, is not conscious — mere second-order representation of the right formal shape isn't enough, there have to be first-order states of the right kind; (7) in a "plugged-in" case where direct stimulation of a self-monitoring area produces a state whose content is that one is now in pain, while no first-order pain state exists and there's no pain behavior beyond the report, she says the subject is not conscious of pain — the subject seems to herself to be in pain, but there is no pain she is conscious of — while insisting that the subject isn't a zombie during the stimulation either; (8) in a crossed-wires case with a first-order nausea plus a higher-order state whose content is that one is itchy, she says the nausea was not conscious, denies that the subject was conscious of an itch, and describes what's left as a conscious seeming-of-an-itch, which she treats as genuinely different from being conscious of an itch. I've tried clustering all eight under an actualist higher-order rule, a self-representational rule, and a plain global-broadcast-or-access rule, and all three seem to survive, though each needs the extra clause she used in (6) and (7) — that a state confers consciousness only on a first-order state that actually exists and is the one it's about. If there's a fourth rule I'm missing, or a version of the dispositionalist rule with a strict "online right now" clause that also survives, I want it named.

The case I need priced is one she hasn't seen, and I built it specifically because I couldn't get the surviving rules to come apart on her old ones. Nadia has bilateral damage to the prefrontal self-monitoring machinery, with intact early and late visual processing: she has first-order visual states with ordinary content and ordinary downstream effects on reaching and grasping, but no conscious vision whatsoever. Surgeons implant a higher-order prosthesis: a device that reads the activity pattern in her visual cortex, decodes it with a classifier trained on her own brain, and stimulates a surviving downstream area so as to produce a state whose content is, by hypothesis, that she is currently undergoing a visual state of such-and-such a kind. The outputs are accurate, they are causally downstream of the first-order states, and they support report and voluntary discrimination; but they arrive roughly 350 ms after the first-order state, they arrive by an artificial route rather than through her own monitoring system, and what they produce is not conceptually articulated — the neuropsychologist describes it as a bare sense that seeing is going on rather than a thought that one is seeing. After implantation Nadia reliably reports and discriminates stimuli she was previously blind to and says she sees again. My questions: is Nadia now having conscious visual states, and if so, does the state become conscious at the time of the first-order state or some 350 ms later, and is there anything left at that point for it to be a consciousness of (i.e., does she allow something like retroactive consciousness)? Then two variants: in the first, the classifier is fed scrambled input so its outputs match the actual first-order state only about half the time, and in a mismatched trial it says "vertical bars" while the real first-order state is a face — is she conscious of the face, of vertical bars, or of nothing? In the second, the device is driven by a camera in a patient whose visual cortex is destroyed, so there are no first-order visual states at all — same bare sense of seeing, same report. For each variant, tell me what each surviving rule predicts and why, give me your single best guess at her verdict with a rough confidence and the reason you're weighting that rule over the others, tell me which variant is the sharpest test, and say what further case I could bring to class that would separate the rules that are still tied afterwards. If you think any of her eight verdicts is in tension with another, say which pair I should press her on.
