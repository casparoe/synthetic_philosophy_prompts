Hey, I need someone to explain something about Bradley's regress, and I'd rather get the actual mechanics than the usual three-sentence version you see everywhere.

Setup as I understand it (correct me if I've got it wrong): take the proposition that Anne admires Bob. It has to be more than just the collection {Anne, admires, Bob}, because that collection could exist while the proposition did not, and the same parts rearranged say something different. But adding a further item — a unifying relation that binds admires to Anne and to Bob — doesn't obviously help, since now you have a new complex with four parts and it has the very same problem, and off you go.

The bit I'm stuck on is whether a certain style of answer quietly escapes the regress, or whether it just relocates it one level down. Call it the roles-not-relations answer: the parts are not held together by an extra entity, they're held together by each of them occupying a slot. The relation admires is unsaturated and Anne and Bob fill its two argument places; the proposition just is that structure of slot-fillings, not a heap plus a binder.

So, what I'd like explained:

(a) What exactly is the regress an argument against — an extra-constituent account, or any account on which the proposition has distinguishable parts at all? I keep losing track of whether the conclusion is "unity can't be one more constituent" or the stronger "unity can't be built up out of the parts by any structural story whatsoever," and what the difference in commitments is between those two readings.

(b) Does the slots/roles version actually get anywhere? My worry is that "x fills slot s of R" is itself a relation between x and R (or between x and the slot), so we've smuggled back in the extra item we threw out. But I'm not sure that worry is fair: maybe occupying a role is intrinsic to the item in a way that standing in a relation never is, and maybe the regress only gets going if you also ask what unifies x with its role. Walk through the slots version step by step against the regress and show me where it bites and where it doesn't.

(c) Relatedly: is the regress fundamentally about content (what makes a string of words a claim with a single sense rather than a list) or about truthmaking (what in the world makes the true ones true), and does it have equal force in both places? I have a vague feeling that an answer which works for one of those jobs does nothing at all for the other, but I can't get clear on why.

Please don't just give me the dialectical history of who said what — I want the moving parts, obv with the tradeoffs laid out.
