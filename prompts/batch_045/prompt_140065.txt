I'm putting together the reading for a graduate seminar on Everettian quantum mechanics, and I keep losing the thread in the probability literature for what feels like a methodological reason: the parties to the dispute seem to be trying to do different jobs. One camp wants an argument that a rational agent who believes in the branching structure should bet at Born-rule odds; another wants something closer to an objective single-case chance attached to branches; another seems content to show that nearly all the measure of the post-measurement multiverse exhibits Born-rule frequencies. Before I assign any of the actual derivations, I want the specification each of them is implicitly answering. So fix the purpose as follows: an account of probability in the Everett picture is adequate if it is good enough to underwrite the ordinary statistical practice of a working physicist who already accepts branching and who has a finite run of data on her desk next Monday — it has to make it rational for her to go on using textbook error bars and significance tests, and it has to let her say why doing so isn't a form of self-deception. Write the desiderata list for that purpose: every requirement a proposed account must meet, each paired with the concrete case or consideration that motivates it, and each flagged as non-negotiable or as the preference of a particular camp.

To start you off, here is my own messy candidate list, which I'd rather you prune, split, and argue with than ratify. (1) Non-circularity: the derivation must not smuggle in probabilistic or decision-theoretic premises that already contain the answer — but tell me how strict that can really be, since any account that ends in a claim about what to care about, or how much to weight future branches, seems to need a normative input, and I suspect a purely descriptive, fully non-circular derivation cannot yield anyone a reason to care. (2) The one-shot irrational case: she measures, once, on a single system, a spin prepared at an angle chosen so that the predicted weight is 1/π. There is no finite stock of equal-weight sub-branches to count, so any approach that works by splitting outcomes into equal-weight pieces has nothing to work with and must still return the right number; note also that the equal-amplitude case, where branch count and weight coincide, is degenerate and should not be allowed to count as evidence for anything. (3) Granularity-robustness: decoherence picks out branches only approximately, and the number of branches depends on how coarsely one describes the apparatus and environment, so the probability of "up" had better not shift because the pointer is described to the nearest millimeter rather than the nearest micron. (4) Conditionalization on self-location: suppose the experimenter on the low-weight branch is the only one who goes on to run a second experiment, so the number of future branches is correlated with the weight of the branch you are on. The account must supply an updating rule for "I am now on this branch" and say how it relates to the Born weights — and I want your view on whether demanding a genuinely branch-relative updating rule is a real desideratum or just the prejudice of people who want objective chance. (5) Continuous outcomes: a measurement of a continuous quantity leaves a continuum of branches, each of zero weight individually, though outcomes plainly occur; the account must distinguish "does not happen" from "happens with probability zero," and must recover the Born density rather than a set of branch probabilities. (6) Finitude: it must give her a reason to expect her actual finite sample, not merely an asymptotic or measure-one result about the whole multiverse — and I want to know whether the typicality step this seems to need is legitimate or a bolt-on. Add the requirements I haven't thought of, especially any that constrain what must be said about interference (why probabilities attach only after decoherence, and not to coherent superpositions), about non-ideal measurements and generalized measurements rather than clean projective ones, and about what happens when nothing remotely like a measurement is going on at all.

Then the part I care most about: I suspect some subset of the requirements on your final list is jointly unsatisfiable, and I want you to say which. My own guess is that exactness-for-a-finite-run — she gets the Born numbers exactly, for the forty-odd data points she actually has — is in tension with granularity-robustness, since real branch decompositions are approximate and exact weights are recovered only in some limit, so that any account satisfying one must fudge the other. But make the case for whichever pair you actually think is in tension, and say whether the upshot is that the list cannot be satisfied in full, or only that one requirement has to be weakened and how. Then tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates — which one most of the existing approaches quietly fail. Finally, and briefly, since this isn't the point of the exercise: score three or four broad families against the list — the Deutsch-style decision-theoretic derivation, the symmetry or envariance-style arguments, the branch-counting and relative-frequency approaches, and the self-locating-uncertainty approach that adds a separability principle. Skip names, attributions, and references; I know the shape of the literature, and I want your reasons rather than a bibliography.
