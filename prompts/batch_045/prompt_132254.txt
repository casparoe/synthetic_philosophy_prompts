I'm in my final year of secondary school, and the argument about AI in the classroom has become unavoidable here: the school now issues every student an account on a licensed assistant, the staff room is split between those who treat it as the end of learning and those who treat objecting to it as objecting to calculators, and I've now had roughly the same exchange about six times with friends from my debating club without anyone moving. I write the humour page for our student paper, and I'd like to turn that argument into something better than another shouty op-ed.

What I want is a piece of sustained, deadpan satire in the form of an in-universe official document: the Year 3 Programme Review of a school district that has fully adopted Assurance-Based Learning, the policy under which students are no longer required to understand the material so long as the answers they submit carry a satisfactory assurance rating. The target is the claim I keep failing to refute in the debating room — that understanding is only ever instrumentally valuable, a means to producing correct answers, so that once a system supplies reliably correct answers, insisting that students also comprehend the material is nostalgia — together with the administrative register that makes that thesis sound like plain common sense rather than a contested one. I'd like the humour to come from getting the epistemology right rather than from general tech-panic: the difference between reliability and understanding, why checking an output seems to require exactly the competence you have offloaded, the fact that an assurance rating is earned on cases already seen while the value of understanding shows up precisely on the ones that haven't been, metacognitive miscalibration (students reporting more understanding as their ability to detect errors falls), and the collective version of the problem, where dependence is universal and no independent auditor is left. It should also be fair: the reform's defenders get the best version of their case, including the point that dependence is nothing new — we have always leaned on instruments, colleagues and textbooks — and that the old system's understanding was frequently just a performance of understanding, and the satire should land on what that case fails to answer rather than on caricaturing it. Nobody inside the document may say the reform is bad; the reader works it out. Please treat these as ordinary bureaucratic vocabulary: the checker ratio, the Designated Verifier in each department, novelty attrition, heritage comprehension as an optional after-school club, and the fact that this review of the programme is itself being carried out by means of the programme. The document must also contain at least one confident, subtly wrong claim — an invented statistic, a misdate, a column in the metrics table that doesn't add up — which the document never notices and which is never flagged to the reader, so that the piece demonstrates its thesis instead of stating it. It should be funny to someone who knows this literature and still readable to my classmates who don't.

Please give me three sections, in this order: Headline and standfirst, about 40 words; The document, about 800 words, laid out as a numbered programme review with subheadings and one small metrics table; What the satire is aiming at, about 200 words explaining which feature of the position each joke is exaggerating and where you think the defenders' best reply actually has force.
