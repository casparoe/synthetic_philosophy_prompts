I work at a mid-sized lab that's roughly one generation behind the frontier, and I've somehow ended up holding the pen on an internal strategy memo about whether we hold our next model back two quarters to run a new interpretability-based deployment gate. The memo is drowning in a single metaphor: every cost — the delay, the eval compute, the slower release cadence, the headcount we can't put on capabilities — gets lumped together as "the alignment tax," and our VP of policy keeps leaning on it, in effect saying it's like a carbon tax: a small rate on a big base, everybody pays, and it's just the price of doing business in a world where the product can hurt people. That framing is carrying two conclusions I'm supposed to write up: (a) that we should eat the cost voluntarily rather than wait for a regulator to impose it, and (b) that the rate is a knob, so we can tune safety up or down until the marginal cost of the next unit of safety equals the marginal risk it buys. Our CTO grumbles that it isn't a tax at all but a tithe — you pay a tithe to something that at least notionally gives something back — and our head of product says it's a seatbelt, i.e. cost of goods sold that we'd pay with no regulator in sight. I don't want to settle this by vibes, and I also can't shake the competitiveness constraint: the slowest, least careful lab arguably sets the pace for everyone, and there are maybe five serious labs across four jurisdictions, at least one of which answers to a state that doesn't care.

So please audit the tax analogy properly. Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the sovereign, the tax base, the rate, the revenue, the incidence, the rebate, the evasion route (is jurisdiction shopping a live option here?) — and for each row say whether the target actually has anything in that slot or whether the analogy is just projecting. Then seperate the disanalogies that matter for the two conclusions we're trying to license from the ones that are decorative. The ones I keep circling: a real tax is levied by somebody with a monopoly on force, is in principle a transfer rather than a pure loss, and has a rebate that someone could in principle issue — none of which obviously exists here, so does "pay it voluntarily" survive, or does it secretly need a coordinator we don't have? And a real rate is continuously tunable and scales with its base, whereas our safety spend looks lumpy in a way a rate isn't: you can't buy 7% of a deployment gate that catches a failure mode, you either have it or you don't. If the knob framing imports a smoothness the target lacks, what exactly does that do to conclusion (b)? Also: taxes are conventionally modeled as deadweight loss, but some of our safety work plausibly pays for itself, since more reliable models make more money — is the tax frame quietly ruling out the possibility that safety and capability are complements rather than trade-offs? Add whatever else you think is load-bearing that I haven't thought of.

One more wrinkle that's making this concrete for us: the actual mechanism on the table is a compute reserve — we'd hold back a fixed fraction of the training budget that can only be spent on evals and interpretability, and if we end up not needing it, it goes back into the next run. Someone in our finance team asked whether that's a tax, capital expenditure, or something else, and the fact that we get the unspent part back makes me think it's closer to a performance bond than to a tax. Where does that leave the memo? I'd like a verdict on whether the analogy was carrying the recommendation or merely illustrating it, and whether tithe, seatbelt, insurance premium, bond, or something else is a better fit for the decision we're actually making — or whether each of those smuggles in its own conclusion the way the tax does.
