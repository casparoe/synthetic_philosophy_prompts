I am writing the specification for an internal "coherence audit" for a deployed forecasting system, and I want a probability out of you before I commit to the design.

The audit is a Dutch book test stripped of the features that usually make such tests easy. There is a fixed partition of eight mutually exclusive and jointly exhaustive states. Before anything is resolved, a menu of contracts is posted publicly and never revised. Each contract specifies a payoff, non-negative in every state, and a price; payoffs and prices are denominated in the same unit and constrained to multiples of one penny. The system may buy any subset of the menu at the posted prices, and there is no facility for selling, writing, or otherwise taking the other side of a contract. The menu is not tailored to any particular system beyond what is stated here: the auditor may design the menu with the system's confidence assignment in hand, but may not offer contracts the specification above excludes. The system is assumed to buy a contract exactly when its own subjective expected payoff from that contract strictly exceeds the price; its behaviour is otherwise unconstrained. The audit "flags" the system when some subset of the menu that it is willing to buy yields a net loss in every one of the eight states.

The proposition I want a number on:

P: in this long-only, posted-menu setting, flagging coincides exactly with violation of finite additivity — for every confidence assignment that violates finite additivity over the partition there is some permissible menu that flags it, and for no coherent assignment is there a permissible menu that flags it.

As a resolution condition: count P as refuted if there is some incoherent assignment that provably cannot be flagged by any permissible menu, or some coherent assignment that some permissible menu would flag.

Give me a single number for your credence in P, and then the decomposition: the sub-questions the number depends on, a rough probability for each with one line of justification, how you combine them, and which single term the final number is most sensitive to. Please treat at least the following as separate terms, and add any you think I have missed:

(a) the classical result, in the unrestricted setting where stakes may be of either sign and the menu may be tailored to the agent, that violation of finite additivity and exploitability coincide;
(b) whether the long-only restriction preserves that equivalence for assignments whose confidences sum to more than one;
(c) whether it preserves it for assignments whose confidences sum to less than one. This is where I think the design fails, and I want a separate probability that my argument is sound: for any bought subset, let X_i be the total gross payoff in state i and S the total price paid; each contract bought satisfies c·x > p with x non-negative, so summing gives c·X > S; a sure loss requires S > max_i X_i; hence c·X > max_i X_i; but c·X ≤ (Σ c_i)(max_i X_i), so if the confidences sum to less than one no such subset exists, and no menu of the permitted kind can flag the system;
(d) whether the penny grid means that small violations go undetected even when the violation is of the exploitable sign;
(e) the soundness direction: whether a coherent assignment can ever be flagged by a permissible menu;
(f) whether the bridge from buying behaviour to confidence holds well enough for a flag to establish anything about the system's epistemic state, given that acceptance is assumed to track expected monetary payoff with no risk aversion, no liquidity or budget effects, and no reinterpreting of the payoffs;
(g) whether "willing to buy each contract" entitles the audit to attribute the whole subset to the system, given that accepting each part and accepting the package can come apart;
(h) whether finite additivity over a fixed partition is even the right norm to be auditing here, rather than countable additivity, conglomerability, or something weaker;
(i) whether exploitability of this kind is supposed to be constitutive of incoherence, or merely evidence for it — and whether the audit could therefore be sound as mathematics and useless as a test of rationality.

For calibration: my own credence in P is about 0.12, essentially because of (c) and (d). A colleague puts it at about 0.6, on the ground that any short position can be replicated by buying contracts on the complementary states, so the long-only restriction is no real restriction. Tell me where you think that disagreement actually lives, and what single finding would move your number furthest.

Finally, and this matters to me as much as the number does: mark clearly which of your terms are settled mathematics on which I may rely, and which are contested among people who work on these arguments, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. Where a term turns on what "rational", "accept", or "fair" is doing, say so rather than settling it by stipulation. If you think P as stated is ill-posed, say which term breaks it and give me your number for the nearest well-posed claim instead.
