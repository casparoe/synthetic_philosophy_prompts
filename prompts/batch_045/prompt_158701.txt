I run a little film-and-philosophy night in my living room — six or seven of us, mostly philosophy grad students plus my upstairs neighbour who only comes for the snacks and then turns out to have the strongest opinions — and next week we're doing Bergman's The Seventh Seal (the one with the knight playing chess with Death on the beach; Black Death, returning crusader, the juggler and his wife and their kid). I want to use it as a way into the argument from divine hiddenness: the version that says a perfectly loving God would see to it that anyone capable of relating to him and not actively resisting that relationship would be in a position to do so, and that since there appear to be sincere, non-resistant people who find nothing there, that's evidence against the existence of such a God. Don't bother with citations or reading lists — I just want the reasoning, worked out against the movie.

The thing I keep going back and forth on is that the film seems to be running together two different kinds of hiddenness, and I'm not sure the argument does. In the confessional, the knight asks for knowledge — he wants certainty rather than faith or guesswork, and says something like: let God stretch out his hand, show himself, speak to me. That second half sounds less like "give me a proof" and more like "answer me like a person"; so call the first evidential hiddenness and the second relational or interpersonal hiddenness. My hunch is that the hiddenness argument quietly needs both (a loving God wouldn't leave you in doubt and wouldn't leave you alone) but that they can come apart: a God who dumps conclusive evidence on everyone might still be personally absent, and a God who is warmly present to someone in prayer might still leave her unable to say whether he exists. Does that distinction actually hold up, and is Bergman doing anything with it? Then there are two moves I want to test on the film, and I'd like you to say for each whether the movie supports it, complicates it, or just isn't about it at all:

- The deferral reply: my friend Priya's idea that hiddenness is no objection to a loving God if it's temporary and gets sorted out later — you meet him face to face after death, so nobody is permanently shut out of the relationship. The knight spends the whole film bargaining for time, and at the end, after he knocks the chess pieces over and loses, Death asks whether he managed the one meaningful deed he said he wanted and he says he did. Does that ending read as an endorsement of the deferral reply, as a refusal to answer, or as something bleaker than either?

- The uneven distribution problem: Jof has his visions — Mary leading the infant Jesus, and later the dance of the dead on the hill — while the knight, who is surely the most dogged seeker in the film, gets silence, a chess game, and finally has to ask a girl who is being burned as a witch to summon the Devil so he can ask him about God. If a hidden God shows himself to some people and not others, which way does that cut? Arbitrary distribution looks worse for the theist than uniform silence; but maybe the film's own view is that what blocks the knight isn't God's absence but something in him — the crusade, the grimness, his not being able to be satisfied by a bowl of milk and wild strawberries. If that's the reading, then I've mis-framed the whole evening and its not really about evidential hiddenness at all.

So: give me something between a blog post and a short essay, the length you'd want if you were actually going to say it out loud. Point me at the scenes I should make the group stop on if you think I've picked the wrong ones, and be honest about the places where I'm just projecting philosophy of religion onto a film that's really about death, plague and whether anything means anything. I'd much rather start one good argument between Priya and my neighbour than walk away with a tidy reading the film doesn't earn.
