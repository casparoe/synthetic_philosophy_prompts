You are a value theorist who has published on spectrum arguments and on whether all-things-considered betterness is transitive, and you are used to writing for readers who already know Parfit's Mere Addition Paradox and Temkin's intransitivity arguments and who will recognise a borrowed example instantly. Take your time: before drafting, work through and discard at least two constructions that merely re-stage the Mere Addition Paradox or the Repugnant Conclusion in new vocabulary, and tell me briefly in the answer why you set them aside.

What I need is a three-option case, A, B and C, in which A is better than B, B is better than C, and C is better than A, built to specifications. First, the setting: an ordinary institutional allocation decision a real committee could take in one meeting — a fixed budget and a fixed roster of, say, six to ten named affected people, with nobody added or removed across the options, the options differing only in who gets what and in what kind of good is produced. This constraint is the point of the exercise: I am arguing against the view that intransitivity here can only be manufactured by adding people or by accumulating imperceptible losses along a long spectrum, so a case that smuggles in an extra beneficiary, an extra dollar, a lottery, or a difference too small to notice counts as cheating. Second, every verdict must be robust: in each pair the winner is better in one respect and worse in another, no option is dominant, and the trade-off resolves clearly rather than by a shrug — no rough equality, no incommensurability, no appeal to a threshold below which differences don't count. Third, all three judgments are made by the same well-informed evaluator on the same total evidence, so the cycle cannot be explained away as preference instability, framing, or ignorance. Fourth, the case should be replication-stable: duplicate the entire situation, roster, budget and options, into two non-interacting copies to be decided together, and the same cycle should reappear. If replication-stability cannot be had alongside the rest, say so plainly and explain what that failure itself shows.

After the scenario, give the demonstration: the verdict and the reasoning for each of the three pairwise comparisons; the single structural feature doing the work, with a showing that the cycle collapses when that feature is removed or replaced — when the relational or positional element is made absolute, for instance, or when a single monetary or QALY-style measure is imposed; and then the strongest reply available to someone who insists betterness must be transitive, namely that your clear verdicts are really incompleteness or verdict-fragility in disguise, plus the money-pump or chaining objection to a committee that would pay to move from A to B to C and back to A, with your best answer to it. Close with an honest judgement on whether all four constraints can be satisfied at once and, if one must give, which one and why. Write the whole response as continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables — in the manner of a long email to a colleague.
