I do a lot of back-of-envelope estimating at work (freight and warehouse planning, mostly) and I'm in a small forecasting group where we score our own predictions afterwards, and I keep runnning into a pattern I don't have a word for. It's become the thing I most want vocabulary for, because I think it's why my multi-step estimates are badly calibrated while my one-step ones are fine.

Here's the shape of it. Say I'm estimating how many warehouses in a mid-size country will be running some kind of automated picking within ten years. I split it into three links: the share of firms that will have heard about the tech seriously enough to evaluate it; the share of those that pilot it; the share of pilots that become permanent installations. For each link I jot a range, take roughly the geometric middle, and then I tell myself the errors are independent across links, so they partly cancel in log space and the product is probably good to within a factor of three. But all three of my ranges were produced inside one background story — roughly, that this spreads sideways through industry contacts rather than being sold top-down by vendors — and if that story is wrong, all three links shift together, in the same direction, at once. The same thing happened last month with a totally different chain: whether a local transit referendum passes, decomposed into registration, turnout among the registered, and margin among those who turn out. Three links, one background story about who shows up to vote on things like this, same feeling.

The bit I actually want named is what happens next, and it's the part I can't find discussed anywhere. If I write the background story down at the top of the page as an explicit stated assumption, everything underneath it immediately starts feeling independent again — I read back over the three links and they look like separate, separately checkable claims, and I go back to multiplying. It's as if writing the shared thing down removes it from the calculation instead of adding it in as one more uncertain node. And I can't tell which of two mistakes I'm making: either I'm charging the same ignorance twice (once inside each link's error bar, and then again at the end when I say the whole thing could be off anyway), so my intervals are absurdly wide, or I've quietly dropped the shared story's uncertainty entirely, so they're far too narrow. A friend said this is just model uncertainty versus parameter uncertainty, but that doesn't seem to capture the part where the act of writing the story down changes how the rest of the argument feels to me, and it doesn't tell me which of the two mistakes I'm making or how to tell in a given case.

So: is there an established concept for this — links of a heuristic argument sharing a background scenario, the dependence going invisible once the scenario is stated explicitly, and the resulting inability to tell double-counting from dropping? If yes, I want to know how it differs from the neighbours it could be confused with: ordinary common-cause correlation, model versus parameter uncertainty, systematic versus random error in measurement, and the general habit of neglecting correlation when you combine estimates. If there's no established concept, coin a term and define it tightly enough that I could look at a given chain of reasoning and say whether it's present, and give me a couple of boundary cases where it is and isn't happening — I'm after the carving, not a label, so a bare name won't satisfy me. I'd also like to know what a defensible version of the independence presumption would even look like here: what would have to be true of my three links for multiplying them to be licensed at all, and is there a way of writing the background story into the argument as a node that actually fixes the problem rather than hiding it?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give me the reasoning — no preamble, and don't restate my question back at me.
