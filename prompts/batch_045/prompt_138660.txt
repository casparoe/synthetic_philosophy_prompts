So I've been chewing on the run-up to the LHC turning on in 2008 — the bit where people were publicly worried that it might make a microscopic black hole or a strangelet and destroy the planet, there were a couple of lawsuits trying to stop it (one in the US, one in Europe, neither got anywhere), CERN commissioned a safety review, and the machine was switched on anyway. (Similar flap a few years earlier at Brookhaven over RHIC, which also got a review and also went ahead.) I want you to run that episode through the expected value fanaticism literature, and then tell me where the mapping breaks.

Facts as I remember them, so you don't have to guess: the main scientific reply wasn't "we calculated the probability and it's 10^-something." It was a base-rate argument — cosmic rays have been hitting the Earth, the Moon, and every other body in the visible universe at comparable or higher centre-of-mass energies for billions of years, so whatever these collisions do, nature has already done it a staggering number of times and we're still here. When someone pointed out that cosmic-ray-produced objects would be moving fast and mostly fly straight through, the answer was roughly: dense astrophysical objects would stop and accumulate them, and we observe those not to have exploded. Assume for the sake of this that all of that is basically right.

What I want is the disciplined mapping. Who is the decision-maker here (CERN? the member states paying for it? the courts? the public whose money and risk it is?), what does fanaticism say their option set and payoff matrix actually looked like — the upside is finite (a bunch of knowledge), the downside is everything, so on an unbounded-stakes picture the experiment looks unlicensable unless the probability of catastrophe is below roughly the ratio of those two numbers. Then: what does fanaticism say they should have done, and does the episode confirm it, embarrass it, or fall into a case the theory never anticipated?

Four things I specifically want you to get into, because they're the parts I can't find my own way through:

1. The cosmic-ray move doesn't lower a number inside a model, it replaces model-based probability with an observational bound from ~10^20-plus natural trials. Does that structural difference matter to the anti-fanatic's toolkit (bounded utility, probability discounting, tolerance thresholds for tiny chances), or is it just a smaller number wearing a nicer hat? And notice the sting: what's left after the base-rate argument is basically model risk — wich is exactly the kind of uncertainty fanaticism is most aggressive about, since your credence that your whole physics is wrong isn't itself astronomically small in the relevant sense.

2. The "many chances" reply to fanaticism (the one that says we don't face lone one-in-a-billion shots, we face long series of them, so the aggregate isn't tiny). Which way does it cut here? The relevant unit could be this one machine, or the whole recurring class of first-of-kind experiments — Trinity, RHIC, the LHC, whatever comes after. If you aggregate over a technology class that persists, the fanatic should be far more worried than the one-shot framing suggests. But maybe it also licences a portfolio defence for the people who wanted to switch it on.

3. Symmetry. Fanaticism is symmetric between costs and benefits, the episode isn't: nobody argued the LHC's upside was astronomical, they argued the risk was zero. That looks like the fanatic and the physicist arriving at the same verdict for structurally incompatible reasons. So is the LHC a confirmation of fanaticism or a coincidence? And here's the version that actually bothers me: the cosmic-ray argument is a local accident of this case, available only because nature already ran the experiment. For a first-of-kind technology with no natural analogue there's no such move. Is the LHC then a misleadingly reassuring precedent, and does the theory have anything to say about how to tell the two kinds of case apart?

4. Fanaticism about fanaticism. From inside the fanatic's own framework, anyone who puts even a vanishing probability on the machine eating the world is threatening unbounded costs on everyone else — and vice versa, so the fanatic must assign astronomical expected cost to living in a world of fanatics. Does that self-application problem have a solution, or does it push toward an institutionalist answer the theory doesn't contain: that the right response to unbounded stakes isn't to compute harder but to change the decision procedure (require near-consensus, require an empirical base rate, hand it to a committee)? Cause what actually happened in 2008 looks a lot more like that than like anyone multiplying anything out.

Please keep it about this case and about the structure of the arguments — don't slide into a general essay on existential risk, and definately tell me where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak.
