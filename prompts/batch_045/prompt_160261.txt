You are a philosopher of language who has also done descriptive fieldwork on a language with no written tradition, and who is used to explaining technical disputes to non-specialists without cheating. Think carefully and at length. Before you write anything final, spend a few hundred words of private brainstorming: sketch every candidate requirement you can think of for the theory I describe below, argue with yourself about each, and discard the ones that are really just preferences of one school. Then commit to the list that survives, and say plainly which candidates you threw out and why.

The setting. I am a journalist working on a long feature about translation — human and machine — and about the claim, which I keep encountering in one form or another, that a system which renders a sentence of one language into another has or has not got what the speaker was talking about right. The classic version of this worry is Quine's field linguist, who hears a word — gavagai — in the presence of a rabbit and cannot settle whether the speakers mean rabbit, undetached rabbit part, rabbit stage, or rabbithood. I have read the popular versions of that argument and I do not need another summary of it. What I want instead is the specification: what a theory of what it takes to fix what a speaker's words are about would have to deliver in order to be adequate.

Fix the adequacy to a purpose, because I think that is where these arguments go wrong. I want a theory good enough for this job: to let an outsider — me, a skeptical reviewer, an auditor of a translation system — decide and defend in public whether a given rendering has pinned down what the speaker was talking about, and to say what would count as evidence for or against it. If the honest answer is that the question has no determinate answer, that is a finding I can use, but only if the theory tells me why it is a finding rather than a shrug, and what I should say instead.

Here are the situations I want the theory to be tested against. Some are invented; treat them as data, and add your own if a requirement needs one.

One. A field linguist finds two words where Quine found one. The first is used of a live rabbit, not of a rabbit carcass and not of a rabbit seen at dusk. The second is used of a live rabbit, of a severed rabbit foot, and of rabbit stew, but not of a rabbit-shaped decoy. Is either of them the word for rabbit, if either is? A theory that must choose between them, and one that must be permitted to refuse, are very different theories, and I want to know what forces the choice.

Two. The speakers assent to the word when an older man points at a rabbit and withhold assent when a younger person does, even though both are plainly looking at the same animal. The linguist cannot tell whether this is a fact about what the word means, a fact about who is allowed to speak about rabbits, or a fact about whom these people defer to. Can a theory separate meaning from the social practice of deference if all it has to go on is behavior, or must the manual absorb the deference into the meaning?

Three. Coordination works perfectly in the middle of the range and falls apart at the edges. The linguist and a speaker hunt together successfully for months on the strength of one word; then the linguist points at a rabbit-shaped rock, then at a rabbit at dusk, and the assent pattern becomes noise. Which range is the theory answerable to — the practical core, or the boundary cases, or both?

Four. The case that matters most for my piece, and the one I have not seen handled: there is no field linguist at all. No pointing, no shared environment, no assent behavior, no questions asked and answered. There is only a large corpus of text in the source language paired with text in the target language — this is roughly the situation of a machine translation system. State what, if anything, a theory must say about whether reference can be fixed in that setting, and what the difference is between the corpus-only setting and the field setting.

Five. Evidence that is downstream of the theory itself. A second linguist produces a manual that pairs the word with undetached rabbit part; the two manuals agree on every sentence anyone has tested and disagree on what the word is about. Years later some speakers learn enough of the linguist's language to be asked which they meant — but they learned the relevant word from the first linguist's own translations. Is their answer independent evidence, or is it contaminated by the manual used to teach them? I want a requirement about what counts as independent evidence here.

Six. The speakers say things that are, on the most natural rendering, straightforwardly false — that a crop failed because the ancestors were angered. A theory under pressure to make speakers come out right about most things will have to say something about error, disagreement and false belief, or it will make them unfalsifiable.

Seven. Symmetry. Whatever the theory says must apply to the linguist's own language; it may not grant determinacy to English while denying it to the language under study.

The deliverable. Give me the requirements list: what an adequate theory must be able to do — the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, the results it must be consistent with — with the case or consideration behind each one. For each, mark whether it is non-negotiable, whether it is negotiable, or whether you suspect it is a prejudice of one camp that has smuggled itself in as a requirement. Distinguish, where it matters, between not knowing which manual is correct and there being no fact of the matter, and say how anyone could tell the difference. Say also whether the theory must be able to state its own conclusion in the vocabulary whose determinacy it is calling into question.

Then the two things I care most about. First: is any subset of these requirements jointly unsatisfiable? If the list itself forces a result — if, say, a behavioral-evidence constraint and a determinate-reference constraint cannot both hold — say so explicitly and show the argument; that is the part of the piece I most want to get right. Second: which single requirement does the most work in excluding otherwise attractive theories?

Only at the end, and briefly: in prose or a small table, score against your list the four broad families of answer — a charity-and-truth-conditions style account, a causal or informational account of how terms get hooked to things, an inferential-role or use-theoretic account, and the deflationary view that the puzzle is a pseudo-problem. One or two lines each is plenty; keep the weight on the criteria. Define any term of art a general reader would trip over, and do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations — I am not going to chase references, and a theory that only works when footnoted is not one I can use.
