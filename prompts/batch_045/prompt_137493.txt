ok so me and my friend Priya spent like three hours last night arguing about whether archaeology can ever recover what people actually *believed* as opposed to just what they did, and I got home and wrote down my gut verdicts on a pile of cases and now I can't get them to line up. I'm on a field school right now (late prehistoric stuff, small sites, alot of badly preserved mudbrick) and we keep getting told to be careful, but "be careful" isn't a rule and it doesn't tell me which inferences to actually make.

Here are my cases. (1) A cemetery upriver, roughly 180 graves over a couple of centuries, every body on the left side, heads west, and every single one with a copper awl at the right hip. My verdict: yes, there's a shared norm about how the dead get treated, but no, I won't go further, so I won't sign on to the idea that they thought the dead needed their tools where they were going. (2) In that same cemetery, three graves oriented the other way, no awl, one of them face down. Verdict: strong yes, and this one even helps — the exceptions read as deliberate, like somebody being excluded. (3) A neighborhood where every house has the hearth in the same corner and the door in the same wall. Verdict: no, I infer nothing about belief there — smoke, sun, roof spans, whatever. (4) Priya's objection, wich I think is the one that actually got to me: a body in a shallow pit is also constrained, you can't arrange a corpse any which way, so why do I read burial orientation as symbolic and house orientation as merely practical? I don't have an answer, and I suspect it means (1) and (3) can't both stay. (5) Hundreds of little clay figurines with exaggerated chests and hips, mostly off house floors and out of trash pits, at sites all over the region. Verdict: no, I refuse to infer a fertility cult or a mother goddess no matter what the old reports say; maybe yes to something thin, that they were handled a lot and discarded domestically. (6) A river site: dozens of swords bent double, spearheads snapped, axes smashed, none of them worn, all of them in the water. Verdict: yes, and here I *am* willing to say something about belief — that these objects were treated as if they had to be destroyed or discharged before being disposed of. But that's the same shape of evidence as (5), wich I rejected, and I can't tell you why that's not inconsistent. (7) One room in a big building, hundreds of clay lumps that had sealed door pegs and jar mouths, impressed by maybe thirty different seals; some of the sealings carry writing and nobody can read it. Verdict: strongest yes of the lot — there's an administrative arrangement, somebody was answerable for what moved in and out. (8) The seals themselves turn up in graves, one per body, all different designs. Verdict: no, I won't infer office or record-keeping from that, they could just as well be personal marks, amulets, or gifts. (9) A painted scene on pots — a figure in a boat with a line running to a horned animal — on thirty vessels from one workshop over two centuries, and then over the next four centuries the motif degrades into a zigzag and just keeps getting painted. Verdict: I think something changed, and I don't think the zigzag pots carry the belief even if the boat pots did; but I also wonder whether I should say the belief was ever in the pots at all. (10) A royal inscription from a nearby city says the king restored the monthly beer offering at the local temple, and right around that date the temple precinct shows a jump in beer jar volume and new ovens going in. Verdict: I feel more confident here than in any of the above, and Priya says that's precisely the problem — that I'm reading the jars through the text and then treating the match as independent, and that inscriptions brag about what kings wanted built, so the match might just be the pots catching up with the brag. (11) Somebody argues that because a site has no big storage rooms, there was no central redistribution. Verdict: no, obviously not, that's just what didn't survive.

The rule I half-came up with walking home, call it the surplus rule: you may infer a belief from objects only where (a) the feature costs more than any practical job requires, (b) it's reproduced across separate production events rather than being one workshop's habit, and (c) there's a way to get it wrong, so that doing it the right way costs something socially. I think it delivers (1), (6), (7) and kills (5) and (11), but I have no idea what it does with (3), (8) or (9), and I suspect it can't handle (10) at all.
