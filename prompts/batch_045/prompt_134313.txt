I'm a journalist — I mostly write a column on psychology and how ideas travel, the social-science-ish beat — and last month I ended up at one of those long conference dinners where the restaurant is too loud and everyone has had one glass of wine too many. Two people at my end of the table, Lena and Marcus, got into an argument about cross-cultural work on philosophical intuitions: the business where you give people in different countries the same little vignette — a Gettier-style case, a trolley case, the side-effect thing where someone's action has a foreseen but unwanted consequence — and you find the answers move around with culture, class, language, and so on. I'm not a philosopher and I genuinely couldn't tell who was winning, so I'm writing it up here as close to verbatim as I can manage. (1) Lena: this should be a big deal, because something like nine-tenths of the intuition data philosophers lean on comes from one very narrow slice of humanity, and if the answers track who you asked, then either widen the sample or stop treating your own gut response to a made-up case as data. (2) Marcus: variation by itself shows nothing until you know it tracks something that matters — people also vary on ordinary perception and memory tasks and nobody concludes there are no facts about memory; besides, a lot of the famous cross-cultural findings came from small samples and the replications have been mixed, and plenty of it looks like performance noise anyway: translation slippage, comprehension, and people reading a question like "does she know?" as a roundabout way of asking whether it's safe to act. (3) Lena: the performance-noise move is unfalsifiable if you deploy it selectively, and dismissing the respondents who disagree with us as "confused" is just the expertise defence with the serial numbers filed off — you pick the expert population using the very intuitions you're testing. And the variation isn't random: answers shift systematically with whether the case is about harm or about purity and loyalty, with whether the protagonist is an in-group member, with small framing changes. That's the pattern you'd expect if there were no single stable folk concept there to be measured. (4) Marcus: grant all of that, real and systematic. You still need a premise to get to "so stop using intuitions," namely that the folk response was ever evidence about the target property rather than part of the phenomenon to be explained. Think about dialect variation: linguists don't take a vote on which dialect is right, they model the variation, and the variation is a clue. A theory of knowledge should predict the pattern of ordinary ascriptions, variability included, so variation is a finding to be explained, not a debunking. (5) Lena: if that's the job you've quietly changed the subject. Now you're doing descriptive semantics of the word "knows" and its translations, but the reason anyone cared about these cases in the first place was normative — what we're entitled to believe, assert, and act on. A descriptive map of ascription habits can't answer that, and if it can't, the variation result really is corrosive rather than merely interesting. (6) Marcus: even so the descriptive facts aren't inert: a normative story that implied nobody ever knows anything, or that we should abandon knowledge talk altogether, would be useless in practice, so the folk data sets boundary conditions on any acceptable theory — a constraint, not a sample to be voted on.

The reason I'm stuck is something a grad student told me afterward at the bar, about her own not-yet-published project. She ran the same participants through matched pairs of cases: a Gettier-ish case and a control that's identical except the true belief isn't luck-dependent, and instead of comparing raw "does she know?" rates across countries she compared each person's *gap* between the two cases. Her claim was that the cross-cultural spread pretty much collapses when you look at the within-person contrast — populations differ in how readily they hand out "knows" at all, in effect a difference in scale use or response style, but they agree about how big the Gettier penalty is. She thought this cuts against Lena, since it suggests a shared underlying competence that the raw numbers hide. But it also seemed to me to cut the other way: if the headline findings flip depending on whether you ask about levels or differences, then the whole survey methodology is far more fragile than either of them was admitting, and Lena's "your evidence base is junk" point gets stronger, just for a different reason.
