I'm a backend/infra engineer, no philosophy background at all, and I got dragged into one of those dinner-table arguments about militaries that I was completely unequipped for. Someone there was defending conscription, someone else said professional all-volunteer forces are obviously better, and a Swiss relative kept going on about armed neutrality like it was a design principle rather than a policy. I kept having the feeling that all three of them were making the same kind of argument I make at work, just in different words, and I want to know how far that feeling actually goes.

So: restate the whole debate about conscription versus professional armies versus armed neutrality in the vocabulary I actually use. I mean reliability and capacity planning stuff: hot standby versus cold standby, warm pools, N+1 redundancy and how much idle capacity you're willing to pay for, time-to-recover versus time-to-detect, surge capacity and autoscaling, drills and game days, single points of failure, defense in depth, blast radius, shared fate, and the difference between failures that are random and failures that come from an adversary. Roughly: a standing professional army is a hot standby you pay for continuously and can use at once; a conscript or militia system is a huge cold pool that is cheap while it sits there but has a nasty activation delay and needs rehearsal to be worth anything; neutrality is refusing to federate your recovery plan with anyone else's, so you cannot borrow their capacity or their time-to-recover.

Three positions to translate, one at a time, as faithfully as you can:

The militia/conscription case, including the version I found most interesting, which is not about money at all: the claim that a nation-in-arms is harder to conquer because the whole population is provisioned for resistance, and that a small professional force is too easy for a government to deploy far away because nobody it cares about pays the cost.

The all-volunteer case: that modern kit makes headcount a poor proxy for capability, that you cannot get real skill depth out of short service, that idle capacity is expensive in a way that shows up everywhere else in the budget, and that compulsion is a bad way to staff anything.

The armed-neutrality case: permanent self-provisioned capacity, no dependence on an ally's response time, credibility resting on being visibly expensive to attack rather than on threatening anything.

Then the part I actually care about, and where I want you to add something I have not seen argued: in reliability work, redundancy works because faults do not care about your architecture. Here the thing you are protecting against reads your runbook. Add an adversary who updates on your design, and translate that too. And then take it one step further with a scenario I made up and want stress-tested: the adversary never does the thing that triggers your cold standby. No invasion, no declaration, no clean threshold event. Just sustained sub-threshold pressure that stays under whatever condition would let you call up the reserve. In my vocabulary that is a cold pool whose activation predicate never fires, which means its effective capacity is zero no matter how big it looks on paper, and meanwhile you are still paying the political cost of maintaining it. Work out what that does to each of the three positions, because I suspect it hurts conscription much more than the professional-force people usually manage to say, and I want to know whether it also hurts neutrality, which seems to depend on being able to say in advance exactly what would make you fight.

Finally, the residue report. Tell me what did not survive the translation at all. My guesses, which you should correct or replace rather than just confirm: nothing in reliability engineering has a metric for willingness or legitimacy, since a node does not get a vote and does not need to believe in the SLO; conscription is the only one of the three that is a claim about who is compelled to serve and who bears the cost, and provisioning people is not like provisioning servers, so I suspect the cost side gets quietly laundered in translation; and the civic-republican sounding stuff about what a conscript army does to the relationship between the state and the population does not seem to have any counterpart in my vocabulary, because it is a statement about the organization rather than about the system it runs. Also flag anywhere the translation cheats, by smuggling in an assumption that is really one side's conclusion, for example if treating survival as the only objective quietly settles the argument against people who think there are things worse than being conquered, or if the word defense in depth presupposes that holding territory is the SLO.

I want to know whether the residue is a real blind spot in how I think about systems, or a sign that the military arguments were never as crisp as they sounded at dinner.
