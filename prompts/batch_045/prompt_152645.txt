I'm in the third year of a philosophy, politics and economics degree and I'm helping to run one workshop session for a course on the philosophy of the social sciences; the unit is on long-run prediction and what it actually means for a forecast to fail. Our running example is Malthusian population theory — roughly, the claim that population tends to increase geometrically while the means of subsistence increase only arithmetically, so that any gain in living standards is eventually absorbed by extra mouths, with misery, vice or restraint doing the restoring. Two friends in my study group, one an economist and one a historian, spent an hour last week talking past each other about whether this theory was refuted or merely premature, and I want a handout that sorts people by the moves they actually make rather than by the label they reach for first. I need to print it for Friday, and I'd rather it be a diagnostic instrument than a summary of who has said what about Malthus.

So please build me a decision tree. Start everyone from the same two stipulations: they accept the description of the theory above, and they accept that, two centuries on, income per head in much of the world is many times its 1798 level and mass famine has not become the ordinary condition of humankind — yet plenty of people still say the theory was right but early. Each branch node should be one question about this case, phrased so that a student could answer it without knowing which position it leads to, and each leaf should be a named position on whether a long-run prediction can survive its own deadline, or unnamed if in fact nobody defends it. The questions I think belong on the tree are these, though please cut or add as the logic of the space requires: whether the theory came with a date, a vague horizon, or only an in-the-long-run qualifier, and whether a vague deadline can be missed at all; whether the core claim is a tendency claim, a pressure that is always operative and reasserts itself once the checks are lifted, or a dated event claim that some outcome occurs by a given time; whether the escape from the trap through falling fertility rather than famine is the theory's own machinery working, a preventive check doing its predicted job, or a mechanism smuggled in after the fact; whether the causal arrow points from population pressure to scarcity or from population pressure to the innovation that averts scarcity, the Malthusian versus the Boserupian direction; whether the mechanism has simply been relocated to a different sink — freshwater, soil, a stable atmosphere, that style of framework — rather than to arable land and food; whether each missed deadline should be scored as the same hypothesis renewed or as a distinct dated hypothesis that died when its date passed, so that near-misses accrue to one theory or to none; and whether there is a symmetry requirement here, given that nobody says a theory was wrong but lucky when its predictions come true for the wrong reasons, so that either something constrains the right-but-early excuse or the move itself is suspect rather than the theory being vindicated.

Where a leaf is a familiar methodological stance — a Popperian falsificationism, a Lakatosian research programme with progressive and degenerating variants, a Kuhnian treatment of accumulating anomalies — name it as such, but I would rather the routing into it ran through the Malthusian case than through slogans about falsifiability. Please also mark the answer combinations the tree permits that nobody in fact occupies, with a clause on whether the vacancy is due to incoherence, unattractiveness or mere unexploredness, and flag any pair of leaves your tree genuinely cannot separate instead of inventing a distinction to separate them. Give me the result as a numbered list of exactly eight items, each a sentence or two long, with no introduction and no conclusion: number the nodes in the order a reader would reach them, and in each item state the question at that node, its branches with the claim that distinguishes them, and then either the numbers of the items those branches lead to or, where a branch terminates, the name of the position and its single most awkward commitment. Fold the notes about empty combinations and about inseparable leaves into the items they attach to rather than appending them at the end.
