hey — I'm a PhD student and I've been stuck on the color phi stuff for weeks, going round in circles with my superviser, so I thought a dialogue might help me see where the disagreement actually bites. Could you write one for me?

Setup, so we're on the same page: blue dot flashed at the top of a screen, red dot at the bottom a fraction of a second later, subjects report a single dot travelling down and changing color midway — a change they say they saw before the second flash ever appeared. Two explanations get defended in this literature. Call the first the Orwellian one: there genuinely was a conscious experience at the earlier time (of a blue-ish dot moving), and afterwards memory of it got edited, so the subject now has a false recollection of a color switch that was never consciously there. Call the second the Stalinesque one: nothing got edited after the fact; the content was authored late, once the second flash was in, but was dated earlier — the record was prepared in advance of the event, so to speak — so there was never an earlier experience that got tampered with.

I want two people arguing for these, and both of them should be good at it. No strawman version of either side, and they should respond to what the other person actually said instead of restating their own position louder. A few things I'd like them to get into:

1. A variant I've been doodling in my notebook. Same two flashes, but on a random chunk of trials a soft click plays at a variable delay after the second flash (somewhere between 0 and 400 ms), and the subject has been trained to press one of three buttons as fast as possible — blue, red, or neither — naming the color of the moving dot at the instant they heard the click. We log the button and the reaction time relative to the click. Suppose it turns out that at very short delays subjects reliably press red with a latency that looks indistinguishable from their latency in a plain color-discrimination task, while at long delays they still press red but with a latency that drifts. Does either side get any predictive purchase here, or do they both fit the same pattern equally well, and if so, what exactly is it that we've learned?

2. My own pet view, which I'd like them to attack rather than humour. Call it the staggered ledger: there isn't a single stream with a single timing. The color content, the motion content and the position content each get settled at different latencies after the flashes, partly independently, and what we call the experience of the moving dot is just the collection of these fixations. On that picture both the Orwellian and the Stalinesque stories presuppose a single determinate moment at which the whole experience occurs, and both are wrong about that — not because the timing is unknowable, but because there is no one thing whose timing it is. I expect the Stalinesque character to say I've smuggled in my own little theater, or that I'm just redescribing the data in fancier words; I'd like her to say that and I'd like the Orwellian to say what he thinks it does to his own revision story.

3. Whether any of this leaks out of the lab. If the timing of experience is indeterminate or disunified in this case, is there any principled reason to think ordinary cases are different — say, the moment at which I decide to raise my hand in a seminar, or the moment a pain starts? Or is phi a special case that tells us nothing about the rest of consciousness?

Both of them should be willing to say where they'd give ground, and where they think the whole debate is degenerate. Keep it in plain speech, like two people at a pub after a conference — and please no citation or reading-list talk, I can't look anything up from where I'm writing this anyway.
