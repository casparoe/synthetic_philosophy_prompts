Hey — I'm in a reading group working through the old Hart–Devlin exchange on legal moralism (the one kicked off by the 1950s British committee report on homosexuality and prostitution), and we completely stalled last week, so I want something written up that I can bring back to the next session.

Here's roughly where we got to, so you know what I already have. Devlin's side, as I understand it: a shared public morality is part of what holds a society together, so a society is entitled to use the criminal law against conduct that provokes genuine intolerance, indignation and disgust, even when no one is harmed and nobody asked for protection. Hart's reply: that at best an untested empirical guess; even granting that some shared morality is necessary, you'd still have to show that this particular norm is load-bearing, and nobody ever does; and Devlin's own concessions about not punishing everything immoral, and about privacy, quietly smuggle the harm principle back in. I've got that much, I dont need it repeated at me.

The thing we got stuck on — and the bit I actually want you to work on — is a case we invented, which I think is a sharper test than the ones they were arguing about. Call it the Shutters. In some jurisdiction, adults can buy a fully private, offline home simulation in which they take part, alone, in vividly realistic cruelty toward convincingly simulated persons. Stipulate away every familiar hook: the simulated persons are not conscious, not copies of anyone, have no moral status, nothing that happens is a harm to anyone or anything; no recording, no second participant, no commercial exploitation, no one else ever finds out, no children involved. About 85–90% of the population feels real, non-hysterical moral revulsion at it and would say it's degrading to the people who do it. Now two variants. Variant A: psychologists and criminologists are about as confident as those fields ever get that using it has no downstream effects at all — doesn't make anyone more likely to harm a real person, doesn't corrode relationships, doesn't spill over into anything. It's a sealed-off indulgence. Variant B: the downstream effect is real but small — heavy users are measurably, in the aggregate, a bit more callous in ordinary life, in a way that never traces to any individual act of harm.

What I want is an essay that answers, rather than lists. Should the state criminalize the Shutters in variant A, and in variant B, and if the answers differ, what exactly is doing the work? Can Devlin's position be read as anything other than an empirical prediction — as something more constitutive or expressive, the law declaring who we are — and if so does that reading help it, or does it just relocate the problem into why anyone else should care about the declaration? Could a Devlinian escape Hart's "you haven't shown this norm is load-bearing" objection by pointing at the mechanism rather than the norm: what matters is whether a society retains any practice of publicly enforcing morality at all, not whether it enforces this one? And if a ban would in fact backfire — the Shutters go underground, a subculture forms, and twenty years on the ban is remembered as moralistic overreach while a neighbouring jurisdiction that never banned them just carried on — does that refute Devlin, or only show the thesis was false here?
