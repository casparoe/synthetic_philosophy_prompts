I work at a foundation that funds work aimed at shaping the long-run future, and in our strategy discussions one picture does an enormous amount of work: history is a door on a hinge. The present generation stands at the hinge; the door itself is everything that comes after; a small angular deflection at the hinge becomes an enormous displacement at the far edge, and the longer the door, the bigger the displacement for the same push. People reach for it to license a strong practical conclusion — that nearly any resource spent on steering the trajectory (reducing existential risk, locking in better values, slowing or accelerating particular technologies) dwarfs in expected value anything spent on the welfare of people alive now, because the multiplier is the length of the future. Other people in the same room reach for the same picture to license nearly the opposite: the door is very long, we cannot see where the far edge ends up, and a push whose consequences are amplified many times over is a push one should be extremely reluctant to make.

What bothers me is that one picture is being used to underwrite both maximal activism and maximal restraint. Before I decide whether it earns its place in our deliberations, I would like it audited.

- Set out explicitly what maps onto what: what is the hinge, what is the door, what is its length, who or what does the pushing, and what plays the role of the displacement at the far edge. Where the mapping requires an identification that is itself contested, say so rather than smoothing it over.
- Then the disanalogies — and, more usefully, which of them matter for the work the picture is doing here and which are inert. Three worry me in particular, and I would like them argued rather than listed. (i) A door is rigid: deflection at the hinge is transmitted mechanically to the far edge. History is presumably not rigid. Later generations have their own capacity to deflect, and if they do, the object may be a chain of hinges rather than one, in which case what we do at ours is not amplified downstream but re-set by whoever comes next. Does the single-hinge picture survive if transmission is jointed rather than rigid? (ii) A door swings symmetrically and reversibly, and it stays on its hinge however hard it is shoved. Some of the outcomes we care about — extinction, or a stable lock-in of bad institutions — are absorbing in a way the picture has no vocabulary for: they remove the hinge rather than move the door. The asymmetry between deflecting the door and destroying the hinge seems to me to be carrying much of the real argument, and I want to know whether it is doing so illegitimately. (iii) All of the amplification comes from the door's length, so the picture supplies no reason at all to think the future is long. It is a picture of leverage conditional on length, not evidence of length. Is it then only an arithmetic fact — expected value scales with the size of the affected population — dressed up as a mechanism?
- Say plainly whether either of the competing conclusions survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the picture was carrying them the whole time and the real argument has to be conducted without it.
- If there is a better analogy for the same target — living at a time of unusually high influence over what follows — propose it, and say what it captures that the door does not and what it in turn obscures. I have been toying with the neck of an hourglass instead of the hinge of a door, but I am not confident what that version would commit me to.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — whether the hinge picture can bear the weight being put on it, and if not, what should replace it in our reasoning — and then give the argument. No preamble, no restatement of what I asked. A table of correspondences is welcome. I am not after references or further reading; I want the carving of the analogy itself.
