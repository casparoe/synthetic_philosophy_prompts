I am a retired field biologist who has lately been sitting in on a philosophy of mind reading group at the university where I used to teach, and I have volunteered to lead the next session on teleological theories of content — the view that what a mental state is about is fixed by the biological function of the mechanism that produces it.

The analogy the group keeps leaning on is that misrepresentation is like malfunction: just as a heart that does not pump blood is a defective heart, a neural state that fails to indicate what it has the job of indicating is a false one. The work it is made to do is to show that falsehood — supposedly the hard case for any naturalistic theory of content — comes for free once you have biological function, so that no separate account of aboutness is owed.

What I want is an audit of that analogy rather than a restatement of it. Please give the mapping explicitly: in the standard illustration — a frog whose visual system fires at small dark moving objects and thereby usually gets it a fly, but occasionally snaps at a passing pellet — what corresponds to the organ, to the organism's needs, to the designer, to the defect, and to the diagnosis of defect? Then give the disanalogies that bear on the conclusion, in particular these three: that malfunction is graded and is judged against what the organ normally does for the organism, whereas falsity looks bivalent; that the heart's job looks determinate in a way the detector's does not, since selection may have cared about flies, about small moving dark things, about whatever the snap usually gets, or about net calories — tell me which of these the analogy rules out and which it leaves open; and that calling a heart defective seems to presuppose a standard of what it is supposed to do, which is very close to the notion the analogy was meant to secure.

Two further cases, because the group stalled on both. A smoke detector that shrieks at burnt toast: its job comes from an engineer's intention rather than from a history of selection, so does the analogy carry over, and what does your answer imply for the biological case? And a creature with no ancestry and no selection history whose detector fires only at falling gravel: on this analogy there is nothing for it to misrepresent, which half the group treats as a reductio and half as a welcome result — which is it, and why?

Close by saying which of the claims the analogy was used to support actually survive, and whether a different analogy would carry them better. Please write in American English, avoid citation entirely — I lost most of my library in a move and cannot chase references down — and keep the whole thing under about 900 words.
