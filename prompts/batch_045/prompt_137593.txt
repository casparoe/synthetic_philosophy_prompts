I teach a first-year interdisciplinary module on rules, norms and coordination, and next term I want to run the fiction and the philosophy together rather than in separate weeks. The novel I keep returning to is Golding's *Lord of the Flies*, and the philosophical material is David Lewis's account of conventions as arbitrarily selected solutions to recurrent coordination problems — sustained not by agreement or enforcement as such, but by conformity together with the mutual and higher-order expectations that make conformity rational — plus the evolutionary and signalling-game models that later develop his framework. I have a draft reading in which the island is a laboratory for the rise and fall of such conventions, and I would like you to write a competing one that I can set against it, rather than a summary of either the novel or the theory.

The things I most want you to work through, in terms of specific scenes and not just in general: First, the conch. Is it best described as a Lewisian convention at all, or as something else — a sanction-backed norm, an explicit agreement, or merely a useful device — and does it matter that it appears to do two separable jobs, convening assemblies and regulating who may speak within them, which may rise and fall independently? Second, the signal fire, which I have started calling a "beneficiary-blind" convention: the party for whose benefit it is maintained, a passing ship, never returns any feedback about whether the signal was received, so the maintenance of it rests on a benefit the maintainers never observe. Does the Lewisian or signalling-game machinery actually predict that such conventions should be especially fragile, and is that the failure Golding dramatises when the fire is allowed to go out? Third, Jack's strategy: my impression is that he rarely offers a competing focal point and instead engages in what I call "prominence-poisoning" — mockery of the assembly, the rival feast, the pig's head, the dance — that degrades the salience of the existing convention rather than competing with it on the coordination problem it solves. Lewis largely treats salience or prominence as an exogenous input; what would it take to model salience itself as something agents can strategically attack, and does the novel supply any resources for that? Fourth, the unravelling. I would like to claim that the breakdown is better described as a collapse of higher-order expectations — boys drifting away because of doubts about whether others will still turn up — than as a change in first-order preferences, but I am not confident that survives the ending, and I would like you to test that claim rather than adopt it.

Finally, I need the reading to be honest about where the pairing fails, since I will be teaching it to students who will notice. Lewis's framework takes agents' preferences as given and asks how behaviour gets coordinated given them; much of what happens on the island looks more like a transformation of preference itself, and it is at least arguable that the rules the boys begin with were never endogenous conventions at all but imported habits of English school life, in which case treating their decay as a coordination failure would be a category mistake. There is also a mechanism the novel seems to propose for how new norms arise — mimesis and affective contagion in the painted faces, the chant and the dance — which is not quite the reinforcement learning or replicator dynamics of the evolutionary models, and I would like to know how much strain that puts on the framework. Please say plainly which scenes resist the reading, and end by saying what, if anything, the novel contributes to the philosophical debate that the argumentative literature does not. Assume I know the plot well, so no synopsis is needed, and please write in British English; around 1,200 words would be ideal.
