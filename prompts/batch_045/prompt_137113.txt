I spent thirty-five years as a physiological optics researcher — retinal and colour-vision work, mostly — and for the past two years, since retiring, I have been attending the philosophy of mind reading group at the university where I took my first degree. They have asked me to give the paper at next month's session, and I would like to be prepared for the discussion afterwards rather than merely to have a text ready. What I want from you is a Q&A preparation sheet.

The thesis I am defending is that qualia, as the tradition characterises them — ineffable, intrinsic, private, immediately apprehensible in consciousness — are not a doubtful posit but an incoherent one; and further, that the reason they have seemed coherent is that the arguments meant to establish their independence, above all spectrum inversion, tacitly idealise a quality space into a symmetric object that it does not in fact resemble.

The argument in the form I intend to give it:

1. The only grip we have on an individual putatively qualitative property is its position in a network of phenomenal similarity and difference relations. Describe the network completely and you have described everything that could matter to the subject.

2. The non-uniformity point, in the form I take from Chase and Sanborn: the structure of hue is lopsided. There are unique hues; orange is at once reddish and yellowish while no hue stands to blue and green in the way orange stands to red and yellow; yellow is intrinsically light and blue intrinsically dark. So there is no wholesale red-for-green or warm-for-cool swap that preserves the structure of the space. The naive inversion is not merely undetectable; it is unformulable.

3. My own addition, which is the part I am least confident about: the general claim ought to be stated as a claim about automorphisms. A modality admits an inversion-style scenario only if its quality space admits a non-trivial structure-preserving permutation. Properly described, colour space is rigid. And the same, I want to say, holds for the other modalities once one includes the affective and bodily structure instead of a tidy geometric model; where it appears otherwise, what we are looking at is a model of the phenomenology rather than the phenomenology.

4. Conclusion: what looked like a hidden intrinsic residue was only a node in a relational structure. The posit does no work, and the conviction that something is left over can be explained away.

The room will be about fourteen people. Three or four intentionalists in the Dretske–Tye line, who will think I have arrived late at a conclusion they already hold and will want to know what is new. Two phenomenal realists of a broadly Nagelian stripe, for whom the explanatory gap is the datum and any argument that dissolves it is a symptom of a defective method rather than a result. One committed illusionist of the younger sort, who will want to recruit me. Two vision scientists from the psychology department, who will want to talk about dichromats, tetrachromats and non-human colour vision, and who know the psychophysics better than I do now. And five or six graduate students who mostly want to know whether the view is compatible with physicalism and whether it does anything for them on the knowledge argument.

What I would like from you:

The eight or so objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how much damage each would do if I handled it badly, and phrased the way each subgroup would phrase it — not as they would come from an internet comment thread. For each, a reply I could deliver in about ninety seconds, and a note on where that reply is soft, begs a question, or concedes more than I would like.

What I should concede in the first two minutes, before anyone has to ask. My own candidates are that the non-uniformity facts are contingent facts about human trichromacy, and that the sophisticated Shoemaker-style inversion is not the naive one my second premise attacks — but tell me whether volunteering either is a mistake.

The single reply that has to land if the paper is to survive at all, and the point at which I should make it rather than wait to be asked.

The question I am hoping nobody asks. My own candidate is the one about whether the posit I am quining is one that anyone in the room is actually committed to — that is, whether I will have spent fifty minutes refuting a position that exists only in introductions to the subject. Tell me whether that is the right fear; and if it is not, tell me what the real one is.

Finally: a retired physiologist informing philosophers that their intuitions are artefacts of a bad geometric model will read as presumptuous, and I would rather not discover this from the third question. Tell me where in the ordering I should disarm it, and how to do that without grovelling.
