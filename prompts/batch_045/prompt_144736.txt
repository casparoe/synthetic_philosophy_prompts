I'm a metaphysics PhD student assembling a diagnostic handout for our graduate reading group on dispositions, and I want the handout to sort people by what they say about cases rather than by which account they say they prefer. Please write me a ten-question, three-option multiple-choice quiz whose scoring key maps answer patterns onto the following leaves: the simple conditional analysis; revised conditional analyses that add a basis-retention or fink-free/mask-free proviso; the causal-basis view, on which to have a disposition is to have some intrinsic property that would bring about the manifestation; the identity view, on which a disposition just is its categorical basis; the primitivist or powers-ontology view, on which the associated conditionals are symptoms rather than analyses; an interventionist or difference-making account; a gradable, context-relative account; and a deflationary view on which disposition ascriptions are inference tickets without robust truth conditions. Wherever an answer pattern lands in a region of logical space that nobody occupies, label it unnamed and add one line on whether that view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

Every question should be a concrete case, phrased so that someone who has never heard the labels can answer it, and no question should ask directly whether dispositions are real, whether the conditional analysis works, or which theory one accepts. Each option should be a judgment some actual account is committed to, not a filler. The classic fink and mask structures should appear, but I also want at least four cases built to split accounts that the standard cases do not, drawn from the following: a probabilistic fink that removes the basis before the manifestation only on some occasions; a self-triggering mask, where the stimulus is itself what activates the masking mechanism; a mimicker that produces the characteristic manifestation without having the disposition; a basis-swap case, in which the microstructure supporting the disposition is replaced by a different structure supporting the same range of manifestations; a single-track case, in which the object manifests in exactly one way and would do nothing else under any other stimulus; and a case in which the object has the ordinary basis but the stimulus is nomologically impossible in its environment. Choose the four that do the most diagnostic work, or use more if they aren't redundant.

Finally, flag every pair of leaves the instrument cannot separate — I suspect the causal-basis and identity views, and possibly the revised conditional analysis and the powers view on mask cases — and for each such pair either supply the further question that would separate them or explain why no case could.
