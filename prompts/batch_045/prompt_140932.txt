hey, I want a short story (call it 700–900 words, don't stress about the count) that does for Dennett's old "Where am I?" setup what decent sci-fi does for an idea — pushes it somewhere the original didn't go rather than restating it.

Quick refresher in case the version you know is fuzzy: the narrator's brain is surgically removed and kept alive in a vat, hooked up by radio to his own body, which walks around doing things elsewhere (he ends up calling the brain and the body by separate names). The puzzle is that "where am I?" stops having one clean answer — there's where the thinking is physically happening, there's where the body is, and there's where he seems to himself to be, and those come apart when the radio link degrades.

Your version: a woman signs up for a procedure where the two hemispheres are separated and each is installed in a different body in a different city, with a high-bandwidth link between them that keeps her feeling like one continuous person. The link is scheduled to be permanently switched off at a set date, months in advance, with her full consent. Before then she has to file one form declaring which body will continue as her — her name, her job, her kid, her debts, the lot. The other body gets a new name and is, administratively, someone else.

Here's the bit I actually want you to chew on. Over those months the two streams have quietly drifted: one has started getting up early to practice something, the other has been quietly building a life the first one wouldn't have chosen, and they've been leaving each other notes in a shared file. Neither is a villain and neither is lying. Meanwhile the clinic's intake material is very calm and practical about all this — it uses some criterion like: you are located wherever your intentions still reach, wherever you can still act. The line should be reassuring and she should find it useless in a way she can't quite put into words, mostly because it answers a question she isn't asking.

Some constraints: keep it concrete and mundane — give each body one silly physical anchor (rosemary on a windowsill, a neighbour's TV through a wall, whatever). No last-minute rescue, no dream reveal, no twist that the procedure was fake. Nobody in the story gets to give a speech about personal identity; any theorizing should come out as bureaucratic language — a form, a brochure, something a nurse says offhand. Let her narration start as a solid "I" and get a bit unstable near the end, so that the reader finishes genuinely unsure whether after the switch-off there's one person left, two, or none — and also unsure whether that's even the right question or whether the only real question was who gets the name.
