I sit on my town council's advisory panel on commemoration, and before we rule on three live items I have been asked to set out, in writing, the principles I am working from. I have written them down and they look coherent to me, but I would like an audit before I put my name to them, because I cannot see my own blind spots and I would rather find the crack now than in a public meeting. I am not attached to any particular item on the list; I would rather be told which one is doing the damage.

The commitments:

(a) Commemoration is the practice of according public honour. A standing statue of someone is not a piece of history teaching that happens to be made of bronze; it is an act of honouring, and the fact that it honours is what is objectionable about the objectionable ones.

(b) What a monument says is settled by the intention with which it was put up. Present-day viewers can reinterpret a statue, or stop noticing it, or use it as a meeting point, but they cannot make it say something its erectors were not saying with it. This is why I am unmoved by the argument that some monument has quietly become a harmless landmark.

(c) Historical agents should not be graded against present-day moral standards. Someone can be a fitting object of commemoration for what was admirable by the lights of their own time, and the fact that they also held views common in that time is not by itself a reason to un-commemorate them.

(d) Some standing monuments nonetheless ought to come down: Confederate monuments in the American South, the Colston statue in Bristol, the statues of Cecil Rhodes that the recent campaigns targeted. What is owed, in those cases, is removal rather than reinterpretation.

(e) Names are a different kind of thing from statues. A street name is primarily a coordinating label, not an honour; that is why renaming is administratively cheap, and it is also why renaming is a poor currency for redress.

(f) When a statue comes down, the plinth should be left empty rather than refilled with a replacement monument, so that the removal itself stays legible to people who never knew what stood there.

(g) The present public, acting through its democratic institutions, has the final say over what stands in its public spaces. There is no counter-majoritarian veto here: no expert body, no duty to a founder's intent, no constraint from what previous generations wanted.

(h) Commemoration can be something owed. The dead can be wronged, and sometimes we owe it to a particular person that they go on being remembered as they were remembered, whether or not the living take any interest.

The three live items, with the verdicts I have recorded:

1. A bronze statue of the surgeon Miriam Ashby, erected in 1971 outside the old infirmary. She devised a cheap operative technique that saved several thousand lives, and she devised it by operating, without consent and without therapeutic intent towards the patients, on inmates of a locked county asylum ward; a minority of her colleagues said so at the time and were overruled. The statue was put up to honour her for the technique, and the technique cannot be separated from the operations, since the published results she is honoured for depend on them. My verdict: remove it.

2. Verrill Street, named in 1899 after a landowner who paid for the town library and who, as chairman of the improvement board, ordered the clearance of the riverside settlement; descendants of the displaced families still live in the town, and they want the pre-1899 name restored. I have been arguing their case, and I have been arguing it in the language of redress: that restoring the name is part of what they are owed, not a tidy-up.

3. A plain granite block in the cemetery marking the workhouse burial ground, put up by the guardians in 1901, carrying no names. The council wants to shift it thirty metres to make room for a path. Nobody has objected and the local history society is indifferent. My verdict: move it, and do not feel bad about it.

What I want from you is an audit of the set, not a survey of the ethics of commemoration. Please do the following.

First, identify which commitments genuinely pull against each other, and rank those tensions by severity, most serious first, rather than flagging every pair as equally problematic. For each real tension, say precisely what would have to give, what the cheapest revision is, and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set.

Second, tell me which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection and why. I would rather be told that two of these only look incompatible than have every awkward pair listed as a problem; some of what looks like a conflict to me may just be a distinction I have not drawn.

Third, test the set against the three cases. Does it actually deliver the verdicts I have recorded, or is there a verdict my stated principles cannot reach? I am particularly unsure whether my position on Verrill Street is available to someone who holds (e), and whether my position on the cemetery block is available to someone who holds (h).

Finally, if a single revision would clean up the most, tell me which one it is, and then tell me which of my three verdicts would have to change if I made it.

Please write in British English throughout, argue for the claims you make rather than attributing them to named thinkers or schools, and work only with what is in this message: do not ask me for further detail about the cases, and do not supply or rely on citations. If the set is consistent as it stands, say so plainly, and tell me which of my verdicts is the one that will surprise people anyway.
