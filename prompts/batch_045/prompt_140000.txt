I build localization infrastructure for a living — mostly support tickets, release notes, and the occasional marketing page — so I end up arguing about machine translation constantly, and I've settled into a position I want stress-tested. Here it is: the complaint that machine translation is shallow, that it barrels along without understanding anything, the way that Hofstadter essay from a few years back argued (I think it was in The Atlantic, some time around 2018, the one with the house where everything comes in pairs and the French possessives collapsing into the same word) — is, as I read it, not really a claim about translation. Either it's a prediction about outputs that better and better systems keep falsifying, or it's a disguised claim about consciousness or real understanding that has nothing specifically to do with translating anything. On the first reading it's definately dying. On the second it's unfalsifiable and also not my problem. Either way I've stopped losing sleep over it.

What I want from you is the other side of that: what would count against it? Concretely, please. I have an LLM I can prompt sitting on my own machine and no web access, so I'd rather have things I could actually go run this afternoon than a tour of the literature — no reading lists, no "see so-and-so 1992", just arguments and probes. Here are two I invented and can't tell whether they're decisive or smuggle the conclusion in. (1) The decision ledger: ask the system to output, next to its translation, a list of every place where the source forced a choice the target language doesn't encode the same way, ranked by how much downstream the choice could matter. If a system I'd otherwise call shallow can produce a good ledger, does the shallowness charge die, or have I just moved the goalposts to a new output? (2) Frequency-matched minimal pairs: take a text where the right rendering turns on something the target language grammaticalizes but English leaves open — say a support email where the speaker says "I told your agent it was fine" going into a language that makes you mark whether you witnessed something or are reporting it — build the pair so both renderings are about equally common in training-ish text, and see whether the system's choice tracks the scenario instead of the base rate. Is that a real test of understanding or just a harder benchmark with a philosophy-flavored name?

Beyond grading my two probes, I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to say which of them a committed shallowness critic would themselves concede as counting against them, not just what people on my side say. Also be honest about whether my own position is insulated: if I've defined "understanding" so that no output could ever settle it, is that a vice, or is it a legitimate "category error, go away" move? And one personal note — modern systems will cheerfully explain why they picked a rendering, and I currently discount those explanations as worthless post-hoc rationalization. Tell me if I'm wrong to, and if there's a cheap way to check whether an explanation is tracking anything real (something about counterfactual behavior, maybe?). The thing that would move me most is a case where two translations are indistinguishable by any quality metric I could build, and yet it's obvious that one came from understanding and the other didn't — or a good argument that no such pair can even be described.
