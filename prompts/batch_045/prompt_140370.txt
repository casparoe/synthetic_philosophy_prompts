I'm on a volunteer oversight committee for a small capital project in my town — a new library branch, nothing glamorous — and I'm the person who has to put a cost and a timeline on my own bit of it (packing up and digitizing the local archive), and then watch the consultant add something like forty-odd percent on top before the number goes in front of the council. Apparently this is required: the guidance says estimates from teams like ours systematically come in low, so you add an optimism bias uplift taken from how comparable projects have historically overrun, you start at the top of the range and only earn your way down by documenting how well you're managing a checklist of known causes, you apply it to the base estimate before any contingency for identified risks, and you keep it as its own line so nobody can confuse the two. Fine. But I'm also in a philosophy reading group that's been grinding through disagreement and epistemic modesty — how much should you defer when people who look like your epistemic peers disagree with you — and the two things keep colliding in my head, because the uplift rule looks like institutionalized modesty: don't trust your own inside-view estimate, trust the base rate for cases like yours. Except the reference-class problem looks like the same problem in both places. Being told to file ourselves under one of six project categories, with a special blending rule if more than about a third of the capital value is non-standard, is a choice about how fine-grained to make the class, and in my reading group nobody could say why some disagreements count as peer disagreements and others don't either. And the escape hatch bugs me more: we're allowed to shrink the uplift by showing that we personally have our act together, which looks like the inside-view story the rule was meant to overrule sneaking back in the back door with a clipboard.

So what does this practice actually presuppose? I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit epistemology and then tell me where it's optional. Is the rule best read as saying planners are badly calibrated, or as saying something about their incentives — and does it even make sense on the second reading, given that a publicly known uplift gives a team an incentive to inflate the very number it gets added to, and given that the historical data is drawn from projects that were approved and managed under earlier versions of the same rule? Is the uplift a correction to what anyone should believe, or just an asymmetry-tolerance convention that nobody's beliefs have to track — and if it's the latter, what happens to the fact that the uplifted number is the one the council actually votes on and the bond is sized on? Take the outside view seriously for a minute: if the base rate is genuinely better evidence than my inside story, why adjust my estimate instead of replacing it, and why is the adjustment a flat percentage when sometimes I have unusually good inside evidence (we've already had the shelving quoted, the room is measured) and sometimes I have essentially none? And is the practice coherent in being one-directional — the official write-ups define the bias as including overestimating benefits and achievability, but the only numbers we're handed are for cost and duration, and it would never occur to anyone here to mark our benefits down by a symmetric amount, let alone to apply a base rate to the question whether towns like ours end up glad they built the thing at all. Where is the practice genuinely underdetermined, so that both someone who thinks you should defer heavily to others and someone who thinks you may reasonably stick to your own judgment could sign off on it, and what specifically would have to change in how our committee works if we actually believed the outside-view story rather than treating it as a spreadsheet convention? I'd of thought the answer is just that it's a crude but harmless budgeting rule, but my reading group made me less sure, and I'd like something I can bring back to the next meeting.
