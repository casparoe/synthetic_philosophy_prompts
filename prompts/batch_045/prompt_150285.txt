I’m a graduate student in a comparative constitutional theory seminar, and last week at a conference dinner I ended up listening to two political theorists—Marta and Dev—argue about whether the United Kingdom, Norway, and Japan can properly be called democracies. I’ve reproduced their exchange below as faithfully as I can, and I honestly couldn’t tell who was winning; I didn’t signal which side I favored. Marta opened by pointing to three real institutional wrinkles. First, Norway’s official 2023 English translation of its Constitution still says, in Article 3, “The executive power is vested in the King, or in the Queen if she has succeeded to the Crown pursuant to the provisions of Article 6 or Article 7 or Article 48 of this Constitution,” and Article 4 says, “The King shall at all times profess the Evangelical-Lutheran religion.” She argued that vesting executive power in a hereditary monarch who must pass a religious test is incompatible with political equality and popular sovereignty, and that the fact that conventions mute this does not erase the formal legal fact. Second, she turned to the UK practice of King’s Consent, which has been described as follows: “King’s Consent is a parliamentary convention under which Crown consent is sought whenever a proposed parliamentary bill will affect the Crown’s own prerogatives or interests (hereditary revenues, personal property, estates, or other interests).” She noted that in modern times “consent is granted or withheld as advised by government,” and that the 1851 edition of Erskine May said “the practical advantage of Queen’s Consent (as it then was, Queen Victoria being on the throne) was that it enabled the Crown to protect its rights without having to resort to blocking a bill after its passage by refusing royal assent.” Marta argued that this is a real pre-legislative power, not mere ceremony: Parliament cannot even debate affected bills without Crown consent, and “The Office of the Parliamentary Counsel has stated that it is entirely a matter of House procedure,” which just means Parliament has internalized a Crown veto. That was her Move 1. Dev replied, as Move 2, that Marta was reading fragments out of context. He pointed to Japan’s 1947 Constitution: Article 1 says “The Emperor shall be the symbol of the State and of the unity of the people, deriving his position from the will of the people with whom resides sovereign power,” Article 3 says “The advice and approval of the Cabinet shall be required for all acts of the Emperor in matters of state, and the Cabinet shall be responsible therefore,” and Article 4 says “The Emperor shall perform only such acts in matters of state as are provided for in the Constitution and he shall not have powers related to government.” He argued that in Japan the legal text itself locates sovereignty in the people and denies the Emperor governmental power, and that Norway’s Article 3 is similarly cabined by parliamentarism: Article 15 requires members of the Council of State to resign after a vote of no confidence and binds the King to grant the resignation, so efficient power rests with a Cabinet responsible to the elected Storting. On religion, he noted that Article 16 says “All inhabitants of the realm shall have the right to free exercise of their religion. The Church of Norway, an Evangelical-Lutheran church, will remain the Established Church of Norway and will as such be supported by the State. Detailed provisions as to its system will be laid down by law. All religious and belief communities should be supported on equal terms,” and that Article 2 says “Our values will remain our Christian and humanist heritage. This Constitution shall ensure democracy, a state based on the rule of law and human rights.” He concluded that the Lutheran clause is a symbolic identity condition on a ceremonial hereditary headship, not a live restriction on democratic decision-making, and that democracy requires only that efficient governing power be democratically controlled, not that every office be elected. Marta’s second turn, Move 3, was that Dev’s Japan example proved her point by contrast: Japan is a democracy because its text was rewritten to vest sovereignty in the people, whereas Norway’s older text still formally places executive power in the King and imposes the religious test, making it at best a monarchy restrained by convention. She added that even Japan is not fully democratized, because the Throne is dynastic and Article 6 has the Emperor appoint the Prime Minister as designated by the Diet, while Article 7 lists acts such as convocation of the Diet and dissolution of the House of Representatives that the Emperor performs on behalf of the people; if a monarch ever acted against Cabinet advice, the legal powers would be real, so dormancy is not the same as absence. On King’s Consent, she replied that a randomly selected citizen president with the same formal powers would be condemned as undemocratic, so the hereditary monarch gets an unearned pass. Dev’s second reply, Move 4, distinguished the dignified and efficient parts of a constitution: ceremonial honor and national continuity can be lodged in a hereditary symbolic office, while the efficient exercise of governmental power is what must be democratically controlled. He argued Marta’s “random citizen president” comparison was a red herring, because the test is not how we would react to an elected official with those powers but whether the actual distribution of effective power and the amendment procedure satisfy democratic legitimacy; the Norwegian Constitution has in fact been amended over time, so if the religion clause persists, that reflects democratic tolerance rather than democratic defect. Marta’s third turn, Move 5, conceded the dignified/efficient distinction was useful but said Dev was drawing it too generously to the monarchy: the line is not sharp, because appointment, dissolution, and consent powers are efficient powers merely delegated by convention, and democratic legitimacy should not depend on a gentleman’s agreement not to use legal powers. She also said the Norwegian religious clause is not merely dignified: it is a legal condition on the person who formally holds executive power, and Article 2’s statement that “Our values will remain our Christian and humanist heritage” constitutionally enshrines one religious heritage as the community’s values, which sits uneasily with equal citizenship even alongside human-rights guarantees. Dev’s third reply, Move 6, was that every democracy contains legal powers that officials promise not to abuse—presidential vetoes, judicial review, reserve powers—so the existence of unused legal power does not by itself make a system undemocratic; what matters is whether effective checks and the overall practice secure popular sovereignty. On establishment, he said the Church of Norway is supported but all religious and belief communities are to be supported on equal terms, so there is no coercion of conscience, and the values clause is paired with democracy and human rights rather than replacing them.

Please adopt the role of a professional philosopher specializing in political philosophy and constitutional theory, and think carefully before answering. Start by brainstorming the distinctions that seem to be doing the work here—democracy, popular sovereignty, formal versus effective legal power, convention versus textual rule, symbolic establishment, constituent power, and the difference between a constitution’s semantic content and its living conventional operation. Then adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the six moves above, rate it on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Finally, say who is more right overall, and explain what, if anything, both parties are missing (for example, about how to think about constitutions whose operation depends on conventions that are not legally enforceable, about what “democracy” minimally requires of a head of state, or about whether a hereditary, religiously qualified symbolism can be reconciled with equal citizenship).
