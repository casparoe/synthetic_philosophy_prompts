I have been defending, and am now close to committing myself to, a reformulation of the causal exclusion argument that I think improves on the version one meets in survey chapters, and I would like it stress-tested rather than corroborated. Let me set it out.

The familiar argument leans on two premises that look hostage to empirical fortune: that the physical domain is causally closed, and that systematic, pervasive causal overdetermination is not to be tolerated. If every caused physical event has a sufficient physical cause, and mental properties are distinct from physical properties, then mental properties either do no causal work or are permanently redundant shadows of causes that were sufficient anyway. I do not think metaphysics entitles me to rule out, a priori, a world in which a physically indeterminate event is settled by a subject's occurrent intention; and a single well-confirmed case of that kind would sink the closure formulation. So I have rebuilt the argument without closure.

(i) Mental properties supervene on physical properties without being identical with them.
(ii) A property is causally relevant to an outcome only if it has causal leverage of its own: it must figure as a difference-maker under some admissible intervention, or, if you prefer the counterfactual idiom, under some variation of it with the rest of the actual situation held as it in fact is.
(iii) The intervention asymmetry. Where a mental property M supervenes on a physical base P, no admissible intervention can vary M while holding P fixed. But, given the fine grain of the physical and the multiple realisability of psychological kinds, there are interventions that vary P while leaving M as it is. M's entire difference-making profile is therefore inherited from P's, and never the reverse.
(iv) The no-inheritance clause. Inherited difference-making is not causal relevance. A property whose counterfactual profile is wholly fixed by the profile of a distinct property does no causal work of its own; it is at best a redescription of work already done.
(v) Hence mental properties are causally irrelevant — which is to say epiphenomenal, unless one is prepared to reject (iv) and help oneself to causal relevance by inheritance alone.

What I want from you is a map of this argument's exposure, not a defence of it. Specifically:

1. Rank the defeaters, from fatal through serious to merely awkward. For each, say whether a defender of this precise version — not of the textbook closure version — should concede it as genuinely counting against the argument, or should dismiss it, and why.

2. The drainage problem. Nothing in (i) to (iv) seems to care that M is mental. Temperature and mean molecular kinetic energy stand in the same relation, as do a neural property and the molecular property realising it, and so on up through every special science. If the argument is sound, macro-causation in general is drained, and that looks like a reductio rather than a result. Does the intervention asymmetry distinguish the mental case in a principled way, or only in a gerrymandered one? What would have to be true for the distinction to be principled?

3. Proportionality in reverse. If causes must be proportionate to their effects, and mental properties are frequently more proportionate than the physical realisers on which they supervene, why does the argument not run backwards and exclude the physical instead? Is bidirectional exclusion a reductio of the argument form, or a discovery that causal relevance is level-relative — and if the latter, what is left of the conclusion?

4. Contrast-relativised causation. On views where causal claims have the form: c rather than c* causes e rather than e*, mental and physical descriptions select different contrasts, and premise (ii) looks too coarse to get any grip at all. Is this a genuine defeater, or only a restatement of the conclusion in a new idiom?

5. The modelling-artefact worry, which troubles me most. Within an interventionist or structural-equations framework, whether a supervening variable counts as a difference-maker can depend on which variables are included in the model and at what grain. If my conclusion is an artefact of admissible modelling choices rather than a metaphysical result, the argument proves nothing. How exposed am I here, and what would actually settle the question?

6. Self-stultification. If (v) is true, my own utterances about my mental life are not explained by their content. Is that a defeater for the argument, or only an objection to living comfortably with its conclusion?

7. Insulation. My version is now a priori in the sense that no finding in physics, psychology or the formal semantics of causal models appears able to touch it: closure might fail, causation might not be transitive, realisation might after all be identity, and so on. Has the argument been qualified into something nothing could dislodge? If so, is that itself a defect in an argument of this kind, or is exposure to empirical defeat simply the wrong standard to apply to it? Take a position on this rather than hedging.

8. What does this version predict that its rivals do not — about computation, about biology, about economics, about what would follow if psychological kinds turned out not to be multiply realisable? An argument that predicts nothing distinctive should worry me.

For each item I want the strongest reply available to me and a note on where that reply is weakest, with an explicit verdict at the end on whether anything here is fatal. I am not asking for a literature survey or for citations: argue the points. And for what it is worth, the defeater I would personally find most decisive is one showing that (iv) does its work only by presupposing the conclusion, or that the no-inheritance clause cannot be stated without either collapsing into a denial of supervenience or draining all macro-causation along with the mental.
