I was living in Edinburgh through the 2014 independence campaign and voted in it, and I am now doing some part-time postgraduate work on democratic theory, where I want to use that referendum as a case study of the boundary problem rather than as decorative background. The facts I am working from, so that we are not at cross purposes: the vote was held on 18 September 2014 on the simple question whether Scotland should be an independent country, needing only a simple majority; it was authorised by the 2012 agreement between the Scottish and UK governments and by the Scottish Independence Referendum Act 2013; the electorate was residence-based and about 4.3 million strong — British, Commonwealth and EU citizens resident in Scotland aged 16 or over, with 16- and 17-year-olds enfranchised for the first time — so that Scots living in England or Wales could not vote while EU citizens living in Scotland could, and the voters of the rest of the United Kingdom had no say at all; the result was 55.3 per cent No to 44.7 per cent Yes on a turnout of 84.6 per cent. What I want first is a disciplined mapping rather than an opinion. Take three or four of the standard answers to the boundary question — everyone whose interests are materially affected, everyone who would be subject to the coercion of whichever state or states result, the constituent-power view on which a people with a claim to self-determination simply is the right unit, and the thinner civic view on which lawful residence and a stake in the community suffice — and tell me what franchise each would actually have produced that September. I especially want four pressure points: the Scots resident elsewhere in the UK who were excluded; the EU citizens resident in Scotland who were included and whose citizenship would have been transformed twice over had Scotland left the UK and joined the EU; the 16- and 17-year-olds, and, if a criterion reaches them, why it does not also reach the 15-year-olds and the not-yet-born who will live longest with the outcome; and the rest of the UK, whose defence, currency, debt and EU membership were all in play.

Then say which criterion the actual franchise best matches and which it flatly contradicts — and then run the case the other way, because what interests me most is what it shows about the theory. Two features strike me as badly handled by the way the boundary question is normally posed. The first is that this demos was not discovered but chosen by a party to the dispute: the Scottish Government drafted the franchise, the Scottish Parliament enacted it, Westminster consented, and each contested element of it — the lowered voting age, the exclusion of Scots abroad, the inclusion of EU residents — was widely taken at the time to favour one side. As I have encountered it, the discussion asks which boundary is correct and seems to have very little to say about a boundary strategically chosen by one of the parties, where who decides is settled by a decision that is undemocratic in just the sense under discussion. Is that a genuine gap, or does one of the criteria already absorb it? The second is a test I have been turning over and cannot make work, which I have been calling the reconstitution condition: the demos competent to dissolve a union must be the same demos, constituted in the same way, that would be competent to reconstitute it, so that a decision which cannot be revisited by the electorate that inherits its consequences — because those voters are dead, newly enfranchised or newly arrived — is defective in a way that has nothing to do with the justice of the cause. Does that condition collapse into one of the standard criteria? Does it condemn cases we take to be clear, such as the American colonies or the secession that produced the Irish Free State, since those were irreversible too? And does it do any work on the problem of the chosen demos, or only relocate it one level up?

What I want from the answer is discipline about the mapping rather than breadth: who the rival claimants are, what each criterion counts as evidence of affectedness or subjection, what verdict it returns on each pressure point, and where it falls silent instead of being made to speak. I would rather have 700 careful words than a tour of the positions available, and I should mention that I am writing this from a borrowed flat with no library and no internet, so argument and example are all I can use and pointers to literature would be wasted on me.
