You are a philosopher of punishment and social norms who has also designed and run classroom simulations, so you know how an exercise behaves in an actual room as opposed to on the page. Before you commit, brainstorm two or three candidate mechanics and tell me in a sentence each what you rejected and why; then give me the one you would actually run, in full.

I teach an upper-division seminar on the ethics of punishment: 18 students, one 75-minute session, one room, nothing but index cards, poker chips, and a whiteboard that can serve as a permanent public record that is never erased. I want them to run into two asymmetries between legal punishment and decentralized social sanction before either is named.

The first: legal punishment is a quantity fixed by someone's judgment about desert, with a sentence and an end date. Social sanction is a quantity fixed by accumulation — anyone may add to it, nobody decides how much is enough, and there is no point at which the sanctioned person has served it. The second, which I think is the deeper one: the ratchet. Joining a sanction is cheap, anonymous, and available to everyone; lifting it requires an identifiable act of forgiveness that no one clearly has standing to perform and that exposes the forgiver to the same sanction. So the punishment is, in practice, irrevocable — and its largest effect is the speech that never happened, which everyone in the room then reads as agreement.

Design an activity where the students hit both of these themselves. Requirements: the disputed matter should be invented and deliberately trivial (with a real controversy they argue about the case and miss the structure), and sanction should attach only to statements made inside the game, never to the students themselves or to anything they actually believe. I want private preference cards whose distribution only I know, voluntary rather than compulsory speech, a limited and anonymous way to register disapproval, a record that persists, and a second phase in which the group tries to lift a sanction and discovers why it cannot. Give me rules, materials, a point scheme that needs no money and no real stakes, a minute-by-minute sequence, and the exact wording I should use to introduce it without giving the game away. Then the debrief: map three or four specific moments in the session onto parts of the asymmetry claim, including the moment they realize they misread the room. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — students playing it as a joke so that nobody feels anything, the sanctioned student enjoying the attention, someone naming the topic in the first ten minutes, my own temptation to reveal the card distribution early, a dominant speaker, students who opt out entirely — and how to handle each. Finally, be frank about what the exercise cannot reproduce and I will have to say in so many words myself: the real stakes, the timescale, the audience outside the room, and the fact that some of these sanctions are genuinely warranted. If you think either of my two asymmetries is the wrong way to put the point, say so and design for the corrected version instead.
