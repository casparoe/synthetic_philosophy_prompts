I'm a novelist, about two thirds of the way through a book set mostly on one ward, and I've written myself into a corner I can't write my way out of: one of my characters, Nadia, is the hospital's ethics consultant, and over the course of the book she gets pulled into three cases, and at the moment all she does in every scene is say "it depends" in slightly different words. My writing group has started counting. So I want her to have an actual method she can run out loud — something a resident could repeat back to her in an elevator, five steps tops, no jargon she'd have to stop and define, but something that genuinely separates the cases instead of collapsing them into the same shrug. I did half a term of a bioethics elective before dropping it, so assume I only know that there's a view where a person's earlier wishes should govern and a view that says what matters is the well-being of the person actually in the bed now, and that the two come apart sharply in dementia. I definitly don't need the names of anybody's positions, and since it's fiction, invented devices are very welcome.

The three cases, in the order the chapters come. (1) Marta, 71, moderate-severe dementia, no longer recognizes her daughter, plainly content most days — hums along to old songs, loves lemon cake, shakes her head and pushes the cup away when anyone comes at her with a pill — and has now got pneumonia. At 34, after watching her grandmother die the same way, she signed a directive refusing antibiotics and hospitalization for exactly this: "once I can't recognize my family." Two wrinkles I invented for the book: the state she lives in requires directives to be reaffirmed every ten years or they lapse, and hers never was (it's 37 years old); and my draft keeps having Nadia compare the earlier self to a novelist who wrote an outline and then lost control of the character — I'd really like to know whether that metaphor is doing any actual work or is just a line I've grown fond of. (2) Ray, 58, early dementia, wrote a directive at 34 refusing aggressive treatment, then rewrote it during a bad depressive episode saying to treat him aggressively and to disregard the first one as the depression talking; the second document's witnessing is botched and he was on heavy sedatives that week, so I gather it's probably void, but it also contains what my draft calls a "recovery clause": that if he ever stops thinking he's worth treating, that's the illness speaking and should be overridden. (3) Odile, 84, no directive at all, minimally conscious after a stroke, on a ventilator her children insist she would have wanted; there is, however, a letter she wrote to her future self at 19, found in a drawer, saying don't keep me going on machines for my children's sake.

What I want from you is the procedure itself, not an essay about the cases: five steps, each a question Nadia asks in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to it, where the procedure stops early with a no, and where it has to stop and hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. Build it so it discriminates — I want Marta's stale document, Ray's two conflicting ones and Odile's non-legal letter to land in genuinely different places — and illustrate each step on whichever of the three cases it bites hardest. One of them should come out somewhere I don't want it to go: I'm fairly sure the honest answer on Marta is that her directive doesn't bind and she gets the antibiotics, which I'd find annoying, since the whole third act turns on her daughter having honored it and felt terrible afterward, so tell me that straight rather than steering toward my plot. Also flag what the procedure can't settle at all — the points where no amount of question-ordering removes the need for someone to just decide — and the kinds of case you'd expect it to misfire on. Give it to me as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no summing-up at the end; and somewhere inside those five, not as a sixth item, say how confident you are in the procedure overall and name the single step you'd least want to defend if a real clinical ethicist walked in and read it over your shoulder.
