I write about science and ideas for a living, and in the last few years much of my beat has become forecasting — election models, epidemic projections, geopolitical risk shops — so the vocabulary I am actually fluent in is the vocabulary of my own trade: pre-registration, held-out data, skill scores against a base rate, overfitting and researcher degrees of freedom, the many-analysts problem, replication, and the difference between a nowcast, a backcast and a genuine forecast. My editor wants me to write the usual can history be a science piece about cliodynamics and Turchin's secular cycles — the roughly century- or two-century-long oscillations in which population growth presses on real wages, elites multiply faster than the offices and incomes available to them, factional competition among those surplus elites sharpens, state finances strain, and polities pass through a disintegrative phase before re-integrating — and I would rather write something else. So here is what I want instead: a faithful translation of that picture, in its strongest and most charitable form, into my vocabulary. Treat it as if you were briefing a data journalist who has to decide whether to trust it: what exactly is the unit of observation and how many independent ones are there; what is the target variable and on what scale is it measured; what is the model class, given that it is a dynamical model rather than the kind of regression I usually write about; what counts as training data and what, if anything, has been held out; what would a pre-registration document committed to before anyone looked at the data have had to specify in order for the theory to be testable at all; which base rate would a working secular-cycles model have to beat to be worth printing; and how would you score, honestly, the much-repeated warning that the United States was heading into a period of serious political instability in the 2020s, which was made well before the fact.

Then I want the residue report, which is the part I actually intend to build the piece around. Tell me what did not survive the translation, and for each item whether it is my vocabulary that is at fault — because it was built for large-N settings with many independent cases, no deep mechanisms and no recurrence — or the theory's, because something it leans on will not sharpen into a claim I could score. I have my own list of suspicions and I would like you to work through it rather than nod along: the suspicion that the effective sample size of history is a handful of cycles rather than hundreds of state-years, so that apparent fit is mostly degrees of freedom; the suspicion that the key quantities — elite numbers, popular immiseration, state cohesion, legitimacy — are proxies chosen with the outcome already in view, which on my beat is a disqualifying move; the suspicion that the theory is very good at redescribing a crisis once we know where it happened and much weaker at telling us when the next one begins, and that this is a fact about the arrow of time rather than an accident; and the suspicion that a large part of what cliodynamics offers is really a backcast — a compelling retro-diction — dressed in the language of prediction. I would like that last distinction, between forecasting, nowcasting and backcasting, used explicitly and applied claim by claim: which parts of the theory can in principle make an out-of-sample call about the future, which can only characterize the present, and which can only ever be fitted to the past. Finish by telling me, as bluntly as you can, whether there is any reading on which this research program has already stuck its neck out with a risky, falsifiable, out-of-sample call and been vindicated, and any on which it has done so and been wrong — because if the answer is that it has never really been exposed, I would rather print that, and say plainly why, than print another rumination on whether history can be a science.
