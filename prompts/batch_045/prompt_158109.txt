I have agreed to give a short talk on Garrett Hardin's lifeboat argument to a graduate reading group in philosophy. I am a retired population biologist who has started sitting in on philosophy seminars, and the group asked me for the one thing they thought a natural scientist could supply: an account of what is wrong with the ecological analogy. While preparing, I built an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept, and I would be grateful for a diagnosis rather than for a verdict on Hardin.

The argument, with my own labels for the parts:

(1) Hardin's case depends on a *ceiling*: a threshold of total occupancy such that taking more aboard sinks the boat, leaving everyone, those rescued included, worse off than if they had been left in the water. If such a threshold exists, then the duty not to destroy the boat's capacity defeats, at the margin, the duty to take more people aboard.

(2) The ceiling is not a fixed quantity supplied independently of the population sitting on it. In the systems I spent my career measuring, the analogous quantity is path-dependent: a population modifies the resource base it depends on, degrades it or builds it up, and overshoot registers in lagging indicators that are legible only after the damage is done. A boat is not special in this respect either: its capacity depends on the bailing rate, on rationing and redistribution among those already aboard, on stores and rigging, on whether boats are lashed together.

(3) Hence at any decision point the ceiling is partly a product of the crew's own earlier choices and of choices still open to it. What presents itself as an exogenous limit is really an inherited one.

(4) An agent cannot cite, as a reason why it is not required to aid, a limit that the agent has itself produced and can now relax. Ought-implies-can gives no purchase to a cannot of the agent's own making.

(5) So the lifeboat argument never applies to the cases it was written for. Whenever the ceiling is to any degree endogenous, and in political and economic cases it always is, the appeal to it carries no force at the moment of decision.

(6) But (4), as I have stated it, is indifferent to whether relaxing the ceiling is wise. The crew can raise its present intake by consuming capital, the seed stores or the rigging, so its present ceiling is, in the sense of (3), a product of choices still open to it; (4) then tells the crew that this ceiling cannot excuse a refusal, while (1) tells it that consuming that capital is precisely what must not be done. The two instructions are incompatible. And if (4) is qualified so that it does not bite in that case, I cannot see how it bites in Hardin's case either.

I find that unacceptable, but I would rather keep (2), which is the claim I would still defend in my old department, and (4), which seems to me sound in the ordinary instance where someone pleads a predicament they arranged.

So I want three things. First, the step you would reject, named precisely, with the reason it is that step and not a neighbouring one. Second, an account of why the step looked fine: every step looked fine to me and to the two philosophers I tried it on, and I would like to know what the step is trading on. Third, the price of each alternative, in particular what I lose if I give up (2), and what I lose if I keep (2) and restrict (4) instead. I have also been toying with a second distinction, between the ceiling, which is total intake, and the margin, which is the rate at which a system can absorb newcomers while still repairing itself, and I suspect the argument needs it somewhere; tell me where, if anywhere, it belongs. If the trouble lies in my reconstruction rather than in Hardin's metaphor, say so plainly. Names for the standard moves involved are welcome; I am not asking for references or suggestions for further reading.
