Me and a friend have been going back and forth about this for weeks and we are not getting anywhere, so I thought I would ask here. Bit of background: I am not an academic at all, I build demand forecasting models for a freight company and I read philosophy in a very amateur book-club way. We are slowly going through Popper's The Poverty of Historicism, the 1957 book version, and I have been the one argueing his side. My friend is really into forecasting tournaments and prediction markets and thinks Popper is basically obsolete: his claim is that Popper's whole attack is just you can't predict black swans dressed up, and that once you forecast in calibrated probabilities and keep honest score, everything Popper objected to dissolves, because nobody is claiming a law of history, just a number that gets updated.

The thing we cannot settle is what Popper would actually say about a system like the one my friend wants to build. Picture a giant history model: every digitised newspaper, parliamentary transcript, market tick, conflict database, migration and harvest and shipping record gets thrown in, and it puts out daily-updated calibrated probabilities for things like this government loses power within 24 months, or an armed conflict over X starts before 2035. It never states a law, only trends and conditional probabilities. The forecasts are published publicly. And its own past forecasts go back into the training data next quarter. If Popper were around today and you sat him down with this thing, where would he actually draw the line? I do not want a summary of his book, I want the argument you think he would make. Specifically: does the model escape his critique just by being probabilistic, conditional and continuously revised, or is there a version of trends are not laws that still bites even when nobody claims a law? Would he treat the fact that the forecasts are public, his Oedipus effect, as a decisive objection, especially in the nasty self-referential case where the model's own predictions become part of the data it later learns from? How would he handle the case that seems to me strongest for him, the model forecasting that some technology arrives around a certain year, fusion at grid parity, machine systems that can do some task, whatever, given that his argument was that you cannot predict the future growth of knowledge because if you could state it you would already have it? Does that kill predictions of timing too, or only predictions of content? And would he care that the same box is used for small concrete interventions versus used to say where the whole system is heading?

Two more things if you can. First, a suspicion I want checked: Popper liked bold falsifiable conjectures, but forecasting rewards hedging toward the base rate, and a well-calibrated model that never makes a single call you could count as refuted looks to me like exactly the sort of thing he would attack as immunised against criticism. Is that fair to him or am I projecting? Second, tell me honestly where my friend is right, which parts of Popper's case a modern forecaster could just shrug off, and name the one feature of a system like this that you think would have genuinely surprised him. I suspect there is at least one and I cannot put my finger on it.
