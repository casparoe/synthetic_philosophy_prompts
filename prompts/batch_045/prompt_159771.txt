hey — I got volunteered to write the 40th anniversary booklet for the volunteer mountain rescue team I'm in. I've got about sixty pages of old logbooks, newsletters and meeting minutes to work from, and I've realised I don't actually know how to tell the story without lying in one direction or the other.

Two people are pulling me opposite ways. One of the founding members wants it as "how we got from ropes and whistles to a proper team" — his draft literally calls their hand-drawn route cards "a proto-GPS". A newer member who did some history at uni says every section has to be "what did this make sense as at the time", and won't let me call anything an improvement at all, because, she says, they didn't know what we know and we don't know what they knew. I think she's right that the founder's version is smug, but her version makes it sound like we never learned anything, and new recruits are meant to read this thing and actually learn from the old mistakes.

Here's where I can feel the problem but can't say what it is:

- The paper map rule. Founders: everyone carries a paper map and compass, no exceptions. We all went over to GPS around 2003 and the rule quietly disapeared. Then in 2019 a team's phones and batteries all died in a storm and they walked out on a paper map somebody happened to have, and we brought a version of the rule back. If I write "the founders were right all along", I'm flattering them using our hindsight. If I write "we independently rediscovered it", that teaches something quite different. Which is the honest one, and does it depend on whether our reason for bringing it back is the same as their reason for having it?

- Some changes really do look like getting better at the job by the founders' own standards: average time to locate a missing walker has gone from roughly six hours to roughly ninety minutes, and we haven't lost a team member since 2004, where they lost two in the first decade. Other changes they'd have hated — the team used to be more or less a drinking club that did rescues, now there's a childcare rota and safeguarding policies. I want to call the first kind progress and the second kind just change, but I'm using our standards to sort them, which is exactly what she's objecting to, isn't it?

- The 1998 fight. Half the team wanted to stay completely informal and unpaid, half wanted a formal agreement with the county and paid standby cover. The informal side lost, most of them resigned, and today we have basically the thing they voted against. The minutes record their speeches but not their reasons. I keep catching myself writing them either as obstacles we had to get past or as tragic far-sighted people we should have listened to. Is the second version just the first one with the sign flipped?

An idea I had: a margin box in each chapter called "things they knew that we've forgotten" — they read the weather off the cloud over one particular ridge, nobody does that now — partly to break the it-was-all-leading-here feeling. Does that actually help, or is it a gimmick that lets me keep the main narrative as it was?

Also, I joined in 2011, I was on one side of a reorganisation, and I'm writing myself in. How much does that wreck it?

I guess the real question is: is there a way to write "we genuinely got better at some things" without writing "the whole history was heading toward us", and if there is, what does the difference look like at the level of individual sentences? The old-timer keeps wanting me to write "finally, in 2003..." and I can feel that one word doing something I don't want to do.
