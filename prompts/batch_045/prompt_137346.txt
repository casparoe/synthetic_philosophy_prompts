A city Naming and Remembrance Commission has asked me to draft its recommendation on a new petition, and I want the recommendation to cohere with the commission's own track record. The precedents are these:

1. A statue of a Confederate general, erected in 1911 by a private heritage association during the height of Jim Crow: removed and placed in museum storage.
2. A colonial-era street named for a merchant who was also a major slave trader: name kept, with an interpretive marker installed.
3. A high school named in 1978 for a segregationist governor who had died in office: renamed over alumni objections, after a two-year local campaign.
4. A clinic named for a nineteenth-century physician who carried out non-consensual surgical experiments on enslaved women: renamed, notwithstanding his real medical contributions.
5. A neighbourhood whose name derives from an enslaving family: no action, on the stated ground that the name no longer functions as a tribute to anyone.
6. A 1930s post office mural depicting the city's founding family: kept after review, in part because it was commissioned by a public arts programme rather than by a private group pursuing a political agenda.

The new petition concerns a public high school named in 1955 for the school-board president who led the fight against desegregation, a name chosen explicitly as a signal of resistance to the 1954 ruling. The school is now roughly 85% Black, and the alumni association that has petitioned to keep the name is largely made up of the students who integrated the school in the 1960s and their children; they say the name now means the school itself rather than the man. The student government is split, and the local branch of a civil-rights organisation supports renaming. Complicating it further: the honoree's family endowed a restricted scholarship fund for students who attend the school bearing the family name, currently supporting about thirty graduates a year; under the terms of the gift, if the school is renamed the corpus reverts to the family and the scholarships end.

What should the commission decide, if consistency with its own precedents is the aim? I would like more than a verdict. Set out the two or three principles that fit the six precedents about equally well, show what each implies here, and give me your best recommendation with the reasons for preferring it. Identify which precedent is the closest analog and why the others are weaker. Then tell me what the commission would be committing itself to either way, whether that principle would retrospectively vindicate or call into question any of the earlier six decisions, and what further fact or hypothetical variant would most cleanly separate the competing principles.
