I host a small philosophy podcast, and next month's guest is a moral philosopher who defends something she calls the succession conjecture: a twist on Scheffler's afterlife conjecture on which what our values tacitly presuppose is not that humanity survives us, but that there will be successors — descendents, posthumans, uplifted animals, maybe artificial agents — who can inherit and carry on the projects we're currently engaged in. On her version, extinction without successors is the catastrophe, while human extinction by way of a handoff to successors is fine, or at least nowhere near as bad; and she thinks a good chunk of what gets called existential risk work is therefore aimed at the wrong target.

Draft me eight to ten questions that make her work for it rather than restate the view, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. I want at least one question each on: whether the conjecture is a descriptive claim about what we actually presuppose or a normative claim about what we should, and how you'd go about telling those apart; the shape of the temporal curve — how much future is enough, whether there's a threshold or it just shades off, and whether extinction in 200 years really differs from extinction in 200,000; whether her view ends up supporting the longtermist's astronomical-numbers arguments or quietly competing with them (my sense is that she's swapped "very many future people" for "some inheritors", which is a much weaker demand and so may not fund the same conclusions at all); whether the badness of losing the future can depend on nobody noticing or nobody caring, and what the view says if people stop caring about the future well before it runs out; and the implication she'll least want to dwell on, that if artificial successors can genuinely inherit then a lot of what gets framed as an existential catastrophe is, on her own terms, a succession.

Two more things. She has a habit of batting questions away with "that's a question about who inherits, not about whether there's an inheritance", so I'd like two or three questions that make that move progressively harder to lean on. And please close with one question about what evidence or argument could actually count against the view.

Sections, in order: "Framing" (2-3 sentences on what you're taking her view to be and where you think it's most exposed), "Questions and follow-ups" (the questions, each with her likely answer and your follow-up, 100-150 words a piece), and "Order and traps" (~150 words on how you'd sequence them across an hour and which ones she'll try to duck).
