I've been asked to give the faculty address at our graduation in three weeks and I'm completely stuck on it, so I'm outsourcing. Roughly eight minutes, so call it a thousand words.

Context: I teach philosophy at a two-year technical college — our graduates are going out as HVAC techs, welders, robotics/mechatronics. About half of them have at some point run into sensitive dependence on initial conditions in a controls or diff-eq class, and the other half have not. Then there are the families, a lot of grandparents, and a row of local employers and scholarship donors who definitely have not. Everyone is going to be hot and bored.

Here's my problem. The obvious commencement move on the "for want of a nail" theme is: small causes have huge effects, therefore you matter, therefore go out and be the small cause that changes everything. I think that move is mostly a cheat and I don't want to say it. The reason is something like this: sensitivity to initial conditions is something you can only ever establish backwards. From basically any outcome you like, you can trace back some chain of tiny differences that would have redirected it — which means "a nail changed everything" is a claim that comes out true for every event whatsoever, and so it gives you nothing to aim with. Going forward you can't tell which perturbation will amplify and which will just get absorbed. Some junctures have plenty of backup routes (ten other people had the same idea, the system had a spare, someone else would have done it that week anyway) and some really don't, and you mostly can't tell which kind you're standing in until it's over.

So the honest thing isn't "you are the butterfly" and it's also not the smug version, "nothing you do really matters." My own half-formed answer is that what you should aim for isn't being the nail but being the kind of action whose value is spread across a lot of downstream branches — something worth doing whether or not this particular chain amplifies, and that doesn't require you to be the one irreplaceable person in the story. Take that somewhere real if you can; argue with it in the margins if you think it's wrong, I'd honestly rather have that.

Things I can't say, which is most of what makes this hard: no telling them they're the butterfly, no "everything happens for a reason" (a solid chunk of that room hears God in it and another chunk hears nonsense), no wink-wink nihilism, and nothing that leaves them walking out of the tent feeling small. It still has to be a commencement address.

Hard nos on examples: no butterfly in Brazil, no Cleopatra's nose, no Waterloo, no Gettysburg. You can use the nail rhyme if it earns its place but I'd rather the speech didn't lean on it. And take the concrete example from the trades we actually teach, not from big-H History — a fitting nobody torqued to spec, a sensor that drifted out of calibration, a bead laid slightly wrong and buried under three more passes. One concrete bit, worked properly, not a list of them.

Last thing: the donors. There's a row of people who paid for these scholarships and need to hear that their money did something, and on the view I actually hold I'm not sure I can promise them that. Whatever you do with them, don't lie and don't get cute about it.
