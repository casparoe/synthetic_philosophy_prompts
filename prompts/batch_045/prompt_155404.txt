Last night a friend and I argued for several hours about whether our emissions policy can wrong people born a century from now. I came away unable to locate the break in her reasoning, and I would like your help finding it. She sets it out as follows.

(1) Call a choice *identity-fragile* when the set of people who ever exist depends on which option is taken. Every realistic climate policy choice is identity-fragile: a carbon tax shifts where people travel and work, whom they meet, and when they conceive, and because conception timing is sensitive to circumstance, within a couple of generations the entire downstream population is different.

(2) A choice wrongs someone only if it makes that person worse off than they would otherwise have been. Wrongness is person-affecting, and the complaint is comparative.

(3) Compare a lax policy L (roughly 3°C of warming) with a stringent policy S (roughly 2°C). Because of (1), everyone who exists under L is a distinct person from everyone who exists under S. The people who exist under L would not have existed under S at all.

(4) The lives of the L-people, though hotter, poorer, and more hazardous than the lives of the S-people, remain well above the threshold at which existence is a harm to the one who lives it.

(5) So no one who exists under L is worse off than they would otherwise have been: their only alternative under S was never to exist, and (4) says that would not have been better for them.

(6) By (2), choosing L wrongs no one. Since the reasoning does not depend on the size of the gap between L and S, no realistic climate choice wrongs anyone, whatever the warming. Duties of intergenerational climate justice turn out to be empty; there remain only impersonal reasons of optimality, if one happens to care about aggregate welfare.

(7) She adds that the argument generalizes rather than reducing to absurdity: a policy that knowingly bequeaths a degraded but survivable world — depleted soils, collapsed public infrastructure, endemic background risk — to a population whose identities depend on that very policy wrongs no one either, so long as those lives stay worth living. She thinks our intuition here is simply a non-identity case in disguise.

Two lines of resistance I tried both stalled. I said that some future people are *identity-robust* — people alive now, and those whose conception timing is insensitive to our policy — and that (3) fails for them. She grants that the same-people case is where the comparative test does real work, but says that set is a demographically contingent remnant that cannot ground duties to generations a century out. I also pressed her on (5), and she replied that nonexistence is not a state a person can be in, so "worse off than they would otherwise have been" is not false for the L-people but undefined, and that an undefined comparative claim cannot ground a duty, so the duty drops out.

I feel the argument proves too much and cannot accept (6) or (7), but every premise looks defensible. Please tell me which step fails and why it looked sound. If I have to choose, I would rather keep some person-affecting constraint than accept (7) — though I am uneasy about abandoning (2) outright, since a purely impersonal view seems to license displacing actual people with better-off replacements. Price the alternatives for me: giving up the comparative person-affecting requirement, resisting (1)'s scope claim about identity fragility, and the move from (4) to (5). Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the step you reject and, in a phrase, the reason — then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of what I have asked.
