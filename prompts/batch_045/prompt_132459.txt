I am writing a novel, and one scene is turning on an argument I want to get right rather than merely plausible. I would be grateful for your help in drafting it.

The setting: a private consortium of nine scientists has built a strain of fungus meant to outcompete a blight that is collapsing the world's staple crops. Each of the nine holds a key, and any single key is sufficient to release the strain into the wild; there is no way to compel, veto, or even detect another's release. If the strain is safe, releasing it averts a famine on the order of two hundred million deaths. If it is unsafe — if it carries an unmodeled failure mode — it triggers an ecological collapse perhaps ten times worse than the blight it was meant to stop. The release is instantaneous, global, and irreversible.

Two complications are load-bearing for the plot. First, the nine did not work independently: all of their assays were calibrated against a single shared instrument, and there is a real chance that instrument is itself miscalibrated in a direction that makes the strain look safer than it is. So their errors are not independent. Second, each of them knows that some of the others may have quietly destroyed their keys, and each has private, unreliable grounds for guessing how many remain live. Nobody knows how many potential releasers there actually are.

Here is my lay understanding of the argument underneath all this, which I would like the scene to dramatize rather than lecture about: when many people can each unilaterally trigger a shared risk, the expected value of acting is much worse than any individual's estimate suggests, because the very fact that you are the one most inclined to act is evidence that your private evidence sits in an upper error tail — you should therefore hold yourself to a far higher bar than ordinary cost-benefit reasoning would set. I have seen this called the unilateralist's curse. If I have garbled it, write the scene around the correct version and tell me in the afterword where I went wrong.

What I need is a dialogue of roughly twelve hundred to fifteen hundred words between two of the keyholders, Vera and Ansel, who have been arguing about this for years and are now in a room together with the release imminent. Vera holds that each of them must apply a severe personal discount — that she should act only if her evidence would still favor release after conditioning on her being the most enthusiastic of however many keyholders remain. Ansel holds that this demand is the wrong fix: that the right response is to change the structure of the decision — pooling their evidence, naming a single trustee, staging a partial or reversible release, requiring two keys — and that the norm of individual self-deflation is both superfluous given a decent institution and self-undermining in ways I would like him to spell out.

Please make them equally competent. Neither should blunder; each should engage the other's actual claim rather than a caricature of it, and each should concede at least one real point and be forced to revise. I would like the correlated-instrument wrinkle and the unknown-number-of-keys wrinkle to be fought over rather than asserted past: my scientifically literate friend tells me that shared error cuts both ways here, blunting the tail-evidence argument while also destroying the hope that nine independent checks protect the group, and I would like the characters to disagree about which effect dominates. Give the scene concrete numbers — priors, error rates, the size of the discount — stated once and then used consistently, and have each technical move cashed out immediately in a claim about those numbers, since my readers will not forgive an exposition dump. End it unresolved, or on a decision whose wisdom is honestly left open.

Finally, a short afterword addressed to me rather than to my readers: which of the two characters' claims you take to be correct, which are genuinely contested among people who work on this, where the disagreement bottoms out, and anything my setup makes impossible to settle.
