I'm giving a 45-minute paper with about twenty minutes of questions at a philosophy of science colloquium next month, and what I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet for this specific room, not a balanced survey of the reduction–emergence debate. The paper defends a package I've been calling the fixed-point account, and I'd rather you try to break it than restate it approvingly back to me. A version of this died in a seminar last spring because I let myself be dragged into a fight about whether water is identical to H₂O, so please don't let the prep sheet route through the standard textbook cases unless one of these people is likely to drag me there.

The position, as I now have it. First: emergence in actual scientific practice is insensitivity of a macro-level description to micro-level detail, but there are three grades of that insensitivity and the literature runs them together — invariance of a macro description under mere redescription of the same micro-trajectory; invariance under perturbation or replacement of the micro-dynamics, the robustness that lets a model get the right macro answers while getting the microphysics wrong; and invariance under wholesale replacement of the substrate, where the same macro law holds of entirely different stuff. Second: the middle grade does all the real work. What scientists call emergence — universality, critical exponents, hydrodynamic and continuum limits — is the claim that a whole family of micro-dynamics flows toward the same fixed point under coarse-graining, and that is compatible with everything a reductionist ever wanted: supervenience, micro-determination, even the claim that macro facts obtain because of micro facts. Third: so the antireductionist's live argument has to run through the indispensability of macro-level descriptions, and my reply is that indispensability is indexed to a budget — to a finite agent with finite computational and descriptive resources facing a derivation that is not merely long but singular, since the infinite limit in which the macro law is exactly true is not the smooth limit of the finite cases at all. Indispensability on that reading is a fact about the modeling situation, not about nature's causal hierarchy, and the inference from it to ontological emergence is a non sequitur. Fourth, and this is my open flank: at a singular limit the macro behavior is not derivable from the finite micro-dynamics even in principle — not merely not by us — and I genuinely do not know whether my budget story covers that case or whether the honest thing is to concede a local in-principle autonomy and give up the headline claim.

The room: a senior philosopher of physics who works on universality and coarse-graining, thinks emergence is just a word for universality, and thinks philosophers manufactured a problem where the physicists had a technique; a second philosopher of physics, more sympathetic, who grants singular limits and infinite idealizations are real and will want to know what the fixed-point vocabulary adds to the point she already accepts; a metaphysician who will run the causal-exclusion and overdetermination argument and ask whether macro properties do any causal work on my view; a philosopher of biology who defends the autonomy of the special sciences and mechanistic explanation, who is my natural ally and who I suspect will discover mid-question that my account gives her less than she hoped; a graduate student who has read the antireductionist literature and thinks multiple realization settles everything; and, reliably, one person from philosophy of mind who will ask whether any of this touches consciousness. Give me eight or so objections, ordered by how much damage they'd do rather than by how likely they are, each attributed to one of these people and phrased the way that person would actually phrase it, in their idiom. For each: what I should concede before being asked, a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds without notes or a screen, and where that reply is soft. If one of them is fatal rather than merely damaging, say so and say what I'd have to abandon. Tell me which of my three grades I should quietly drop if I'm losing the room and need to shorten, and close with the question I'm hoping nobody asks — my own candidate is whether making indispensability budget-relative means ontological emergence comes and goes with improvements in our computing power, but if you think the real nightmare question is a different one, give me that instead. No citations, no bibliography, and please don't tell me to look anything up: I know who argued what, and I won't be able to check anything in the room.
