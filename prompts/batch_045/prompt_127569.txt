For a forthcoming interactive feature I need a short diagnostic instrument: a "which position on the politics of artifacts do you actually hold?" exercise that a general reader can complete in about four minutes, before we tell them what any of the positions are called. The news peg is a decision my city is about to take. It is approving a fleet of app-controlled e-scooters whose firmware cuts motor power to walking pace inside three low-income neighbourhoods; the transport department describes this as a safety measure, a tenants' union describes it as a curb on movement, and the operator describes it as a contractual requirement from its insurer. Whichever way the council votes, my readers will have an opinion about whether the scooter, the firmware, or the contract is the thing that has politics — and they will disagree about it in ways they cannot articulate. I want the piece to sort them by how they rule on cases, not by which label they find attractive.

Constraints on the questions. Eight to ten of them, each a concrete artifact or a concrete change to one, each with two or three answer options phrased as verdicts an ordinary reader would recognise. No question may name a position, a school, or a theorist; no question may use the terms determinism, mediation, inscription, affordance, social construction, or neutrality. At least half the cases should be ones I have not seen used in this literature — please draw on these, and add better ones of your own: a shared-house thermostat that learns one tenant's schedule and not the others'; a hospital shift app that lets a nurse drop a shift instantly but not offer it to a named colleague; a school-issued laptop whose webcam can be enabled remotely by an administrator; a self-checkout aisle; an open-source tool built and governed entirely by the people who use it; a cooperative delivery platform that was designed to flatten hierarchy and whose members now use it to rank each other's punctuality. You may use the two canonical ones — the low parkway overpasses that buses cannot pass under, and the mechanical tomato harvester — but each must be rewritten as a decision under uncertainty rather than as a parable with a known moral, and you should include the objection that the overpass story rests on contested history. I also want one case whose politics appears only once the artifact is deployed at population scale but not in any individual use, and one in which the entire complaint is about what the artifact prevents rather than what it compels.

Build the tree from the logical space first. I want three or four separating questions that do most of the work, each isolating one distinction: whether the political character survives the loss of the designer's intention and its replacement by hostile users; whether rebuilding the same function in a different material form removes the political character; whether the artifact determines outcomes or merely inclines them, and whether resistance or workaround counts as a refutation; whether the politics is located in the labour and ownership relations of production or in the moment of use; whether the possibility of exit, refusal, or redesign dissolves it. Show the combinations those questions allow, then place the actual positions in that space, and note the unoccupied cells: for each, say whether nobody is there because the combination is incoherent, because it is coherent but unattractive, or because it is merely unexplored and defensible.

The leaves I need covered, in plain language my desk can typeset, with a one-line gloss each and one consequence each has to swallow that it would rather not: artifacts as neutral instruments whose politics belongs entirely to users' ends; technology as an autonomous driver of social change; artifacts that are inherently political because their technical arrangements require or strongly favour a social order; politics located in the process of development rather than in the finished thing; the social-constructivist reading on which an artifact's meaning and biases remain open until a relevant community closes them; the view that artifacts are delegated social relations made durable; the critical-theory view that a technical code encodes a hierarchy but can be rewritten by subordinate groups; the feminist-STS claim that gendered and other hierarchies are built into design and its ergonomics; the postphenomenological claim that artifacts mediate perception and agency and so co-shape what a moral decision even is; and the position that hostile design is itself a harm to be removed rather than a bias to be redesigned. Flag explicitly any pair of leaves your instrument cannot separate, explain why the diagnostic questions collapse them, and tell me what further case would pry them apart even if it will not fit in the published version.

Deliverable, for our interactives desk: the questions with their options; a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto leaves, including weights for the load-bearing questions and a tie-break rule; a tree or flowchart version of the same instrument for the print edition; and a short note to me on which two questions are doing most of the sorting and which one I should expect readers to complain is unanswerable without more detail. Do not give me citations, bibliographies, or suggestions for further reading — the published piece carries no footnotes, and I need nothing a reader could not check against their own reactions.
