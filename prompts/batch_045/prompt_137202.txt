I'm writing the methods appendix for an internal proposal at the hospital where I work: we are deciding whether to extend continuous overnight post-operative monitoring, with automated alerts, to two further wards. A colleague on the committee keeps invoking what he calls Good's theorem — the result that, for an expected-utility maximizer, a free observation cannot have negative expected value when evaluated before the observation arrives — and treating it as a general license to gather more data. I think he is overreaching, and before I write the section I want a careful, non-hand-wavy assessment of how much the theorem really supports a decision like ours.

I have drawn up a list of candidate ways in which the result might fail to license more monitoring. Please sort them into tiers by how seriously each limits the practical force of the theorem for a decision of our kind: Tier A for genuinely live problems for us, Tier B for worries that are real in principle but require a setup we do not have, Tier C for pseudo-problems that the theorem already handles or that are being described in the wrong terms. Give each item a severity score from 0 to 5 and at most two sentences of justification, and where an item is a red herring say plainly what the mistake is. The items: (1) ex post regret — the guarantee is ex ante, so a particular alert can leave the agent, once it has arrived, worse off than if it had never come, which matters when people are evaluated or feel accountable after the fact; (2) information that is free to acquire but expensive to use — attention, computation, and the opportunity cost of the clinician time that responding to alerts consumes; (3) agents without commitment power who re-optimize after every new observation while having dynamically inconsistent preferences (ambiguity aversion with max-min updating, hyperbolic discounting, regret-based or other non-expected-utility rules), so that the argument that one can simply plan to ignore the signal no longer secures the result; (4) observations that change the world rather than merely report on it — a workup that both informs and alters which treatments remain possible, or that reshapes the option set; (5) strategic settings, including the Hirshleifer-style effect in which better information destroys risk-sharing or insurance gains, and game-theoretic cases where a player's information makes them worse off because others respond to it; (6) unbounded or ill-defined expectations, where the relevant expected utilities or their differences do not converge, so the quantity the theorem is about is not well defined; (7) rule-following or constrained agents who cannot adjust their action in response to what they learn, or for whom learning creates obligations that force a worse action; (8) the acquisition of information being itself visible, so that commissioning a test signals something and changes how counterparties behave.

One complication our setting forces, which I have not seen discussed: on our wards an alert is not optional to act on. Protocol requires a response to every alert, so the agent cannot replicate the ignore-the-signal strategy that the standard proof leans on; I have been calling these mandatory-response decision problems and using that as the modelling assumption in my appendix. Please redo the ranking under that assumption and tell me, item by item, which entries move tiers because the ignore-it escape hatch has been removed — in particular whether (3), (7), or any of the others turn from red herring into live problem once the agent is required to be responsive to the signal. Finally, give me two or three sentences of honest summary for the committee: under exactly what description does additional monitoring have non-negative value, and what would have to be true of our wards for that description to apply?
