I'm an engineer on an interpretability tooling team, no philosophy background at all, so please dont laugh at my vocabulary. We keep having the same argument: when our sparse autoencoder hands us a "feature" — some direction in activation space that lights up on, say, code with an off-by-one bug — is that feature something the model really has and we just discovered it, or is it a coordinate system we imposed that happens to be useful? Nobody can settle it, so I've decided to make fun of us instead.

Write me a parody of an internal RFC / design doc, the kind that gets circulated on an eng mailing list, proposing that the company adopt a strict "one concept, one direction" policy: every concept the model represents has to register exactly one canonical direction in activation space, with an owner, a stable ID in the feature registry, and a quarterly recertification. Give it a number and a title (RFC-xxxx), use the usual sections (Summary, Motivation, Non-Goals, Rollout Plan, maybe an FAQ), about 600–700 words. The narrator is a totally sincere staff engineer who has definately never noticed that his policy is a metaphysical position, and the tone stays deadpan — no winking, and nobody ever says the word "realism."

The comedy has to come from real features of the interpretability situation rather than invented ones. I'm thinking: superposition, i.e. way more candidate features than there are dimensions to put them in; polysemantic neurons; the way a single clean feature shatters into six finer ones the moment someone trains a bigger dictionary; the fact that which directions you even get depends on the basis or dictionary you picked; the fact that we mostly find features we went looking for; and the fact that a linear probe finding a direction doesn't show the model uses it for anything downstream. Someone who actually reads interp papers should feel the joke is fair to both sides.

Take a side though: I want the punch to land on the realists or on the "it's just a useful fiction" crowd, not on both equally — pick one and commit to it. At the end add a few lines saying which one you went after and why, and where the parody takes liberties or leans on a contested reading.
