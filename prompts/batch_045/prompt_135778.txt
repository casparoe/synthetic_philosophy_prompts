I TA an undergrad language acquisition course and I'm putting together the section on the poverty of the stimulus, and I keep running into the same analogy in the notes my professor handed down: the language faculty is like an operating system that ships pre-installed on the machine, and what the environment supplies is just the peripheral stuff — the printer drivers, the locale settings, a handful of config switches that get flipped once and then stay put. Last week in a reading group one of the NLP people used it to shut down a whole discussion: if the child is basically born with the OS and the input only flips some switches, then the fact that a model trained on a trillion words ends up doing something syntax-ish tells us literally nothing about how children do it, because you can't get the OS out of the data no matter how much data you pile on, and the relevant comparison is data per learner anyway. I half believe this, but I also suspect the analogy is doing more work than the argument does, and I have to explain it to a room of sophmores on Thursday without overselling it.

So: audit it. Give me the correspondence table first — what plays the OS, the hardware, the installer, the drivers, the switches, the user data, the "version" — and be explicit about which of those are supposed to be empirical claims about the kid and which are just pictures. Then the places it breaks, sorted by whether they actually matter for the conclusion it's being used for. I had a list going: nobody hands the child a manual or an error message, so there's no analogue of negative evidence; the "switches" in actual parameter accounts don't look independent or one-shot; a driver install either succeeds or fails whereas the child's path is gradual and noisy; an OS is written by someone with a purpose; the hardware/software line is way cleaner than any brain/module distinction probably is; an OS doesn't get reorganized by being used; and when I pushed my professor on the messy cases he said "well, more like dimmers than switches," which I think might just be giving up whatever the analogy was for. I can't tell which of these are load bearing for "so the ML stuff is irrelevant" and which are decoration.

Then the part I actually care about: does the conclusion survive. My worry is that the analogy quietly builds in that the innate part is a discrete, specifiable, linguistically specific thing that data can only trigger, and that once you retreat to the weaker and more defensible version — the learner has strong architectural biases, and the real question is quantitative, how much bias plus how much data gets you there — the trillion-word model stops being irrelevant and becomes evidence about the trade-off. If that's right, say so, and say what the OS picture would have to add in order to block it; if I'm wrong and the conclusion survives intact, tell me why. Also run it on two cases I want to use in section: (1) a kid who gets only a couple of hours a week of usable input and still ends up indistinguishable from her classmates by school age, and (2) a hypothetical learner whose input is complete and clean but who never gets corrected on anything and never hears an ungrammatical sentence — what does the analogy predict in each, and does the prediction match what people actually think happens? And if there's a better analogy for whatever the real view is, or if the honest answer is that analogies are the wrong tool here, I'd definately rather hear that than a nicer version of the same one.
