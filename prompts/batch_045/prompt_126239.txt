**Question 4 (25 marks).** Answer with reference to Ned Block's distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness, and to Global Workspace Theory (GWT).

*(a)* Set out Block's distinction. Explain why GWT is standardly described as a theory of access consciousness rather than of phenomenal consciousness, and say what would have to be added to it for it to be a theory of phenomenal consciousness as well. *(10 marks)*

*(b)* A defender of GWT might add the *Broadcast Sufficiency Thesis* (BST): for any subject S and representation R, if R is globally broadcast in S's workspace — that is, if its content is made available to S's systems of reasoning, deliberation, voluntary action, verbal report and long-term memory — then R is phenomenally conscious for S. Now consider the *Silent Broadcaster*: a possible subject whose early visual representations pass every behavioural and neural test we have for global broadcast, but who sincerely reports, of those representations, that there is nothing it is like for him to undergo them. Assess whether the Silent Broadcaster is a problem for GWT, and whether BST is an adequate reply to it. In answering, you should address (i) whether GWT is best understood as an empirical identity claim about phenomenal consciousness or as an account of a functional property that may be distinct from phenomenal consciousness, and (ii) what sort of evidence the Silent Broadcaster's own report could be. *(15 marks)*

BST and the Silent Broadcaster are stipulated for the purposes of this question and are not to be attributed to any author.

**Script 4 (candidate 227).**

[1] A state is access-conscious, in Block's sense, when its content is available to the subject: available for verbal report, and available to guide reasoning and deliberate action. A state is phenomenally conscious when there is something it is like for the subject to be in it — when it has qualitative character, the hurt of a pain or the particular way a tomato looks. The point of the distinction is that these are two properties and not one, and that they can in principle come apart. Block's overflow argument brings this out: the visual experience of a crowded scene is far richer than what the subject can report on or reason with at any given moment, so phenomenal consciousness is not exhausted by what is accessed. The two properties therefore stand in need of separate theories, and a theory of the one should not be mistaken for a theory of the other.

[2] Global Workspace Theory, in Baars's original form, postulates a workspace of severely limited capacity. Specialised unconscious processors compete; the coalition that wins gains entry to the workspace and has its content broadcast to the rest of the system, while the losers remain unconscious. Baars's metaphor is a theatre, with a bright spot on the stage and a dark and largely unconscious audience; the neuroimaging tradition associated with Dehaene and his collaborators ties broadcast to a late and sudden ignition, long-range cortico-cortical synchrony, and a distributed frontoparietal network. The workspace is thus an anatomical structure — a network of prefrontal and parietal regions — and it is precisely because it is one that the theory makes testable predictions about which contents will ignite and which will not.

[3] This is why GWT is standardly read as a theory of access. Everything it explains — the bottleneck, why we can carry out only one demanding task at a time, which contents reach memory and report, why the same stimulus is now seen and now missed — is a matter of a content's availability to the rest of the system. It says nothing about why available contents feel like anything at all; that is the hard problem, and GWT does not so much as address it. What GWT gives you, then, is access consciousness. To extract phenomenal consciousness from it you would need the further claim that availability itself constitutes the feel, and that further claim is exactly what BST supplies.

[4] BST, however, is not really an addition. GWT already holds that to be broadcast is to be conscious; that is simply what the theory says, and BST does no more than spell out what it says. On GWT the Silent Broadcaster is not a possible subject at all: if his visual representations are broadcast, they are conscious, and there is no gap in the theory into which a broadcast but unexperienced representation could be inserted. The case therefore requires no reply. It is excluded by the theory's own identity claim, and a hypothesis that excludes a case is not damaged by that case, whether the case is imagined or actual.

[5] Suppose, nevertheless, that we waive BST and take the case on its own terms. Then it is damaging, and damaging in a way that goes beyond the mild observation that GWT is silent about phenomenal consciousness. The Silent Broadcaster shows that global broadcast and phenomenal consciousness can come apart, and a theory that identifies them is falsified by a single such dissociation. The evidence here is as good as first-personal evidence ever gets: there is no better authority on what it is like to be a subject than that subject's own sincere report, and this subject reports that there is nothing it is like. GWT predicts experience; he reports none; the theory is refuted.

[6] None of this, though, should be taken to impugn GWT's standing as an empirical hypothesis. GWT is a theory of access: its claim concerns which contents are broadcast and what follows from their being broadcast. Phenomenal consciousness is a further topic which GWT never undertook to settle, and if the two properties should turn out to be distinct, the theory can simply be stated as a thesis about the one and leave the other alone. The Silent Broadcaster is thus irrelevant to GWT properly understood. In sum: GWT is untouched by the case; BST, should one want phenomenal consciousness as well, is available at no extra cost; and the access/phenomenal distinction, properly drawn, is what allows us to see all of this.
