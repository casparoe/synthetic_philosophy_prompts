In the technosignature-search community there is a stable, largely unwritten set of norms that I would like reconstructed as an epistemology, because I cannot tell whether they hang together.

Programmes report their results as an excluded volume: a figure for how much of the parameter space — a region of sky, a band of frequencies, a range of transmitter power and duty cycle — has been swept to a stated sensitivity with no detection. They are scrupulous that this figure is not a probability, and they decline to publish any posterior for claims like the galaxy contains no other technological civilization, or such civilizations are rarer than one per million galaxies. Yet the same programmes justify themselves, and raise money, by saying that the silence is puzzling: given the age of the galaxy and how little time a steadily expanding or self-replicating presence would need to fill it, we should have found something by now. Two further asymmetries belong to the same package. A candidate signal must be independently confirmed at a second observatory before anyone may call it a detection, whereas a null result is published at once and needs no confirmation. And deliberate transmission is treated as something requiring collective deliberation and is mostly not done, while listening is treated as requiring none.

What view of evidence does this package presuppose? I am interested in the following, in this order.

First, the excluded-volume figure. Does it do real evidential work, or is it bookkeeping? A null result seems to bear on a hypothesis only insofar as that hypothesis said we should have found something by now, with some specifiable probability; on the familiar multiplicative decomposition of the expected number of detectable civilizations, the silence looks like it constrains only a conjunction of loosely pinned factors and leaves the bare existential claim untouched. Is that a feature of the evidence, or a symptom of hypotheses too vague to be tested? Say what must be added to the hypothesis before the silence carries weight, and whether adding it is legitimate or is just tuning the theory to fit the data.

Second, the refusal to publish posteriors. Does the practice strictly presuppose an error-statistical or severity-based epistemology, on which evidence for a claim requires that the test would probably have failed had the claim been false? Or is it equally well licensed by a broadly Bayesian view with diffuse priors, which would simply say that our searches have covered such a tiny fraction of the search space that the update is negligible? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits: are there rival epistemologies that would generate exactly the same policy, and if so, is the practice underdetermined with respect to all of them?

Third, the apparent inconsistency. If null results carry no evidential weight, in what sense is the silence puzzling? Does the puzzlement presuppose an expectation that the community's own official epistemology forbids it to hold — and is that expectation, once made explicit, defensible or merely a relic of mid-century optimism about interstellar travel? Correspondingly, does the asymmetric treatment of candidates and nulls presuppose that a false positive is more costly than a false negative, and is that asymmetry about reputation, about the science, or about something else?

Fourth, the transmission asymmetry. What does it presuppose that listening is safe and that being detected is a risk? Does it presuppose that another civilization's intentions are likely to be unhelpful or unknowable, and if so, how can the same community hold that while also expecting to learn something from a signal it might receive? Is the asymmetry better explained by a precautionary principle, by the non-excludability of the risk, or by something about who gets to decide?

Finally, what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced. Suppose the community adopted an explicitly Bayesian practice with a stated prior over the factors and published a posterior after each survey: what would it have to report, how would telescope time be reallocated, and what would happen to the search for extraterrestrial intelligence's public case? Conversely, suppose it dropped the puzzlement entirely and treated the searches as pure exploration with no paradox to explain: would anything about the practice become unjustified?

Please answer in American English, in continuous prose, without citations, references, or reading suggestions: reconstruct the implicit epistemology in your own terms and say plainly where you think the practice is coherent and where it is not.
