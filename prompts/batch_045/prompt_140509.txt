I am designing a ninety-minute session for a continuing-education reading group of educated non-specialists — a retired judge, a physician, and a high-school physics teacher, say — who follow artificial intelligence in the news but have no background in computer science or machine learning. The session belongs to a short course on the problem of overseeing systems that eventually come to know more than the people who are supposed to be overseeing them. I have to teach them exactly one idea, and I want a plan for the explanation rather than the explanation itself.

The idea is the central argument for scalable oversight through debate: that a human who could not answer a hard question on her own can nevertheless get at the truth by staging a contest between two systems that both know more than she does, because her judgment is only ever comparative — which side's account hangs together better, once each side has been pressed on the details — because it is costlier to maintain a coherent falsehood under cross-examination than to state the truth, and because the reward structure can be arranged so that each side is trying to expose the other. Call this the 'asymmetric-cost-of-lying' rationale for debate.

Here is what I need from you.

First, the order. What has to be in place before the idea can even be stated — presumably the problem of scalable oversight itself, and the thought that simply asking a system to explain itself is not by itself enough — and in what order the remaining pieces must arrive: the move from answering a question to judging a contest, the comparative character of the judge's verdict, the role of cross-examination, the incentive structure that makes each side want to find the other's weak point, and the residual limits of the mechanism. For each piece, tell me what depends on what, and tell me what breaks in the explanation if two of them are swapped.

Second, the one example that carries the whole session. It must have: an object-level question the judge could never settle on her own and has no cheap way to look up; a false answer that forces the liar to commit to further, apparently checkable details, so that defending the lie really is harder than telling the truth; and a severely limited verification channel — say, a single expensive check the judge may spend once, at the very end — so that the judge's own resources are scarce and the debate has to do the work. It must lack anything that lets style, confidence, or credentials carry the verdict, since the whole point is that the judge is tracking argumentative merit rather than being persuaded, and it must lack any cheap or repeatable way for the judge to verify the object-level claim. Give me the concrete scenario, and tell me why you chose it over a courtroom cross-examination, which is the first analogy this audience will reach for and which I suspect is misleading in specific ways, and over the simpler setup in which one system is merely asked to show its reasoning.

Third, the misconceptions, and where in the sequence to defuse each. They will predictably form some of these: that the judge must secretly know the right answer in order to score the debate at all; that debate works by averaging two opinions, or by letting truth 'win' in some automatic way; that the more fluent or confident debater will simply be more persuasive and will prevail regardless of accuracy; and — the one I most want handled — the failure mode I have started calling 'symmetric collusion'. This is the situation in which both debaters notice that if they converge on a single false but mutually consistent story, the judge will find them equally convincing, will have no comparative signal to go on, and will fall back on a tie-break that does not track the truth, so that colliding is at least as good for each of them as fighting it out. For each misconception, tell me at exactly which point in the sequence you would head it off, what you would say there, and why raising it earlier or later would either confuse the group or leave the misconception intact. In particular, tell me how the sequence should be built so that when symmetric collusion finally appears, it reads as a precise limit on a mechanism the group now genuinely understands, rather than as a reason the whole idea was never going to work.

Fourth, the check. Give me one question I can put to the group at the end whose answer shows they have the mechanism rather than the slogan — something that makes them apply the dependency structure to a variant, such as a variant in which the reward is not zero-sum, or one in which the judge's tie-break is known to be biased toward whichever answer is shorter — and tell me what a genuinely correct answer and a merely superficially correct answer would each sound like.

Fifth, what I should deliberately leave out at this level and say so. I take it that the actual training loop, the game-theoretic machinery, and other scalable-oversight proposals should be off the table; tell me what else you would exclude, how you would mark the exclusion for the group, and what you would say if someone asks about it.

Do not give me a reading list, a bibliography, or citations of any kind; I need the architecture of the session.
