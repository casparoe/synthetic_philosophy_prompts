Hey, I'm trying to get a gut-level grip on Aumann's agreement theorem — specifically which assumption is actually load bearing, not the formal proof (I've seen the information-partition version, I want the intuition for *why* it goes through).

Here's the bit I keep going in circles on. Standard setup: two Bayesians, a common prior, private signals, they announce their posteriors, and once the posteriors are common knowledge they have to be identical. Which gets summarised as something like "people who share a prior can't agree to disagree."

Now tweak it. Common prior over the state, common everything, with one addition: independently for each agent, with probability ε = 0.1 that agent is a "confabulator" — they don't compute anything, they just announce a number drawn uniformly from [0,1], totally independent of the state. Otherwise they're an ordinary Bayesian and announce their honest posterior. Each agent knows whether *they themselves* confabulated; each is unsure about the other. ε, the noise distribution, and the whole structure are common knowledge. Signals are continuous, so the posterior values a sane agent might announce have a genuine density rather than sitting on a finite list.

Concretely: Bob's signal is strongly informative (say his posterior is 0.9); Alice's signal is completely uninformative, she's effectively just at the prior of 0.5. They announce, hear each other, announce again, and so on.

My guess is that this settles into a stable disagreement: Bob never moves, because Alice's number is just a known function of his own number and carries nothing new, while Alice doesn't move all the way to 0.9 because she can't rule out that his number was confabulated — so she trades off the density of "sane Bob announces 0.9" against the uniform noise density and lands somewhere strictly in between. End result: two honest Bayesians, one common prior, all announcements public, and they sit there at different numbers forever.

Is that right, and if so what's the diagnosis? Is "the posteriors are common knowledge" strictly stronger than "both numbers are on the table", and is that gap basically where the whole theorem lives? I'd love the explanation at the level of "here's the quantity being averaged, and here's the cell it's averaged over." Two follow-ups if you have the energy: does the surviving gap shrink continuously to zero as ε → 0, or does something jump discontinuously at ε = 0? And does it get messier if Alice's signal is mildly informative rather than useless — i.e. is Bob's failure to move in response to her number itself evidence that he's a confabulator?
