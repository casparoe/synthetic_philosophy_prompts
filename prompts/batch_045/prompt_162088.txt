I sit on the admissions committee of a professional institute, and we are arguing about replacing our written qualifying examination with a mandatory 400-hour unpaid field apprenticeship. One member has defended the change on explicitly signaling-theoretic grounds. His claim, as he put it, is that the apprenticeship should not teach the candidate anything the clients care about; the uselessness is the point. A peacock's tail is a handicap, he says: it is expensive precisely because it is wasted, and that waste is what makes it an honest advertisement of the bird's quality. So we should design the requirement so that it is a genuine deadweight loss to the candidate, and it will then filter for the quality we actually want.

A second proposal complicates the picture: another member has suggested letting candidates buy out half the hours by paying a fee to the institute. He thinks this is merely more humane and changes nothing about the filtering. I think the two proposals screen for quite different things, but I want the argument laid out cleanly before the next meeting rather than asserted.

Please audit the peacock analogy as it is being used here. Specifically:

Give the correspondence explicitly—a table is fine—saying what plays the role of the peahen, of the tail, of the fitness cost, of the underlying quality, and of the mate's response, in both the biological case and our case.

Then identify which features of the biological setup are doing the real work. I have in mind, among others: that the cost must fall differentially on different candidate types and in the right direction, and not merely be large; that the cost is a deadweight loss rather than a transfer to anyone; that the display cannot be borrowed or faked for a fee; that the receiver's preference and the signaler's display are parts of one equilibrium, no one sets the tariff; and that the ornament's expression is condition-dependent, which makes it partly an index of the trait rather than a pure handicap at all. Tell me which of these are necessary for signal honesty and which are incidental, and whether the handicap principle in its home domain really requires waste rather than merely differential cost.

Then run the same list against our case and say where the mapping breaks and how badly: what our requirement would actually screen on if adopted; whether the fee buy-out is a variant of the same mechanism or a different one with a different name; what it imports that we lack, given that the committee both sets the size of the cost and is the party reading the signal, and that restricting entry benefits the incumbents; and whether the receiver here has any incentive to read the signal accurately.

Finally, say whether the conclusion the analogy was brought in to support—that waste is a feature rather than a bug, and that a useless requirement is therefore a better filter than a useful one—survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if it does not, whether some other device (a work sample, a forfeitable bond, an audition, a tournament) is the thing my colleague is really reaching for.
