I draft legislation for a living, in a jurisdiction with no citizen-initiated lawmaking, and I have been reading about direct democracy on the side. There is a two-part pattern I keep running into, and I want to know whether the literature has a name for it as a single phenomenon, or whether I should just coin one.

The first part is about what reaches the ballot. In Switzerland, opponents of an adopted federal statute can force a popular vote by submitting 50,000 signatures; in California, qualifying an initiative statute takes signatures equal to 5 per cent of the votes cast in the last gubernatorial election (8 per cent for a constitutional amendment), collected within a 180-day window, which in practice means paying gatherers. So the filter that selects questions for the ballot is organisational capacity, not political salience. It also seems non-neutral about content: a binary question on one symbolically charged dimension survives translation into a vote; a package of committee-traded compromises, the kind of thing a legislature is actually good at producing, does not.

The second part is anticipatory. Where a group with demonstrated signature capacity opposes a bill, the bill appears to get weakened in committee, or the operative detail is pushed down into rules the voters cannot reach, or the legislature declines to legislate and leaves the field to an agency or a court. No vote is ever held; the outcome is produced by the possibility of one. And the anticipation seems to track credibility rather than numbers: a small, concentrated, well-financed interest can cast a shadow over an entire policy area, while a large and diffuse grievance casts none.

What I want named is the two parts together, plus the dependence of the second on the first: the threat only bites where someone could actually qualify a measure, so the skew in what gets voted on and the distortion in what gets legislated are the same mechanism seen from two ends.

Nearby things I have run across feel too broad: talk of lawmaking done in the shadow of some other institution, and general claims that officials anticipate the reactions of those who can sanction them. The first seems to be about courts or hierarchy rather than a popular vote; the second covers any anticipation, including of a plain election, and so does not distinguish a system with a signature trigger from one without.

So: does this combination have an established concept? If several partial concepts each cover a piece, say which covers which and exactly where each falls short. If none covers it, propose a term with a definition tight enough to settle these cases:

1. A genuinely mandatory referendum, where no one has to threaten anything and the vote is automatic.
2. A proactive citizen initiative, where the sponsor wants the vote and the legislature responds by passing a watered-down version to take the wind out of it the same phenomenon or its mirror image?
3. A bluff: a group with no real capacity that is believed to have it. Should the concept be indexed to actual or to perceived capacity?
4. The case where the trigger loses control of the outcome a vote called for intra-party reasons, whose result the caller neither wanted nor could specify. Does the phenomenon presuppose that the trigger's preferred outcome is the likely one?

Two further things I care about more than the label. First, how this differs from ordinary interest-group influence in a purely representative system, given that groups there also get bills weakened what does the formalised, published threshold-and-deadline trigger add? Second, what empirical signature would distinguish this from a legislature simply moderating bills that are publicly unpopular: what observable difference follows from capacity rather than from opinion?

Please argue from the structure of the thing. I have no access to a library and cannot chase up references, so a reading list or citations is useless to me; a bare name is not much better. I want the carving: what exactly this is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
