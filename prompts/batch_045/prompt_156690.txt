You are a philosopher of biology whose research specialism is the theory of biological function. You are equally fluent in the selected-effects (etiological) tradition and in causal-role (systemic) analyses, and you have spent real time on the cases where the two come apart. Please think carefully before writing: first work through each of the three cases below separately on each account, note explicitly where your initial verdict feels unstable or where you are tempted to say "it depends," and only then draft your answer. If you think either formulation below is a strawman, say so in a single sentence and substitute your own.

For definiteness, use these formulations (they are meant to be the strongest simple versions of each view, not easy targets):

- Etiological / selected-effects: a trait token X has the function F just in case F is (roughly) the effect for which ancestors of X were selected. Functions are thus historical properties of traits, and a trait can retain a function it can no longer perform.
- Causal-role / systemic: X has the function F just in case X's performing F contributes to some capacity C of a containing system S whose exercise we are currently trying to explain. Functions are thus contemporaneous dispositions, and they are relative to a chosen system and capacity.

The cases:

Case A (no history at all). A synthetic-biology group assembles a bacterial cell whose genome contains a de novo designed gene g; no homolog of g has ever existed in any organism, and its product performs a step in cell-wall assembly that no ancestral mechanism ever performed. Knockouts lyse immediately, so from the moment the strain is founded, g is under intense purifying selection in the chemostat where it is grown. Does g have a function in the founding cell, before it has ever been reproduced? After one generation? After ten thousand? If the etiological account returns "no function at t = 0," is that a reductio of the account, or its correct and interesting prediction about first tokens?

Case B (orphaned effect). A trait T in a wild population has been shaped by selection for effect E over many thousands of generations. An anthropogenic change — say the extinction of the only predator from whom T concealed its bearer — makes it the case that T can no longer produce E, because the relevant receivers are gone permanently. Bearers still develop T, and T still produces effect F, an incidental effect that was always present but never selected. T is now nearly neutral and drifting; in one subpopulation, F happens to be mildly beneficial, and T is beginning to be maintained by selection for F. Is E still T's function? Is F? Both? Neither? Does the verdict change for that subpopulation? And what should be said about the drift phase, when nothing is selecting for either effect?

Case C (which system?). A bacterium secretes a siderophore that scavenges iron. Secreting costs the individual cell roughly 15% of its growth rate, and the iron-bearing complexes are taken up by any nearby cell, so the trait is costly within groups and beneficial to the local group. Groups with more secretors outcompete groups with fewer, so the trait is maintained by between-group selection despite within-group selection against it. The causal-role account asks what the trait contributes to a capacity of a containing system, but here two candidate systems — the secreting cell and the local population — yield opposite verdicts: the trait subserves iron acquisition by the colony while undercutting a capacity of the cell that bears it. Is that relativity to a chosen system a virtue (functions are posits internal to an explanatory project) or a defect (the account can say only what the trait does for whichever system we pick, never what it is for)? And does the etiological account do better here, or does it merely relocate the difficulty onto the question of which level selection acted at and how we could tell?

Two further things I want addressed:

(i) A colleague has floated a "smoothing" proposal meant to stop the accounts from giving discontinuous verdicts around cases like A and B: instead of a sharp cutoff at the first selection event, attribute functions by a weighted profile of the causal roles the trait's token-lineage has actually occupied, with recent generations weighted more heavily, so that a function attribution strengthens and fades gradually rather than switching on all at once. Does this dissolve the dispute, merely relocate it (onto the choice of weighting and the individuation of token-lineages), or collapse into one of the two accounts under another description?

(ii) A second colleague argues that only the etiological account can underwrite malfunction: a heart that cannot pump is malfunctioning because pumping is what hearts were selected to do, whereas on the causal-role view a non-pumping item simply lacks the role and there is nothing for it to fail at. Is that right, and if it is, is it decisive — or can the causal-role theorist reconstruct malfunction by relativizing to the capacities of a reference class of systems?
