I chair the clinical ethics consultation committee at a 400-bed community hospital, and I have been asked to write down, once and for all, how we actually reach a recommendation. The constraints matter: we meet weekly, we have roughly forty minutes per consultation, our written recommendation must be signed by at least three of the five members and countersigned by the attending physician, it is read by families and occasionally by hospital counsel, and although it is formally advisory it is in practice almost always followed. The five of us do not share a moral outlook and are not going to. One member reasons in terms of side-constraints and the distinction between ordinary and extraordinary means; one is a straightforward consequentialist who thinks in expected QALYs; one works in a care-ethics register where the unit of analysis is the relationship and the family rather than the patient; one is a lawyer who mostly asks what is defensible after the fact; I am on the committee only because I took two philosophy courses and got assigned the paperwork. So I do not want a resolution of our disagreement. I want a procedure for aggregating across moral theories that none of us fully accepts, in a form that five non-specialists can run in forty minutes in a room with a family waiting, and that is honest about which of its steps are arithmetic and which are value judgments in a lab coat.

We tried the obvious thing already and it broke in three specific ways, which is why I am writing to you rather than just using it. We each named the theories we give weight to, assigned credences, had each theory score the available options on a 0–100 scale, and took the weighted average. First, it recommended options that no theory on our list endorses — in one case, continue the ventilator but stop the pressors, which the consequentialist regards as pointless cruelty and the deontologist regards as impermissible once you have granted that stopping is killing; the average liked it only because it was midway. Second, the numbers were not comparable: a 70 from the consequentialist is most of the way to the best available outcome, while a 70 from a constraints-based theory is a flat prohibition with no room to trade, and we had no defensible way to normalize the two onto one scale or to say where each scale's zero and ceiling sit. Third, when one member assigned a small credence to a theory on which a certain action is catastrophic in a way that dwarfs everything else, that theory swallowed the calculation entirely and the other ninety-odd percent stopped mattering. I want your procedure to have something to say about each of these three failure modes — options no theory endorses, intertheoretic comparisons of how much a theory cares, and stakes that are unbounded or lexicographically dominant — and also about two further wrinkles: whose credences go in (the five of us averaged, versus the patient's own values if we know them, versus some outside moral expert), and what to do when the theories on our list disagree about how to aggregate under moral uncertainty in the first place.

So please give me the decision procedure itself: the questions in order, what counts as an adequate answer to each at the level of rigor a non-philosopher can actually produce in a few minutes, the points at which the procedure halts with a no, and the points at which it must stop and hand the decision to a human being because the question is one of values rather than facts. Then run it on three cases from our last year. (1) A daughter, acting as surrogate, insists that everything continue for her father three weeks after a catastrophic brain injury that the treating team regards as incompatible with any meaningful recovery; the team wants to withdraw life-sustaining treatment and the two of us who think in constraints disagree about whether this is a case of ceasing futile treatment or of killing. (2) One ICU bed remains during a respiratory surge: the first patient arrived earlier and is far sicker, with roughly a 25 percent chance of survival, the second arrived later with roughly a 70 percent chance, and our members split between sickest-first and most-lives-saved reasoning. (3) The withdrawal case again, but now one member reports a 3 percent credence in a view, shared by the family's religious tradition, on which the patient may still have a conscious inner life and withdrawing nutrition and hydration is the killing of an innocent person with a disvalue so large it cannot be traded off; on our old method this fixes the answer at never withdraw, and does so for any nonzero credence, which no one on the committee is willing to sign. Case 3 is the one where I expect to dislike the answer, and I would rather have the procedure tell me something I hate than have it quietly rigged to avoid that. Please also say plainly what the procedure cannot do, and give me a short format for recording the reasoning so that a future committee, or a lawyer, can audit what we actually did.
