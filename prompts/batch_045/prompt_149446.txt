I'm writing the take-home final for an upper-level seminar I'm TAing this term — cross-listed between philosophy and economics, nominally on rationality, decision, and the mind — and I want one essay question on satisficing and bounded rationality that I can set for 20 marks and expect a strong student to answer in about 90 minutes.

The problem is that the material invites recitation. Everyone will arrive knowing the definitional package: satisficing as stopping at an aspiration level rather than surveying everything and maximizing, bounded rationality as rational choice under limits on time, information, and computation, the usual job-search or house-hunt illustrations. An answer that restates those definitions a bit more elegantly should not clear a B. What I want the question to force students into is an argument I've been having, inconclusively, for months with a friend who works in operations research and reads the same blogs I do. His line is that satisficing is not really a rival to maximizing at all: once search is costly, a threshold stopping rule is just what expected-utility maximization prescribes, so bounded rationality collapses into entirely ordinary rationality applied one level up, and whatever is interesting in the view reduces to a contingent empirical claim about how large the costs happen to be in the cases we care about. My reply has been something like a threshold-setting regress: an aspiration level is only sensible relative to the distribution of options you expect to encounter, and acquiring that distribution looks uncomfortably like the exhaustive search satisficing was supposed to spare the agent, so either the satisficer gets a well-tuned threshold handed to it from outside, or the threshold has to be adapted endogenously as the agent succeeds and fails — at which point the thing being assessed is a dynamical process rather than a decision rule, and it stops being clear what the benchmark is. We keep failing to settle it, partly because we keep sliding between evaluating the mechanism (what gets computed, how much of it) and evaluating the outcome (how well the agent does), and between asking whether satisficing is *justified* and asking whether it is *rational*. A question built around that slide is, I think, where the real difficulty lives.

So please write me the question, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme. The question should be self-contained: it must give students enough setup that they never need to consult the primary sources, and I'd rather it not reward exegesis of what the historical figures in the literature actually held. The marking scheme should name five must-hit points, with marks for each out of 20, and for each point a one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong — for instance by treating bounded rationality as merely a psychological claim about what people can do, or by thinking that if satisficing is derivable as optimal under search costs then it says nothing at all. I also want a sentence or two on what separates an A from a B answer; my guess is that a B can state the regress while an A can say what follows from it about the status of the view, and can say what would have to be true of an environment for a cheap stopping rule to do well or to fail badly, but I'd like to see what you think the discriminator should be. Finally, when you hand back the model answer, tell me how confident you are in each substantive claim it makes, flag any claim that is really an attribution to a figure in the literature rather than a point about the structure of the problem, single out the one claim you are least confident about, and give me your probability that the question as written is genuinely fair and answerable inside ninety minutes.
