One of my PhD students slid this under my door and I've been chewing on it for a week without finding a clean exit. Context, so you know where I'm standing: I'm about as anti-Humean about reasons as they come — the fact that the bus is cheaper is a reason for you to take the bus whether or not you care about saving money, and whether or not you'd ever notice. But I've also always wanted to insist that reasons are essentially *for* somebody: the reason relation has a subject slot in it, and a reason with nothing in the slot sounds to me like a debt with no debtor. Here's her argument, tidied up a bit and with my own numbering:

(1) Whether C is a reason for A to φ isn't constituted by any fact about A's desires, aims, plans, or other motivational states.

(2) It isn't constituted by A's beliefs either: that the bridge is rotten is a reason for you not to drive over it even if you've inspected it yourself and reasonably believe it's sound.

(3) So nothing on A's side of the relation does any constituting work. The subject term is just a free slot that the relation happens to have; everything that makes it the case that C is a reason comes from C.

(4) If the subject slot does no constituting work, then whether it's filled at all is irrelevant to whether there is a reason. A consideration can favour something — count in favour of it — without being a reason *for* anyone.

(5) So there can be free-floating reasons. In a universe that never contains a single agent — nothing that can act, deliberate, or respond to anything — there could still be reasons: reason for the world to go one way rather than another, reasons that aren't reasons for anyone or anything, that explain nothing and move nothing. And by the same token, even in our own case, the reasons we have are the reasons they are independently of us; that they're addressed to us is a mailing detail rather than part of what they are.

(6) I don't think there is any such thing. There being reason for the world to be different, with nobody it's a reason for, isn't a reason — it's value with a verb stuck on it. And I don't love the bit about us either, because I think being addressed to a agent is part of what a reason is.

(7) So (1) is false and some broadly Humean, response-dependent story about reasons is true after all — which is the one thing I am least willing to believe.

Every step looks fine to me individually and the conclusion is the one I've spent fifteen years arguing against, so I know something has gone wrong and I cant say what. If I had to point, I'd point at the middle of the argument, but I've got two competing hunches and I can't make either do the work I need. One is that (2) is where she cheats, because beliefs might be relevant to reasons in a way desires aren't — something like the gap between what there's most reason to do and what you have most reason to do given your evidence. The other is that (5) and (6) aren't even about the same thing, so that (7) never gets off the ground. Both feel like fudges when I try to write them up.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I'd rather give up almost anything else in the list than (1) or (6), so tell me what I'm paying to keep those two, and price the alternatives as well — including, if it turns out cheaper, the option where reasons aren't the basic normative notion at all and I keep (1) by taking value or fittingness as primitive instead. If the trouble is an equivocation or a shift in scope between two steps, show me the two readings and which one each premise needs, in the wording I'd actually use.
