I host a long-form interview series on arguments from the history of political thought that are now more often cited than read. The next episode is a single-topic conversation about Rousseau's case against the theater: the argument of the letter he wrote in reply to d'Alembert's article on Geneva in the *Encyclopédie*, where the proposal was that Geneva build a playhouse. Assume, as a framing device, that Rousseau himself is the guest. He is at home in the examples he actually used — Geneva, Paris, Sparta, Rome, the pulpit, the bar, Molière and Racine — and he knows nothing later than the printing press; no question may invoke broadcasting, recording, or any later technology.

The preamble I will read for listeners compresses his case into five claims, and your questions should keyed to these:

1. A theater is a barometer in one direction and a cause in the other: where a people's mores are already corrupt, plays merely keep them occupied; where the mores are sound, plays would introduce the corruption. This is why he can leave the Paris theaters alone while opposing one in Geneva.
2. Drama does not reform. Tragedy arouses the very passions it is said to purge; comedy is supposed to correct vice by exposing it to ridicule, but the ridicule always lands somewhere other than on the spectator, and in the case he dwells on — the *Misanthrope* — it lands on the one sincere man on stage, so the audience is tutored in laughing at the very virtue it is said to be taught.
3. Tears shed at imaginary misfortune are a bargain: they purchase the sense of a good disposition at the cost of nothing, and leave the spectator as they found him.
4. The actor's trade is the trade of appearing to be someone other than oneself and of being paid for that appearance; a republic that honors this trade teaches its citizens to prize the counterfeit above the real. (He will insist that the dishonor attaches to the trade rather than to the individuals who practice it.)
5. The alternative is not austerity but other pleasures: open-air civic festivals, dances for the married, military games and public contests, in which the same people are at once performers and spectators.

I want ten to twelve questions, in a deliberate running order, with these requirements:

- No question asks him to restate the case; each latches onto a specific joint in one of the five claims, and you label which.
- The sequence should progressively close off his most reliable escape, which is to redescribe any corruption as a symptom of a city that was already corrupt. I want three questions, spaced through the hour, that force him to say what a playhouse would do *in Geneva* — a city he concedes is not yet corrupt — so that the escape is unavailable by the last of them.
- At least one question on each of the following, with the follow-up keyed to the reply you expect him to give: (a) the asymmetry in claim 1 — what makes the same institution inert in Paris and active in Geneva, and whether "the mores produce the amusements and the amusements confirm the mores" is an explanation or only a way of making every outcome consistent with the theory; (b) claim 4, pressed with the advocate, the preacher and the diplomat, who also trade in manufactured emotion and are honored for it; (c) claim 3, reformulated as my own proposal, which you should put to him in his vocabulary: that the damage of drama is not to belief, since no one leaves the playhouse holding a new proposition, but to the calibration of response — drama drills us in reacting to cues engineered so that feeling is easy and action unnecessary, until responsiveness migrates from real need to the well-designed cue. Ask whether this rescues the argument, and whether he accepts what it apparently commits him to: that the harm is cumulative, a function of how often one attends, and that private reading is worse than public spectacle; (d) claim 5 — who writes the songs, stages the pageants and chooses the symbols, by what taste, and how that taste is to be trusted; and, if the answer is the legislator or a council of elders, why a magistrate's pageant is not the playwright's illusion under another name, given that both work on the imagination rather than the understanding; (e) the economics — he objects that a theater taxes the poor to amuse the rich, so ask whether his objection is really to the funding rather than to the stage, whether a free, publicly funded theater with amateur citizen performers and plays written by the city would be immune to it, and what he would say if Geneva offered him exactly that.
- Open with one cold-open question that puts him on the spot in a single sentence, with no throat-clearing.
- Close with a question about what would make him give the case up: what he would have to observe in a city that had kept a theater for three generations, and how he can specify that observation in advance without making it unfalsifiable — since any corruption he found there could be blamed on other causes, and any decency he found could be credited to the theater's having distracted the people from worse.

Format each item as: the claim it targets; the question as I should speak it aloud, in at most sixty words; one line on the reply you expect; one follow-up, in at most forty words. Keep the vocabulary of the question itself free of later jargon — no "social conditioning," no "virtue signaling," nothing he could not parse — even where the thinking behind it is contemporary.
