I am a novelist, and I need a document that will appear more or less verbatim in a chapter I am drafting, so it has to read like a genuine leaked paper rather than like prose winking at the reader.

Please work as follows. Begin with a brief brainstorming pass, in your capacity as a philosopher of collective memory and political myth, on what actually makes a defeat serviceable as national moral capital: why a total, misfortune-shaped loss is more usable than a partial or culpable one, why the manner of the enemy's winning matters, how commemoration quietly edits out inconvenient detail, and what a nation is supposed to do if it has no adequate loss of its own. Only then write the piece.

The piece itself: an internal consultation memorandum of roughly 600–700 words, in British English, in a deadpan civil-service register, headed with a document reference, a date, and a distribution list, issued by the fictional Bureau of Commemorative Settlement — the body in my novel charged with seeing that every member state is in possession of an adequately usable defeat. Its proposal, advanced in perfect earnest, is a Loss Substitution Scheme: states lacking a suitably edifying catastrophe, whether because they won, because what they lost is embarrassing, or because their defeat was somebody's fault in particular, may apply to lease another nation's lost cause on a renewable basis together with a re-narration package, and will be scored against a Usability Audit whose criteria include the completeness of the loss, the moral tidiness of the losers, the unflatteringness of the winners, and convenience of the anniversary.

The comedy must come entirely from the mechanics. The memo should be recognisably working with the real grammar by which lost causes are manufactured and maintained — the sort of thing Masada, Dunkirk and the Lost Cause are the standard examples of — exaggerating that grammar rather than inventing a fresh absurdity, and never naming or explaining the joke. It should stay legible to a general reader while making someone who studies nationalist myth concede that the satire is fair. My fictional states and their defeats are yours to invent; please do not attach invented statistics to real countries. Write throughout in British English.
