ok so I keep coming back to the UC Berkeley grad admissions thing from 1973 and I think it's a much better stress test for Simpson's paradox than the tidy textbook versions, but I can't get the mapping straight in my head.

Numbers as I remember them — correct me if I've got them wrong, but argue from the structure either way: about 12,700 applicants for fall 1973, men admitted at roughly 44%, women at roughly 35%, gap way too big to be chance. Break it down by department and it mostly evaporates: in the six largest departments women do about as well as men in most of them (82% vs 62% in one, 68% vs 63% in another, and so on), and the story is that women were bunching into the departments with low admission rates — English rather than engineering — even among qualified applicants. The department-adjusted pooled comparison apparently comes out with a small but statistically significant bias in favor of women. I also half-remember something like 4 of the 85 departments being significantly biased against women and 6 significantly biased the other way, which is both the bit I'm least sure about and the bit that seems to matter most.

What I want is not an explanation of Simpson's paradox and please God not the kidney stone one. I want the case mapped onto it carefully and then taken apart:

1. The three slots are obvious — sex, admission, department — but is department actually playing the role the paradox needs it to play? My hypothesis, argue with it: department is not a confounder here but a mediator. Which department you apply to is plausibly itself downstream of being a woman, given advising, fieldwork climates, who gets encouraged into what, so conditioning on it doesn't remove the bias, it just books the bias under a different line item. Call it the laundering test: if the variable you adjust for is plausibly an effect of the variable whose influence you're testing, the adjustment isn't neutral. Is that the right test, does it actually bite on Berkeley, or am I smuggling in a causal story that 1973 admissions data can't support?

2. Second test I've been kicking around: a partition is only admissible if it answers to something an agent could actually choose between. An applicant really does pick a department, so "men do better overall, women do better within every department" is actionable for an applicant, and it smells like a sure-thing-principle setup — whichever department you were going to apply to, applying as a woman is not worse. But the university is not an applicant; it doesn't choose the mix of applications. So the same table says "no bias" to the admissions-committee question and something quite different to the "is this institution producing this outcome" question. Does the paradox have any resources for telling those two questions apart, or does it just emit one number per question and leave you to notice that you were asking two?

3. The uncomfortable version: if you get to choose the partition after seeing the outcomes, can you more or less always find one that flips the sign? I have a feeling some version of that is true but I don't know how strong, and it matters, because if it's strong then "we broke it down and the gap went away" is a move available to anyone who dislikes an aggregate. So what licenses a partition — causal structure, convention, the stakes, who's asking, something else?

Then run it the other direction: what does Berkeley do to Simpson's paradox as a tool? My read is that the episode shows the paradox is a symptom that the aggregate question was ill-posed rather than a discovery about the world, and that all the real work is in the part the paradox is silent about — why women applied where they did, whether the residual department-level pattern means anything, and what Berkeley should have done in 1974 if the aggregate table was literally all it had. Tell me if that undersells it: is there something the paradox framework genuinely predicts or explains here that you'd have gotten wrong without it? And where it's genuinely silent, say so instead of forcing it to speak.
