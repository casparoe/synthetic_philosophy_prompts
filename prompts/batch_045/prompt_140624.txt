I convene a small closed-door working group that a philanthropic foundation runs twice a year to broker what it calls pluralist cooperation agreements: two organizations that disagree about what is worth doing each take on part of the other's agenda, conditional on the other doing the same, so that both end up further along by their own lights than if each had spent everything on its own priorities. The people who walk into these meetings divide fairly reliably into two camps, and after the last three sessions I've concluded that they are arguing past each other rather than with each other, so I want to fix that at the next one. I'm asking you to draft the document I should have commissioned at the start: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between the Cooperationists and the Non-Alienationists. The Cooperationists hold, roughly, that where agents disagree about value and each faces opportunities that are cheap for them and dear for the other, refusing to trade is often straightforward moral waste, and that the new reasons an agreement generates (promissory, reciprocal, evidential) are genuine reasons that enter deliberation rather than considerations that override it from outside. The Non-Alienationists hold, roughly, that a first-order moral conviction is not a holding to be liquidated: performing an action you judge wrong in order to get someone else to perform one you judge right is either unintelligible (you cannot act against your all-things-considered judgment) or corrupting, because the dispositions that make someone a reliable judge and agent are degraded by being used as currency — and they add that the advertised gains from trade are systematically overstated. Both camps are sophisticated and neither should be played as a strawman; where a formulation would only be signed by a careless version of one side, say so and repair it.

Four cases came up repeatedly last time, and I'd like them used or improved. First, the non-convexity case: one party favors a vaccination drive whose payoff is threshold-shaped (coverage below roughly the transmission threshold buys almost nothing) while the other favors surgical camps with large fixed costs, and an even split leaves both below their thresholds when either side going all-in would have cleared one of them — I take this to raise a question about whether moral trade can leave both parties worse off than no trade. Second, the revealed-conviction case: an intermediary matches anonymous partners, and the Cooperationists worry that an appetite for trade signals weak conviction or a discounting of the counterparty's values, so that apparent gains from trade are partly an artifact of adverse selection; the Non-Alienationists read the same fact as showing that trade selects for and cultivates low-conviction partners. Third, the erosion case: a food-system reform coalition and a faith-based relief agency have swapped support on each other's campaigns for a decade, and the coalition's own members now seem markedly less moved by the consideration that originally organized them, with recruitment and retention shifting accordingly. Fourth, the bystander case: two large funders trade over a shared pool in a way that predictably shifts costs onto a constituency that rejects both of their value systems. I'd also flag three cruxes I suspect matter more than the participants admit: whether a trade requires anything like interpersonal commensurability or only cheap-for-you-and-dear-for-me comparisons; whether the reason generated by an agreement is a reason or a price; and how confident either side can really be about the other's value function, since a systematically biased estimate would undermine the practice from the Cooperationist side alone.

What I want is a document both camps could put their names to. Section 1: eight to twelve numbered agreed claims, each substantive enough that an outsider learns something from it — nothing on the order of both sides want to do good; I want agreement about what the practice is, about when the disputed moves even arise, about what the cases show, and about what would count against each side. Section 2: a table of four to six cruxes, with columns for the crux stated neutrally, the Cooperationist formulation (worded so a Cooperationist would sign it), the Non-Alienationist formulation (likewise), one concrete case or argument both sides would accept as bearing on that crux, the Cooperationist prediction about what it will show, and the Non-Alienationist prediction written down in advance. Section 3, short: which of their disagreements you judge to be verbal or merely stipulative — about trade, about acting against one's judgment, about whether integrity is a value or a side constraint — and which are genuine, plus anything you think both sides would accept as actually settling one. Please note anywhere you had to invent a distinction to make a formulation signable, and feel free to tighten my four cases so that each side would accept the description; if you think one of my proposed cruxes is confused, say why rather than dutifully including it. No citations, no bibliography, no named authorities invented or real: I want the argument, not the literature. Aim for roughly 900 to 1,300 words, in register for a document that will be projected on a screen and edited live by people who dislike each other's phrasing.
