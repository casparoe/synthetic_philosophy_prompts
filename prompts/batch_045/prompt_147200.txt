I have been reading about intelligence on my own for a couple of years now — no training in philosophy or psychology, just books and long argument threads — and I keep losing the same arguments because everyone in them is quietly using a different standard for what would even count as an answer. So before I read any more definitions of intelligence, I want the specification: what an adequate account of intelligence has to deliver.

The purpose I want it good enough for is three disputes I actually care about. First, whether the chatbot I use daily is intelligent in any sense at all, or whether calling it that is only a way of speaking. Second, whether "a machine much smarter than us" is more than a slogan. Third, whether I am being inconsistent when I say my dog has some intelligence and the spreadsheet that balances my accounts has none.

Here is my tentative list, each with the case that makes me think it is a requirement.

It must separate intelligence from stored knowledge. Two systems give identical answers to every question I can ask in some domain; one looked them up in an index it was handed, the other worked them out. If a theory cannot tell these apart, it is not a theory of intelligence.

It must separate intelligence from speed and from brute resources. Take a program that wins by trying every possibility, and then the same program slowed by a factor of a million: I want to say it is not less intelligent, only slower. But I cannot say that in full generality, because slow it down enough and it never answers at all. So I propose a fixed-budget rule — comparisons of how much intelligence something has must hold time, memory and hardware fixed — and I want to know whether that rule is workable or whether it quietly makes every comparison between very different kinds of system undefined.

It must handle jagged systems: ones that beat the best humans at one narrow game and fail at things a child does. Does intelligence admit a single ordering, or is it a profile? If it is a profile, what becomes of the "smarter than us" claim?

It must say when two problems are the same problem. Someone solves a puzzle stated in words and cannot solve the same puzzle drawn as a diagram; a system solves a task in one setting and collapses when the surface details change but the structure does not. Without something here, "able to handle new problems" is empty.

It must handle collections. An ant colony, a market, or an invented case I keep returning to: a thousand slow reasoners wired together so that their combined verdict arrives in a second, where no single one of them does anything I would call intelligent. Is the whole intelligent, or is this a different thing we honour with the same word?

It must handle damaged or unusual hardware: a person who can no longer form new memories but still reasons well; a savant who produces answers he cannot explain; someone whose body cannot produce the answers a test would accept. Whatever intelligence is, it should not be taken away by a broken output channel.

It must be able to tell me I was wrong about who has it. If the tests we build are validated against the people we already call intelligent, the theory just returns my own judgements and settles nothing about the dog or the spreadsheet. Is that a genuine requirement, or am I asking for magic?

It must keep intelligence distinct from the things we confuse it with — knowledge, speed, wisdom, character, being useful — and must distinguish having some from having a lot, the way a mouse and a rock differ.

What I want from you: sort these into genuine desiderata and mere prejudices of one camp, given the purpose I stated; restate the genuine ones more sharply than I have; add the two or three requirements a specialist would insist I have missed, with the case behind each; and mark which are non-negotiable and which I could drop if dropping one buys me the rest. I have three suspicions I want tested rather than flattered: that the jaggedness requirement and the "smarter than us" claim are already in conflict; that the same-problem requirement is just the stored-knowledge requirement in disguise and can be deleted; and that the damaged-hardware requirement is sentimental and will do no real work. Flag any subset of the final list that cannot be jointly satisfied — that is the part I most want — and name the single requirement that excludes the largest number of the usual kinds of answer. Then, in a few lines each and no more, score the standard families: the does-well-on-our-tests account, the general-problem-solving-across-many-environments account, the single-number psychometric account, and the biological or neural account. I do not know the literature well enough to name them properly, so use whatever the real families are.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the one thing about this list that I most need to hear — then give me the reasoning. No preamble, and do not restate my question back to me.
