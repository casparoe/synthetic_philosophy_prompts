I do machine learning, mostly Bayesian experimental design — active learning, batch acquisition, mutual-information style acquisition functions, the usual — and I somehow ended up on a hospital committee that decides whether to offer a new polygenic risk score to asymptomatic patients, where a decent chunk of people say they'd rather not know. Roughly where I've landed: (1) Good's theorem — for an expected-utility maximizer a free observation never has negative expected value, and it's strictly positive exactly when the observation could change what you do. The thing that actually convinces me there is the you-can-always-ignore-it argument: the no-information plan is still in the strategy space, so conditioning can't cost you anything. (2) Blackwell: if one experiment refines another it's weakly better, but informativeness is only a partial order, so a very detailed signal about variables you can't act on can easily be worth less than a coarse one about the variables you can. I don't think that contradicts (1). (3) I don't think rationality requires expected-utility maximization: ambiguity aversion understood as maxmin over a set of priors is fine by me, and I also don't think a rational agent has to be able to bind their future self to a plan fixed in advance. (4) In my own work I use acquisition functions that score candidate experiments by how much they shrink posterior uncertainty and that never look at the decision the data will feed, and I've more or less stopped feeling guilty about that. (5) I think patients who decline the risk score are making a mistake, and my argument for saying so is basically (1). (6) Seperately, I think preferences about the evidence itself are perfectly respectable: caring about suspense or anticipation, not wanting to have seen something, being glad you never looked. I don't treat those as a defect in the agent. (7) I'd also like to lean on (1) for something like a general epistemic norm — gather more evidence, and don't leave evidence you already have sitting unused. (8) And I keep coming back to what I call observation footprints: cases where running the experiment at all is visible to someone whose reaction then changes your payoff — a regulator's data request tipping off the market, a sequencing run telling a competitor what my lab switched to last month — and I've been treating those as genuine counterexamples to (1) rather than as cases where the information simply wasn't free.
