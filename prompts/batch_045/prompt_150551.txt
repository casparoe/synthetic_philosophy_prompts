I go to a small reading group that does one book a month, and this month's was Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling — the 1843 one published under the name Johannes de silentio, about Abraham and the binding of Isaac. Two members, Marco and Nadia, got into a proper argument that ran about half an hour past finishing time, and since I'm the one who picked the book they've both asked me to referee it. I honestly cannt tell who's ahead, so I'm handing it to you. I don't want a tour of the secondary literature and I don't want citations — I've already read a pile of summaries and none of them settled anything. Here's more or less how the exchange went.

Marco opened by saying Abraham isn't a hero of faith at all, just a would-be murderer who got lucky: if the angel hadn't stopped him he would have killed his son, and no private revelation turns that into something admirable, and the moment 'God told me to' is allowed as a justification then every atrocity is justified in advance, since absolutely anyone can say it. (2) Nadia said that is precisely the thing the book is denying — the first of the three problems it sets itself is whether there is a teleological suspension of the ethical, whether the single individual's absolute duty to God can outrank the universal — and of course anyone can claim it, that's the price of faith being inward and unmediable, not a refutation of it. (3) Marco said that if it's inward and unmediable then it can't do any normative work for anyone else: either Abraham can't explain himself, in which case there's nothing to learn from him and no way to tell him apart from a man with a tumour, or he does explain himself, and then he's appealing to something public after all — and he accused the book of wanting both at once. (4) Nadia said no, there is a discriminator, and it isn't the content of the command but the shape of the movement: the knight of infinite resignation gives up Isaac, makes the movement, and is at peace with the loss, and anybody can understand that, whereas Abraham makes that movement and then a second one, believing he will receive Isaac back in this life by virtue of the absurd, and climbs the mountain rejoicing rather than resigned. A fanatic, she said, doesn't expect the son back, and a tragic hero like Agamemnon or Jephthah or Brutus has a public purpose you can weigh and publicly mourn; Abraham has neither. (5) Marco said that phrase is just a flattering name for having no grounds, and since the whole thing is inward the man calmly resigned to killing his son and the man expecting him back are indistinguishable from outside, so the criterion is unfalsifiable by construction, i.e. decorative. (6) Nadia said that pushes you onto the third problem, which she thinks is the one the book is really anxious about: whether it was ethically defensible for Abraham to conceal what he was doing from Sarah, Eliezer and Isaac. Silence isn't evasion there, it's the signature of the category; if Abraham could speak and be understood he'd be a tragic hero rather than a knight of faith. (7) Marco said that's worse rather than better: a duty to God that makes it impermissible to justify yourself to the people you're about to destroy isn't a higher duty, it's a shut door, and the cost is borne entirely by the people who can't answer back. (8) Marco then tried a reductio with a case from his own family: his sister-in-law Hannah was her disabled brother's only carer when she became convinced God was telling her to leave and set up a free clinic abroad, and she wasn't resigned to losing him, she was certain that if she obeyed he'd somehow be given back to her — cared for, things restored. By Nadia's criteria, he said, that's textbook double movement, but we'd call her brave or negligent depending on how it turned out, so the criterion only fires in hindsight. (9) Nadia said it proves her point rather than his: Hannah's expectation has content, a mechanism and an identifiable public good, and the moment you can point to a purpose outside the relation to God you're back inside the ethical, so the test does work — it just returns the verdict that Hannah isn't Abraham. (10) Marco said that makes the category empty by construction: any case that could actually guide action turns out to be a tragic-hero case, and the only ones left in it are cases with no describable purpose whatsoever.

There's one more thing neither of them would touch and I'd like you to have a go at it too: what if the angel hadn't come. If Abraham counts as a knight of faith partly because he believes he'll get Isaac back in this life, then a version where the knife falls and Isaac stays dead seems to falsify that belief — and yet it also feels like the purest possible case of faith, the one where nothing external rewards it. Does the double movement survive that version, or does it collapse into belief in a future restoration, which is at least a claim with content you could be wrong about?

What I want is an adjudication of the argument as it was actually made, not a general essay on Kierkegaard: work through the ten moves in order and rate each of them from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a sentence or two of justification, then tell me who is right about what, who is more right overall, and what both of them are missing or helping themselves to without argument. Write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you'd write an email to a friend — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, and no citations or reading suggestions.
