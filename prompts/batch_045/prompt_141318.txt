Hey — I was listening to a long-form philosophy podcast on a drive (one of those three-hour interview things; the guest was a metaphysician who works on material objects) and he sketched the special composition question roughly like this. I reproduced it as fair as I can:

"The question is just this: when does a bunch of things make up one further thing, rather than being nothing but a bunch of things? Van Inwagen's answer is: only when they're the parts of a living organism — some things compose something just in case together they're caught up in a single life. So no tables, no chairs, no mountains: strictly speaking every ordinary sentence about furniture is false, and all we really mean is 'there are particles arranged tablewise'. The opposite answer, Lewis's, is that any things at all compose something — there's an object that's the fusion of my coffee mug and the top half of the Eifel Tower, and it's exactly as much an object as the mug is. But honestly the whole dispute is verbal: both sides agree about every particle and its arrangement, they just disagree about whether to apply the word 'exists' to the pluralities, so there's no fact of the matter, and basically everyone accepts now that the interesting version of the debate is settled. This isn't idle, though. Take a large model running across a few thousand GPUs in a data centre: it monitors its own health, routes around failed nodes, keeps its weights coherent over time. That's a self-maintaining activity, so on van Inwagen's criterion there really is such a thing as the model, over and above the servers — not merely hardware arranged modelwise. And everyone agrees on that much anyway, since a whole is nothing over and above its parts — it just is its parts — so the only real question is which pluralities we get to count."

I dont know enough to tell where the unfair bits are, but two things made me sit up. The "basically verbal" move sounds like it eats his own conclusion: if there's no fact of the matter about whether some things compose, I don't see how you can then turn around and conclude something substantive about the GPU cluster — you'd just be reporting how you like to talk. Though maybe I'm missing what a verbal-dispute claim is supposed to do. And the bit where he says every sentence about tables is flatly false seems hard to square with the paraphrase stuff two sentences later.

Can you go through it more or less sentence by sentence: what would a defender of the "life" answer accept, what would they wince at, what would a universalist object to, and is the "a whole just is its parts" line being smuggled in as if it were uncontroversial when it's actually a contested thesis? Then the part I care about most: if you fix the misrepresentations, does his conclusion about the model still follow, or was it resting on them? And how would you actually pose the question of whether a distributed, self-repairing computational process is one thing — is the composition question even the right frame, or does something else do the work?
