I make my living, such as it is, from other people's pricing mistakes. For several years I have been finding and closing small inconsistencies — an asset quoted at two prices on two venues, three exchange rates that do not quite multiply to one — mostly in crypto, sometimes in FX. I taught myself to do this; I have no formal training in economics or philosophy. Reading about rational choice on my own, I keep running into the comparison that people in my world reach for: that a person with cyclic preferences is like a market with an arbitrage in it, and the money pump is just the arbitrageur. I have met it in trading forums and in at least one popular book about how markets and minds both price things. The use it is put to is always the same, and always breezy: arbitrage in a liquid market is small, lasts milliseconds, and is closed by the first person to notice; nobody is actually money-pumped in real life; therefore the whole apparatus of coherence — transitivity of preference, and the harder question of what a present self owes its future selves — is a solution to a problem that never arises at a size worth caring about.

That conclusion does not sit right with me, but I cannot say where the comparison fails, and discomfort is not an argument. So: audit it. Give me the correspondence stated explicitly, including the parts I would not think to map — what plays the role of the price, of the resting quote, of the depth of the book, of the arbitrageur, of prices converging, of the bid-ask spread — and then say where each piece has no real analogue on the other side.

There are particular places where I suspect the mapping is doing concealed work, and I would like them addressed rather than gestured at:

Arbitrage profits get competed away because many traders see them at once, but the loss to whoever posted the stale quote is not thereby reduced. If several people compete to pump me, does my loss shrink, or only their shares of it? Is the claim that the inefficiency is small a claim about its size or about how it gets divided?

Arbitrage is self-liquidating because prices move in response to being traded against. What moves when a pump is run? My preferences are not quotes that get re-posted by a market maker. If the answer is that I would notice and revise them, notice that this reply assumes a single agent who persists and learns — which is very close to what the diachronic question was supposed to be about.

An arbitrage is bounded by the depth of the book. What bounds a pump, if the cycle can simply be run again? And is a small loss per cycle anywhere near as reassuring as a small profit per cycle?

In a market, a triangular inconsistency means that some quote is stale and somebody takes the other side of it; the market as a whole is not poorer for it. In a pump, the loss concentrates on one party and the gain on another. Is there a free lunch here, and for whom? And is the fact that someone could profit from my inconsistency even the same kind of claim as the claim that I am doing something wrong? Prices can be inconsistent without being irrational; what work is the extra word doing in the case of persons?

Then there is the possibility that the pump was never a prediction at all but a demonstration — a way of showing what my preferences commit me to, or that no single quantity they maximize could exist. If that is the right reading, the it never happens in practice reply seems to miss the point entirely. But then I want to know what the diagnostic reading cannot deliver either: does it tell me which of my preferences to give up, or does it just tell me that one of them has to go?

Two features of the agent case seem to me to have no counterpart in the market case, and I would like to know how much they matter. First, I can commit in advance not to trade at all, and a person who plans ahead can defeat a pump by refusing the first offer; an order book has no such move available to it. Second, the victim of a pump is spread over time, and the self who agrees to the first trade is not the self who pays at the end. The arbitrage picture is a picture of one price system at one instant. Does importing that picture quietly assume the thing that was in dispute?

Finally, the bid-ask spread: in a market, inconsistencies smaller than the spread are not worth trading, and the analogous thing in me looks like vagueness, or imprecise preferences, or simply the cost of making up my mind — in which case a pump extracts something negligible per cycle, and the analogy starts to look like it supports the breezy conclusion after all. Does it?

Where a disanalogy is real but irrelevant to the conclusion, please say so and move on; I want the ones that carry weight. If the analogy cannot do the work being asked of it, tell me what comparison would — I have been toying with cyclic preferences as the analogue of a program whose behavior is undefined, where the compiler does not repair your code but exploits the gap, sometimes far from where you left it. Grade that if it deserves grading, and offer something better if there is something better. And if the honest verdict is that the arbitrage comparison does support the relaxed conclusion within some restricted range — very small cycles, one-shot encounters, an agent able to pre-commit — I would rather be told that than handed a defense of coherence I cannot myself check.
