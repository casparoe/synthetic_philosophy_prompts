I belong to a small reading group on institutional design, and last session we worked through a concrete proposal that I'll call conditional approval: a city housing board would put to a council vote a composite wellbeing index (weights over median rent, displacement rates, commute times, and so on, measured five years out), then let traders buy contracts that pay off on the realized value of that index only if a given development is approved, with all bets called off if it is rejected. If the approve-branch price settles above the reject-branch price, the project goes ahead.

I raised what seemed to me a fatal objection: the value/belief distinction the proposal rests on does not hold at the joints that matter. Fixing the index requires a discount rate, a population boundary, and a stance on inequality, each of which is a moral choice; traders pricing the index five years out are also implicitly pricing whether the council will revise the index in the meantime and what they privately think it ought to have measured; and the reject-branch price is an estimate of a counterfactual that, by construction, never gets measured, so nothing ever scores it. A friend brushed this off with the line from Robin Hanson's futarchy manifesto — we would vote on values, but bet on beliefs — as though naming the distinction dissolves the worry.

I want that sentence taken apart. Please lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses it could be expressing, and organize the menu around the choices that actually do work: (i) what the value vote is over — ultimate ends, a single measurable outcome variable, a measurement procedure, or a legitimating ritual; (ii) what the market's number is an estimate of — a conditional expectation of the measured index, the causal effect of adoption, what other traders will pay later, or what the council will subsequently decide the index to be; (iii) what authority the price has — advisory, triggering, or constitutive of the decision; (iv) whose values count and at what time. For each reading give me a verdict, with reasons: platitude, substantive but contested, or straightforwardly false. Then say which reading my friend must have been relying on for his reply to do any work at all, and whether that reading survives scrutiny.

Two requests about manner. First, be committed: commit to each verdict and to a ranking of the readings, and tell me plainly if you think the slogan, on the only reading that is both true and relevant to my objection, turns out to be answering something other than what I asked. Second, where a reading fails specifically because of features of my version of the setup — the called-off counterfactual branch, the revisable index, traders betting on other traders — say so explicitly rather than making a general point about prediction markets. I don't need citations or a reading list; I want the disambiguation and your verdicts on it, plus a one-sentence note at the end on which single reading I should press him on at the next session.
