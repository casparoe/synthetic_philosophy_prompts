I've been picking at Marx on my own for a while now -- no classes, just internet arguments and a used copy of a very big book I've only gotten halfway through -- and something about totally ordinary jobs has started to bother me. I can't tell if I've actually understood him or if I've built a version of him that's easy to knock over, so I wanted to lay out what bugs me and see where the seams are.

Here's the thing. Where I live, the story we tell about jobs is that both sides can walk away. My employer can let me go, I can quit, nobody is holding a gun to anyone, so whatever the two of us agreed to is fair pretty much by definition. But the two sides don't walk away with the same stuff. When I quit, I leave with my paycheck and my own toolbox. Everything I actually made stays: the code, the customer list, the dumb little workaround I figured out that saves the place two hours a day. All of it just belongs to them. When the owner "quits" -- sells up, retires -- he walks away with the whole thing, including whatever my work added to what it's worth. And nobody finds this strange.

The bit that really gets me: if you hire someone to build you a deck and then refuse to pay, everyone agrees you've stolen something from them. But if you hire someone at a decent, freely negotiated wage and then sell what they made for a lot more than the wage, that's just business and the worker is meant to have no complaint whatsoever. We even seem to think the opposite direction in other parts of life -- if somebody writes a song on their own time, people feel the song is theirs in a way that isn't just about what anyone happened to pay them.

So what I want to know is what the normal practice actually presupposes. Does it presuppose that what you sell when you take a job is your capacity to work rather than the things your work produces, so that once the wage is paid there's simply nothing left that could be owed? Does treating "you both said yes" as closing the question commit you to the view that nothing can be wrong with a voluntary deal no matter what's going on in the background -- that a person with no savings and a family to feed is just as free as the company offering the job? And why is it that we save the word "exploitation" for sweatshops and wage theft and never use it about a well-paid union job at a very profitable company? That usage looks like it assumes the only thing that can be wrong with a job is pay being too low or someone being forced, rather than something about who owns the place and who decides how the work goes.

Two cases that break my brain and that I don't think the people arguing about this online ever address. First: picture a company owned equally by everyone who works there, no outside boss at all. They vote on everything, including what they pay themselves. Because they have to compete with other companies to survive, they keep voting, year after year, to pay themselves a bit less and work a bit harder. Nobody stole anything, nobody forced anybody, they own the tools and the product and the decisions. Is there anything left for an "exploitation" or "alienation" complaint to be about? My gut says no and I have a feeling the Marx people would say yes, and I can't explain either reaction. Second: a warehouse that's almost entirely robots, with one guy watching screens. If there's basically no human labor in it, where does the profit come from, and does that break the whole story or not?

What I'm after, if you can: the view of value, or of freedom, or of property that this ordinary way of doing things only really makes sense under; which rival views it quietly rules out; whether the different pieces of the practice -- either side can walk, the worker leaves with nothing, "exploitation" meaning low pay -- actually fit together or pull in different directions; and what would concretely have to change about how workplaces are run if you took the Marx-flavoured picture seriously instead of the one we seem to be using.
