Two people I share an office with got into an argument last week about a guy in my building who has two offers and has to pick one by Friday: a well-paid, secure job writing logistics software at a freight company, or a badly paid three-year apprenticeship with an old man who restores pipe organs, with no guarantee of work at the end of it. He keeps saying he genuinely can't rank them and its driving him nuts. Mara thinks hard choices like this are just ordinary uncertainty wearing a costume. Dev thinks they show something real about value. I'm not going to tell you which one I agreed with, because I honestly kept flip-flopping. Here's roughly how it went.

1. Mara: There's nothing metaphysically special here. Every option has some amount of "how good a life would this give me," we just don't know the numbers, and we don't know them very precisely. The feeling of "I can't rank these" is a fact about how little we know — about the job, about ourselves in ten years. Add enough information and the ranking appears. Positing a special kind of value that resists comparison is a costly explanation for what is really just ignorance and imprecision.

2. Dev: Imprecision can't be the whole story. If the two options were *merely equal* in value, then a small improvement to one would tip the scale decisively. Offer him $500 more a year on the software job and he's supposed to take it. He isn't, and he'd say the raise is irrelevant. Equality is shattered by tiny improvements; whatever is going on between these two options isn't shattered by them. So it isn't equality, and it isn't better-or-worse either. There's a fourth thing: the options are *on a par*. And the practical upshot is that in a hard choice the answer isn't out there to be discovered at all — by committing to one, he makes himself into the kind of person for whom that was the right answer. His will is doing normative work, not just his evidence.

3. Mara: The $500 is irrelevant because $500 is inside the margin of error of a comparison about an entire life. That's exactly what imprecision predicts. And your dramatic bit about the will is unnecessary: deciding resolves uncertainty about his own future tastes, and once he commits he'll come to care more about what he chose. That's self-discovery plus sunk-cost psychology, not a new metaphysics of reasons. Also, what *testable* difference does "on a par" make? If it predicts everything imprecision predicts, its just equality with a fancier name.

4. Dev: Here's a testable difference. Imprecision is supposed to wash out under repetition. If you face fifty small, independent money-vs-time tradeoffs, you'll converge on a rough exchange rate and mix happily between them. Now face fifty small craft-vs-security tradeoffs. No rate emerges, ever, no matter how many times you look — and, more importantly, he wouldn't accept a one-for-one swap of the sort he'd accept between two near-equal sums of money. Genuine parity doesn't produce a rate even in the limit. Imprecision should.

5. Mara: No stable rate emerges because these goods aren't fungible across contexts: an extra hour in the workshop isn't worth the same when you're exhausted as when you're fresh, and security isn't worth the same when you're thirty as when you're fifty. Unstable rates aren't absent rates. And look at what people actually do — they take the software job and mess about with organs on weekends. That's mixing, which is precisely what a workable rate predicts.

6. Dev: Mixing isn't trading. Keeping the day job and restoring on Sundays is what you do when you're *satisficing under a priority ordering* — "enough security first, then whatever craft I can get" — not what you do when you've found a price. And here's the real discriminator: with commensurable goods, some amount of one outweighs any amount of the other. Name a number and money beats time. Ask him whether some salary would beat the apprenticeship and he refuses to play, not because he's confused about the number but because the question seems to be in the wrong currency.

7. Mara: Refusal to trade at any price is about identity and symbolic meaning, not about the structure of value. Nobody will sell their grandmother's ring for any sum, and nobody thinks rings are incommensurable with cash. Press people instead of taking their first refusal at face value and they usually name a number. And you've got a bigger problem: your story predicts no hard choices once security is secured, but the hardest choices people actually report are between two options that both clear every threshold — two good careers, two cities, two people they could marry.

8. Dev: Those are hard because above the thresholds there's nothing left to measure them against, which is my point, not yours. And look at the shape of the whole exchange: I gave four signatures — insensitivity to small improvements, no rate under repetition, resistance to compensation, and the way the road not taken keeps a claim on you afterwards — and I explain them with one posit. You've explained them with four separate psychological auxiliaries: margins of error, context-dependence, symbolism, identity-constitution. Mine is doing more work with less.

9. Mara: Yours is doing *no* work. You've posited an unobservable relation that makes no difference to anything anyone should do, and then used it to explain four things we don't even agree are happening. My four explanations are four instances of one thing — bounded cognition plus the fact that choices partly constitute a person — and unlike yours, mine generates predictions you could actually go and check.
