I got into an argument in my group chat about Molyneux's problem (mostly LessWrong / ACX-adjacent people, so of course someone immediately said "the map is not the territory" and someone else said it's really a question about whether there's a shared latent space, neither of which did any work for me) and I realized my gut verdicts on the variants don't line up with each other at all. I want to know what principle I'm implicitly using, or which verdict I should throw out. Setup I'm holding fixed throughout: someone born blind, or effectively so, who has learned by touch to tell a cube from a sphere instantly and by name; sight is restored in adulthood; first session; no touching unless I say so. My verdicts: (1) cube and sphere on a table, good light, asked to point at the cube — I bet against him, maybe 60/40, and even if he's a bit above chance I don't think he *knows* which is which. (2) same setup, but he isn't asked to name anything, he's told "hand me the cube" and reaches for it — I think he reaches toward roughly the right object and his hand opens to roughly the right width, even though if you froze him mid-reach and asked "which one is the cube" he'd be guessing. That pair is the one I can't reconcile: same visual input, different outcome depending only on whether he's answering or acting. (3) reverse the direction: a sighted-from-birth person, blindfolded, feels both and is asked which is the cube — trivially yes, not even a question, which seems unfair if the information available is supposed to be symmetric. (4) a man who lost his sight at 20 and had it restored at 55, cube and sphere — trivially yes, so it isn't "newly seeing" that's doing the work. (5) the born-blind man is allowed to pick the objects up and turn them over while looking at them — I think he gets it in under a minute and it sticks, which is much better than (1), even though the visual information is arguably worse, since the things are moving around up close. (6) instead of cube and sphere, two irregular lumps of clay he's handled hundreds of times and separates instantly by touch but can't describe in words — I think he does *worse* than on the cube, even in the handling-while-looking version, which feels backwards, because his hand is far better at telling those two apart than at telling a cube from a sphere. (7) a blind carpenter who spent years cutting and sanding wooden cubes and spheres for toy blocks — I think he beats the average newly sighted person, maybe even succeeds in the setup from (1), and it doesn't feel like that's just "more tactile practice"; it feels like knowing the procedure that produces the thing.

My own first pass at a principle was: cross-modal transfer succeeds just when (i) the property is one both modalities can carry in a format that isn't proprietary to either one — shape yes, maybe size and motion, color and probably texture no; and (ii) the subject has used representations of that property to guide the *same kind of action* in both modalities; and (iii) the habit in the old modality is hooked up to a description or a generative story rather than being bare felt familiarity. I think (ii) is what's carrying case 2 and (iii) is what's carrying case 6, but I can't merge them into one thing, (i) doesn't explain the asymmetry with (3) at all, and none of them obviously explains (5).
