Hi — I could really use some help drafting a toast. My half-sister (we share a dad, she's eleven years older than me) just retired after about forty years in palliative care, most of them running a hospice she basically built from nothing, and I'm giving the dinner toast. For context: I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and for the last three years I've been shadowing her and her team for a novel about a hospice chaplain. I still get the clinical details wrong constantly — she read a draft and said my death scenes were theologically confident and technically incoherent, which was fair.

Here's the problem. About half the room will be her own clinicians — people who have heard her hold forth on killing versus letting die for decades and who will catch every sloppy move I make — and the other half are family: our dad, who's 88 and quietly believes that stopping a feeding tube is starving someone, and her two adult kids, and a lot of cousins who mainly know her as the person who shows up. She had a thing she called the furniture rule, which she'd explain to new staff roughly like this: before you decide whether anyone killed or merely allowed someone to die, you have to say what counts as furniture. The bed rail, the oxygen line, the syringe driver already attached, the chair pulled up next to the bed — whatever you've silently decided is part of the room becomes the baseline, and the very same set of bodily movements gets described as an act or as an omission depending on where you drew that line. Her view was that the doing/allowing distinction is real and load-bearing but that almost everyone draws it in the wrong place, because it doesn't live in the moment of death at all — it lives in the prior, mostly unspoken agreement about what the furniture is and who put it there. She also banned a few phrases on her ward, especially letting go and allowing nature to take its course, because she thought both were ways of describing a lot of skilled deliberate work as if nobody had done anything.

So I need a five- to seven-minute toast that works for that room, and the distinction has to do real structural work in it rather than being a nice theme. It can't imply that withdrawing treatment is just a gentler word for killing — dad will hear that as a confession, and she'd hate it too — and it can't imply that the distinction is nothing but a legal fiction or a metaphysical given handed down from on high, because her staff will wince and she'll correct me from the head table. I tried three drafts and every one either turned her into a saint of letting-go or accidentally implied she'd been quietly killing people for forty years. There should be one joke that both halves of the room can enjoy — ideally at my own expense, since I'm the one who's been getting this wrong for three years — and it needs to land as affection, not as a lecture or as an ethics tutorial. She's also asked that nobody call her gentle: she says gentle is the word people use about clinicians when they don't want to say competent. And please don't end on and in the end there's no real difference or she always did the right thing; she'd spot either as a cop-out, and so would half the room.
