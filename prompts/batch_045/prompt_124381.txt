I write a weekly column on science and ideas, and the piece I am currently stuck on began as a straight report of an argument I overheard at a dinner party a fortnight ago. It was between two old friends of mine, Ines, who works in international criminal law, and Tomas, who designs programming languages — both sharp readers, neither with any professional stake in philosophy. A philosopher I showed my notes to laughed and said they had blundered into a famous technical problem with a large specialist literature, and then refused to say anything more, on the grounds that I would learn more by reconstructing it myself. I have since concluded that I cannot tell who had the better of it, which is a problem, because the column is supposed to show readers what a real philosophical dispute looks like from the inside rather than in summary. The exchange started when a third guest, Rob, remarking on a mutual friend's decision to fire a subcontractor who had refused to sign off on a falsified safety report, said: I don't have any strong feelings about it. I'm not angry, I don't feel much either way. But I still think it was wrong. Ines and Tomas both pounced on that, and what follows is their exchange as close to verbatim as I could manage from my notes, numbered so that you can refer back to it.

(1) Ines: What fascinates me about Rob's remark is that it shouldn't be possible on my view, and yet I still think my view is roughly right. Here is the rough version: moral words don't describe anything. They register a stance — roughly, I'm against this, don't do it — and they try to get other people to share it. That is the only account I know of that explains why moral judgment is tied to action: if you genuinely judge something wrong you are, to some degree, moved not to do it, and no ordinary fact about the world works like that. 'Wrong' is nearer to a hiss than to a measurement. (2) Tomas: Then you have bought the psychology by selling the logic. Consider: if firing him to cut costs was wrong, then firing him for refusing to lie to the auditors was wrong too; firing him to cut costs was wrong; therefore firing him for refusing to lie was wrong too. That argument is valid — anyone can hear that it is valid. But on your account the first premise expresses no attitude at all: it is a conditional, and you can accept the conditional while holding no view whatever about cost-cutting firings. So the argument comes down to: [silence], 'Boo, firing him to cut costs', therefore 'Boo, firing him for refusing to lie'. There is nothing there from which the last one follows. You have kept the oomph of moral language and lost its validity. (3) Ines: That is the famous objection, but it is answerable, because attitudes have structure. To accept that conditional is not to hiss at anything; it is to be in a higher-order state — roughly, being against the combination of being for firing him to cut costs while being indifferent to firing him for refusing to lie. To be in that state is to be disposed to disapprove of anyone who is for the first and shrugs at the second. Once you allow attitudes about attitudes, the validity comes back as a kind of practical incoherence: you cannot coherently be against A, be against being-for-A-while-indifferent-to-B, and remain indifferent to B. So the argument is valid after all; the gap you think you see is an artifact of imagining attitudes as single, flat feelings. (4) Tomas: Two problems. First, the trick requires that every attitude have an exact opposite: a state inconsistent with it, such that you must be in one or the other. Belief has that, in disbelief. I don't think the emotional repertoire does; there is no state such that you must either approve of something or be in it. Second, and this is what I actually care about: even if you pull it off, you will have built a formal system with the same shape as the logic of moral sentences. That is not an explanation of why moral sentences have that shape. What needs explaining is that the clause 'firing him to cut costs was wrong' means the same thing whether it is asserted, denied, wondered about, supposed for the sake of argument, or sat in the if-half of a conditional. Rob can wonder whether it was wrong; the wondering and the believing are about one and the same thing. Unless meanings are detachable from the act of asserting, that sameness is an accident — and on your account it is an accident. (5) Ines: I concede the second one bites, and I concede the hardest case for me is supposition: a judge saying, purely for the sake of argument, suppose firing him was permissible — would damages still be owed? Nobody in that room has any attitude toward the firing; they have bracketed it. And yet 'permissible' there means what it always means. But I would say I can explain the sameness too: the sentences mean the same because they play the same role in a shared practice of coordinating what we are for and against, not because they pick out a common state of affairs. And you owe me something before I give up: if 'wrong' names a real property, it is a very odd one. Every descriptive fact about that firing — the motive, the contract, the effect on the man and his family — Rob can set out in full and still go on to ask, yes, but was it wrong? If the facts were all there was, the question would already have been answered. The gap between the facts and the verdict is exactly what my view predicts and yours has to explain away. (6) Tomas: The gap is real, but explaining it isn't my job here: I am attacking, not defending. And you haven't dealt with Rob. He has no feelings about it and still thinks it was wrong. If judging something wrong just is having an attitude toward it, that is as odd as saying you want something while having no desires. (7) Ines: That is only a counterexample if attitudes have to be feelings. A standing policy — he would avoid it, he would criticise it, he would expect others not to do it — is an attitude even when it is cold; Rob's flat affect is a fact about his temperament, not about what he thinks. Tomas, in an email the next morning: That move is what makes me suspicious of the whole programme. Any piece of behaviour can be reinterpreted as a standing policy, so no evidence could ever count against it; a view that can accommodate everything explains nothing.

What I would like from you, if you will indulge me, is an adjudication rather than a survey. Please take the numbered moves in order — treating the last one as containing both Ines's reply and Tomas's reply to it — and for each say whether it is clearly right, mostly right but in need of a qualification, mostly wrong, or clearly wrong, and in a sentence or two justify the rating; and where a move succeeds only against a view the other person does not actually hold, say so, because I suspect some of their fire misses. Then: who has the better of the argument overall at the point where they stopped? What is each of them missing or quietly ignoring? And where does the burden of proof now lie — if the exchange were to continue on another evening, who should be expected to produce the next argument, and what would that argument have to accomplish? I should add that I am not after a reading list or a guide to the literature; I want the shape of the dispute, so that I can tell my readers who won without getting a letter from an angry philosopher.
