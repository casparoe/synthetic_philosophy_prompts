I help run a short internal "AI literacy" series at the insurance company where I work — four lunch sessions, mostly for colleagues in claims and underwriting who keep being told by vendors that a system "understands" their documents. For the last session I want to skip the slide deck and hand out something that makes the room laugh and then makes them unsure what the word "understands" is doing in that sentence. What I have in mind is a parody standards document: a Committee Draft of a fictional ISO/IEC standard on symbol grounding conformance for language systems, around 700–800 words, written in a straight-faced standards register (shall/should, normative versus informative annexes, NOTES, a conformance clause, a table of levels), with an invented technical committee and invented vendors, and a short closing appendix headed something like "Frequently Anticipated Objections."

The joke I want to land is the regress at the heart of the symbol grounding problem. The standard should define grounding in a way that quietly lets a system satisfy it by acquiring more symbols — grounding in a Canonical Reference Ontology that is itself specified entirely in Canonical Reference Ontology terms, grounding "in your own document corpus" (that is, in more text), grounding in a second language, which the standard notes adds no grounding and considerable cost — and should then keep conceding, in deadpan NOTES, that nothing in the document distinguishes a system whose symbols are about the world from one whose symbols are only about each other. Please give me conformance levels along the lines of G-0 through G-4, where the top level requires genuine causal and sensorimotor contact with the sorts of things the symbols are about, the required hardware is "not included in this edition of the standard," and no conforming implementation is known to the committee — which does not prevent vendors from self-certifying at G-4 by redefining "causal" so that attention over a retrieved corpus counts as contact with it. The conformance-testing procedure should get its own clause, including a worked example in which the tester asks what "red" is and the system answers in words, and a self-attestation loophole under which a system may report that it is grounded. One clause should handle a vendor exemption request: the argument that none of this applies because the system is subsymbolic rather than a manipulator of symbols. The standard should grant it in a way that immediately takes it back — the exemption covers only the stages of the pipeline before tokenization, which is not the part the customer is complaining about.

Two things I care about. First, keep grounding separate from the nearby worry about factual accuracy: the standard can note, approvingly and without breaking character, that grounding an output in a retrieved corpus really does reduce hallucination, while insisting that this is not the property it certifies — a system can be reliably true-shaped and still not be about anything. Second, I don't want a strawman. Someone who has actually read the philosophical literature should find the satire fair: don't let the document conflate the symbol grounding problem with consciousness or the hard problem, don't run the Chinese Room argument together with the argument from the regress of definitions and sensorimotor grounding, and don't imply that the literature claims grounding is impossible or that this is a problem only for AI. Let the comedy come from the gap between what the problem actually is and what a committee that has to ship a standard can say about it. Deadpan throughout — no winking at the reader; a claims adjuster who has never heard the phrase should still enjoy it, and a philosopher should recognize that the author knows the target. No real company or person's names, no citations or reading lists: everything invented except the problem itself.
