hey — I help run a tiny student interview series attached to my cog sci seminar this term (we get one guest for 40 minutes, record it, and post the audio to a shared drive that maybe twelve people ever listen to). Next week's guest is a philosopher of cognitive science I'll call Dr. Marisol Vance, and she defends something she nicknames the "crutch curve" account of intelligence: the idea that intelligence isn't a system's peak score on a task but the shape of how its performance falls off as you strip away the supports it's currently leaning on — how often it gets feedback, how closely the test resembles what it was trained on, whether it has tools or external memory or scratch space, how much of the computation is quietly being done by the structure of the environment itself. Her slogan is that you find out how intelligent something is when you take the crutches away, and she likes to talk about "area under the degradation curve" instead of best-case performance. I've only skimmed her position paper and I half buy it, but I also suspect the view has an escape hatch every few pages, and I don't want to spend the episode nodding along.

Could you draft me eight to ten interview questions that never let her just restate the slogan, plus a follow-up wherever you can guess the move she'd make? The specific things I want hit: (1) whether this is just out-of-distribution robustness with a sexier name, given that she'll probably answer that robustness is always relative to a single task while her curve is a partial order over whole families of degradation — I want that reply made progressively harder to lean on; (2) the no-free-lunch-flavoured worry that *which* degradations you choose to apply already smuggles in an assumption about which tasks and environments matter, so the "general" in general intelligence hasn't really been cashed; (3) bacteria, tardigrades and slime molds — they survive brutal perturbations and keep functioning, so either they're intelligent on her account or she has to say the degradations have to be of the right, representational kind, at which point she seems to have sneaked cognition back in through the back door; (4) her habit of dismissing measurement worries with "that's a measurement problem, not a problem for the account," which I'd like boxed in; (5) what her view says about us — take away language, literacy, sleep, other people, and human performance collapses too, so is she committed to human intelligence being mostly institutional scaffolding, and if she bites that bullet what's left of "artificial general intelligence" as a research target?

Also, if you can work these in: one question on the psychometrics side, i.e. the fact that people who do well on one kind of cognitive test tend to do well on seemingly unrelated ones, and whether her account predicts that or just tolerates it (is intelligence one number on her view, or a family of seperate curves that can't be compared?); one on the old Chinese-room-style worry, whether something that degrades gracefully but doesn't understand anything is intelligent by her lights; one on how you'd actually run a degradation audit in practice without every lab tuning its system to the particular degradations the audit happens to test; one on whether an "intelligence explosion" — a system improving itself — is even a coherent idea if intelligence is resistance to losing your supports; and then a closing question about what empirical result would actually make her abandon the view, ideally with a concrete case (something like a system with a completely flat degradation profile that we'd still refuse to call intelligent, or one that falls apart fast but relearns a new regime faster than any person). Keep the questions in spoken-register English, since they'll be read out loud, and please don't pad them with biography stuff about her career — I can do that part myself.
