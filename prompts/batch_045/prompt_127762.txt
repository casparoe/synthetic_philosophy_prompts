I'm on the volunteer buying committee for a community kitchen — we order dried goods and some fresh produce in bulk from maybe thirty possible suppliers, and for years the process was basically: whoever is on shift that week calls three places, takes the first one that's acceptable, done. This year one of our members built us a little script that pulls the catalogs, applies a weighted score (price, delivery reliability, whether they'll break a pallet, how far the van has to drive) and ranks everything in about two seconds, for free. So obviously we got into an argument: if searching and comparing is now essentially costless, is there anything left in "satisficing" for us, or should we just always take the top of the list? I went looking for something better than the dictionary definition and ended up listening to a podcast episode where a fairly confident econ-adjacent host lays out Simon's idea roughly like this:

"Bounded rationality is the observation that people aren't smart enough or don't have enough time to actually maximize, so they satisfice instead: they pick the first option that clears some threshold of good-enough and stop looking. But satisficing is really just maximizing in disguise, because choosing where to set the threshold is itself an optimization problem — you weigh the cost of searching further against the benefit of possibly finding something better, so a satisficer is really an optimizer who has folded search costs into the calculation. What makes this more than a curiosity is that the shortcuts people use under these limits are exactly what produces the predictable mistakes in the biases-and-heuristics literature: we grab a convenient option and stop, so we end up with worse outcomes than we could have had. One distinction people miss is between satisficing as a stopping rule — when do I quit looking — and satisficing as a choice rule — how do I pick among the things I already found — and mixing those up is why satisficing looks irrational when it isn't. And the practical upshot is that once search and comparison become cheap, as with any modern comparison tool, the whole apparatus collapses back into straightforward maximizing: satisficing was a workaround for expensive search, and if you've automated the search you should just optimize."

I've never read Simon himself, only second-hand stuff, and I genuinely can't tell which parts of that are Simon, which parts are actually Kahneman-and-Tversky wearing a Simon costume, and which parts the host is improvising. My gut says the "just maximizing in disguise" line is too glib and that the "cheap search kills satisficing" conclusion doesn't follow — partly becuase our option set isn't given to us (new suppliers show up, prices move weekly, and the weights in that scoring function were set by three people arguing over coffee, so I don't totally trust them as an objective) — but I can't say why, and I'd of thought the stopping-rule/choice-rule distinction he draws is doing some real work even if I can't tell whether Simon would put it that way.
