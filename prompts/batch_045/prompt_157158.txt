im TAing for a metaphysics module and next week's seminar is on dispositions — the fink/mask cluster of cases. The group is small, mostly first-years, and they glaze over the moment I put up a slide of bullet points, so I want to open the session with a villanelle instead. Yes, really, and yes I know villanelles are normally about grief or art — but hear me out: two lines that keep coming back and can't displace each other is pretty much what a fink *is*.

Rough idea for the two refrains — don't feel bound to my wording, just to the job they do:

- one line insisting on the disposition ascription: something like "The glass is fragile. The wire is live."
- one line insisting on the non-occurence: "Nothing breaks. Nothing shocks. Nothing happens."

The form should make them keep meeting without either one giving way, and I really don't want the final quatrain to tidy it up with a moral. If the poem has room for why both claims can be true, put that in the middle stanzas — the interfering condition, or the stimulus doing something to the object itself — not in a resolving couplet. Let the last two lines just sit there with the tension showing.

The cases I want somewhere in there, one or two a stanza, done accurately enough that someone who's actually read this literature can tell fink from mask from the reverse case and doesn't wince:

- the vase packed in sawdust that wouldn't break if dropped, and is fragile anyway
- the wire that's live but wired to a device that kills the current the instant anything conducting touches it — so no shock, but not because something blocked the current on its way: because by then there wasn't any
- the one where what prevents the manifestation is itself triggered by the stimulus (I think these get called antidotes? not 100% sure on the label, go with the case rather than the word)
- the reverse one: something that isn't fragile, or isn't live, but where the manifestation happens anyway because of something else rigged up — a charge inside the vase, a second circuit that closes on contact. That's the stanza where the first refrain should start to sound off, or at least where a careful reader notices it's now doing something different, because here the ascription really is false.

Keep it under a minute read aloud, rhythmically regular-ish but not sing-song, and it should still land as a poem for someone who's never opened a metaphysics paper. Please don't name any philosophers in it and don't smuggle the theory in as a lecture — no footnotes, no winking at the literature.
