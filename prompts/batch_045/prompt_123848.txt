I'm a senior at my school, and I help run our philosophy club. Over the summer I read a chapter in a popular book — one of those "why philosophy never solves anything" books; I no longer have the reference — and I've been arguing with it ever since without being able to say exactly where it goes wrong.

The chapter's core passage, which I copied into my notes, reads like this:

"A great many philosophical disputes turn out to be disputes about words. The two sides utter the same sentence — 'numbers exist', 'the statue is identical to the clay' — but they hear it differently, and once each side says plainly what it means by the key term, the disagreement dissolves. That is the mark of a merely verbal dispute, and it is also the test for one: a genuine disagreement cannot be made to vanish by defining your terms, because a genuine disagreement concerns how the world is rather than what we mean. Once this is seen, a large part of metaphysics collapses. Questions like whether numbers, properties, or composite objects exist have no fact of the matter: under one precise meaning of 'there is' the answer is yes, under another it is no, and there is nothing more to be right or wrong about than there is in the choice between Celsius and Fahrenheit. Ontology is lexicography in a lab coat. The standing objection — that this deflation applies to itself, since the claim that a dispute is verbal is also advanced in some language or other — simply confuses levels: the deflationist is not asserting a first-order claim about what exists, only recommending which language to speak, and a recommendation is not the kind of thing that can undermine itself."

I'd like an audit of that passage before I trust it. Please go through it and sort the claims into what someone who actually works on these questions would accept, what is a simplification of a subtler thesis, and what is straightforwardly wrong — and be specific about which distinction is being collapsed in each case. My own instinct is that the second sentence is doing far more work than it appears to: our club once spent a whole afternoon on a question where everyone agreed on every fact on the ground and still refused to agree, which suggests that either "dissolves under disambiguation" isn't the right test, or it isn't the test for what the chapter thinks it is. But I don't want to overcorrect by positing a hidden fact of the matter that nobody could ever discover; that feels like moving the goalposts rather than answering.

So please test the view on my case rather than arguing about it abstractly. Two years ago my school folded the Debate Club and Model UN into a single "Forensics Society." The merged club inherited the Debate Club's trophy case, its budget line and its registration in the state league; its constitution was rewritten and it now admits the Model UN members. Half our club says the Debate Club no longer exists; the other half says it does, and has merely changed its name, its rules and its membership. Both sides agree on every factual matter: the roster, the schedule, the league registration, the money, the fact that the old charter was never formally repealed, and the fact that nobody now says "Debate Club" for anything. One side then says: nothing anyone could discover tomorrow would settle this, so it is a decision rather than a discovery. The other side replies: a decision, yes, but a constrained one — some ways of continuing to use "the Debate Club" fit what we already do with that name better than others, and there is a right answer about which those are. There is also money attached: an alumni association has offered a grant to "the Debate Club," and whether the Forensics Society may accept it depends on the answer.

I have a term for what I think is happening and I want to know whether it holds up. Call something a *load-bearing* verbal dispute: one where the first-order question genuinely dissolves once meanings are fixed, but where a second-order question — which of the available meanings we should now use, given what that choice costs, licenses and rules out — immediately takes its place and is contested just as hard. Is that a coherent kind, a restatement of something the literature already recognizes under another name, or a confusion? Does the chapter's test have any room for it? And does the presence of the grant money show that our dispute is not verbal after all, or can a dispute be verbal and still have real stakes that both sides are arguing about?

Finally: the chapter's conclusion is that most of metaphysics should be abandoned. Say clearly how much of that conclusion actually follows from the parts of the passage that survive your corrections, and what someone who accepted the corrected version would still be entitled to think ontology is doing.
