The setting: a reading group I attend on Kuhn and paradigm shifts. Two members, Marisol and Devin, spent most of a session arguing about whether incommensurability is a real phenomenon with any epistemic bite, using a test case they constructed themselves rather than a historical one. I genuinely could not tell who was winning.

The case they built (a hypothetical, not a real episode — please take it exactly as stipulated). Start from the mid-2000s reclassification of Pluto, in which a body everyone had counted as a planet stopped counting as one under a new dynamical definition keyed to whether a body clears its orbital zone. Now suppose that two decades later a rival definition, a geophysical one keyed to whether a body is rounded by its own gravity and has undergone internal differentiation, becomes standard among planetary geologists while the dynamical definition remains standard among dynamical astronomers. Stipulate further:

(1) The two groups share every observation, dataset, instrument and model, and never differ about any prediction.
(2) On actual solar-system bodies they disagree only about a handful of marginal objects, and about those both sides agree the decision is conventional. But when handed descriptions of merely possible bodies — a rounded, differentiated object orbiting inside a debris belt; a Mercury-mass body far beyond Neptune; a geologically active moon twice Mercury's size — each group sorts them confidently and they sort them differently, and each finds the other's sorting not just wrong but unmotivated: neither can see what would make those things belong together.
(3) A computational-historiography study finds that models trained on one group's literature predict the other group's citation and collaboration patterns well, but systematically fail to predict which anomalies each group flags as a priority.
(4) Students migrating between the groups take about a year to become fully functional in the new one, and report that during that year they could follow every argument but could not see why the other side's questions mattered.
(5) The two taxonomies carry different inductive consequences: because the geophysical definition admits some large icy moons, generalizations of the form planets tend to have such-and-such a feature receive different support in the two vocabularies, and each group's favored generalizations are projectible within its own vocabulary.

The exchange:

Marisol 1. This is a clean case, not the usual muddle. There is total evidential agreement and yet no shared taxonomy: the groups do not merely disagree about which things are planets, they disagree about which similarities are the kind-making ones, and that disagreement is invisible in the actual data and shows up only in how the category is extended to new cases. It also has consequences, because each taxonomy makes a different family of generalizations projectible. So they are not simply using different words for the same investigative situation.

Devin 1. I grant the phenomenon and deny that the label does work. A disagreement about how to extend a term to possible cases is a disagreement about which definition to adopt, and a definition is a stipulation. The right response is the explication move: choose the definition that serves the inferential purposes you have, and say that is what you are doing. Calling that incommensurability turns a choice into a tragedy.

Marisol 2. Explication assumes your purposes are fixed in advance and shared. Here they are neither. Each group's research agenda — what it would fund, what instrument it would propose — is shaped by which similarities it treats as kind-making. You cannot appeal to purposes to settle the taxonomy, because the taxonomy is part of what gives you your purposes.

Devin 2. You cannot have it both ways. Either the taxonomy is chosen to serve purposes, in which case the dispute is practical and settles by negotiation, or it is chosen because one taxonomy makes better-supported generalizations available, in which case it settles empirically, since the groups share all the data and will converge. Neither route leaves an epistemic residue.

Marisol 3. The empirical route does not close. Which generalizations get tested depends on which ones your vocabulary makes projectible, and which ones get tested determines what evidence comes to exist. The taxonomy shapes the evidential base that was supposed to adjudicate the taxonomy. That is a path-dependent loop, not guaranteed convergence.

Devin 3. That is ordinary Duhem-style holism, which everyone accepts and which has nothing to do with meaning. And your own stipulation (4) cuts against you: after a year people work fully in the other framework and even report understanding its questions while failing to see their point. Understanding is translation. What remains is a difference in interests, not in meaning and not in what the evidence supports.

Marisol 4. You keep collapsing three things. (a) Whether the vocabulary translates. (b) Whether the taxonomies coincide. (c) Whether the research problems are shared. Stipulation (4) settles (a), and I never denied it. (b) is what the possible-cases divergence establishes, and it survives full translatability. (c) is what the historiography result establishes. The interesting claim is that (b) drives (c): a taxonomy reorganizes the space of admissible questions, so one community can be unable — not merely disinclined — to arrive at a question the other treats as central. That is a limit on rationality at a time, and it is not holism, because holism concerns evidence underdetermining theory whereas this concerns the space of alternatives being invisible.

Devin 4. I accept the three-way distinction and I accept (b) and (c) as you describe them. But the inference from (b) to (c) requires that a question be literally unformulable in the other vocabulary, which is false: you can always introduce a predicate, define it stipulatively and ask about it. Your own example proves it, since each group can state the other's questions and simply does not care. So the residue is motivational, and motivation is not a kind of rationality that incommensurability threatens. Notice also what your view costs: if a taxonomy can hide alternatives from an entire community, then the fact that no one in a field sees an alternative is no evidence that there is none. That is a sweeping conclusion to buy from a case in which no prediction ever differed.

Marisol 5. I will weaken the claim rather than abandon it. The unit of incommensurability is the research program, not the sentence. You can of course ask any single question in the other vocabulary; what is missing is the package — a family of questions and generalizations that nothing in your practice would have led you to assemble. Comparability of sentences plus shared evidence does not give comparability of programs, which is the only comparability that matters for whether a community's verdict was rationally forced.

Devin 5. Comparability of sentences plus shared evidence is all that rational theory choice ever required; the rest is the sociology of attention. And notice that your retreat makes the phenomenon scalar. Once it is scalar, whether it threatens rationality depends on how much of a program is invisible at a given time, which is an empirical question about real cases that this thought experiment does not settle.

They stopped there, unconvinced in both directions.
