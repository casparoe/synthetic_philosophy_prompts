The case I keep coming back to is deinstitutionalization: the emptying of the big public mental hospitals in the US and UK from the 1950s into the 1980s. The broad-brush version, and I'm not attached to any exact figure (if a number really does load-bearing work for you, just say what you'd have to go check): inpatient populations in state psychiatric hospitals peaked around the mid-1950s and then fell by something like 80–90% over the next few decades; the drivers were a mix of the new antipsychotic drugs from the mid-50s, the 1960s push toward community treatment and a civil-libertarian objection to indefinite commitment, and — from the other side of politics — state governments keen to shut expensive institutions; and the widely reported outcome was that a lot of severely ill people ended up cycling through homelessness, shelters, jails and underfunded community clinics, with the promised community system never built at the scale that was supposed to replace the hospitals. I know parts of that causal story are contested (some people argue the link to homelessness is overstated and that housing policy and deindustrialization matter as much, and others argue that for many patients discharge was a genuine improvement), so don't spend time adjudicating it — just tell me what, if anything, turns on the contested bit.

The lens is Chesterton's fence and the Burkean background it comes from: the rule that a reformer who can't see the use of an institution isn't entitled to remove it, and has to go away and find out why it was put up before laying a hand on it; and behind that, the Burkean thought that a long-lived practice embodies more knowledge than any one person can state, so its being unjustified-in-words isn't evidence that it's unjustified. What I want is a disciplined mapping in both directions, and where the theory has nothing to say, say that instead of forcing it. On the mapping side: who exactly is the "reformer" here (it wasn't one deliberating agent — it was a coalition of people who wanted the same change for opposite reasons), what is the fence (is it the building, the statutory power to commit, the custody function, the shelter function?), what would have counted as really knowing why it was there, and what would have counted as having discharged the burden before acting. Then four wrinkles I've been chewing on, which I'd like you to take seriously rather than fold into the standard formulation: (1) orphaned fences — a practice can outlive its original rationale and quietly pick up a second job, so "I know why it was built" is not the same as "I know what it is holding up now," and Chesterton's rule as usually stated only asks about the first; (2) morally disqualified reasons — here the reformers often did know why the institution existed (partly to warehouse people and to relieve families), and found the reason real but unacceptable, which the rule seems to give no guidance about; (3) irreversibility — staff, buildings, local knowledge and statutory authority don't come back cheaply once dismantled, so the option value of waiting looks asymmetric in a way the rule never mentions; and (4) the standing problem — the rule is addressed to a lone reformer facing a gatekeeper, but in a real policy process "show me you understand the fence" is a rhetorical test, and the people best able to articulate the reason are often the ones with a stake in keeping the institution while the ones it harms are the least articulate. Finally, the other direction: does this episode vindicate the Burkean/Chesterton line, or does it show that the rule has almost no content — it would have told us to understand the asylum first, which the critics largely did, and it would equally have told the defenders to understand their own institution, and it can't tell apart, in advance, the reforms that turn out great from the ones that go badly? And is Chesterton's fence saying anything that "reforms have unforeseen consequences" plus loss aversion wouldn't already predict — I'd like to know if it's adding anything or just dressing up status quo bias.
