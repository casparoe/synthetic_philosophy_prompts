I'm a journalist — I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine — and I'm trying to work out whether I actually believe the thesis I want to hang a piece on. So please argue for it as hard as you can, and then tell me where it breaks.

The topic is higher-order evidence: the stuff that makes you suspicious of how you arrived at a belief rather than of what the belief is about. You find out the lighting was bad, or that you were exhausted, or that everyone on your side of some argument finds this particular argument compelling, or that the study was paid for by someone who needed it to come out this way. The standard framing is that the healthy response is to revise your confidence until the first-order belief and the second-order verdict line up again, and that if they dont line up you're in some embarrassing state — akrasia, incoherence, whatever — that has to be explained away.

I want to argue roughly the opposite: that the lined-up state is the special case, and that akrasia is what a normally functioning agent looks like from the inside, at least sometimes.

Here's the machinery I've been using to think it through, which I havent seen anyone set out like this, so feel free to tell me it's confused. Call a correction function whatever rule you use to adjust a first-order confidence in response to higher-order evidence about your own reliability: discount by some factor, defer to the unbiased version of yourself you imagine, split the difference with someone you regard as a peer, whatever. The demand that you end up coherent is then the demand that your confidence be a fixed point of your own correction function — apply the correction once, apply it again, and nothing moves. But nothing about being a sensible correction function guarantees that a fixed point exists; if one exists there's no reason there's only one; and if there are several, there's no reason your first-order view and your second-order verdict should meet at one just because you've noticed they disagree. On this picture akrasia isn't a failure of nerve, it's what being at a non-fixed point feels like.

I also want a second plank, which might be a different point wearing the same clothes. Some higher-order evidence doesn't say your instruments are noisy; it says your sample was selected by someone with a stake in the answer. Unreliability you can discount for. Selection you can only discount for if you know the rule that chose which questions reached you, and mostly you don't — you notice you've only ever looked into the claims that flatter your side, but there's no factor to multiply by. That looks like a case where the tension is permanent rather than transitional.

What I need:

1. The strongest version of the argument, in a form I'd be willing to defend in print. Assume I know the usual moves — move toward what you'd have thought without the bias; there's something supposed to be irrational about believing p while believing you shouldn't believe p — and don't spend the piece setting them up.

2. An example I could open with that isn't the coloured light or the drugged reasoner. Something a reader would recognise from their own life: finding out the assistant that agrees with you is sycophantic, noticing your confidence in a claim tracks who you follow rather than what you've read, realising you only ever checked the sources that supported you.

3. The best objection, and what I'd have to concede to it. My own worry is the obvious one: that I'm relabelling a mistake as a healthy state, and that any view on which the akratic state is sometimes correct owes me an account of when it's correct and when it's just stubbornness.

4. What would have to be true for the thesis to be false. Not that plenty of people disagree — what fact about belief, or about reasons, would sink it.

Two constraints. I can't check sources and I'm not going to name-drop philosophers in a general piece, so no citations, no reading lists, no attributing the view to anybody: I want the argument itself. And it has to be writable at the level of a decent magazine — no jargon walls, define anything technical you reach for, but don't flatten it into vibes either. If the thesis only works inside the jargon, say so and tell me which plank I'd have to cut.
