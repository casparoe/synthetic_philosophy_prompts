Two friends of mine — Rhys, who does something in theoretical stats, and Marta, who's in phil physics — ended up having a proper row in the kitchen after a seminar on objective chance last week, and they've both asked me to adjudicate. I took notes on my phone at the time and I honestly cant tell who's got the better of it, so I'm passing it on.

Background they both accepted: Lewis's Principal Principle — roughly, that your credence in an outcome should line up with its objective chance, provided the rest of your evidence is "admissible", where the usual gloss is that admissible evidence is evidence whose bearing on the outcome comes entirely by way of the chances.

The case they kept coming back to: a lab rig where the chance of an atom decaying by 3pm is set by a dial at noon; the dial is itself set by a fair coin, so right now, at 11am, the chance of decay is 0.5, and it'll end up being either 0.9 or 0.1. At 11am a note arrives from the lab, reliably, saying only: the chance at noon will be 0.9. Nothing about the dial, nothing about the coin, nothing about what actually happens.

1. Marta: You should obviously go to 0.9. But the PP, as stated, says 0.5 — the present chance is 0.5, and a report about chances is about as admissible as evidence gets. So either the PP is false, or it's rescued only by the word 'admissible', and nobody's ever given a non-circular account of which evidence is admissible. The usual gloss is circular: it defines admissible evidence as the evidence that reaches you only via the chances, which helps itself to the very thing the principle was supposed to do. What's left is: believe the chances unless you've a reason not to. That's close to empty.

2. Rhys: The fix isn't 'admissible', it's indexing to times. Chances are time-relative anyway. Read the PP as: match the chance that governs the outcome — the one in force over the period in which it happens — not the chance at the moment you happen to be thinking. Your evidence here includes a report on the noon chance, the chance governing the decay is 0.9, so 0.9. No circularity, and 'admissible' isn't carrying any weight in this case at all.

3. Marta: Fine, granted — that sorts out that case. But it only sorts out evidence about chances. The PP still has to say something about evidence about outcomes: a crystal ball, a forecaster with a 99% track record, a text from the future. There the only move available is: inadmissible, ignore it, stay at the chance. And then the entire content of the principle sits in the line between admissible and inadmissible, which is precisely the line nobody can draw without circularity. 'Match the chances except when you shouldn't' isn't a principle, it's a placeholder.

4. Rhys: There is a non-circular way to draw it, and it's causal: evidence is admissible iff it doesn't depend on the outcome — isn't causally downstream of it, doesn't counterfactually track it. The lab note reports a dial setting upstream of the decay, so admissible; the forecaster's report tracks the outcome, so inadmissible. And that isn't a fudge, because it gives the principle real bite: in a genuinely chancy world you can't get better-than-chance knowledge of a chancy outcome by any route that doesn't run through the chances. The forecaster case only feels like a counterexample because you're picturing a world the PP says can't exist.

5. Marta: That last claim can't survive, and here's the case. Same rig, but the note says: the chance at noon will be 1. Everyone agrees information about future chances is admissible — that's the whole point of deferring to chances. But a chance of 1 just is the outcome. So the note hands you certainty about a chancy outcome in advance, by a route that isn't downstream of anything, and there's nothing incoherent about the set-up. So either you drop the 'no foreknowledge' claim, which was the bit that was meant to give the principle bite, or you say chances of 0 and 1 are somehow special, which is ad hoc. Neither is comfortable.

6. Rhys: I'll drop it, partly — what I actually need is weaker: chances are the only channel, and your back-door case runs through chances, so it's consistent with that. Every substantive principle has a messy boundary; that's a research programme, not a refutation. And your case doesn't really describe a violation anyway: at 11am, before the note, 0.5 was the right credence, and once the note arrives the PP with time-indexing gets you to 1 on its own. There's nothing left over needing an explanation.

Can you go through the six moves one at a time and tell me who's right about what — rating each move somewhere from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a line or two of justification each — rather than just declaring an overall winner? And if they're both missing something, or both working with some shared assumption that's wrong, say what that is.
