I'm putting together a short position paper for a metaphysics-of-science reading group and I want an audit of the package I've ended up with before I try to defend it out loud. Here, numbered so we can refer back, is roughly where I've landed: (1) a Humean, best-system account of lawhood — a regularity is a law just in case it appears as an axiom or theorem of the system that best balances simplicity against informational strength in describing the total mosaic of particular fact, with no governing, no primitive necessitation, and no irreducible powers or dispositional essences; (2) Humean supervenience as a thesis about the furniture of that mosaic — local intrinsic qualities at spacetime points together with spatiotemporal relations, with everything else (chance, causation, the truth of counterfactuals, mentality) supervening on that; (3) naturalism about what the mosaic contains — that isn't an armchair matter, we read it off our best physics, which is currently quantum, and we take on board whatever non-classical structure that turns out to involve; (4) realism about objective, non-epistemic, single-case chance — some events really have a chance of occurring that isn't a summary of anyone's evidence, and a rational credence about one upcoming event should track that chance; (5) a verdict on a case I've been calling the Fold case — worlds W1 and W2 agree in their entire mosaic up to and including a spin measurement performed at noon today, and differ only afterwards, because in W1 the lab goes on to run a million further trials with an almost exactly fifty-fifty split while in W2 the building burns down that evening after three more runs, all of which come out "up": my verdict is that the chance at noon of "up" is one half in both worlds, since what happens later cannot make a difference to what the chance is now; and (6) an unapologetic scientific realism about unobservables and about modal structure — electrons and entangled states are real, and there is a mind-independent fact of the matter about which counterfactuals hold.

What I want is an audit, not a survey of the literature: go through the pairs and tell me which are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and for each real tension what exactly would have to give and what giving it up costs me. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and be explicit about the mechanism — "quantum mechanics creates a problem for (2)" is not an answer until you tell me which feature does the work and why it can't be absorbed. Two constraints on what counts as a cheap fix: I count it as expensive to help myself to primitive modality or to dispositional essences, and I also count it as expensive to make lawhood depend on our descriptive conventions, interests or simplicity metric in a way that leaves the laws non-objective. I'd also count it as expensive to end up an eliminativist about laws altogether. If a tension in my set only arises given some further assumption I haven't stated, say what that assumption is, because I may not be making it.

I should flag that the objection I keep being handed by other people in the group is the combination of (1) and (4) — they say a Humean cannot have objective single-case chance — and I've been dismissing it on the grounds that a best system can perfectly well be probabilistic. I may be being complacent there, and the real damage may be somewhere else entirely, so please don't just take my own diagnosis of where the problem lies: if (5) is the commitment my (1) cannot deliver, say so plainly and tell me what I would have to believe about chance instead. Finally, someone at the reading group mentioned a view they called "super-Humeanism" and I couldn't work out whether it is just my (1) with (2) drastically thinned down — which would be a cheap fix for whatever trouble (2) is in — or whether it is the eliminativism I said I'd count as expensive. Tell me which, and tell me which single revision to this whole package would clean up the most.
