I direct a small qualitative team at a contract research organisation, and for the past year we have been building the item pool for a patient-reported outcome instrument for a chronic condition that has no biomarker: the symptoms are, for practical purposes, whatever patients say they are. The sponsor wants a draft pool in six weeks. What we actually do is concept-elicitation and then cognitive-debriefing interviews: forty-five minutes, semi-structured, one interviewer and one notetaker, recorded, transcribed, and coded the following weekend by three people with psychology degrees and no philosophical training, none of whom will read anything I give them beyond a one-page sheet. The output has to survive review by a psychometrician and a clinician and, eventually, a regulator asking what evidence we have that our items mean what we claim they mean. We are also running in three languages, and I do not trust our back-translations.

I have been reading about Dennett's heterophenomenology — the idea, as I have it, that we treat subjects' utterances as texts to be interpreted rather than as authoritative verdicts on their own experience, and that the proper object of study is what people say about what goes on in them rather than any inner show we are supposed to take at face value. I want that turned into something my team can run. So: give me a protocol, in the order we would execute it, from the moment a transcript lands to the moment a candidate item is admitted, held back, or killed. For each gate I need the question to ask, what would count as a sufficient answer to it, the conditions under which the protocol simply returns a no, and the points at which it has to stop and hand the decision to a named human being because the question has stopped being one about evidence and become one about what we are trying to measure — whether the instrument is for detecting change in a trial or for giving patients a vocabulary, for instance. I have been using two homemade devices and I would like you to tell me whether they survive the translation or whether they quietly reintroduce the error the method exists to avoid. My two-route rule: a candidate item enters the pool only if the symptom concept turns up both in spontaneous recall during open elicitation and under structured probing, and only if no interviewer named it first. My gloss-swap test: later in the same interview, substitute the clinician's proposed gloss for the patient's own word, and count the concept as the patient's only if they go on to use the gloss unprompted; if they merely assent to it when offered, we treat it as ours rather than theirs.

Then run the protocol on three cases, and I would rather you told me now if it kills an item my sponsor is attached to than let me find out at the pre-submission meeting. One: patients volunteer brain fog constantly, endorse it heavily, and cannot define it beyond metaphors that look like they came from a patient forum; the sponsor wants it as a headline item. Two: when the pain items are asked before the fatigue items, mean fatigue severity comes out roughly a point lower on the 0–10 scale than when the order is reversed, and I cannot tell whether we have an item or a framing artefact. Three: a patient reports a symptom in the room, in front of the clinic nurse, that their own daily electronic diary for the same fortnight does not support. A fourth, smaller thing while you are at it: the sponsor insists our items be worded as first-person claims about inner goings-on, and I have a suspicion the third-person stance of the method should make me resist that, but I cannot tell whether that is what the method really implies or a misreading on my part. Please do not point me at papers or reading lists — I have no library access from the field sites, so argue from the position itself, and note where the protocol is doing real work and where it is only a judgement call with procedural clothing on.

Structure the response in these sections, in this order and roughly these lengths: What the method commits us to, in our vocabulary — about 250 words; The protocol — about 700 words, as numbered gates with question, sufficient answer, stop rule and escalation; Worked applications — about 700 words, one subsection per case in the order I gave them, plus a short note on the item-wording question; Where the protocol misfires — about 250 words, the questions it cannot settle for us and where a person must decide; The one-page version — about 200 words, the checklist I can pin up above the coding desk.
