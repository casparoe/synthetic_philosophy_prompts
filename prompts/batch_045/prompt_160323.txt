I'm putting together a grad seminar on disagreement and I keep hitting a shape of case I don't have a word for. Setup: I publish an argument for P. A colleague I count as a real peer — same training, no known bias on this question — reads it, takes my case fully on board, and lands on not-P. So her verdict already contains my evidence, plus her own. Now I cant work out which way the arithmetic should go. On the one hand my first-order case looks weaker than I thought: someone holding my evidence and more still rejects the conclusion. On the other hand, her disagreement looks like it should count for less than an ordinary peer's, because it isn't an independent check on my reasoning at all — its a verdict on a case she has already discounted, formed by someone who knew all along that I hold P. Equal-weight conciliationism seems to just help itself to one of these without argument, and I can't see how to say both things at once.

Second case, which may or may not be the same thing: in a small subfield everyone has read everyone else, so no one's view is independent of anyone else's, and yet we all still treat a rising headcount of opponents as ramping up the pressure to move. Is that just my phenomenon with more people in it, or a different failure with the same symptoms?

So: is there an established concept for disagreement whose epistemic force is unclear because the disagreeing party's verdict already incorporates your own evidence? If there is, tell me which nearby notion it is and which it isn't — I assume the independence condition and the higher-order-evidence literature are both within reach of it, but neither looks like quite it. If there isn't, coin something and give me the boundary cases, specially the ones where a non-independent disagreement still moves you. I want the carving, not a label. And please be explicit about which claims here are settled among people who actually work on this and which are live disputes — I don't want to teach a contested view as the orthodoxy. British English, cheers.
