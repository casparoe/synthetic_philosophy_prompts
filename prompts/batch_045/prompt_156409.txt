I'm a first-year PhD student in economics, and next week I have to run a single fifty-minute methods section for second-year undergraduates on why an unrealistic assumption in a model is not automatically a defect — Friedman's F-twist, from the lead essay of his 1953 *Essays in Positive Economics*, the passage where he writes that "Truly important and significant hypotheses will be found to have 'assumptions' that are wildly inaccurate descriptive representations of reality, and, in general, the more significant the theory, the more unrealistic the assumptions (in this sense)." The students have had intermediate micro and one applied econometrics course and no philosophy at all: they arrive able to fit a regression and with no view about what a model is. The department treats the essay as the most influential thing written on economic methodology in the twentieth century, and it wants them to come out of the hour able to defend a simplification rather than either sneering at it or shrugging at it. I don't need the F-twist explained to me; I need a plan for the hour.

I have a rough distinction I've been trying out on my friends, and I want to know whether to build the session on it or scrap it. I separate (i) *cancellable* idealizations, where the falsehood can be removed — you relax the assumption, walk a short repair path, and the prediction barely moves; (ii) *compensated* ones, where the falsehood stays but the error it introduces is offset by other errors elsewhere in the model, so the net prediction survives for a reason you can actually state; and (iii) *inert but fragile* ones, which cost you nothing while you predict inside the range you fitted on, but which become the thing that drives the answer as soon as a policy question perturbs exactly the quantity the assumption sets to zero, to infinity, or to a single representative value. My claim is that (iii) is where the as-if defence is most dangerous — a model can predict beautifully and still be unusable for the question we care about — and that the students' econometrics training actively hides this, because for them a good fit vindicates whatever sits inside the box. Before any of that can land, though, something has to be in place for the F-twist even to look tempting: they need to hold apart a model's assumptions, its derivations, and its empirical claims, and they need to feel the difference between predicting within a regime and extrapolating across an intervention.

So tell me whether that three-way distinction is the right spine for fifty minutes or whether one of the three does no work and should be dropped, and in what order the components have to arrive and why. Then the carrying example: my instinct is to build the whole thing on one assumption from their own micro course — price-taking behaviour under perfect competition, or perhaps zero transaction costs — and I want your verdict on which, what such an example must have (a repair path we can actually walk in five minutes; a falsehood separable enough from the rest of the model that students can watch the model function without it) and what it must lack. I think a joke case like the spherical cow is actively harmful, because it makes the entire topic feel like a punchline; and I suspect Friedman's own example, the expert billiards player who is said to play as if he were applying the equations of motion, is a poor carrier as well, since the falsehood there lies in the description of the agent's reasoning rather than in the system's physics, so it quietly changes the subject. Give me the misconception map too — the ones I already expect are "unrealistic just means approximately true," "if it predicts well then the causal story inside is confirmed," and "adding realism always improves a model" — tell me which misconceptions students will form that I haven't listed, the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off, and the single question I can ask in the last five minutes that would show me the idea has landed rather than that they've memorised a slogan. Finally, say plainly what I should refuse to go into at this level and why: I'd guess the realism/antirealism dispute about unobservables and the models-as-fictions literature, but tell me what else to leave out and openly name as out of scope.
