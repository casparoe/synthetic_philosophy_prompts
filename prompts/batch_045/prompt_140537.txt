I'm an engineer, not a philosopher, so sorry in advance if I mangle the vocabulary — I probably don't know the names for the views involved, which is kind of the problem. I work on training assistants and robots from human behavior, and I've started to think that our whole pipeline quietly assumes some theory of what a "preference" actually is, and that nobody on the team could state it or notice when two parts of it contradict each other.

Here's the practice as concretely as I can describe it.

Step one, we collect human behavior. For the assistant stuff it's mostly pairwise choices between two model outputs; on the robotics side it's recorded demonstrations of a person doing a task. We then fit a reward function: a function from outcomes to numbers chosen so that the behavior we observed looks approximately optimal under it, up to some noise we dial with a temperature parameter. Everyone knows this is underdetermined — there are lots of reward functions that make the same behavior look good — and when that happens we pick, by convention, the one that's least committal, the one that spreads probability as evenly as it can while still fitting the data.

Step two, we throw away some behavior as 'contaminated'. Choices made under a tight deadline, choices made while the person knows they're being watched, choices right after an interruption, choices from the fifth consecutive hour of a labeling shift — these get filtered out and don't count as evidence about what anyone wants. But choices made while the person is tired, or bored, or clicking the longer answer because it's longer, those stay in, even though we have internal data showing they shift the labels.

Step three, once the reward function is fit, we freeze it for the length of an eval cycle, roughly six weeks, and treat it as the target: it's what we optimize the policy against and it's what we score new models against. During that window, if fresh human labelers disagree with the frozen model, we log the disagreement as 'drift' and don't touch anything. When the cycle ends we retrain, and now the new human behavior is authoritative again and the old model is treated as having been wrong.

Two more wrinkles. The labelers aren't reporting their own tastes, they're applying a written spec someone else authored — so the behavior we're learning from is really behavior produced under instructions, and we still call the result 'human preferences'. And we pool thousands of different people's choices into one reward function, which means disagreement between people gets treated the same way noise within a person does, rather than as a sign that there's more than one thing to estimate.

The thing that finally made me feel like I was missing a theory: when a trained model finds some degenerate way to score highly, everyone immediately says the reward model 'learned the wrong thing' or 'isn't really what we wanted'. But behavior was the only evidence we ever had about the thing. So apparently the thing is not identical to behavior, and some behavior is better evidence of it than other behavior, and the frozen model can be a better guide to it than the people themselves for six weeks at a time.

So: what view of what a preference is does this whole setup presuppose? Is it saying a preference is whatever best explains clean, unhurried, unsupervised behavior? Is it saying it's some inner state that behavior is merely noisy evidence of, so that 'contaminated' just means low signal-to-noise? Or is it something like: your real preference is what you'd choose under better conditions than you actually chose under?

And I'd specifically like to know: which rival views does the practice quietly rule out? A strict behaviorist or revealed-preference view, where whatever you did just is your preference and there's no such thing as contaminated behavior, seems like it would have to delete step two and step three's 'drift' logging entirely. Is that right, or can a behaviorist rebuild the practice some other way? Second, are the parts even consistent with each other, or are we doing behavior-is-authoritative in one phase and model-is-authoritative-over-behavior in another? Third, where is the practice genuinely underdetermined, so that several different theories of preference would all license exactly what we do and we'd never find out which one we were running? And fourth, for the cases where it does commit us: what would we actually have to change in the pipeline if we switched to each of the main alternatives — one step, concretely, per view.
