I am writing a paper for a final-year philosophy module on minds and machines, and I want to use Ridley Scott's *Blade Runner* (1982; I have in mind the longer cuts, including the unicorn dream and Gaff's origami figure) as the object of a sustained reading rather than as decoration. The scenes I care about are: the opening empathy test administered to Leon and the later one administered to Rachael; Rachael's discovery that her memories are implants and her reaction to it; the fact that the Nexus-6 replicants are organic, grown rather than assembled, with an incept date and a four-year span; Roy's dying monologue, in which he recalls what he has seen and grieves that it will all be lost; and the standing ambiguity about whether Deckard himself is a replicant.

Please draw the following distinctions and hold onto them. First: *explanatory* computationalism — the claim that the best science of cognition describes cognitive systems computationally, and that cognitive explanations are computational explanations. Second: *constitutive* computationalism — the identity claim, that to be in a cognitive state just is to occupy a computational state, so that what makes something a mind is its abstract organisation rather than what it is made of. Third: *architectural* computationalism — the claim that the brain literally is a digital computer in the engineering sense, running discrete symbol-level programmes over a stored architecture. I take it that the third is now hard to defend (the wetware is continuous, stochastic, massively parallel, embodied and metabolically expensive, and analogue and neuromorphic computing muddy the digital picture), and that the second can in principle survive the death of the third; part of what I want from you is whether the film has anything to say about that gap.

Then there is the implementation problem: even granting constitutive computationalism, one needs a non-arbitrary account of which computation a physical system performs, given the familiar triviality worries associated with Putnam and Searle (that any sufficiently complex open system implements arbitrarily many computations) and the Chalmers-style attempts to constrain implementation by the causal organisation of a system's states. And alongside it the content problem: computations are specified over syntactically individuated states, while mentality seems to require states with content, so a computational story alone may not yet have said what a mind is.

Here is the reading I have been toying with, and which I want you to test rather than to endorse. Call it *provenance sensitivity*: our attributions of mentality seem to track not merely a system's organisation but where that organisation came from — whether it was born, raised, trained, enculturated, or merely installed — and the film is, on this reading, a sustained exploration of that fact. Rachael's false memories, Roy's four years with no childhood, and above all the question of Deckard's origin would then be the film's real subject. But note the tension: if constitutive computationalism is true, then whether Deckard was manufactured should be metaphysically irrelevant, and the fact that the entire drama of the film hangs on it looks like a commitment to something very like Kripkean origin essentialism, which is precisely an anti-functionalist view. So: does the film presuppose the falsity of the view it is conventionally used to illustrate? Or can someone who accepts constitutive computationalism explain why the origin question matters — because of what it implies about his implanted memories, his epistemic position, the moral question of whether he may be retired, or the social and legal meaning of the category rather than its metaphysics?

I want a reading that is faithful in both directions. Where the idea illuminates a scene or a choice, say so and say what it shows. Where the film resists the reading — where it is really about mortality, empathy, exploitation and the authority to classify persons, and pressing it into an argument about the computational theory of mind distorts it — say that plainly, and say what the honest critic should concede. Take a view about which of the three theses the film can plausibly be made to speak to, and which it cannot. Close by saying what, if anything, the film adds to the debate that the arguments alone do not: whether it dramatises something — the first-personal experience of discovering one is engineered, the dependence of mentality on a history one did not choose — that the literature leaves out.

And do not attempt verbatim quotation from the film — I am interested in what the scenes do, not in the exact words.
