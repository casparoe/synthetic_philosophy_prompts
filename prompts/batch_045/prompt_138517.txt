Some context first. I'm retired — forty-odd years in geochemistry, mostly mass spectrometers and arguing with people about detection limits — and about a year ago I joined a reading group that does moral philosophy, so I've picked up some of the vocabulary but I may well be using it wrong.

What I keep circling around is heroism, and specifically how we handle it institutionally. We hand out medals, we have a stock phrase for it (something along the lines of above and beyond the call of duty), we build monuments, and yet the soldier who did no more than he was asked is in no way criticised — nobody says he shirked. Then look at how much the line moves. Identical risk of death: if you were ordered to cross that field and you didn't, you're in prison; if you volunteered to cross it when nobody asked, you're a hero. A lifeguard who swims out to a drowning child is doing her job, a sunbather who does the exact same swim is on the front page. And I'm told that in a fair number of European countries simply walking past is itself a crime — if that's right, the very same inaction is a criminal offence in one place and mildly disappointing in another. There's also no negative counterpart: nobody thinks the medal winner has banked some credit he can spend later on being a jerk, and as far as I can tell courts mostly wont let a history of good deeds buy down a later bad one. Hard praise, selectively applied blame, and the praise doesn't transfer.

So: what picture of duty does that whole apparatus presuppose? I've got some more specific things I'd like sorted out.

In my old field, whenever you found a sharp line in a dataset you had to ask whether it was a real phase boundary or just a line somebody had drawn for bookkeeping — a cutoff that does useful work for us without cutting nature at a joint. Is the duty/beyond-duty line more like the first or the second? What is supposed to fix where it sits: what you've consented to, the role you occupy, how much the act costs you, how much it helps, what everyone else on the scene is doing? And if it's the last one — if my duty to wade in shrinks as the number of other bystanders with boats and phones grows — then how much of supererogation is really about what I owe, rather than about the crowd?

Second, is the practice actually committed to a genuine third category here — acts that are permissible and yet specially worth praising — or could you get the same pattern out of a deflationary story, where praise is just the incentive we reach for in the region where compulsion is unenforceable or too expensive to police, and the absence of blame is just us declining to enforce? If so, does that story predict the no-credit-transfer bit better than the real-supererogation story does, or worse?

Third, the asymmetry bothers me. We seem happy with a positive third category above duty but not with a negative one below it — the nasty-but-permitted stuff, never visiting your mother, quietly letting a friendship die, declining to be mildly generous when it would cost you nothing. Nobody gets a medal for that region and nobody gets punished either. Is the refusal to recognise a mirror-image category a principled part of the practice or just a gap in it?

Last one: suppose we ditched the binary — medal or nothing — and scored acts on a continuous scale of merit, the way I'd report a concentration. Would anything actually be lost, or does the concept of supererogation definately depend on there being a ceiling with a cliff above it? I'd like to know which parts of the practice strictly need the category, which parts merely sit well with it, and which parts several different theories would license equally.
