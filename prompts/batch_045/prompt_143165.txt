I teach a graduate seminar in formal epistemology and I am grading a take-home on Bayesian confirmation theory; answers were capped at 1,000 words. Below is the question I set, followed by one student's answer in full.

Please assign a mark out of 20, and give point-by-point comments organized by part of the question: for each substantive claim, say whether it is correct, and where it is not, identify exactly where the reasoning fails and what additional premise, if any, would repair it. I am most interested in the mistakes that survive a first read — claims that sound right because they echo something in the literature, or that would be right on a slightly different reading of the terms, but that fail as stated. If a claim is defensible on one reading and not on the most natural reading, say so and explain the difference. Please do not soften the assessment and do not substitute a general impression for specific comments. You may assume the seminar has already covered Bayesian confirmation theory, conditionalization, and the law of likelihood.

The question

(a) [6 marks] State the problem of old evidence as exactly as you can, making explicit the assumptions about confirmation and about updating on which it depends. Say what happens if the new hypothesis has prior 0 or 1, and whether the problem then disappears or only changes shape. Then say whether the problem is defused if the old evidence is not certain but merely very probable.

(b) [8 marks] Consider two replies.
(i) The language-expansion reply: enrich the agent's language so that the claim that H entails E can be expressed, assign that claim a probability strictly between 0 and 1, and treat the scientist's derivation as learning it, so that conditionalizing on it raises the probability of H. Explain why this is supposed to work and what it costs.
(ii) The counterfactual-prior reply: in place of the agent's actual prior, use the credence she would have had in H had she not known E; update that on E, and take the result as the confirmation E confers on H. Assess whether this is a credence the agent has or could have, whether it is determined by her actual doxastic state, and whether it is the quantity a Bayesian account of confirmation should care about.

(c) [6 marks] Is the problem best understood as a problem about evidence, or as a problem about the arrival of a hypothesis for which no prior had been fixed? And is it a problem only for accounts that measure support by credence-raising, or does an account that measures support by comparing how well competing hypotheses predict the evidence face it too?

The student's answer

(1) If E is already known, P(E) = 1; and since H entails E, P(E|H) = 1; so Bayes's theorem gives P(H|E) = P(E|H)P(H)/P(E) = P(H). Conditionalization therefore cannot raise the probability of H. Since Bayesianism identifies confirmation with probability-raising, and identifies rational change of opinion with conditionalization, it has no way of representing as rational the scientist's change of mind when her new theory turns out to account for evidence she had all along. That this is not a merely formal curiosity is shown by the standard example: the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion was on the books long before general relativity, and deriving it from the theory is a paradigm case of confirmation. The assumptions doing the work are the two just named: confirmation as probability-raising, and conditionalization as the update rule. If H has prior 0 or 1 it stays there under conditionalization whatever happens, so the argument above is vacuous in that case; but that is a separate difficulty about priors for new theories, not a solution.

(2) The language-expansion reply works by changing the subject from E to something the agent does not already know: the fact that H entails E. Extend the language with a sentence C expressing that entailment relation. The agent is not certain of C, since she has not yet carried out the derivation, so P(C) < 1; when she does the derivation she conditionalizes on C, and because H together with C yields E, learning C pushes probability towards H, so that P(H|C) > P(H). The confirmation that E apparently confers is thus recovered as confirmation conferred by a newly discovered entailment. The cost is that the agent must be less than certain of a necessary truth, and it is unclear how a principled prior for such a claim is to be set: the solution buys confirmation by making the agent logically non-omniscient. A Bayesian who insists on logical omniscience cannot use it.

(3) The counterfactual-prior reply avoids all of this. Ask what the agent's credence in H would have been had she not known E. This is fixed by her own conditional credences, since her actual prior is the result of updating that hypothetical prior on E, and updating is invertible once P(E) is specified. Use that quantity as the prior, conditionalize on E, and the result can exceed it, which is exactly the confirmation we were looking for. Nothing here requires uncertainty about necessary truths, so the reply is strictly better than the language-expansion reply.

(4) At bottom the difficulty is that the evidence has already been used up: the scientist either built H so as to fit E, or fixed her prior with an eye to E, so conditionalizing on E a second time would be double counting. This is the same point that is made outside the formalism by the requirement that confirmatory evidence be novel — evidence that went into the construction of a hypothesis cannot confirm it — and the problem of old evidence is just that requirement written in the language of probability. Relatedly, the problem in its strict form depends on the link between theory and evidence being deductive; where the link is statistical and H confers on E a probability less than 1, P(E|H) is not 1 and a known E can still discriminate between H and its rivals. Since most scientific evidence is statistical in this sense, the problem is partly an artefact of the hypothetico-deductive setting in which it was raised. Finally, the problem is not really about evidence at all: it is about the arrival of a hypothesis for which no prior had been fixed, since a Bayesian needs a prior for H at every time and H was not available for consideration before. That is what makes it hard. It is, however, a problem internal to Bayesianism. An account that measures support by comparing how well competing hypotheses predict the evidence is untouched by it: the law of likelihood compares P(E|H) with P(E|¬H), and neither of those quantities depends on whether the agent already knew E.
