I work in the deregulation unit of a state government. Every week my team of three — none of us lawyers, none of us with more than a day per rule — reviews long-standing provisions in the administrative code and recommends to a political appointee that each be repealed or retained. Typical items: a requirement that licence applications include a notarised paper copy; a prohibition on selling a particular product within a fixed distance of a school; a rule that two named officers must sign any payment above a threshold; a form field nobody can explain. Most have no written rationale, and the people who might have explained them have retired.

Please take the role of a political philosopher who has also spent years doing practical regulatory reform. Think carefully before drafting: spend a short paragraph listing the ways a naive application of Chesterton's fence — do not remove an institution until you can say what it was for — goes wrong in a setting like mine. I am especially worried about two failure modes. First, the absence of a discoverable reason is not evidence of the absence of a function: some rules prevent harms that are invisible while the rule works and untraceable to the rule afterwards, so silence from the public is worthless as evidence. Second, a rule's original purpose and its present function can come apart, so finding out what it was erected for may not tell me what it is now holding up.

Then write the procedure itself: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with "retain", where it licenses repeal even though I never learned the original reason, and where it hands the decision to the appointee because it is a question of values rather than facts. It must handle rules whose benefits are invisible, rules that people have arranged their conduct around, rules that are cheap to administer but costly to comply with, rules whose effects are or are not reversible, and the risk that the reason I end up stating is my own reconstruction. It needs a budget and a stopping rule so that it cannot be used to justify keeping everything. Note that Burkean deference to inherited arrangements and Chesterton's permission to destroy once the use is understood pull in different directions, and say explicitly which one the procedure is adopting. It should fit on one page and be usable by someone with no theory.

Apply it to three of the cases above, including one where the honest output is "retain" and my director will not like it, and then say what the procedure cannot do. State how confident you are in each substantive claim and single out the step you are least sure about.
