I've been reading the rationalist blogs and forums since the sequences were still being posted, and I've now ended up on the four-person program committee of a small foundation that has roughly $4M to disburse over its next two funding cycles, about eighteen months. Our remit is narrower than it sounds: we fund conceptual work only — philosophy, decision theory, a bit of strategic modeling — by small teams with no lab access, and we can't fund anything whose main output would be a policy campaign or an empirical machine-learning result. We've decided that the theme for both cycles should be the singleton question: whether humanity's trajectory is drifting toward a single decision-making agency able to enforce its will at the highest level of political organization and permanently foreclose the alternatives, as opposed to a multipolar outcome in which no such agency ever exists; whether that would be good; and what, if anything, our grantees could say that would be useful to anyone. I don't want another rehearsal of the standard arguments for and against concentrated world authority, which I've read many times over; what I need from you is an ordering. So: what are, in your view, the most important open questions in this area, ranked, and what makes each of them worth our money? Be explicit about the criteria you're applying and about the trade-offs between them — how much other work depends on the answer, whether someone with a philosophy or theory background and no compute can actually make progress in eighteen months, how neglected the question already is, and what concrete evidence appearing over the next two or three years would move it up or down your list. I'd also like you to say plainly which of the questions that sound important you'd deliberately defund, either because they're downstream of something else or because settling them wouldn't change what anyone does.

Three things I can't adjudicate on my own, and which I'd like you to place inside the ranking rather than treat as separate side issues. First, I've become convinced that the concept bundles together arrangements that shouldn't be evaluated together: a veto-only arrangement that can block certain technologies but cannot command anything; a domain-scoped regime that governs a single technology class above some capability threshold and nothing else; a sunset or leasehold arrangement with a fixed term and a planned dissolution; and one that binds itself in advance to constitutional rules meant to preserve value diversity beneath it. My guess is that the familiar objections — value lock-in, the corruption of whoever holds the lever, the loss of the error correction you get from rival centers of power — bite very differently across these four, and that the target actually worth aiming at may be a strange one, but I can't tell which. Second, there is an epistemic case against concentrated authority that I think keeps getting merged into the tyranny case and shouldn't be: even a wholly benevolent single agency makes its own mistakes correlated and eliminates the possibility of an outside check, and I want to know whether that argument is genuinely strong or whether it's really a disguised preference for letting many actors run experiments. Third, it seems to me quite likely that if a singleton does arrive it will be a byproduct — one actor's decisive lead in a general-purpose capability, rather than anything anyone chose — and if that's right, I'm unsure whether the whole normative debate about whether a singleton would be desirable is worth funding at all, or whether the only questions with any leverage are the ones about how the transition itself goes.

Finally, since we're a committee that has to act: tell me what you'd want us actually to be tracking. If we spent a small slice of this money keeping two or three live indicators of whether the world is drifting toward concentration rather than remaining multipolar, which indicators would those be, how would you define each one so that a grantee could compute it without access to proprietary data, and what reading of each would make you want to redo this entire prioritization from scratch? Please write your answer as continuous prose in American English, the way you'd write a long email to a colleague — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables anywhere — and open with the ranking itself, so I can read the bottom line before I read your defense of it.
