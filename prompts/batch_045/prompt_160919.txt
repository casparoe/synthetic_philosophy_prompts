I'm retired — thirty-odd years in freshwater ecology — and I've been going to a philosophy reading group at the library for about a year. We've just had two sessions on the relational, Ubuntu-ish family of views about personhood, where a human being is supposed to become and stay a person by being held in relations with others rather than by carrying some set of capacities around inside them. Half the group thinks that's a real claim; the rest think its just our ordinary intuitions wearing nicer clothes. I want a case that decides it.

So build me a thought experiment. One paragraph, realistic — a hospital ward or a care home, no sci-fi, no ancestors or gods turning up — in which the patient's clinical state is such that the standard capacities view (a person is the sort of being with self-awareness, agency, some ability to reciprocate) has to say personhood is gone or hanging by a thread, while the relational view has to say: still fully a person. And nobody should get to settle it by declaring that she's still one of us; the verdict has to fall out of what the relations actually do in the case, not from anyone's say-so.

There's a device I keep coming back to and I'd like the case built around it — call it reasons-holding, or give it a better name. The idea: the people around her go on registering and acting on her standing commitments — the thing she always insisted about her brother, the promise about the house, the old joke that settles whose turn it is — so that her reasons keep having force in the room even though she can't generate any new ones. The relations are meant to be doing work there, not just turning up in sufficient numbers.

After the scenario: walk through what each view says and why, then name the feature that's doing the work. Then vary it — what if the reasons-holding lapses (family drifts away, or there never was a family and staff do it by rote off a chart)? Does the relational view then have to say she stops being a person, and if not, what's carrying the verdict instead? Finally, give me the capacities theorist's best reply (my guess: I've relocated personhood into other people's attitudes and got the direction of explanation backwards) and tell me honestly whether the case shows the relational view is saying something extra or just redescribing the same intuition. Plain prose please — I'll be reading it out to six retired people and one of them will definately fall asleep.
