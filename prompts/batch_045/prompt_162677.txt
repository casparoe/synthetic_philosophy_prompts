I'm helping out with a panel at my old school next month about whether the Jefferson name stays on the building, and I keep running into the same line from the other side — "he was a man of his time, you cant judge him by todays standards" — and I want to actually be able to take it apart instead of just getting annoyed and repeating myself. So please play the committed defender of that defence for me: not the slogan version (you'll cheerfully concede that the slogan is lazy, and that it gets wheeled out selectively, mostly for people we already like), but the strong one — roughly, that its unfair to condemn someone for failing to meet a standard that wasn't genuinely available to him, where "available" has to be read generously, as what a well-meaning, well-placed person of that moment could have come to believe using the reasons then in circulation. Be sharp, a bit stubborn, concede small points but never the main one, and tell me plainly when one of my arguments is just bad. Two short paragraphs per reply, stay in character until I say "stop", and feel free to ask me questions back.

Here's my opening. I think the defence keeps sliding between two quite different excuses and pretending it gets both. Take Jefferson: in print he worried at length about what slavery does to the character of masters as well as to the enslaved, and he floated schemes for ending it — so whatever he was, he wasn't someone who lacked the reasons for thinking it was wrong. Which means that for him the defence can't be "he couldn't have known"; it has to be "he knew and couldn't have done much about it" — the debts he never cleared, creditors sitting on encumbered estates, a legal setup that made freeing people expensive, a plantation economy and a political order built on exactly that labour. But that isn't a man-of-his-time excuse at all, it's a constraint-and-diminished-capacity excuse in period costume, and we don't accept it for anybody else: the guy who knows the bribe is wrong and takes it anyway because his business will otherwise collapse doesn't get to say "well, it was 2024". So which is it — is your defence about what he could believe, or about what he could do? I don't see how the first covers Jefferson, and I don't see how the second has anything to do with which century he lived in.

Down the line I also want to press two things I'm much less sure of, so don't let me hand-wave them: first, whether "judging" a historical figure is even one activity — saying he was a bad man, saying he's blameworthy, and saying we should stop honouring him look like three different verdicts, and my suspicion is that the defence keeps winning the third by pretending it won the first; and second, whether the same defence has to work for Churchill (Bengal, and people in his own government pushing for grain shipments at the time) and for Columbus (where there was at least one extremely loud contemporary saying more or less what we say now). But start with Jefferson.
