In a nationalism reading group I'm in, "traditions are invented" has become the move that ends arguments. Most recently it was used against me on a case I invented to test it: the region of Vardania, whose national epic was forged by two philologists in the 1890s, whose "traditional" costume was designed by a government committee in 1958, whose flag-day holiday was instituted in 1968 — and whose population nonetheless voted ninety per cent for independence in a 1991 referendum and now sincerely experiences these practices as inherited. My interlocutor concluded that because Vardania's traditions are invented, Vardania is not really a nation (or is a nation only in a second-rate sense), and that its claim to self-determination is correspondingly weaker than that of a neighbour whose traditions are older and unengineered.

Lay out, as a list I can bring back to the group, the distinct precise theses that slogan could be expressing — for instance, that every practice has a first date of origin; that practices presented as immemorial are often recent; that they were deliberately instituted by identifiable agents for state-building purposes; that they lack substantial continuity with the past they invoke; that they are presented as ancient while known by someone not to be; that nationhood itself has no essence over and above such practices — and say for each whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and whether it would actually support my interlocutor's conclusion. Two points I especially want pinned down. First, whether any reading escapes both the vacuity problem (if nothing counts as non-invented, then the neighbour's older tradition is invented too and the contrast my interlocutor needs collapses) and the genetic-fallacy problem (a practice's origin does not settle what it obligates or authorises now). Second, what becomes of the question "how old are nations?" under each reading: is it a question about first dates, about continuity over time, or about the social preconditions — print vernaculars, mass schooling, censuses, standardised rituals — that first made nation-sized imagined communities possible? Say also whether it makes a difference that Vardanians do, or do not, know how their traditions began, and tell me which reading people who reach for this slogan are usually implicitly relying on. If "nations are imagined communities" is doing relevantly different work, add a short note on that too.
