I am teaching a graduate seminar on early modern idealism, and next week's session is on what is now called Berkeley's master argument: the passage in the *Principles of Human Knowledge* in which he dares the reader to imagine trees in a park, or books in a closet, with nobody by to perceive them, and then points out that the dare is one you can accept only by failing it — to frame the thing in your mind just is to supply the very mind you were trying to do without. I would like to open the session by handing round a short poem rather than a handout of premises, and I should be grateful if you would write it.

The form I want is a pantoum: four-line stanzas rhyming ABAB, in which the second and fourth lines of one stanza become the first and third lines of the next, so that the poem cannot get rid of anything it has once said. In the closing quatrain let the poem's first line return as its last (and, if you can manage it, its third line as the second); if the strict interlock fights the sense, keep the interlock and bend only the closing rule.

The point of the form is that it should carry the argument rather than decorate it. The lines that keep coming back should be the ones that smuggle the perceiver back in — the eye, the mind, the "I" that will not go away — so that the reader watches the failure happen again in each new setting instead of being told that it happened once. Let each recurrence shift slightly: a change of tense or speaker, a different antecedent for a pronoun, a punctuation mark that alters the scope of a negation. One of those returns should register the standing objection to the argument, namely that showing we cannot perform a certain act of imagination does not by itself show that what the act was meant to reach is impossible — that the reasoning may slide between conceiving a thing and conceiving that a thing is so. The poem should not resolve that dispute, and it should not quietly declare Berkeley the winner; the objection should be there in the verse, as something the returning lines themselves make the reader feel.

Constraints: sixteen to twenty lines; unobtrusive iambic pentameter of a kind that would not sound out of place in 1710; a single concrete setting (a locked library at night, a walled garden after closing, an unlit room) rather than abstraction. Inside the poem, no philosophical vocabulary and no names — no "idealism", no "matter", no Berkeley or Hylas, no Latin tag. It should work as a poem for someone who has never heard of Berkeley, while a specialist should be able to say which lines are the argument, which line carries the objection, and whether the poem cheats. Please do not let it cheat: nothing asserted that the reader has not been led to.

Write it in British English, and send the poem alone, with no preamble and no prose explanation of the argument.
