I'm in a tiny reading group that's slowly working through Lewis's big book on the plurality of worlds — the one where he defends concrete modal realism by basically doing a cost-benefit analysis, weighing the theoretical payoffs (an account of modality, counterfactuals, propositions, properties) against the costs. Our TA kept leaning on the distinction between quantitative and qualitative parsimony: counting how many things a theory posits supposedly doesn't matter, or barely matters; what matters is how many kinds of things it posits and how much primitive ideology it needs. Lewis's pluriverse is quantitatively enormous but qualitatively cheap, since other worlds are just more ordinary stuff of the same sort as tables and donkeys, aggregated worldwise. I found that pretty convincing at the time. Then a friend in the group who does philosophy of physics handed me this over coffee and I've been chewing on it for a week, and the conclusion is one I really don't want.

(1) Ontology is settled by inference to the best explanation: posit the entities your best overall theory needs, trading explanatory gain against theoretical cost.
(2) Theoretical cost comes in two flavours, quantitative and qualitative, and only the second is a real cost. Nobody thinks a cosmology with a trillion galaxies is worse off than one with a billion just on that count; we already accept that our own world may be infinite in space and time and contains vastly more than we will ever observe.
(3) So adding more entities of a kind you already accept is close to free — it costs you nothing as long as it buys explanatory power.
(4) Now consider the plenitude theory: every way a concrete world could be is concretely realized somewhere, and there is nothing at all except concrete individuals with their properties and relations. It's at least as qualitatively parsimonious as Lewis's own view, and arguably more, because Lewis still needs a principle of recombination to say which arrangements get realized, plus counterpart theory, whereas the plenitude theory needs no restriction.
(5) And it buys a genuine explanatory payoff: it explains why the constants and initial conditions of our universe look fine-tuned for life. If every arrangement is realized, observers will find themselves in the rare life-permitting regions, so no designer and no brute coincidence is needed.
(6) But it also entails that the overwhelming majority of observers with exactly our evidence are Boltzmann brains — brief fluctuations out of chaos with fake memories — rather than ordinary creatures in an orderly universe. So the theory says our evidence is systematically misleading about what reality is mostly like.
(7) A theory that says your evidence is misleading can't then lean on that evidence for support: it pulls the rug out from under the very inference that recommended it. So you cannot rationally accept the plenitude theory, however well it seemed to explain things.
(8) But (7) can't just be a special fact about this one case. If self-undermining theories can still be what IBE recommends, then deceiver hypotheses — the demon, the simulation — are back in business, since they fit the evidence perfectly and are cheap on kinds. And if they can't, then (1) through (5) are in trouble, since they have exactly the shape of the argument Lewis gives, and they deliver a theory no one can accept.

So something has to go, and I can't see which. I'd most like to keep (2) and (5): the point about kinds rather then headcounts still seems right to me, and the anthropic explanation of fine-tuning is the strongest card my side has. I'd least like to give up (7), because without it I don't know how to answer the sceptic. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost me — does it cost me Lewis, does it cost me the fine-tuning argument, or is there a slide somewhere in the middle that means it costs me nothing at all? I also have a nagging feeling that the plenitude theory needs a measure over observers before (5) gets off the ground, and a measure sounds like exactly the sort of extra posit that would make it less parsimonious than Lewis — but I can't tell whether that's the real diagnosis or a different problem I've wandered into.
