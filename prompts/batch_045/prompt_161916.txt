I hold a version of the Jain doctrine of non-one-sidedness (anekāntavāda), and I want to know what would count against it — not a survey of objections, but a map of where the view is actually exposed.

Here is the version I hold, stated plainly. Truth and reality are complex and many-sided; any substantive claim about a thing made from a finite standpoint (a naya) is true only as qualified. The canonical Jain schema of conditioned predication (syādvāda), in its sevenfold form, holds that for a given claim P one may assert, each prefixed by "in some respect": that P; that not-P; that both P and not-P; that P is indescribable; and the three combinations of indescribability with the first two. The point is not that contradictions hold, but that unqualified assertion mistakes a partial view for the whole, and that the object is complex enough to sustain mutually incompatible partial characterisations — as in the familiar illustration of the blind men and the elephant. To this I add three clauses of my own: (i) no finite standpoint is privileged over any other; (ii) the qualification is inherited — if P holds only from a standpoint, then everything that follows from P is indexed to that standpoint too; (iii) the doctrine governs normative and evaluative claims as much as descriptive ones.

What I want from you:

- The observations, cases, arguments and formal results that should move someone in my position, ranked from fatal through serious to merely awkward, with the ranking argued for rather than asserted. Do not lead with the stock self-refutation charge (is "everything is standpoint-relative" itself standpoint-relative?) — I think I can answer that one, and I want to know what is left once it is set aside.
- For each item, say separately whether a committed non-absolutist would concede it as counting against the view, or dismiss it as presupposing the very absolutism in dispute. I suspect much of what critics call a defeater is question-begging, and I would like that sorted from the genuine ones.
- The view's distinctive predictions. Does it predict anything its rivals do not — for instance, that apparently intractable disagreements should dissolve under sufficient standpoint-taking? If so, is that testable in any ordinary way, in science, in cross-cultural ethics, in law? I do not want citations or reading lists; I want the shape of the evidence and how much it would weigh.
- The collapse question: under pressure, does the view turn into one of its neighbours — plain fallibilism, relativism about truth, dialetheism, or the thesis that every dispute is verbal? Which collapse is a live risk and which is a straw man? A demonstration that it collapses into a rival I would treat as a serious defeater.
- The iteration problem, which I cannot settle myself. If "in some respect" behaves like an operator on claims, what happens when it is applied to a claim that already contains it? Does the sevenfold scheme close under iteration, and if there are claims the schema cannot express at all, how much of the doctrine's coverage do they cost it?
- The limiting case. "Partial" suggests partial relative to something complete. Does the view require the intelligibility of a complete, unqualified grasp of the whole — the sort of thing Jain thought attributes to the omniscient — for its central notion to do any work? And if that limiting case is incoherent, or dispensable, what exactly breaks?
- The insulation worry, honestly assessed. The doctrine looks able to absorb any criticism as one more partial view, true from a standpoint. Has it been qualified into unfalsifiability? If it has, is that a defect here in a way it is not for other views — given that its own recommendation is epistemic humility — or is it rather what the view should be expected to look like if it is true? I want a verdict either way, including the possibility that the insulation charge is simply misplaced.
- Clause (iii) is where I am least sure of myself, so tell me whether the normative extension is where the view is most exposed or where it is safest, and why.
- What would move me most personally: a case in which two parties share every standpoint-relevant feature, the disagreement between them is plainly substantive rather than verbal, and none of the seven modes yields a correct description of the matter. Tell me whether such a case is available, or whether the doctrine has a principled reason to deny that one could arise.
