Our school rewrote its academic-integrity policy this year, and I was the student member of the committee that drafted it, so I have spent a good deal of time with the document. The more I read it, the less I understand what it takes a large language model to be.

The rules as they came out are these. Using a generative AI to produce any part of a submitted essay counts as cheating and is processed exactly like plagiarism: same hearing, same penalty as turning in another student's work. Using a spellchecker, a grammar checker, a translation dictionary, or a calculator is fine and never has to be disclosed. Using an AI to explain a concept, quiz you, or suggest a thesis is fine, but the moment it produces a sentence you submit, it is not. Brainstorming is permitted, outlining is a gray area, drafting is prohibited. Enforcement leans partly on AI detectors, and students have been called in on the strength of a detector score alone, which has already caught at least one classmate who simply writes in a flat, generic style.

What puzzles me is that the policy seems to treat AI output as if it belonged to someone else — you are punished for passing off another party's writing as your own — while the same document, and the same teachers, say that the model does not know anything, has no beliefs, and is only predicting plausible continuations of text. Nobody proposes citing it as an author, and nobody thinks it can be held responsible when it invents a source. Meanwhile the policy bans the model in exactly the places where it permits tools that do far more of the thinking for us, and it polices AI-ness by inspecting features of the finished paragraph rather than by asking how the paragraph was produced, even though the causal history is supposedly where the offense lies.

So my question is what this bundle of rules presupposes. Does it only make sense on the assumption that a language model is a tool like a calculator, with the ban really about something else — the decay of a skill, or a worry about what the assignment is supposed to measure? Or does it quietly rely on the model being a kind of agent, or a simulated person, whose sentences can be someone else's in the relevant sense, so that submitting them is a species of deception rather than mere unauthorized assistance?

I would especially like to know whether the parts of the practice pull in different directions. We hold the student responsible for the text while exempting the model for its falsehoods; we deny the model beliefs while treating its output as attributable to someone other than the student; we locate the wrong in the essay's causal history while enforcing it through stylistic properties of the text. Is there a single account of what these systems are under which all of that is coherent, or is the policy underdetermined, licensing several quite different accounts? And concretely: if our school decided that the best description was neither tool nor person but a simulator that can be prompted into any one of many personas, what would have to change — about disclosure, about detectors, and about what the offense is supposed to be?
