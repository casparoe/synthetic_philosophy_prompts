ok so our reading group is doing a few weeks on animal and (briefly) AI welfare, I'm the person who keeps saying "it's a matter of degree," and I've been asked to write down my actual view on moral status so everyone else can pull it apart. I'd like to know if it holds together before I hand it out. Where I've landed:

1. Moral status is continuous. There's no point where it switches on and no point where the rules change — I don't think there's a threshhold at which persons get rights and everything else gets mere interests, and I don't think rationality, personhood, or being alive mark anything morally special.
2. What sets the degree is only facts about the subject's consciousness. Concretely, what I've been calling bandwith: roughly how much experience is bound together into a single scene at a time, multiplied by how intense it is. Thermostat: nothing bound together, so zero. Bee: something small but not zero. Human: a lot. An ant colony, a firm, a nation: nothing bound together, so zero as such — only the individual ants or people count.
3. Also depth: a being whose experience reaches backwards and forwards — memory, anticipation, projects, things it's in the middle of — matters more per second than one whose experience is a bare flash of sensation, even at equal bandwith and equal intensity. This is most of why I think a mammal's pain matters more than a fish's, and it's the part of the view I'm most attached to.
4. Aggregation is plain addition, over subjects and over moments. Lots of small minds can outweigh one big one. And I explicitly reject the idea that experiences get a bonus for belonging to the same life — "same subject over time" does no independent work.
5. But I also think killing a healthy adult dog is clearly worse than killing a puppy of the same breed, even where the puppy has more expected future weighted experience and even holding the effects on other animals and people fixed. The adult has more at stake, a denser knot of things it's in the middle of.
6. I don't think a billion bees add up to one high-status being; they stay a billion low-status beings. I know that sounds like it contradicts 4, but I think it doesn't.
7. I think there's a real difference, not just a useful rule of thumb, between killing a person and killing a bee: killing a person needs justification, killing a bee doesn't. And I take all of this to be substrate-neutral, so a good enough simulation of a bee brain has exactly a bee's moral weight.

So: tell me which pairs genuinely pull against each other and which only look like they do, ranked by how badly they conflict rather than all flagged the same. For each real conflict, say what would have to give and what giving it up costs me elsewhere in the set. I'd also like to know whether there's one revision that cleans up the most at once, and which of these is the load-bearing mistake if there is one — I genuinely can't see where it is, and I'm not already planning to drop any particular one of them.
