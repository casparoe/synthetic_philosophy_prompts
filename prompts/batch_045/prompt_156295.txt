Hi — I need a two-page research proposal for a one-year dissertation fellowship application, and I want the framing nailed down before I write the rest of it. Two ground rules: no citations, no bibliography (I'll fill those in myself, and nothing that depends on looking something up), and write it for a committee that's one decision theorist plus two people from other areas who are going to ask why any of this matters to anybody.

You probably know the two cases, but here's my version so we're working from the same setup. Counterfactual mugging: a highly reliable predictor flips a coin with a tiny chance of heads. Heads comes up and you're asked to hand over $100; if it had come up tails, you'd have been handed $10,000 if and only if the predictor had predicted you'd pay in the heads case. XOR blackmail: you recieve a letter, sent if and only if exactly one of these holds — you have termites (rare, expensive), or you would pay up if you got the letter.

My half-formed idea, which I keep going round on: every treatment of these two cases leans on some notion of what my decision procedure would output in the branch that didn't happen, but I think that notion is doing two quite different jobs in the two cases, and nobody separates them. In the mugging, the counterfactual output is what the *payoff* is made of — you pay because the algorithm that pays is the one that gets handed money in a branch you now know isn't actual. In the blackmail case, the counterfactual output is a *signal* — refusing is meant to be good because whether this letter reached you at all depends on what the algorithm does. One is about the shape of the payoff structure in an unactualized branch, the other is about what your total evidence would have been. My suspicion is that once you pry those apart, the standard pair of verdicts looks a lot more fragile than people let on, and possibly for different reasons in each case.

The wrinkle I want to build the project around, which I haven't seen anywhere: construct a hybrid case that runs both mechanisms at once over the same decision, so they can be made to pull in opposite directions. Something like — the predictor hands you $10,000 in the unactualized branch if you're the paying type, *and* decides whether to tell you the coin outcome based on your disposition too, so your act both buys a counterfactual payoff and screens off part of your own evidence. Then vary the knobs: how accurate the predictor is (does the blackmail-style verdict flip somewhere short of perfection while the mugging-style one doesn't?), and who the counterfactual beneficiary is — a copy of you, a stranger who happens to run the same algorithm, a stranger who doesn't. If the case for paying survives all the way out to the unconnected stranger, I think we've learned something uncomfortable about the view rather than about the case.

What I want in the proposal: state the question so it has a determinate answer (I'm scared mine is three questions in a trenchcoat); say why the existing positions leave it open — concretely, which move in each of the standard theories carries the weight and what happens if you refuse to grant it; the method, month by month-ish, for a year in which I'm also teaching and drafting another chapter; what the possible conclusions are and what would decide between them; and an honest bit on what would count as the project failing. Also tell me if this is two projects, and if so which one I should actually write — I keep getting stuck on whether a payoff to a counterfactual copy you know isn't actual is even the right kind of thing to be a reason, and I can't tell whether that's the heart of it or a different paper.
