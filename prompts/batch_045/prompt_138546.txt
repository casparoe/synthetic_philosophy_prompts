My reading group did a session last week on iterated amplification and distillation — the scheme where you start with a model that can only handle small pieces of a hard question, let it (or a human–model team) break the question into easier sub-questions, recursively amplify those, then train a new model to produce the resulting answers in a single pass, and repeat the whole thing over rounds — and I have not been able to shake an argument my friend Jan made afterwards, over too much wine and a whiteboard. His claim was that the scheme cannot preserve what makes it worth wanting, and the version he wrote out has eight steps, each of which looked fine to me at the time.

(1) The only reason to trust an amplified system's answer is that it was produced by a particular kind of process: the problem is split so no single step has to carry the whole thing, sub-answers get checked and cross-checked, and an overseer can intervene at any node. What we want from distillation is that the fast model inherit whatever it is about that process that made the answers trustworthy — not merely the answers. (2) But the properties that do most of the work are largely silent in the output: the process that checks and finds nothing wrong emits exactly what the process that never checked emits, and the disposition to defer when out of one's depth produces no visible difference except on the rare inputs where it fires. (3) Jan actually ran a toy version of this. Synthetic audit task: chains of forty elementary steps with one planted inconsistency; the amplified system solves it by splitting the chain and having sub-agents flag suspicious steps, and it works. After distillation the student matches the teacher on held-out chains of the same length and composition. When he trained on the intermediate flags as well, the student produced flags that looked right but had stopped predicting its own mistakes — decorative, he called them, form without function. (4) So: if a property leaves no trace in the input–output distribution, or leaves a trace that a learner can reproduce without reproducing the property, then what distillation transmits is underdetermined by the training signal. Many models fit the same data, and the loss cannot tell the one that checks from the one that does not. (5) Therefore the distilled model's answers are not trustworthy in the way the amplified system's answers were, and after enough rounds whatever we originally meant by alignment has been quietly traded for something weaker: matching the teacher on the distribution we happened to sample. (6) None of this is special to machine learning. The condition in (4) governs any case where one agent comes to trust another on the basis of observed behaviour: if a property leaves no behavioural trace, then no quantity of observed outputs could give anyone evidence that the teacher has it. (7) So we have no evidence that any expert, colleague, referee, or institution possesses the process-properties that make their judgment trustworthy, and testimonial and expert knowledge generally is unfounded. (8) Worse, the argument eats its own premise: if it is sound, we never had reason to trust the amplified system in the first place either, so (5) cannot be drawing the contrast it needs.

I cannot accept (7) or (8), but I also cannot find the exit, and Jan is unhelpfully cheerful about it: he says the argument is valid and one of the premises simply has to go. My own guess is that something slips either between (4) and (5) or in the generalisation from (5) to (6), though I cannot say what, and I notice I may just be refusing the conclusion because I dislike it. Of the premises, the one I am most attached to is (2) — it seems to me true of the properties I care about that they are mostly silent in the output — but I would rather be told that (2) is false than keep it at the cost of an argument I think is broken. Which step do you reject, why does that step look fine on the way through, and what does rejecting it commit me to? If there are two or three places one could cut, please price each of them rather than picking silently, and tell me what the strongest version of Jan's argument looks like after I have made the cheapest cut.
