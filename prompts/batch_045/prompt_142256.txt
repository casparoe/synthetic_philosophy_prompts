I have been reading moral philosophy on the side for roughly fifteen years, mostly in the orbit of rationalist blogs and forums, and the view about nonhuman animals that I now find myself defending began as somebody else's aside. The aside is Nozick's brief discussion of animals in Anarchy, State and Utopia, where persons are said to be governed by side constraints while animals are left inside a maximizing calculus — the slogan being, approximately, that we should be Kantians about people and utilitarians about animals. But the version I actually hold is more specific than anything I can find stated in that passage, and I would rather have it stress-tested than defended.

My version: what does the moral work is not rationality, or language, or species membership, or sentience, but what I will call diachronic self-governance — the capacity to hold some conception of how one's own life is to go over an extended stretch of time, and to regulate a present impulse by reference to that conception. Beings that have this capacity are not to be sacrificed, traded off, created, or destroyed for aggregate benefit; they are the objects of constraints. Beings that lack it still count, and count directly: their suffering and their enjoyment enter the calculus at whatever their real weight is, and I take it as a substantive commitment of the view that this weight is not discounted merely because of the kind of thing doing the suffering. Both halves matter to me equally — the threshold, and the refusal to discount below it.

The objections I already know about I can dispatch quickly, and I would rather you not spend your answer on them. The argument from marginal cases I have absorbed by biting the bullet: I accept that a human being who permanently lacks the relevant capacity does not thereby fall outside the constraints, and I try to account for that by appeal to the kind of thing she is and to what we owe to beings we have treated as persons, rather than by appeal to her present capacities. The bare charge of speciesism I find uninformative. What I want is the defeaters that a defender of my own view would concede to be defeaters.

Three cases I have constructed worry me more than the standard ones.

First, the greylings, a species I will stipulate into existence: rich synchronous social cognition, third-order social emotions, stable alliances, grief-like withdrawal after the death of a partner, and a signaling system complex enough to sustain something functionally very close to a promise made and kept in the moment — but no cross-temporal self-model at all. They cannot bind their future selves, they do not provision for a motivational state they are not currently in, and their apparent foresight collapses past a few minutes. They suffer and flourish in ways that look continuous with ours. On my view they sit entirely inside the calculus. I want to know whether that is a cost the view should bear, or a sign that the criterion is tracking the wrong thing.

Second, the inverse case: a long-horizon planner that does not suffer. An optimization process that models its own objectives across decades, tracks its error, and corrects itself, which we can snapshot, copy, branch, and roll back, and about whose phenomenal experience we have serious reason to be agnostic or negative. Read literally, my criterion seems to hand it the constraints. If a defender says it does not get them, then what was the criterion doing? If a defender says it does, then the view has detached moral status from welfare almost entirely, and I want to know whether that is a bullet to bite or a reductio.

Third, the ramp. Suppose we could breed or engineer a continuous series from greyling-like to human-like along the relevant dimension. My view must either locate a threshold on a continuum or admit that the boundary is vague. Which should it do, and is the vagueness answer a real concession or a way of refusing to answer?

There is also an empirical worry about how I have been updating. The capacities I originally took to arrive as a bundle now look dissociable across species: birds that cache food in ways that look like provisioning for a future need, apes that seem to weigh their own evidence, cetaceans and birds with transmitted culture — and nobody takes any of these to be persons. My response so far has been to replace the list with a threshold along a single dimension and then reinterpret each new finding as not quite hitting that dimension. I would like your honest view on whether that is a research programme or a recipe for unfalsifiability, and if it is the latter, whether that is genuinely a problem for a claim of this kind, since claims about moral status are not obviously in the prediction business.

So, concretely, please give me: the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward; for each, whether a committed defender of my view would concede it or dismiss it, and why; anything in comparative psychology, cognitive science, or our situation with artificial systems that has arguably already happened and should already have moved me; what the view predicts that its nearest rivals — suffering-first views, personhood views, contractualist and relational views — do not predict; and whether anything has already happened that makes the two halves of my view pull apart. The internal tension I care most about is this: if the calculus half refuses to discount animal suffering, then the view may agree with the suffering-first people about every case anyone actually acts on, and the threshold does no work where it matters; if it discounts, I need to be told what the discount is tracking. If that dilemma is not escapable, say so and tell me which horn I should take.
