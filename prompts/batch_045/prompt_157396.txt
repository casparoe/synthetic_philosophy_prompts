I am putting together a one-page handout for a graduate seminar on Wittgenstein and the theory of concepts, and I want the handout to be a decision tree rather than a prose survey. The seminar keeps running together three claims that I take to be independent: that the instances of a general term share no single feature; that the term's boundary is not sharp; and that no rule a speaker could state in advance settles every possible application. My hope is that a tree built out of diagnostic questions will force students to see which of these they are actually denying, and that arriving at a leaf will show them that their own combination of answers already commits them to a position with a name.

Build the tree so that it starts from a commitment almost nobody in the room would reject — something like the claim that a competent speaker can correctly apply a general term to a case no one has classified before, and that some such applications are simply wrong — and ends in named positions. Every branch node should be a question about a concrete case, phrased so that a first-year student could answer it without knowing which view it leads to, and so that no node amounts to asking whether the student accepts a theory. Aim for eight to ten internal nodes and eight to ten leaves.

The leaves must include, each characterized substantively and not only by its label: the classical view that correctness is fixed by a condition a speaker could in principle state; the view that for at least some terms the extension is fixed by an underlying nature the speaker may be ignorant of, with deference to whoever knows it; the view that it is fixed by what the speaker's community would say, or by facts about the speaker's social environment; family-resemblance and prototype-or-exemplar views; an inferential-role or conceptual-role view; a quietist position on which the demand for an explanation of correctness is itself what needs dissolving; and a skeptical position on which no fact about the speaker or her community settles what she meant, so that correctness is a matter of what the practice licenses rather than of what is the case. Add leaves for combinations you think are missing from that list, and mark a leaf unnamed if you think nobody defends it.

Three further constraints. First, the extension-fixing cases I care most about are: a liquid indistinguishable from water in every respect available to an ordinary speaker but with a different chemical composition; a thing the community's experts confidently classify as a member of a kind, which later turns out not to be one; and a novel practice governed by rules where it is unclear whether an old word for the practice applies, such as whether a performance produced entirely by a machine counts as music, or whether a competitive activity conducted entirely through text counts as a game. Use at least these, and add cases only where they earn their place. Second, where the answers to two nodes are logically independent of each other, say so explicitly rather than nesting them as though one settled the other, and tell me which of the three claims in my first paragraph each node is really testing. Third, flag at least two pairs of leaves your tree cannot separate — family-resemblance and prototype views, or a quietist dissolution and a deflationary theory of correctness, are the pairs I expect — and say either what further question would pry them apart or why no question will.

For each leaf, give the name, one sentence stating the position's most awkward commitment, and one case, from my list or of your own, on which the position yields a prediction most students will find counterintuitive. Then identify two or three combinations of answers that the logical space allows but that have no defenders, and diagnose each as incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Finally, mark where the debate about vagueness would attach to your tree and say in one sentence why you have kept it off the main trunk, since several students will raise it and I would rather the handout anticipate them. Do not include a bibliography or any references by title or date; I will supply the readings, and I want the handout to stand on the cases and the questions alone.
