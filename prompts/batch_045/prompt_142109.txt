I sit on my university's student health benefits committee, and we have now spent two meetings failing to write down what we think we are already doing. Everyone agrees on the slogan — the plan pays for treatment, not for enhancement — but every attempt to turn the slogan into a rule founders on the same question: a baseline relative to what? Here is roughly where we landed, and I want to know what position we have accidentally signed ourselves up for. The draft says the plan covers any intervention whose aim is to return a student to the functional level they would have occupied but for a specific identifiable setback — an illness, an injury, a congenital condition, a diagnosed disorder — or to prevent such a setback; and that it does not cover interventions aimed at lifting a student above that counterfactual level. Then come the items we could not bring ourselves to exclude and therefore wrote in by hand: contraception and fertility treatment; vaccines; correction of ordinary age-related decline such as presbyopia, hearing loss and cataracts; help for conditions that are statistically entirely normal in our student population, including short stature with no hormone deficiency and moderate anxiety; and academic accommodations such as extra exam time. And then the items we did exclude: cosmetic procedures that improve on a student's pre-existing appearance, stimulant medication for a student with no diagnosis, and coaching that mainly raises a competitive score rather than any underlying capacity.

What bothers me is that the handwritten exceptions seem to eat the rule. If we pay for reading glasses, then the relevant normal is not the species-typical function of a fifty-five-year-old but some youthful baseline that almost nobody of that age actually meets, so we are not restoring normal function in the statistical sense at all. If we pay for help with conditions that are statistically normal, we are not even restoring what is typical for the student's age group. If we pay for vaccines we are routinely raising people above anything unaided nature would have given them, which is the thing the rule says we don't do. And the two exclusions cut against each other: extra exam time and score-focused coaching both make a student better off in a competitive, roughly zero-sum contest, yet we fund one and refuse the other on the grounds that only the second is an advantage — even though extra exam time does not restore any capacity in the student, it changes the test, which looks like neither therapy nor enhancement but a third thing our slogan has no room for. Worst of all, our counterfactual-self baseline seems to make responsibility irrelevant: a student who destroyed their own hearing with loud headphones gets the same coverage as one born hard of hearing, while the same university happily bills students for equipment they break and runs an entire conduct system premised on holding people responsible for what they brought on themselves. A friend on the committee also pointed out that for congenital conditions the but-for self is someone who would have been a different person entirely, so our rule may be quietly relying on some view about who counts as the same individual across counterfactual variation.

So: what theory of the therapy/enhancement distinction, or of what medicine is for, does a policy like ours actually presuppose? Please reconstruct the implicit view, tell me which parts of it the policy strictly requires and which are merely a comfortable fit, say whether the parts are consistent with one another, and let me know how much of the same policy a rival theory — a welfare-based one, a threshold or capabilities-based one, a view that makes compensation for bad luck central, or a normal-function view — could equally have produced, and what in the document would have to be rewritten if we swapped our implicit theory for one of those. Don't give me reading lists or citations; I want the argument, and I have no way to check references anyway.

Please structure the reply with the following sections, in this order: "The implicit theory" (about 250 words), "Strict presuppositions vs. comfortable fits" (about 150 words), "Where the handwritten exceptions bite" (about 200 words), "Rival theories that would sign off anyway" (about 150 words), "What would have to change" (about 150 words), and "Bottom line" (about 75 words).
