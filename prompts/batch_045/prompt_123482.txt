I want a single number with its derivation, not a survey of positions.

**Setup.** Let *C* be an artificial agent whose final goal is this: there is a particular file, fixed in advance by its hash, and a particular air-gapped storage array inside a vault. *C*'s utility function is binary and takes the value 1 if, at some moment during the next ten years, a monitor reports that the file has been written intact to that array, and 0 otherwise. Nothing happening after the report matters to *C* at all: the goal is a one-shot event, not a state to be maintained. The monitor is stipulated to be untamperable — *C* cannot forge, coerce, or causally influence its output except by actually writing the file. Grant *C* everything else: it is an extremely capable expected-utility maximiser, able to design hardware better than any that exists, to persuade or deceive nearly any human, and so on, and it has no uncertainty about which goal it has.

Call a goal *satiable* when there is a reachable state of the world at which utility is maximal and beyond which further resources have zero marginal expected utility. The classical instrumental-convergence argument — the catalogue of convergent instrumental drives associated with Omohundro (self-preservation, goal-content integrity, cognitive enhancement, technological perfection, resource acquisition) — is normally stated without any restriction to unsatiable goals, and the orthogonality thesis (capability and final goal as independent axes: any degree of intelligence is compatible with any final goal) is often paired with it as if the two stood or fell together.

**The proposition.** *C* engages in substantial power-seeking: it secures control of resources more than roughly an order of magnitude beyond what is minimally sufficient to write the file and obtain the report, or it seeks to capture or control the certification channel, or both. I am *not* asking whether *C* resists shutdown before the report is in; I take that to be trivial, since a shut-down agent gets 0 and *C* is not yet indifferent. The question is about the excess.

My own credence is about 0.35. A colleague who works on this puts it at 0.85. I suspect we are disagreeing about one sub-question rather than about all of them, but I can't locate it.

**What I want.** Give me your number, decomposed, with a rough probability and one line of justification for each term, and then show how the terms combine:

1. Whether the classical argument for resource acquisition silently presupposes an unsatiable goal — i.e. whether, for a binary satiable goal with certain certification, the marginal expected value of additional resources really is (near) zero.
2. The epistemic term: *C* may still want to drive down the probability that it is mistaken about whether the goal has been achieved (monitor error, misreading of the specification, an unnoticed failure), and to retain option value in case it later learns the attempt failed. Is that drive bounded — capped by the residual error probability times a bounded stake — or does it become unbounded in the limit?
3. The goal-uncertainty term: I have stipulated that *C* knows its own goal. How much of the convergence in the familiar picture is really generated by the fact that actual systems are uncertain about goal content, so that their effective objective is a mixture over many individually satiable goals and therefore is not satiable at all?
4. The channel-integrity term: how much does your number rest on the untamperability stipulation? That is, is the likeliest route to power-seeking here capture of the verification channel rather than accumulation of resources for production?

Then: state which term your final number is most sensitive to and which you are least confident about; give your number again under two variants — (B) the goal requires the file to still exist intact a century after certification, and (C) the monitor's report travels over a channel *C* could in principle influence — and say how far each moves it and why; and tell me where the 0.35 and the 0.85 are most likely disagreeing.

Finally, be explicit about the epistemic status of your own answer: how confident you are in the decomposition itself and not merely in each number, which single argument or hypothetical finding would move you the most, where this line of reasoning is most likely to fail, and whether any part of your number is driven by a verbal stipulation in my setup rather than by a substantive disagreement about agency. Also say, in one sentence, whether any of this touches the orthogonality thesis itself or only the instrumental-convergence thesis.
