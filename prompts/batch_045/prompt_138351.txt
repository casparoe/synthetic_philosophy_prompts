hey — I teach a first-year seminar on information and democracy: 24 students, one 80-minute block, no laptops in the room (we do everything on paper and index cards, I hand things out). I want a single-session game that makes them feel the difference between an epistemic bubble and an echo chamber — the usual distinction (Nguyen's version, I think): in a bubble the other side is simply missing from your feed, in an echo chamber the other side is present but has been discredited in advance, so more exposure to it hardens you rather than moving you.

The problem with every version of this I've tried is that the two look identical from the inside — everyone feels like they've looked into it and come to their own conclusion — and if I explain the distinction up front the whole thing dies. So I want the difference to show up in the data the game produces, not in anything I say. They shouldn't hear either term until afterwards.

What I have in mind, roughly, and you should throw out whatever doesn't work: a fictional controversy nobody has priors about (I was thinking an invented 1970s transit project — did it fail because of the engineering or because of the financing? — with a ground truth only I know, so I can score them at the end and being wrong costs them something). Over four or five rounds each student recieves a private bulletin of source-cards. Group A's bulletins just omit the sources that tell against the project. Group B's bulletins include those same sources, but they've been attributed, in an earlier round, to a source their own in-group already has a reason to distrust. Then one round where I open the archive and every source is available to everyone: same intervention, same room, same minute, opposite responses. Points for accuracy, so there's a real if small stake — a participation credit and a coffee voucher for the winning team.

Two things I especially want built in:

1. A diagnostic that seperates the two mechanisms from behaviour, not from self-report. My idea was a steelman round — before any reveal, everyone writes the strongest version of the other side's case — on the theory that the bubble group literally can't (they've never seen the arguments) while the echo-chamber group can write it beautifully and still won't move. If you've got a less transparent instrument, or one that doesn't tip the design, use it instead.

2. The bit I care most about and can't find a game for anywhere: the fixes are different, and asymmetric. Adding sources fixes a bubble; adding sources to an echo chamber makes it worse. What moves an echo chamber is trust repair, and trust repair has a cost structure — the only channel that seems to work is an in-group member vouching for the discredited source, and vouching spends their own credibility, so it's a volunteer's-dilemma-ish setup: everyone would be better off if one respected person went first, but each individual loses by going first. I want that priced into the game as an actual move students can make or decline, with a payoff scheme that makes declining individually rational, so that the room visibly gets stuck and can see itself stuck.

What I need from you: the fictional case and the actual card text (or enough of it that I can write the rest myself), a materials list, setup instructions, a round-by-round timeline with minute marks, the payoff scheme, the diagnostic and how to score it in five minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the session onto the two mechanisms.

Also, honestly: what will go wrong the first time I run it, and what do I do in each case? I assume at least these three — someone works out the design by round two and starts playing along, the distrust priming in group B doesn't take, and nobody cares enough about a fake transit project to hold an opinion at all. And be frank about what the game can't reproduce and I'll have to say out loud: the years-long trust history, the identity investment, the fact that real ones are entered into voluntarily by trusting the wrong people.

Last thing: give me a confidence note on the design as a whole. How confident are you that it actually seperates the two mechanisms rather than just measuring general open-mindedness or persuadability, and single out the one claim you're least sure of.
