I am putting together a set of short teaching dialogues for a third-year decision theory course, and I need one on a variant of Newcomb's problem that I have been using in supervisions. The variant is mine, so nothing can be lifted from a textbook, and I want the argument conducted on the case rather than on slogans.

The case. Tomorrow morning you will be shown two boxes. Box A is transparent and contains $1,000. Box B is opaque; it contains $1,000,000 if and only if the Predictor's prediction, sealed tonight, says that you will take Box B alone, and is empty otherwise. You may take Box B alone or both boxes; the contents are not displayed to you before you choose.

Tonight, one hour before she seals her prediction, you must press one of two buttons, and she is told which one you pressed:

- DISCLOSE: you hand her an exact specification of your decision procedure and she simulates you. Her prediction is then correct with probability 0.99.
- CONCEAL: she is given nothing about you and predicts by tossing a fair coin. Her prediction is then correct with probability 0.5, and is statistically independent of everything about you.

Her prediction is always a prediction of what you will do in the room tomorrow. Separately, and before you press either button, you may install a commitment device: an irrevocable arrangement that will physically stop you from taking Box A tomorrow. The Predictor is informed of any such device and takes it into account.

The two speakers. Mira holds that at the moment of choice you should take both boxes: the prediction is already sealed, the money is in Box B or it is not, and nothing you do now alters that, so taking Box A as well adds $1,000 whichever way the prediction went. Aris holds that you should take Box B alone: your taking Box B alone would be strong evidence that the prediction said so, and it is that evidential bearing, not causal influence, that should guide you. Both are experienced philosophers who have taught this material for years; neither is slower than the other. They are arguing in front of you in the antechamber, an hour before you must press a button, each trying to get you to adopt their package of advice — which button to press, whether to install the device, and what to do at the boxes.

Assume they have both done the arithmetic correctly and agree on it, and do not let the dialogue stop to re-derive it: disclose and take one box is worth about $990,000; conceal and take both boxes about $501,000; disclose and take both boxes about $11,000; conceal and take one box about $500,000; disclose with the device in place about $990,000. The interesting material begins once those numbers are on the table.

What I want from the dialogue:

1. The argument stays on this case — the two-stage structure, the two buttons, the device, the figures above — and not on generalities about causation, correlation, or free will.
2. Both of them must answer the claim that is actually their opponent's strongest, and I mean the strongest version: Mira must face the fact that her package is worth roughly half of Aris's, and Aris must face the fact that his recommended act at the boxes is one he would regard as a mistake if he learned the contents. Neither may dispose of a point by calling it an intuition, a confusion, or a verbal dispute.
3. Every speech after the opening two responds to something said in the speech immediately before. I will be reading specifically for speeches that restate a position instead of replying to one.
4. No citations, no authors' names, no experiments, no surveys, no polling of students, and no appeal to what most decision theorists hold. They may name their own position once each; after that, argue it.
5. Let the disagreement be relocated rather than dissolved. They should end up agreeing about the arithmetic and about which packages are available, and still disagreeing about something sharper and more local than what they started on — I have in mind the question of what it is rational to do about your own predictability, given that being predictable is worth a great deal to a one-boxer and costs a great deal to a two-boxer. No closing move of the form we were really talking past each other or it depends what you mean by rational.

Format: no narration, no stage directions, no scene-setting after the first line — just the speeches, each headed by the speaker's name in bold. Hard limit of 900 words for the dialogue itself. After the final speech, add two lines, each of at most 30 words, headed **What Mira still has to answer:** and **What Aris still has to answer:**, in which each names the single question the other has left open. Those two lines do not count against the word limit.
