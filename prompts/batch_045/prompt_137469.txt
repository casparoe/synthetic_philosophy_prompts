I am writing a paper on the ethics of torture and keep running into a recurring pattern in the arguments that I cannot name, and I would rather not coin a name for it until I know whether the ground is already occupied.

The pattern shows up most clearly in the ticking-bomb case. The case is always told as a one-off: one captive, one device, one interrogation, after which the absolute prohibition is restored and nothing else has changed. Critics say the stipulation is unrealistic; defenders say we are asking about principle, not practice. What interests me is that the one-off stipulation looks to me not merely optimistic but self-consuming, in three separate respects.

Capacity. Interrogational torture cannot be performed on demand. To be available in the emergency one needs people already trained, premises, a protocol, a chain of authorisation. The standing capacity must pre-exist the occasion and outlive it, so the act cannot be the exception that leaves no trace: the exception presupposes exactly the standing arrangement the prohibition was meant to rule out.

Doctrine. If the act is to be lawful rather than merely excused afterwards, some authorising rule or necessity defence has to be articulated, and a rule is general in form. It will still be available in later cases where the stipulated features are absent, or merely asserted by someone with an interest in asserting them.

The agent and the audience. After the first interrogation the officials involved are people who have done this, and future captives and future interrogators know it. The meaning and the expected cost of the practice change, not through abuse or bad faith but as a necessary consequence of the first instance having occurred.

The common structure, as I see it: the act is permissible, if it is, only under a condition — that it be isolated, unpractised, unprovided-for — which its own performance necessarily destroys. The justification therefore applies at most once, and a reason that can by its nature apply only once seems an odd thing to build into a code.

The near misses I have tried and rejected: the slippery slope (mine is not a prediction about what happens if people are weak or corrupt; it would hold under perfect compliance), dirty hands (that concerns the agent's moral remainder, not what the act does to the later choice set), talk of a state of exception (that concerns sovereignty and legal order, and the exception there is constitutive and repeatable rather than used up by its own occurrence), moral hazard (about incentives, and contingent). I have also wondered about the old puzzles about nuclear threats, where a threat is useful only if there is some chance of its being carried out though carrying it out would be ruinous — but that is about credibility and dispositions, whereas mine is about changes to the world the act brings about.

So my question: is there an established concept for an act that destroys the condition of its own permissibility, or more precisely for a justification that is essentially one-off because satisfying it necessarily eliminates its own conditions of application? If there are several neighbouring notions, tell me which is closest and exactly where my case departs from each. If nothing fits, coin and define one: the definition, at least two clear instances, and at least two cases that look like instances but are not — I want the boundary drawn, not merely the centre — and then say what follows for how the ticking-bomb case ought to be argued. A bare label will not help me; the carving is the point. And if the honest verdict is that what I have described dissolves into two or three familiar things, I would rather be told that than handed a name.

I do not need references, a reading list, or a literature review; I will chase anything myself. Please keep the whole answer under 600 words.
