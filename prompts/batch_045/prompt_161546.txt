Analyze the reception of the four-colour theorem through the lens of Carroll's 1895 dialogue about the justification of deduction. The facts I want you to work from are these: in 1976 Appel and Haken announced a proof that depended on a machine check of a very large set of reducible configurations, and it was widely resisted on the ground that no human being could survey it; in 2005 Georges Gonthier produced a machine-checked formalisation of the theorem in a general-purpose proof assistant, and that is generally taken to have quieted most of the remaining doubt.

First the mapping, done carefully. In Carroll's dialogue the Tortoise grants the two premises of a one-step modus ponens argument, grants the conditional linking them to the conclusion, and still declines the conclusion; each further conditional Achilles extracts is met with the same demand, and the list grows without end. Read this as a thesis about rules rather than premises: no stock of accepted propositions, however large, amounts to drawing an inference, since inferring is something one does with a rule. Now say whether the four-colour controversy is an instance of it. The 1976 objector grants that the program ran and reported the configurations reducible and the case analysis exhaustive, and still withholds the theorem; the reply adds that the program is sound, and that compiler and hardware did what they were supposed to do. Is that Carroll's regress, or a different one?

In particular, separate two things I suspect get conflated: a logical regress about the relation between rules and premises, and what I will call a reliability regress, in which each link — code, compiler, machine, the physics of the machine — is supported by a further contingent premise about the trustworthiness of an artefact. Say which of the two Carroll's argument actually generates, which one the four-colour controversy was really about, and, if the case mixes them, where each runs out.

Then run the analysis in the other direction. Does the later formalisation answer the Tortoise? A proof checker does not supply a new premise; it implements a rule in a machine, and a user's acceptance of its verdict is itself an inference from a report. So argue whether the formalisation closes the regress, relocates it into a small kernel, or changes the subject. Test your answer: would a Tortoise who refused modus ponens be moved by a certificate issued by a checker that has been independently re-implemented many times over? What would have to be true for a machine to do for deduction what Achilles could not do by adding premises? And where Carroll's argument is silent about this case — about error rates, about the social division of epistemic labour among mathematicians, about what the community was entitled to believe in the interval — say so rather than forcing the regress to speak. Conclude by saying what the episode shows about the regress itself: a genuine problem about the justification of deduction, or an artefact of an unrealistic demand about what justification must look like.
