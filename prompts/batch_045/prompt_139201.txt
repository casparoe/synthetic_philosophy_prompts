I organize a small graduate-run colloquium series in my department — eight or nine people in a seminar room, a paper circulated in advance, forty minutes of questions — and next month's speaker is a visiting philosopher I would rather not name, who has spent roughly a decade defending a broadly Davidsonian, partitionist account of self-deception: the claim that the self-deceived agent really does, in a sense worth taking literally, intentionally bring it about that she comes to believe what she has reason to think false, and that the coherence of this is secured not by a single unified mind somehow believing both p and not-p, but by a division of the cognitive system into relatively insulated sub-systems, one playing the role of deceiver and the other of deceived. The turn in her recent work is what made me invite her. She now argues that the partitions need not be realized wholly inside the skull: one of the standard ways an agent insulates the unwelcome belief is by deliberately reshaping her own informational environment — muting the colleague who raises the topic, joining a group in which the topic never comes up, and above all curating a recommendation system (a feed, a set of accounts, a search and watch history) whose output she can then encounter as if it were independent evidence arriving from the world. Her slogan is that the environment does part of the work that intra-psychic repression was supposed to do, and that in the twenty-first century most self-deception worth studying has this partly outsourced structure.

I am moderating the Q&A and I badly want to avoid what happened last term, when the speaker spent the whole hour restating the paradoxes at textbook level and the room went flat. Could you draft me eight to ten questions, ordered, that press on the joints of her position rather than on its exposition? Assume an audience that already knows the standard static problem (how the very same person can be said to hold both the unwelcome belief and the comforting one) and the standard dynamic problem (how an intention to make oneself believe something can avoid being self-defeating, given that forming the intention seems to give away exactly what one is trying to hide). I want at least one question each on: whether the environmental version of partitioning is genuinely a case of self-deception rather than of plain willful ignorance or evidence-avoidance, given that the agent's intention now looks like an ordinary intention to change her inputs; whether her account can still deliver the distinctive phenomenology people report in these cases — the nagging sense that something is being kept at bay, the selective anxiety — or whether that has to be read off the view as an optional extra; how she handles clinical cases such as anosognosia for hemiplegia and Anton's syndrome, where there is no curation and arguably no intention at all, and whether she bites the bullet and counts these as a separate phenomenon or stretches the account to cover them; what work, if any, is left for the notion of a motive, as opposed to a mere bias in evidence-gathering, once the partitions are partly in the world; and whether the predictive-processing alternative — self-deception as the over-weighting of a prior's precision, on a continuum with delusion and confabulation — explains something her view leaves unexplained.

For each question, sketch the reply she is most likely to give and then one follow-up designed to make that particular reply do real work instead of gliding past it: if she says the sub-systems are merely sub-personal and so involve no homunculus, press on what makes the resulting state hers rather than a mere property of her surroundings; if she says some difficulty is an empirical rather than a conceptual one, press on which empirical finding would settle it and why she is not thereby conceding the substance. Please arrange the sequence so that her two most reliable escape routes — that partitioning is sub-personal and thus metaphysically cheap, and that hard cases are someone else's empirical problem — become progressively harder to reach for, and close with a single question about what could count as evidence against the view as a whole. No restating-the-view questions, no icebreakers, and please don't pad the answer with references or a reading list: I have the bibliography, and what I need is the dialectic.
