I am a PhD student in political science working on problems at the intersection of political theory and the measurement of political violence, and I am applying for a nine-month dissertation-completion fellowship. The selection committee wants a short proposal (roughly 900 words) for the one remaining chapter of my dissertation, and I would like help drafting it.

My half-formed idea is this. The public and academic fight over whether the world has become less violent looks like an empirical dispute, but I suspect a large share of it is not: it is a dispute about which quantity is being tracked, disguised as a dispute about the quantity's direction. Everyone agrees that there are multiple defensible measures, and then each side reports its own measure's trend as if that settled the original question. My suspicion is that the interesting philosophical work is not "have we defined violence correctly?" but something more structural: some concepts support determinate trend claims because their instances are homogeneous enough to be summed on a single scale, whereas others are more like index concepts, where a trend claim is only determinate once a weighting has been stipulated, and the weighting is not itself a matter of fact. I want the chapter to argue that violence is a mixed or patchwork case: determinate at the level of fine-grained sub-kinds (say, intentional one-on-one killing of adults by strangers), indeterminate at the level of the aggregate, and that the appearance of a single empirical controversy is produced by sliding between these levels.

Concretely, I want to work with a small formal apparatus: a family of measures specified by three parameters — an incidence variable (realized events, victim-hours of suffering, or ex ante risk-weighted expected victimization), a denominator (population-year, person-lifetime, or per social encounter), and a severity weighting (which may or may not admit lexical priority among kinds of harm). I have some examples where these parameters pull the trend in different directions: a constant annual per-capita homicide rate is compatible with rising lifetime risk when life expectancy rises, and with falling per-stranger-encounter risk when the density of anonymous contact grows faster than the population; and measures of realized harm can fall while measures of coercive capacity rise, if private violence has been displaced by policing, surveillance and incarceration rather than eliminated. I want the proposal to use these to pose the question precisely.

What I need from the proposal:

- A precise statement of the chapter's question, formulated so that it has a determinate answer rather than being a request to "think about violence." I want it to separate the factual question (on a fixed well-specified measure, what is the trend?) from the normative question (which measure should carry the argument?) from the metaphysical question (is there, independently of any stipulation, a fact of the matter about the trend in violence as such?).

- Why the existing answers leave the question open: why better data alone will not settle it, why more careful conceptual analysis or stipulative definition will not settle it either, and what each of those two obvious approaches would miss.

- The method I would actually use over the nine months, at a realistic level of detail, and why that method rather than the alternatives. I would like the approach to be measurement-theoretic and purpose-relative: map the leading purposes for which people make violence-trend claims (forecasting, assigning blame or credit to institutions, comparing eras, estimating the moral urgency of intervention) onto the measures those purposes actually require, and see whether the disagreement can be re-described as a conflict between purposes rather than a conflict about the world.

- The candidate conclusions I might reach, and which considerations would decide between them. I have in mind three: a unitarian view (there is one fact of the matter about the direction of violence, and we are currently unsure of it); a conventionalist view (there is no such fact beyond stipulated weights); and the patchwork or localist view I sketched above, on which determinacy holds within homogeneous sub-kinds and fails for the aggregate. I want the proposal to say what kind of evidence or argument would discriminate among these.

- What would count as the project failing, stated honestly: for instance, if the purpose-relative mapping turns out not to discriminate between measures at all, or if the sub-kinds themselves turn out to be measurement-dependent.

- An honest treatment of scope, including what I should cut for a single chapter in nine months and what would have to be deferred.

- Finally, a short note on the practical deliverable: I would like the chapter to end with something usable — an audit template that anyone making a violence-trend claim could complete, stating their incidence variable, denominator, severity weights and purpose, and separating the empirical content of their claim from the stipulative content.

I will write the literature section and add all citations myself, so please do not propose a bibliography, a reading list, or any author-and-date references: refer to positions and arguments descriptively instead. Please also keep the proposal self-contained, since it should be intelligible without access to any external sources.
