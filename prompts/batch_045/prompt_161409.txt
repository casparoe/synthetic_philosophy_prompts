I'm writing a comparative briefing on statutory limits on working time for a labour-market think tank, and the thing I keep getting stuck on is an apparent inconsistency inside the rules themselves rather than in the commentary about them. I'd like you to reconstruct the theory of work and leisure that the rules presuppose.

The package I'm looking at has three parts. First, the mandating of leisure: France's thirty-five-hour statutory week and the reduction days that go with it; the wider European rule that paid annual leave must actually be taken and cannot simply be bought out for cash; the comparatively recent provisions requiring larger employers to negotiate about employees' use of digital tools outside working hours; and, older than any of these, the way the law protects time off *in common* — the weekend, the public holiday, the long political fight over Sunday trading — rather than merely guaranteeing each person a fixed quota of free hours somewhere in the week. Second, the exemptions: overtime limits and premia are routinely disapplied for senior and salaried staff, whose time is reckoned in days worked or deliverables produced rather than in hours, and for whole occupational categories, on the ground that their work is autonomous or that their pay makes protection unnecessary. Third, karoshi: Japan treats death and suicide from overwork as compensable work-related events, assessed against an administratively set threshold of overtime hours in the months beforehand, has since 2014 had a national framework aimed at preventing them, and routes the consequences through compensation to families rather than treating such deaths as ordinary failures of the employer's safety duty — all this in a working culture in which a great deal of overtime is unpaid and nominally voluntary.

Three things strike me as pulling in different directions. The law seems indifferent to *when* free hours fall if leisure is private recuperation, yet it has historically fought hard to make them simultaneous, and staggering would in many ways be more convenient; if leisure were mere input to productivity, caps should be set by productivity evidence and by nothing else. The caps regulate the average working week but say nothing about its dispersion, so a country can meet them while a substantial minority work far longer than everyone else and another minority cannot get the hours they want. And the rules refuse to let a worker sell their leave, which presupposes that the worker's own trade-offs about time cannot be trusted, while simultaneously holding employers responsible for hours that the worker appears to have chosen.

Two distinctions I've been trying on the material. Call the first *synchronised leisure*: the hypothesis that an hour of free time is worth more when the people you would spend it with are free at the same moment, so that what the law really protects is co-availability rather than rest. Call the second *the temporal Gini*: the claim that what matters is not the mean but the spread of leisure, and that a cap on the mean is close to silent about the right tail.

Please do the following. Reconstruct the implicit theory of work and leisure that the package as a whole only makes sense under, and say how it sits with the 'calling' picture of work that Weber described, on which idleness is a moral failing and leisure wants earning. Mark clearly which commitments each element strictly requires and which it merely fits well with, and where several rival theories would license exactly the same rule. Finally, tell me what would concretely have to change in policy — which instruments you'd add, drop, or redesign — if the synchronisation story, or the dispersion story, were taken seriously as the justification instead.
