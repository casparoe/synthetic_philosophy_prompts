Earlier this year I was my brother's surrogate decision-maker after a catastrophic brain injury, and I was the one who consented to donation after the withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment. I have since been asked to sit on the committee that is revising our hospital's policy on the determination of death and on organ donation, and before I put my name to anything I would like to know whether the views I find myself holding actually hang together. Here is the set, stated as plainly as I can, in the terms we actually use at the bedside.

(a) Death is one thing: the irreversible loss of an organism's capacity to function as an integrated whole. It is a biological state, not a legal fiction, not a convention, and not a function of what we value or what we would like to do with the body — and for any given patient there is a determinate time at which they die. The neurological and circulatory criteria are, on this view, two tests for one and the same state.

(b) Whole-brain death — irreversible loss of all function of the brain, including the brainstem, properly tested — is death of the human being, not merely a licence to stop treating.

(c) Higher-brain or personhood-based accounts are wrong. A patient in a permanent vegetative state, or with end-stage dementia, is alive; such patients are not dead and are not candidates for organ procurement.

(d) Determination of death by circulatory criteria after the withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment is legitimate. Our protocol specifies a period of continuous asystole with no intervention (five minutes), after which death is declared and procurement for controlled donation after circulatory death may proceed. I accept this practice.

(e) The right reading of "irreversible" in (d) is permanent rather than strictly irreversible: circulation will not resume on its own, and it will not be restarted because a decision has been made not to restart it. I take strict irreversibility to be unachievable at the bedside, since the same circulation could in many cases be restored by intervention.

(f) The dead donor rule is non-negotiable: the procurement of organs must not itself cause the death of the donor, and no one may be made dead by, or for the sake of, what we take out of them.

(g) I am inclined to vote in favour of adding thoracoabdominal normothermic regional perfusion to our controlled donation protocol: after the declaration of death, restart circulation in the torso using a circuit so that the abdominal organs are reperfused and preserved, while the vessels supplying the brain are surgically occluded so that cerebral circulation is not restored.

(h) I think it can be right to maintain the body of a brain-dead pregnant woman on a ventilator for weeks in order to bring the fetus to viability, and I do not think that the possibility of doing so shows that she was alive all along.

(i) Whether a patient is dead should not depend on anyone's intentions, decisions, or plans for their body. The determination should come out the same for donors and for non-donors.

What I want is an audit rather than an introduction. Go through the set and tell me which of these commitments actually pull against one another, which pairs merely look like they do, and for each real tension what would have to be given up and what giving it up would cost me — including whether some of them can be repaired by a gloss rather than abandoned. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equal, and tell me if there is a single revision that clears up more than one of them at once. I would also like to know whether any item is doing more work in my thinking than I realise, or whether the set is consistent only because I am using a term in two different senses.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the single most important thing you think I have wrong or right about this package — and give the reasoning afterward. No preamble and no restatement of what I have asked.
