I'm a PhD student in philosophy — my own stuff is on expertise and peer disagreement, so I only know the social epistemology side from the outside — and a friend in the political theory reading group has handed me an argument I cant get out of. He says the notion of testimonial injustice (Fricker-style: a credibility deficit that's down to identity prejudice) either collapses or proves too much. Here it is:

(1) A speaker suffers testimonial injustice when she gets less credibility than she's due, and the deficit is caused by identity prejudice.
(2) How much credibility a speaker is due is a function of how reliable she is — nothing else.
(3) Reliability shows up in a track record: the hit rate of her assertions in the relevant domain, as recorded.
(4) Whose assertions get recorded and checked depends on who has been taken seriously, and past credibility judgements have been infected by identity prejudice.
(5) So where prejudice depressed someone's credibility, it also depressed her recorded record; a hearer who calibrates scrupulously to the record will give her less credibility than she's really due, and the shortfall traces back to prejudice. A postdoc in my dept calls these "inherited deficits" — deficits that come through the evidence base rather than through the hearer's own stereotyping.
(6) By (1) that's a testimonial injustice even though nobody in the room is personally prejudiced; by (2) and (3) it isn't, because the hearer gave exactly what the evidence supported. So the account both requires and forbids the same credibility judgement.
(7) Either "credibility due" has no determinate content — it depends on which baseline you pick, calibration to the available evidence versus what a prejudice-free hearer would have given — in which case the concept can't tell you anything in the cases it was built for, or else any reliance on track records and expertise counts as testimonial injustice, which is absurd: my supervisor isn't wronging anyone when he declines to take my word for the mass of the Higgs, and neither is a referee who rejects a sloppy paper.
(8) So the framework is either empty or trivializing.

I want to say he's wrong but I can't locate the step. My instinct is that (1), and the thought in (6) that the discount can still be a wronging when nobody present is prejudiced, are the bits worth keeping — that seemed to be the whole point of introducing the notion — and that (2) or (3) is the cheapest thing to give up. But that's just a hunch and I'd rather know what I'm paying. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular: if I keep (1) and the (6) thought, do I have to say that the credibility due to a speaker isn't fixed by her reliability at all — and if so, what does fix it, and does that commit me to the (7) claim that my supervisor is committing an injustice? Is the calibration-baseline / counterfactual-baseline distinction doing any real work here, or is it just relabelling the problem? And is there a version of the account that handles inherited deficits without having to say the hearer's own attitudes are prejudiced?

(Also if you think the argument is fine and the conclusion is true, say that — I'd rather know than be comforted.)
