---

Ryle's target is what he calls the intellectualist legend: the idea that knowing how to do something — ride a bike, make a joke, reason well — is really just a matter of knowing that such-and-such is the case, i.e. that competence is a species of propositional knowledge. His main argument against it is the regress argument, which is basically a version of the same regress you get in Lewis Carroll's *What the Tortoise Said to Achilles*. The underlying point is that no rule can determine its own application: given a rule you still need a further rule telling you how to apply it, and then a third, and so on. Applied to knowledge-how: to φ intelligently I must first consult a proposition telling me how to φ, but consulting a proposition is itself an action that has to be done intelligently, so I would need to consult a proposition about how to consult propositions, and then a proposition about that, and so on forever. Since we obviously do manage to act, and to act intelligently, the intellectualist analysis has to be wrong: intelligent cannot be defined in terms of intellectual, and knowing how cannot be defined in terms of knowing that. The two are just different kinds of knowledge.

Ryle's own positive view is that knowing how is a disposition — nothing over and above a tendency to produce the right kind of behaviour in the right circumstances, a single-track habit if you like. That is why he thinks knowing-how is prior to knowing-that: you have to know how to weigh evidence and follow an argument before you can come to know any proposition, but not the other way round. It also explains why he is happy to say that an animal, or even a machine, that reliably produces the right behaviour knows how to do the thing in question, since there is no further propositional fact that has to hold of it.

Stanley and Williamson revive intellectualism. Their claim is that knowing how to φ is a matter of knowing, of some way w, that w is a way to φ — knowledge of a proposition, but grasped under a practical mode of presentation rather than a theoretical one, and that is what connects it to action. Their main evidence is linguistic: knows-how ascriptions behave like other embedded-question ascriptions (Hannah knows how to swim means roughly the same as Hannah knows how one can swim), and knowledge-wh is just knowledge-that. Against the regress they argue that the argument only gets going if you assume that employing your knowledge that p requires occurrently contemplating or entertaining p, and that assumption is false: you can know a fact without having it before your mind. So they escape the regress, and they also escape the objection from gradability and from procedural memory, since on their account knowing how is a propositional attitude and propositional attitudes come in degrees just as much as abilities do. One consequence they seem to be stuck with is that you could know how to swim without being able to swim, since knowledge-that doesn't by itself require any ability — but that's a bullet worth biting in exchange for the linguistic data.

On balance I think Ryle's regress is the strongest argument available here, but Stanley and Williamson's reply does get them out of it, so (b) comes out roughly a draw.
