I teach a team-taught upper-level seminar called "Self-Reference and Self-Replication," and I'm currently drafting the final two sessions, which turn to Hofstadter's typogenetics chapter of *Gödel, Escher, Bach* and to the claim the chapter is built to support: that a strand of DNA getting itself reproduced is, in structure, the same trick Gödel used to get a formal system to talk about itself. My colleague from the genetics department has a standing objection which I have never adequately answered: that the whole thing is wordplay with a codon table drawn beside it, and that the only reason a typogenetic strand can "refer" to its own reproduction is that Hofstadter designed the enzyme table so that it would. A student put the sharpest version of the worry last week: if self-replication were really self-reference, then any genome coding for its own DNA polymerase would already be a Gödel sentence — and it plainly isn't, because the polymerase is sequence-agnostic and will copy any template you put in front of it. The student's question is really about whether the "self" in self-replication is semantic at all, and I don't think either of us has the vocabulary to say precisely why it isn't.

What I want from you is not an exposition of Hofstadter, and not a defense of him either, but a translation and a residue report. My own first degree was in molecular genetics — codons, reading frames, frame shifts, tRNA wobble, the degeneracy of the code, polymerases and their error rates and processivity, reverse transcription, prions, quasispecies and Eigen-style error thresholds, selection coefficients, homologs and motifs — and that is the vocabulary I think in; my logic is much rustier than my biology, so assume I know the rough shape of the incompleteness argument (arithmetization of syntax, a diagonal or fixed-point construction, a sentence that in effect speaks about its own unprovability) but not its details. Take the isomorphism apart piece by piece — strand, enzyme, binding preference, the operations enzymes perform on strands, translation, the resulting phenotype, the constraint Hofstadter labels the Central Dogma of Typogenetics (which I have never been able to verify his enzyme table actually enforces, since some of the operations seem to determine which base ends up where), the self-reproducing strand, and the surrounding talk of levels and strange loops — and restate each piece as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary of real molecular genetics, flagging where you have to idealize the biology to make the mapping work, and correcting me where I have the mapping backwards.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually want, in three parts. First: which pieces translate cleanly, which translate only after you have quietly assumed something the biology does not give you, and which cannot be stated in molecular-genetic terms at all — for instance, whether biology has any notion of exact sequence identity of the kind a self-reproducing string seems to need, whether it has any counterpart to validity, negation, or consistency, and whether the genome-codes-for-its-own-polymerase case is a real instance of the isomorphism or a counterexample to it. Second, as a stress test: I have been toying with a variant I call population typogenetics, in which strands sit in an ensemble rather than alone, copying is error-prone at some rate per base, translation is degenerate so that distinct strands can yield the same enzyme set, and strands have fitness depending on the enzymes they produce; I have also tried adding an operation that lets an enzyme determine the identity of a base it writes back into a strand. Restate both modifications in molecular-genetic terms and tell me what becomes of the isomorphism under them — in particular whether the object that survives in the noisy ensemble is better described as a quine or as a quasispecies, whether the relevant fixed point is a fixed point of a mutation–selection operator rather than of a substitution function, and whether the Central Dogma of Typogenetics turns out to be a consequence of the enzyme set or a stipulation one could simply revoke. Third, the verdict I have to defend to my colleague: is the residue best explained as a limitation of molecular genetics, which has no way to express semantic self-reference because it describes mechanisms and not meanings, or as a sign that the DNA–Gödel isomorphism was always vaguer than the chapter makes it sound? If something substantive does survive the translation, I want it stated as a thesis I could put on a handout — something like a claim about what any self-reproducing system must contain — and not as a metaphor. Please argue from the structure of the two systems rather than from sources; I do not need citations, quotations, or a reading list, and I would rather have a clean argument than a well-supported one.
