After thirty-odd years as an experimental spectroscopist — my stock in trade was asking what a given measurement could and could not tell you about a molecular state, and how much the measurement itself disturbed the thing measured — I now sit in on a graduate reading group in the philosophy of science at the university where I used to teach in the chemistry department. Last week the group spent a session on Fitch's paradox of knowability, and I left with the feeling that I had been shown a trick with mirrors without being able to say which mirror. The instructor's summary was that the result is an artifact of the logic of factive operators and that no one should build a metaphysics on it. That may well be right. But before I decide whether to spend the winter on it, I would like to see the argument in a vocabulary I actually trust, which is the vocabulary of the laboratory rather than of modal logic. I am not asking for further reading; I want the translation done in front of me.

Here is the argument as I have reconstructed it in my notes. Write K for 'it is known by someone at some time that', ◇ for 'it is possible that'. The knowability principle says that every truth is knowable: p → ◇Kp, for arbitrary p. Now substitute for p the proposition q ∧ ¬Kq. If that conjunction were true, knowability would give ◇K(q ∧ ¬Kq); knowledge distributes over conjunction, so we get ◇(Kq ∧ K¬Kq); and factivity — Kφ → φ — applied to the second conjunct yields ¬Kq inside that same possibility, contradicting Kq. So the substitution instance is not knowable, hence, by the knowability principle, not true; and since q was arbitrary, every true q satisfies Kq: all truths are in fact known. Since plainly some truths are not known, the knowability principle must be given up. The step I am least confident about is the application of factivity to the embedded ¬Kq; if I have mis-stated it, correct me before you translate.

What I want is the following. Restate that argument, step by step and as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I used professionally: observables and their possible values; experimental arrangements and the counterfactual question of what outcome a given arrangement would produce; back-action, i.e. the disturbance a measurement visits on the system; records, meaning physical tokens whose state carries recoverable information; and admissibility, meaning an arrangement that could actually be assembled and run in this universe with the laws held fixed and with no limit on time, money or apparatus size. My fluency: comfortable with measurement theory and with counterfactuals over apparatus settings, shaky with modal logic, so define any logical machinery you rely on and express the modal structure as a claim about which arrangements are admissible rather than as a relation between worlds.

In particular, I want you to run the translation twice, because I cannot decide which of two readings of 'knowable' is the honest one in my vocabulary. On the first, call a proposition settleable if some admissible arrangement would produce an outcome from which the proposition could be correctly recovered; on the second, call it recordable if some admissible arrangement would, once run, leave some physical token in existence from which the proposition could in principle be recovered. These come apart in the lab: an arrangement can settle a question without leaving a durable trace, and a trace can exist while being corrupted or misinterpreted. For each reading, say whether the collapse to 'every truth is settled' or 'every truth is recorded' goes through, and if it does not, identify precisely which step fails and which ingredient is responsible — factivity, distribution over conjunction, the unrestricted 'someone, at some time', the strength of the modality, or the self-referential substitution. I am aware that 'could be correctly recovered' conceals a choice between a nomic tie between outcome and proposition and mere good evidence; tell me which choice the argument needs.

Then give me the residue report. My own attempt foundered on a case I sketched for myself: a single spin in a trap, the proposition that its z-component was up at noon and that nothing anywhere in the forward light cone carries a recoverable record of that fact. In my vocabulary the analogue of the collapse seems to fail, but for what look to me like merely physical reasons — no local arrangement can establish a universal negative over all records, and any arrangement that determines the z-component thereby creates a record, destroying the second conjunct — whereas the philosophical argument's failure is supposed to be logical and self-referential. Say whether that impression is right, and whether the difference is a defect of my vocabulary or a sign that the argument is doing less than it appears to. Tell me, too, whether restricting ◇ to physically admissible arrangements rather than to something broader changes the result, and whether that restriction is a legitimate constraint on the knowability principle or a change of subject. Finally, name anything in the philosophers' dispute that has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all — I suspect the fact that knowability was offered as a constraint on what truth itself is, rather than as a pious hope about our epistemic reach, is one such thing, but I would like your view on whether that residue indicts the laboratory vocabulary or the argument. A short table for the residue would help me more than prose.
