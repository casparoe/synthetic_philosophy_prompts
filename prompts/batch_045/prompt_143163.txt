So I'm in this EU politics reading group this semester — mostly political science grad students plus one law student who keeps correcting everyone on article numbers — and I've got to give a short presentation next week on the democratic deficit. I dont want the standard thing, the "the European Parliament is weak and the Commission isn't really elected" story; everyone in the room has heard it, and it doesn't even seem to fit the case I actually care about.

Here's what I want you to do. Take the Greek bailout showdown of 2015 as your case: Tsipras and Varoufakis against the Eurogroup, the referendum Tsipras called at the end of June, the No vote on July 5 (61.3% against the creditors' proposal, as I remember it), the banks shut and capital controls slapped on with something like a €60-a-day withdrawal limit, Varoufakis resigning right after, and then the government agreeing a week later to a third programme that was harsher than the one voters had just rejected, passed through parliament with opposition votes and a big Syriza rebellion, followed by the snap election that September. Run that sequence through the sovereignty / democratic deficit debate properly, and I mean disciplined: who the relevant actors are, what each of them actually had available as an option, what would have counted as a democratic mandate for any given move, and what the standard positions — input versus output legitimacy, the no-demos argument, pooled or shared sovereignty, ever-closer-union as a teleology — say the actors should have done. Don't just conclude "the EU is undemocratic." Tell me which step in the chain is supposed to be the illegitimate one on each view, and where two of the views disagree about which step that is.

My roomate — she's deep into MMT stuff, so adjust accordingly — floated this at dinner: the coercion here wasn't unaccountable technocrats overriding a demos, it was elected governments in other member states forcing an elected government to do what its own voters had just told it not to do, with everyone able to go home and say Brussels made them do it. She calls it "mandate laundering," and the claim underneath it is that the deficit isn't a hole in EU-level democracy at all, it's a cost that national democracies export upward and then disown.
