I build and run server infrastructure for a small online game — I'm entirely self-taught, and my only exposure to philosophy is arguing about it at dinner with my brother-in-law, who teaches it — and a few weeks ago we had an incident that I have not been able to stop turning over. A duplication exploit let some players copy a rare, tradable item; we caught it about eleven minutes in and rolled the whole cluster back to the previous snapshot, twenty-six hours earlier. That undid the dupes, but it also erased twenty-six hours of legitimate play, including an item one player had finally won on his fortieth attempt and immediately screenshotted. Our community manager posted that it was "just data" and offered store credit, and the thread went up in flames. I didn't post anything, because I realized I had no idea whether the thing that player lost was a real object that had been destroyed, a convenient way of talking about some rows, or nothing at all — and I'm the person who wrote the rows.

Since then I've tried to read about it on my own, and there seems to be a debate about whether virtual objects and virtual harms are real, with roughly three camps: that virtual objects are genuinely real, on the grounds that players organize their behavior around them the way they organize it around the money in a bank account; that they aren't real, and talk of swords and houses is just shorthand for code, pixels, and player experience; and that the objects don't matter but the harms do, because grief and frustration are real whatever their cause. I can't tell whether these people are disagreeing about anything I could ever test, so I want to try the translation the other way around. Here are the cases I actually care about, all of which came up for real: a unique item that briefly existed twice, one copy per shard, during the replication-lag window; an item whose row was destroyed by a rollback and then recreated differently when we replayed the event log through a newer build, so that the same events produced an object with different stats; an item that our terrain generator would have placed in a particular chunk from the world seed but that never existed as a row because no player ever loaded that chunk; a shut-down game kept running on a private server from leaked binaries, whose items the studio says do not exist and which players still trade for actual money; and a guild's shared treasury, tracked in a spreadsheet outside the game entirely, which has no row anywhere but which everyone in the guild treats as one object with real effects on what they do.

So here is what I want. Restate those three positions, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually work in: authoritative server state, events versus snapshots, transactions and their guarantees, replication lag and eventual consistency, rollbacks and replay, migrations, soft deletion, determinism from a seed, garbage collection, and above all the question of where the system boundary is drawn — client, server, my database, the player's memory, a third-party wiki. Then give me the residue report, because that's the part I'm paying for: which claims about virtual objects and virtual harms survive the translation unchanged, which only survive if you quietly change what they mean, and which cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all. I especially want you to be honest rather than helpful here — don't smuggle in an answer by defining "real" as "appears in authoritative state" and calling it a translation, and don't tell me the whole thing is merely a verbal dispute about the word "real" unless you can show me that one of my six cases stops being puzzling once the word is fixed. Finally, give me your verdict on the residue: is what's left over pointing at something my framework genuinely cannot see about objects, subjects, or the social facts that make a row authoritative in the first place, or is it evidence that the philosophers are in a smaller disagreement than it looks like from the outside?
