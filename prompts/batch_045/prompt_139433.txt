I'm a research assistant on an edited volume my supervisor is putting together on lock-in and contingency in the history of technology, and I've been asked to draft the concluding chapter, which applies the classificatory scheme he has been developing to a case he never actually ruled on. Last year he ran a seminar in which he delivered a verdict, week by week, on a series of cases, always in one of three forms: locked in and it mattered; locked in but harmless; would have arrived anyway. My notes give me nine verdicts. QWERTY over Dvorak: locked in but harmless — the mechanism was real (typing instruction, manufacturers' expectations, the cost of retraining a workforce) but he holds that the evidence for Dvorak's superiority is thin, so the road not taken would have left us roughly where we are. VHS over Betamax: locked in but harmless — recording length, licensed manufacturing and rental inventories did the work, but home video would have arrived on any of the cassette systems of the period. Alternating against direct current: would have arrived anyway — transformers make long-distance transmission cheap, so the grid would have come back to AC even if the contracts of the 1890s had gone the other way. The light-water reactor: locked in and it mattered — a head start inherited from the US naval reactor programme fixed the fuel cycle, the regulators' expertise and the utilities' supplier base, and a heavy-water or gas-graphite path would have been a recognisably different industry. Agriculture: arrived anyway — domestication turned up independently in several regions. Standard-gauge railways: locked in and it mattered, for roughly a century, after which container traffic and bogie exchange made gauge differences cheap to work around. TCP/IP against the connection-oriented, carrier-backed suite: locked in and it mattered — a permissionless, end-to-end network is not what the telephone world would have built. Calculus: arrived anyway — Newton and Leibniz arrived separately, and he would bet on a third independent invention within a couple of generations. And the 110–120V/60Hz against 220–240V/50Hz split: locked in but harmless — electrification was coming regardless, and the voltage difference is now a nuisance that a transformer or a switch-mode supply absorbs.

The case I have to write up is the one he scheduled for the session he cancelled: whether the fact that the first atomic bombs were built in the United States in 1945, rather than in Germany or in the Soviet Union, is a locked-in outcome or one that would have arrived anyway. He has always insisted that the verdict here concerns the technological and industrial path and not the decision to use the weapons, and he has said in print that the physics was international, that the fission results were published before the war, that the German effort never came close, and that the Soviet Union tested a device four years later. Before I commit to a verdict I want to know what rule he is actually running, because I can think of at least three that fit all nine verdicts equally well and that come apart on this one: a rule about whether some self-reinforcing mechanism selected the outcome; a rule about whether the road not taken would have left a relevantly different world; and a reading a classmate of mine proposed, that his verdicts are really always about the level of description — types converge, tokens get locked in — which fits everything but may also predict nothing, since it licenses both answers at once. I also have two half-formed refinements of my own that I cannot tell are helpful or merely question-begging: a fourth label, would have arrived anyway but much later, on the grounds that a four-year lag is not the same thing as no difference; and the thought that every verdict needs a fixed date at which the two branches are compared, since the gauge case suggests the difference between them decays. A related thing I can't reconcile on my own: in print he treats QWERTY as the paradigm of lock-in, while in the seminar he filed it under harmless, and I don't know whether that is a change of mind or a sign the labels are doing two jobs.

So: state the candidate rules that fit his nine verdicts as sharply as you can, say what each predicts about the 1945 case, and give me your best guess at his verdict with the reasons for preferring it. Tell me whether my two refinements rescue the scheme or quietly rig it, whether my classmate's levels reading can be distinguished from the others by anything he has already said, and what the QWERTY discrepancy actually shows. Finally, I need the further case that would separate the readings — what should I put in front of him next that would force him to commit? Every candidate I've managed to come up with gets the same verdict under all three readings, which may mean I'm not looking in the right place.
