I do a column on science and ideas, and I've let myself be talked in to running a breakout session at a science-writers' conference next month — 90 minutes, call it 18 people, mostly reporters who cover climate, medicine and AI. The brief I've been handed is how to write about fights where both sides really are looking at solid data, without doing lazy both-sides-ism and without concluding somebody must just be wrong. I want to build it around the Jain thought that a claim is true only from a standpoint and the thing itself has more aspects than any one standpoint catches — but I don't want to say any of that up front, or name it until the end.

So design me the activity. A staged case with private information handed to each participant, roles, whats on the handouts, a timeline, and some sort of scoring rule — no real money, nothing that needs printing in colour — arranged so people hit the problem themselves: plain "X is true" keeps blowing up in the room, while making people prefix every assertion with the standpoint and the evidence it comes from turns out to change what the group can actually settle, not just how polite everyone's being. Build in whatever twist is needed so that "everyone has a bit of the truth" isn't where they end up — I want them somewhere more demanding than that, and I want them to feel the difference rather than be told it.

Then the debrief: map what happened onto the actual view, tell me what'll go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be straight about which part of the doctrine the game just can't stage and I'll have to say out loud in words. Half of them have sat through a "false balance" panel before and are slightly sick of it, so it can't feel like one of those.
