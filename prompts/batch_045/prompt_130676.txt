You are an analytic philosopher who also reads machine-learning engineering discussions fluently, and who has some practice turning muddled technical disagreements into explicit ones. Please work in two passes. In the first pass, brainstorm the logical space in note form: what the independent commitments are, which combinations of them are genuinely available, and where the real fault lines lie. Do not attribute views to named researchers, and do not cite, link, or recommend any reading: I want the structure, not the literature, and a model without web access should be able to do this task in full.

The context: I am a software engineer at a mid-sized AI company, no formal philosophy training. I run a short onboarding seminar for new engineers in which we try to get people to state, precisely, where they disagree about AI risk — because in practice the same three words ("it's dangerous", "it's hype", "alignment is hard") get used by people who mean completely different things. I want a decision tree that takes a new engineer from a single shared starting point to a named position, in the same spirit as the "which interpretation of quantum mechanics do you actually hold?" trees that get passed around: branch on the claim that separates people, not on the label they like.

The starting commitment, which everyone in the room has already granted, is: machine-learning systems will keep improving at a broad range of economically relevant cognitive tasks, they will be deployed widely and competitively, and their deployment will substantially change how institutions work.

Please build the instrument to the following specification.

Structure. 8–12 internal nodes, numbered N1, N2, … Each internal node is one question with two or three answers, phrased so that a competent engineer who has never read any of this literature can answer it, and phrased so that answering it does not reveal which leaf it leads to. Nodes of the following kinds are useless to me and must not appear: "do you think AI could kill everyone", "are you optimistic or pessimistic", "do you agree that alignment is hard", "how worried are you on a scale of 1 to 10", or any node that mentions a person, an organization, or a paper. Every node should be answerable by yes/no or by picking one of two or three concrete pictures of how things go.

Leaves. 8–10 leaves, numbered L1, L2, … Each leaf carries: a name, or the word "unnamed" if nobody defends the bundle; two sentences stating its most awkward or least comfortable commitment (the thing its holder has to swallow); and one sentence on what single observation would most plausibly move someone off it. These leaves must all be reachable:
1. the position on which the whole risk literature rests on a category error and there is no distinctive risk here beyond that of any powerful general-purpose technology;
2. the position on which the risk is real but is entirely a matter of human misuse and capability diffusion, and would disappear in a world of perfectly cooperative institutions;
3. the position on which the bad outcome is a gradual erosion of human control, bargaining power, or meaningful agency through competitive and economic pressure, with no discrete catastrophe anywhere in the story;
4. the position on which a decisive strategic surprise is a serious possibility: some system or small coalition of systems acquires a durable power advantage that is not recovered;
5. a position that shares most of the empirical picture with (4) but locates the badness in multipolar competitive dynamics between many systems and their deployers rather than in any single system's aims;
6. at least one leaf for the thought that the outcome could be very bad even if everything works roughly as intended, because a stable settlement forms around the wrong values and then persists.

Two distinctions my colleagues keep conflating, which the tree therefore has to separate:
(a) capability versus agency. A system can be far better than any human at a task while having no aims that persist from one episode to the next. Include a node that splits people who require a system with persistent goals somewhere in the causal story from people who do not, and make clear what each side's bad-outcome story then has to look like.
(b) the shape of the transition. Do not make this a simple fast/slow binary: add a third option, that capability improves continuously but the concentration of effective power changes discontinuously, because deployment structure, market structure, or capital requirements shift much faster than the models do.

A test case the tree has to handle. Take the following scenario, which I use in the seminar: it is a few years from now, and a model is released with publicly downloadable weights. It beats the best available humans at essentially every economically relevant cognitive task you care to name. It has no goals that persist across sessions, it does not seek anything, and within a month it is running on infrastructure controlled by hundreds of organizations and by individuals. Two engineers can both look at this scenario, both call it dangerous, and still belong on different leaves. Trace both engineers through your tree, say which node separates them, and say why that node is doing the work. If your tree cannot separate them, say so at that point and add the node that would.

After the tree. Do three more things. First, list the answer-combinations your nodes permit that no actual position occupies: for each, say in one sentence whether the region is empty because the combination is incoherent, because it is coherent but unattractive, or because it is simply unexplored. Second, name any two leaves the tree cannot separate and give the extra question that would separate them. Third, note in one sentence where a reader's answer would depend on an empirical guess rather than a value commitment, since I want to know which of the nodes my engineers could in principle go and check.

Constraints and audience. No citations, no bibliographies, no links, no named authors, no dates attached to specific papers. Where you need a quantity, give a range and mark it as illustrative. Define any term of art in one clause when you first use it; the readers are engineers with no philosophy background. Assume no internet access: every claim you rely on should be either conceptual or common knowledge among technically literate people.

Format. Present the tree in a machine-readable way: a numbered list of nodes with their answer options and the branch each answer leads to, then a table of the leaves with columns for name, awkward commitment, and what would move someone off it, then the post-tree notes. Keep the prose between sections short.
