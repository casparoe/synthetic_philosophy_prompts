I'm in a reading group on rational belief — we've done the lottery paradox and the preface paradox, and I'm drafting a short position paper on whether they really are the same problem. Before I present it I want someone to audit the package I've landed on, because I have the feeling that one or two of these theses are doing work the others can't support, and I genuinely can't locate where. Here is where I stand, more or less in the order I came to believe them.

(1) Belief is a threshold on credence: it is rational to believe p just in case my credence in p is high enough, with the threshold set by the standards in play. I like this because it keeps belief continuous with the credences I actually reason and act with. (2) The lottery verdict: in a large fair lottery it is not rational for me to believe of ticket #17 that it will lose, even though my credence that it loses is 0.999999 and I would stake almost anything on it. So high credence is not sufficient. (3) What is missing is discriminating evidence: statistical evidence about a class cannot rationalize a belief about a particular member of it, and I have nothing that distinguishes #17 from its competitors. (4) The preface verdict: an author who has checked each of the three hundred assertions in her book can rationally believe each of them and can also rationally believe the preface claim that at least one is false; she need not retract anything, and I am happy to conclude that a fully rational person's beliefs can be jointly inconsistent. This is where I give up closure for rational belief. (5) But I want to keep closure for knowledge, I take knowledge to require rational belief, and I reject pragmatic encroachment: stakes change what I should do and perhaps what I may assert, but not what it is rational for me to believe or whether I know. (6) The two paradoxes are therefore not the same paradox, and (3) is what separates them; I would defend that line as principled rather than as a disjunctive patch, since a theory with one rule for statistical cases and another for everything else is not a theory. (7) I hold a knowledge norm of belief: it is irrational to believe what you do not know. (8) And I do not want any of the repair work to be done by a modal condition like safety or sensitivity — those have always struck me as redescribing the verdict rather than explaining it, and I suspect they overgeneralize to perfectly ordinary beliefs.

What I want is the audit, not the survey: which of these actually pull against each other, how badly, and what each tension costs to fix. My own guess is that (4) is the load-bearing commitment, but I can't say why and I'd rather not surrender it. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad; tell me for each what would have to give and what that would commit me to elsewhere; say which single revision would clean up the most with the least collateral damage; and flag any pair that only looks inconsistent, since I'd rather not throw away a thesis over a phantom. Finally, my group will certainly ask about a case I've been using as a test, so say what the surviving package predicts about it: a proofreader has read every page of a manuscript, found no error, and knows from years of blind re-checks of her own work that she misses roughly one error in two hundred. Her grounds here are not a base rate over a class of similar claims; they are a measured error rate for the very procedure she just ran on each page. May she rationally believe that page 41 is clean?
