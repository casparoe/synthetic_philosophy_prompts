I've gotten kind of obsessed with Escher's Drawing Hands and the idea that some systems have no bottom level, no outside place where the rules come from, and I cant stop turning it over. Would you write me a short story about it? Here's the setup I keep imagining, but change whatever you like as long as it stays about the same knot:

A valley in a dry country has shared one irrigation system for a couple hundred years. Everything is in a single ledger in the sluice house: who gets water on which days, how the sluice keeper is paid, how disputes are settled. On the last page is the rule for changing the rules, call it Rule 30: any rule may be changed or struck out by a majority vote of the households at the annual meeting, and this rule may be changed the same way.

There's a drought on. One faction proposes changing Rule 30 so that any change to the water schedule needs a two-thirds vote instead, on the grounds that the schedule shouldn't lurch back and forth depending on who bothered to show up at a meeting. The vote is taken: 14 in favour out of 24 households. A majority, but not two-thirds.

That's where I want the story to actually start. Half the valley says the new rule is in force, because the only rule that existed at the moment of the vote said a majority was enough. The other half says it isn't, because a rule that changes the rule for changing rules has to pass under the terms it is itself creating. Both of those sound right to me and they cant both be, and that's the feeling I want the story to have.

Two things I'd like you to add, because I think the usual version of this puzzle is too tidy:

1. The valley also has a reader, a paid outsider who interprets the ledger when it's unclear. But her contract, her fee, and the households' obligation to accept her ruling are all written into the ledger too. So she isn't really outside the system the way everyone assumes. Does adding her change anything, or is she just a third hand drawing the same two hands?
2. Drawing Hands is symmetrical, each hand drawing the other. This valley isn't: the water schedule can be rewritten any year, the reader's fee can only be changed once a decade, and her ruling stands for that year no matter what the meeting decides afterwards. The loop has a fast part and a slow part, and I'd like the story to notice that.

Please land the ending on something concrete, some actual decision about who gets water this season, rather than shrugging. And keep it a story: no character should give a little lecture explaining the philosophy, and no final paragraph telling me what to think. Around 1000 words. I care much more about what it feels like to be stuck inside the loop than about anyone resolving it.
