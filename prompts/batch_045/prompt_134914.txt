I'm in a graduate seminar on causation and explanation this term, and we have now spent three weeks on causation by omission without anybody moving, so I want a structural diagnosis of the impasse rather than another verdict on which side is right.

Here is how the fight looks from inside it. One side holds that if the plant's dying depends on my not watering it, then my not watering it caused the plant to die: the counterfactual is clean, the explanation and the blame would be unintelligible otherwise, and a metaphysic that cannot accommodate the cases we most need causes for is a metaphysic with the wrong subject. The other side holds that causes are happenings with locations in space and time joined by processes, that an absence is not a happening, that nothing occurred when I failed to water the plant, and that what does not exist at a time cannot stand in a causal relation at that time; on this view omissions figure in explanations but not in the causal structure of the world, and counterfactual dependence is at best a guide to causes rather than what causation consists in. A third group says that 'cause' is context-sensitive and both sides are right in different contexts, which mostly just annoys the first two. A fourth maneuver tries to replace omissions with double prevention, i.e. with whatever positive event diverted the mechanism that would otherwise have acted, and I suspect that maneuver only works for some cases.

The wrinkle I want to add is a distinction we invented in section, because I think it explains the standoff. Call an omission anchored when it sits inside a live causal setup: there was a standing disposition, an intention, a role, or a mechanism poised to fire that then did not fire — the nurse assigned to the IV line, the irrigation system whose timer was installed but whose clock battery died, the bystander who weighed intervening and decided against it. Call it bare when nothing in the vicinity was ever poised: no agent with a role, no plan, no mechanism, no consideration — the plant on the windowsill that nobody had any relation to, or the Great Wall of China's failure to water it. Most textbook cases cluster at one end or the other, and nobody says which end they are generalizing from.

My hypothesis is that the friends of omissions are tacitly reading off anchored cases, where there is plenty of positive structure to point at, and the skeptics are reading off bare ones, where there is none — so that the real hidden question is not about the word 'cause' at all but about whether unmanifested dispositions, standing intentions and poised mechanisms are themselves part of the causal structure of the world. I am not confident in this, and I want it tested rather than flattered.

Give me your answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion: (1) what kind of disagreement this is — verbal, substantive, different questions under one heading, a hidden metaphysical or formal question, or bedrock intuitions; (2) whether my anchored/bare distinction actually does explanatory work or merely relocates the dispute; (3) what each side in fact says, as described above, that supports your diagnosis rather than merely restating it; (4) what would count as progress given that diagnosis; (5) whether progress should be expected at all, and why not if not. Please don't survey the literature or point me to readings — I have no library access from here and I've had three weeks of the survey already.
