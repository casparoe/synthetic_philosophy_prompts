So I've been reading Arendt on lying in politics and I think I've basically swallowed the position whole, and I want someone to try to talk me back out of it. The version I hold has two parts. First, the asymmetry claim: the liar has a structural advantage over the truth-teller in any given exchange, because he starts from what his audience already half-believes or wants to believe, and because he is free to invent while the truth-teller is stuck reporting something he didn't make. Factual truth ends up being both unusually stubborn (facts just sit there; they don't evaporate because someone denies them) and unusually fragile (one organized lie can kill a fact politically, and no amount of arguing resurrects it) — and the fragility wins in practice, because the liar is a man of action and the truth-teller, by temperament, mostly isn't. Second, and this is the part I care about most, where I think I've gone past her: the interesting modern case isn't the liar who wants you to believe a rival account of what happened. It's what I've started calling defactualization — saturating the space so that the ability to tell fact from fiction atrophies, and the payoff is indifference and cynicism rather than false belief. If that's right, then the standard remedy (more speech, more fact-checking, better information hygiene) doesn't merely fail, it feeds the thing it's aimed at.

What I want: what would count against this? Not "Arendt is gloomy" — actual things, observations and cases and arguments, that should move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. And please separate what a defender of the view would herself concede counts against it from what she'd shrug off as a misdescription. Three things I don't have good answers to: (a) whether the asymmetry claim is a claim about the liar as an individual and quietly breaks down when lies are produced collectively — party lines, coordinated messaging, algorithmic amplification — since the discipline that keeps everyone on message might substitute for the reality check the lone liar lacks; (b) whether the fragility of facts is a property of facts or of a particular media environment, in which case the view is fitted to the early 70s and could be straightforwardly false in a world of cheap, ubiquitous, hard-to-edit records (I go back and forth: does total recording help truth, or does it just make the flooding cheaper too?); (c) whether the whole thing only works for brute facts — who said what, whether the bodies were found — and quietly fails for the aggregate, interpretive, partly conventional claims that most political lying actually runs on (the economy is failing, the war is being won), where nothing anchors the claim the way a corpse does.

Last thing: is there anything this view risks at all, or have I built it so that every apparent counterexample — people not believing the lie, liars getting caught, institutions holding the line — gets reabsorbed as more evidence for defactualization? I'd count that as a genuine problem. For what it's worth, the thing I'd personally find most convincing is a historical case where an organized lie was successfully sustained for decades and then the machinery for it was dismanteled from inside rather than outcompeted by facts; my hunch is that's what actually happens, and I genuinely can't tell whether that vindicates her or buries her.
