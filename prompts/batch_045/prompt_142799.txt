I write literary fiction, and the novel I am finishing is set in an intentional community on a cold northern coast whose founders resolved, in a document they still read aloud once a year, to do without guilt: no confessions, no apologies, no restitution ledgers, no closing ritual of being forgiven. What they wanted instead was what they called the open-room life — the idea that because everyone's days are visible to everyone else, wrongdoing would be absorbed by the ordinary pressure of continuing to be seen rather than discharged by a verdict and a payment. The book traces how that arrangement curdles into something like surveillance, and also follows the one character who genuinely flourishes under it, which is the part I understand least well. Alongside the book I host a companion interview series for my publisher — long-form conversations between novelists and philosophers — and next month my guest is a moral philosopher who has spent her career defending the primacy of shame: the claims that shame, not guilt, is the more basic and the more educative moral emotion, and that a culture can to some real extent shift its moral-emotional repertoire, though not by decree. Her best-known line is that guilt is an accountant's emotion and shame is a novelist's, that guilt closes a file whereas shame is what you feel when the story you are in stops being one you could tell. Among her more technical idioms: guilt is harm-tracking while shame is identity-tracking; the kind of shame worth defending is constitutive shame (shame at who one is becoming, relative to an ideal one has in some sense authored) as opposed to global self-devaluation; and what a culture can actually redesign is not the emotion but the visibility structure — who sees what, what must be said aloud, which institutions dramatise which failures.

Draft me eight to ten interview questions for her, none of them of the what-first-drew-you-to-this kind, and for each one the follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. I want at least one question on the undetected transgression: in my community, a character quietly falsifies a record no one will ever read, harming nobody and never being found out, so guilt would seem to have nothing to bite on while shame apparently still does — ask her what, exactly, the shame is about there, and follow up on her probable reply that shame answers to who I am becoming rather than to what I did by pressing whether that is simply the novelist's occupational preference for character over act dressed up as a moral psychology. I want a question on the empirical literature on shame-proneness, the body of work finding that shame-prone people conceal, externalise blame, and lash out, and on her almost certain response that the instruments are measuring global self-devaluation and humiliation rather than her constitutive shame — the follow-up should ask how the two look different from the outside, especially in a setting where everyone is watching, and whether her distinction is one the data could in principle see. I also want a question out of something a classics-reading friend told me and I only half believe: that in the Nicomachean Ethics shame is classed as a feeling rather than a virtue, and is said to be fitting in the young but not praised in an older person, since an older person ought not to do the sort of thing that calls for it. Ask her whether the primacy thesis commits her to recommending more shame in the mature agent, or whether she wants a developmental story, and then follow up on whether the notion of mature shame is coherent at all.

The remaining questions should cover: shame that arrives with no agency whatsoever — my narrator is ashamed of a grandfather she never met, and the philosophical literature also leans on cases like being seen unclothed or degraded by the wrong person — where I want her pushed on whether that reach is shame's glory, because it tracks what we belong to, or its disqualification, because it moralises luck, with a follow-up ready for her likely answer that the boundaries of the self are negotiable and that shame is the emotion of membership, asking whether on that picture shame has quietly become guilt with a wider plaintiff and the guilt/shame distinction has stopped doing any work. Then a question on whether the degeneration I have written is a contingent failure of implementation or an intrinsic consequence of shame's dependence on an audience, with a follow-up demanding the smallest structural change that would have saved the open-room life. Then a question on what evidence could count against the view — empirical, conceptual, or anthropological — and on whether there are agents for whom guilt functions and shame does not, and whether such cases cap the scope of her claim. I would also like one question that turns my own craft into evidence for or against her, since fiction runs on disclosure, and a closing question about the clean conscience: a person who has confessed, apologised, and made full restitution and now feels nothing at all — ask her whether that absence is a failure of moral perception, and whether a moral life without any residue after payment is one we should want. Flag, briefly under each question, what you take her most likely answer to be, so that I can hear when she is giving me the standard reply rather than the real one.
