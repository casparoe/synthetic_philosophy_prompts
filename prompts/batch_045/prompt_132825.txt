About three months ago I started working as a legislative aide for a city council member (housing committee), and there's one sentence that keeps ending arguments in our office. Last week we were deciding whether to back a rezoning deal with a developer who's been fined twice for wage theft: about 400 new units, but it would bulldoze a block of rent-stabilized apartments. When I pushed on what happens to the people living there, my boss said "you can't make an omelette without breaking eggs," and the meeting moved on. I don't want to be the naive staffer who thinks politics can be done clean, but I also don't think I actually know what that sentence committed anyone to.

So: can you lay out, as a list I could argue from, the different precise claims that sentence could be making? Is it just "every action has costs"? Is it "consequences are all that matter"? Is it "there are real constraints on what you may do, but necessity switches them off"? Is it "holding this office licenses things that would be wrong for a private person to do" — and if so, does the wrongness get erased, or does it stick to the person as guilt they're supposed to feel afterwards? Is it a claim about there being no better option available, or a claim about me being too squeamish, or a claim about who is allowed to judge? Same question for the older version of the idea, about needing to be able not to be good when necessity forces it, and for the way people say "dirty hands" now.

For each reading, say whether it's trivial, actually defensible, or false. And this is the part I care about: which reading would have to be true for the slogan to really justify that vote, and is the reading people probably had in mind one of those?

Two complications I can't stop thinking about, please fold them in:

1. In our case the eggs and the omelette are different people. The tenants getting displaced are almost definately not the ones who'll end up in the new units. Does the slogan quietly assume the same people are both, and does it survive once they aren't? Does it change anything if the people getting broken had agreed in advance to a rule that permits it?

2. I've been daydreaming about drafting something for the council, call it a necessity declaration: if you vote for a measure you openly admit does serious harm to identifiable people, you have to file a public statement naming who gets hurt and why, and you give something up for it — your committee seat for the rest of the term, or any future paid work touching that project. Later there's a review that can say "this wasn't necessary after all" but can't undo the vote. Which reading of the slogan does that thing presuppose? Does any reading commit you to wanting it? And would a council that adopted it be admitting the vote was wrong, or just being prudent?

Dont bother with a reading list or with telling me which thinker said what — I can look up names later. I want the menu with verdicts, and then a straight answer on whether that vote is defensible and under which reading. Somewhere around 700–900 words.
