I run a small printing business, I'm in my fifties, and for the last couple of years I've been keeping a notebook of things that puzzle me, mostly about everyday experience rather than anything I've studied. Here is the one I keep coming back to. Some of the things I'd count among the best experiences of my week have parts that are straightforwardly unpleasant, and not in a way I merely tolerate in exchange for what comes after. The first thirty seconds of a winter sea swim is, as far as I can tell from the inside, just cold and frightening; the dread before a difficult climb is dread; the burn in my legs on the last hill is a burn. But if you told me the swim could be arranged so that the shock was removed and everything else left exactly as it was, I'd refuse the offer. The bad part isn't a fee I pay for the good part — it seems to be part of what I'm getting. Contrast this with the dentist: the pain there is a price, and I would delete it from the experience without losing anything I wanted. I've started marking these two apart in my notes as the ingredient case and the price case, and I want to know whether that distinction is real or whether I'm fooling myself with introspection, and whether anybody has a name for the first one.

The reason I think it might be a different thing from what people usually talk about is this. When I ask what my pleasures have in common, I come up empty: the taste of a mango on a hot afternoon, the click when a hard crossword clue finally resolves, the relief of taking off boots after a twelve-hour shift, and the swim all seem to share no felt ingredient whatsoever, and I half-remember from school that Mill distinguished higher and lower pleasures, but that was about ranking them, not about what they feel like. A friend who did a philosophy minor tells me my puzzle is just the "heterogeneity problem" and that it's been discussed to death. That doesn't sound right to me. As I understand that worry, it's that pleasures differ from one another; my puzzle is about a single experience, where the pleasantness doesn't seem to be located in any part of it or in the sum of the parts, and where removing the unpleasant parts would make the whole thing worse rather than better. I also don't think it's the same as enjoying sad films or scary stories, because those involve things that aren't really happening to you, whereas the cold and the burn are as real as anything gets.

So: does the thing I've been calling the ingredient case have a name, or a literature, or is it a variant of something already catalogued? I don't want a label on its own — I want the carving. Specifically: if there is an established concept here, what is it, how does it differ from the heterogeneity worry and from the nearby puzzles about enjoying fear or sadness, and which of them actually covers my swim? If there isn't one, give me a name and a definition, and tell me which side each of these falls on, and why: a long run that hurts while you're doing it; the sting of chilli in a hot curry; scratching an itchy bite; grief at a funeral that you wouldn't want to be without; boredom that makes the following relief land harder; a task you hate every minute of and would still call one of the best things you've done. And tell me what would settle the question of whether the ingredient case is a genuine feature of the experience or an illusion my memory manufactures afterwards — especially since, if the pleasantness isn't in any part of the experience, I'm not sure what it would mean to say the experience is pleasant at all.
