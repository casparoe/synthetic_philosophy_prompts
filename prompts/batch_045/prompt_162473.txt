I would be grateful for your comments on some notes I have written on Gödel's modal ontological argument. A word about why this has become urgent for me: I took my doctorate in mathematical logic, left academia for industry about fifteen years ago, and over the past year I have begun attending church with my wife after a long stretch of settled atheism. I am trying to work out, without self-deception, whether anything in this argument could actually do work for someone in my position, or whether it is a beautiful machine that outputs something I do not want and would not recognize. What follows is reconstructed partly from memory and is partly my own speculation. I would like you to check the technical claims, tell me where I have gone wrong, and tell me which parts are worth developing.

The axiomatization I am using. Write P(φ) for "φ is a positive property," □ for metaphysical necessity over an S5 frame, and let properties be formed freely by abstraction (λx.p for a proposition p is allowed).

- Ax1. P(φ) ∧ □∀x(φ(x) → ψ(x)) → P(ψ).
- Ax2. P(¬φ) ↔ ¬P(φ).
- Ax3. P(φ) ∧ P(ψ) → P(λx.(φ(x) ∧ ψ(x))).
- Ax4. P(φ) → □P(φ).
- Ax5. P(NE), where NE is defined below.
- Def G: G(x) := ∀φ(P(φ) → φ(x)).
- Def ess: φ ess x := φ(x) ∧ ∀ψ(ψ(x) → □∀y(φ(y) → ψ(y))).
- Def NE: NE(x) := ∀φ(φ ess x → □∃y φ(y)).

Phase 1 (possibility). From Ax1–Ax3 one derives first that every positive property is possibly instantiated, and then ◇∃x G(x). I am skipping this derivation, partly because I remember it least well and partly because I think the point is dialectically important: some presentations simply assume P(G), or assume ◇∃x G(x) outright, which I read as evidence that this step, not the "existence" step, is the fragile one.

Phase 2 (necessitation). The argument shows G(x) → G ess x (this is where Ax4 and Ax2 are needed), and since a God-like being has every positive property it has NE, so G(x) → □∃y G(y). The whole derivation is necessary, so □(∃x G(x) → □∃x G(x)); combining with ◇∃x G(x) gives ◇□∃x G(x). My claim: at this point axiom T (□p → p) is all one needs to reach □∃x G(x), and the characteristic S5 principle (◇□p → □p, equivalently ◇p → □◇p) is idle — the conclusion follows in KT, and the heavy modal machinery everyone associates with the argument is decorative.

Phase 3 (the collapse). Here is my reconstruction, which I would most like checked. Fix any proposition p and consider the lifted property [p] := λx.p; note that [¬p] is literally the complement of [p], so by Ax2 exactly one of the two is positive. Suppose p is true at the actual world. Let U be the universal property and E the empty one; by Ax2 exactly one of U and E is positive. If U is positive, then since necessarily everything with [p] has U, Ax1 gives P([p]). If E is positive, then since E necessarily implies everything, Ax1 again gives P([p]). Either way p → P([p]); by Ax4, □P([p]); and by Ax2 (taken as necessary) □(P([p]) ↔ ¬P([¬p])). But if ¬p were possible, then at some accessible world the same reasoning would yield P([¬p]) there, contradicting □P([p]). So p → □p for arbitrary p, and with T we get p ↔ □p: modality is trivial. Notice that the derivation uses only Ax1, Ax2 and Ax4 — not Ax5, not the essence machinery, and apparently nothing beyond K plus the necessity of the axioms. I take this to be a decisive objection, and indeed a proof of inconsistency: the axioms entail that nothing is contingent, and I am as certain as I am of anything that some things are.

Phase 4 (where I think the real interest lies).

(a) An asymmetry I think I have noticed. Define "negative" by N(φ) := P(¬φ). Then N satisfies the analogue of Ax2 — exactly one of φ and ¬φ is negative — but it does not satisfy the analogue of Ax1, because negating reverses the direction of entailment: closure under necessary implication for P becomes, for N, closure under being implied by. So there is no parallel proof of a necessarily existing maximally bad being, and the blame falls squarely on Ax1, i.e. on the assumption that positivity is the sort of notion that propagates upward along entailment.

(b) A reframing I have been playing with. Ax1–Ax3 say, roughly, that P behaves like an ultrafilter on the algebra of properties (upward closed under necessary inclusion, closed under finite meets, and for each φ deciding between φ and ¬φ); Ax4 says membership in it is necessary; the "positive properties are possibly instantiated" theorem says no member is empty at every world. On this reading, G is the intersection of the ultrafilter, and the argument is a machine for showing that a certain filter is not free. Two consequences I find striking. First, if P were closed under arbitrary intersections, then G itself would be positive, hence possibly instantiated, and the conclusion would follow in one line without Ax5, without essences, and without any modal ascent — which suggests that the delicate, load-bearing move is exactly the refusal to assume more than finite closure. Second, in set theory an ultrafilter closed under intersections of small subfamilies need not be principal once the underlying set is large enough (measurable cardinals), so "complete" does not by itself mean "generated by a single object"; I cannot tell whether that caveat carries over to an algebra of intensional properties over possibilia, or whether it is a spurious analogy that collapses on inspection.

(c) A generalization I want to defend. Any argument equipped with (i) a dichotomy axiom for a qualitative predicate of properties, (ii) the necessity of that predicate, (iii) upward closure under necessary implication, and (iv) the ability to lift propositions into properties, will force p → □p for every p, in any modal logic at least as strong as K, and independently of whatever existence axioms it carries. If that is right, the modal collapse is not a defect of Gödel's particular choices but a structural feature of perfection arguments generally — in which case the interesting question is not how to repair the argument but what the collapse tells us about the concept of a greatest conceivable being.
