hey — I'm writing the final for my upper-level value theory seminar (third/fourth year, they've done Bentham, Mill and a bit of Sidgwick, plus two recent papers on what pleasure actually is) and I want one long essay question on the heterogeneity problem for hedonism. The thing I keep getting in their essays is: heterogeneity gets raised, Mill's move about quality of pleasures gets wheeled out, and then they stop. I want a question where that answer gets a B at best.

Roughly, I want them forced to do three things: say precisely what the heterogeneity problem is a problem *for* (is it a problem about what pleasure is, or about why pleasure is good, or about whether "maximize pleasure" is even a usable decision procedure?); handle the two cases below, which I made up so that the standard moves come apart; and then take a stand on whether hedonism survives and what it would have to give up.

I'm also giving them a label to work with, "the one-lever requirement." Define it in the question as: hedonism is supposed to hand you a single scale — one lever — such that (a) any two options can be ranked on it, (b) there's a common unit, and (c) the ranking is fixed by hedonic facts and nothing else. The question should make them say which of those three the heterogeneity worry actually threatens and which one the hedonist could give up and still count as a hedonist.

Two cases, use these (or better versions if you see the point):

Case 1, "the swap." Priya's experience of finishing a hard proof has exactly the felt bodily character your itch-scratch has for you — the tingle, the warmth, the located relief — while her itch-scratch has the quiet, almost featureless "yes, go on" character that finishing a proof normally has for the rest of us. So the felt quality and the activity have come apart. Ask them: which of Priya's two experiences is more pleasant? Whatever they say, make them show the cost. If pleasantness travels with the felt quality, the intellectual pleasure is pleasant only by accident and the paradigmatically "higher" pleasures are just vehicles for a bodily tingle, so Mill's ranking is in trouble. If pleasantness stays with the activity and the attitude, then what-it's-like is doing no work, hedonism starts to look like a desire or activity theory wearing a hedonic mask, and the thing that made hedonism attractive in the first place — that the goodness of pleasure is somehow evident from the inside — drains out.

Case 2, "the split judges." Two people who have lived through both lives — one mostly bodily and athletic, one mostly scholarly — disagree, stably and with no identifiable error (no misinformation, no distraction, no weakness of will, nobody is self-deceived) about which life contained more pleasure. Neither can be shown to be making a mistake. Ask: is there a fact of the matter about which life had more? If yes, what makes it true, given that the only evidence there is — how it felt from the inside — comes out opposed? If no, then the aggregation that utilitarianism runs on has no unit, and the hedonist has to either posit a hidden common currency and say how we'd ever detect it, or admit that "amount of pleasure" isn't a quantity but only an ordering relative to a judge.

So, what I need from you:

1. The essay question itself, worded for students, with the one-lever requirement and both cases built in, and a note of how long they've got (three hours, and this is one of four questions, so they should be able to do it in about 45 minutes). Assume they have no texts in the room — so no quotation marks from Mill or anyone else, no citations, nothing that requires having a book open. It should be answerable from what came up in seminar plus their own head.

2. A model answer of about 700 words. Please make it committed — not an endless "the hedonist might reply… and then the objector…" ping-pong. Pick the strongest line and run it, and let it be seen to have a cost.

3. A marking scheme out of 20, built around five must-hit points with the marks each is worth, plus a one-line note on how students typically get each one wrong. And please say explicitly what separates an A from a B, naming the specific move each has to contain. My rough sense is that a B answer notices the circularity in the competent-judge move; an A answer says what a *non*-circular version of the quality move would even have to look like and what it would cost the theory.

One more thing: flag anywhere the model answer quietly helps itself to something I probably haven't taught them (technical formal stuff, a distinction from a paper I haven't assigned). And give me a one-line hint I could write on the board if half the room freezes.

oh — and please don't let the question collapse heterogeneity into incommensurability, or into the mere claim that pleasures have no common sensory feel. I want them to see that pleasures might share a non-sensory property (a functional role, an attitude taken toward them, being the unimpeded exercise of an activity) and that conceding that is a different concession with different costs. If your question can't be answered by someone who's memorized "quantity versus quality," we're good.
