Hey — I'm putting together a one-page handout for the philosophy of science reading group I run (mostly grad students, plus a couple of visting molecular biologists who keep insisting that they really do find real groups out there). It's going to be a decision tree: start from "some classifications let you predict better than others, and that needs explaining" and end at a named position about whether the sciences carve nature at its joints.

What I need from you:

- Eight branch nodes max, six to nine leaves. Each node has to be phrased as a question someone can answer without already knowing which view it leads to — a first-year should not be able to game the tree by recognising the label.
- Leaves I want to be reachable: Kripke–Putnam style microstructural essentialism, Boyd's homeostatic property cluster kinds, Millikan-ish historical/etiological kinds, Dupré-style promiscuous realism, something Fodor-flavoured about special-science kinds being real without reducing, the "perfectly natural properties" line you get from Lewis/Armstrong, and a flat-footed conventionalist or eliminativist leaf. Plus one of my own: the view I half-hold is that "natural kind" is indexed to a research programme's track record of successful induction over some window of time, so a class can be a kind in 1970 and stop being one in 1990 with nothing in nature changing. Call it what you like, but show me where it sits in the space, or whether it just collapses into something already on the list.
- At least three of the questions have to run on invented cases instead of the usual water/jade/tiger/mule examples, because with those everyone just recites memorised answers. I made up two you can use:
  1. The velmorphs. A deep-sea survey collects about forty species from six lineages that diverged 200 million years ago and have never exchanged genes. All of them have an elaborate feeding organ that is nearly identical in structure, built out of different tissues, and downstream of different developmental pathways. "Velmorph" is the only label anyone uses for the lot; it predicts the mechanics of that organ beautifully and predicts nothing else about the animals.
  2. Orpiment glass. A manufactured material with one name, no shared microstructure, wildly varying composition, produced by a single patented process, where every sample has an optical property the process reliably produces.
  Invent at least one more case yourself, and put a short bracketed note after each question saying what distinction it's meant to be separating.
- Where the tree can't seperate two leaves, say so rather than pretending there's a clean question that does it.
- Build the logical space first, then place the positions in it: list the combinations of answers nobody occupies, and for each say whether it's incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored.

Last thing: give me a short closing note on where the tree does violence — which branch a defender of one of the leaves would say misdescribes their view — and what argument or what kind of case would make you redraw it (merge two leaves, split one, add a node). I'd rather have that than fifteen confident leaves.
