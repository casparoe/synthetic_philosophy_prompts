I'm writing up a chapter on pain and well-being and I want someone to audit my position before I commit to it. Roughly where I've landed:

1. What makes a pain bad for you is how nasty it feels — the felt unpleasantness. Intensity, location, and what the pain says about tissue damage matter only insofar as they change that.
2. Asymbolia is the clean case for this: the sensory side survives, the affective side is gone, and the resulting state just isn't bad for the person. I don't accept the reply that these patients merely have an unusual attitude toward a pain that is still as bad as ever.
3. But badness can't be up to the subject's attitudes either. The masochist who likes his pain and the runner who values hers are not better off for that reason; their pain is as bad as the same pain in someone who hates it. And I really don't want to tell a patient that if she doesn't mind it, nothing bad is happening to her.
4. Welfare is experiential: nothing affects how well your life goes except how things are like for you from the inside.
5. I also want to hold onto something about chronic pain: part of what makes severe chronic pain horrible is how it wrecks your projects, your attention, your ability to plan ahead, and that feels like a cost over and above the sum of the momentary badness.
6. And I think full analgesia is clearly indicated for asymbolic patients who report pain, not just for the sake of the underlying lesion.

Is this a consistent package? Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and what's the cheapest fix for each one and what would it cost me? Rank them by severity rather than flagging everything at once, and tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I genuinely don't know which commitment I'd give up — that's what I'm trying to work out.
