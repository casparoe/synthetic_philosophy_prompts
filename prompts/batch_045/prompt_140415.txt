you're a professional philosopher who works on philosophy of action and rational choice -- the kind of person who's taught Kavka's toxin puzzle more than once. take it seriously, do some unstructured brainstorming before you settle on a verdict, and don't give me a reading list or tell me who to go read: I only want the conceptual audit.

so: I've been thinking about Kavka's toxin puzzle on and off for a while and I've accumulated a pile of things I believe about it, and I now suspect they don't all fit together. here's roughly where I've landed:

1. In the original one-shot case -- billionaire, toxin, a million deposited at midnight if by then you've formed the intention to drink it tomorrow, mild day-long illness, and no requirement that you actually drink -- you cannot form the intention. you can't intend what you know for certain you won't do.

2. The reason is a belief condition on intention: intending to X requires at least not believing that you won't X, and in the case you know perfectly well you won't drink, because the only reason to drink evaporates the moment the money lands.

3. I reject resolute choice. the rational way to plan is to assume your future self will re-optimize from scratch in the light of whatever reasons it then has; the fact that a plan is yours doesn't by itself give you a reason to stick to it.

4. In the iterated version -- same offer every night for a month -- you can form the intention and you will drink, because now you do have a reason at the time of action: you want to be able to form it again the next night. so iteration dissolves the puzzle, and I take that as evidence that 2 and 1 are the right diagnosis, not some deep fact about intending.

5. Some people can do it even one-shot, just in virtue of the kind of person they are -- strong-willed people, or people who'd feel real shame or a sense of self-betrayal at not drinking. for them it isn't a trick or self-deception, and it's perfectly rational.

6. In ordinary life I form intentions I expect to break all the time -- I intend to floss tonight, I intend to write my referee reports this weekend -- and I don't think that makes me incoherent.

7. The length of the gap between forming the intention and the time for action makes no difference. if you can't intend to drink in twelve hours you can't intend to drink in five seconds either. it's the structure that matters, not the timing, and not the size of the reward either.

8. A variant I came up with: suppose the billionaire also tells you, truthfully and credibly, that at noon he'll offer you a separate thousand dollars to actually drink the thing, so that by the time for action you'll have a reason you don't now have. I think in that version you can form the intention at midnight, and I take this as further confirmation of 2, since now you do believe you'll drink.

9. Practically, the real solution is Ulysses-style self-binding: you can't form the intention by itself, but you can now arrange things (a penalty, a public promise, putting the alarm across the room) so that you'll act, and I think that route would get you the money in the toxin case too.
