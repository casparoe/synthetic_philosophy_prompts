I write historical fiction, and I am stuck on a scene. My narrator, a Nyāya-trained court pandit, must persuade a visiting Buddhist interlocutor — and through him my reader, who has never met the word pramāṇa — that knowledge by comparison (upamāna: coming to know an unfamiliar thing through its resemblance to something already familiar) is a knowledge-source in its own right, not perception, inference, or testimony in disguise. His opponent grants perception, inference and testimony but denies that comparison is a fourth. Do not write the scene and do not explain the doctrine to me; give me a plan for how the explanation should run in about a page and a half of dialogue, for an intelligent reader with no Sanskrit and no philosophy.

I need the dependency structure: what must be established before the claim can even be stated, beginning, I assume, with what a pramāṇa is meant to be — a reliable route to a piece of new, correct cognition rather than a manner of reasoning. A complication I have written in: earlier in the argument the pandit concedes that recollection is not a pramāṇa, since genuine knowledge must present something new, so he cannot lean on the fact that he remembered the cow. Where does that concession bite, and how does he route around it?

One case should carry the whole thing. My provisional version: a forester's description of a gavaya, a wild ox-like animal, as cow-like; a sighting years later with no speaker present; the animal half-hidden in brush, so that the resemblance is only partly seen. What must such a case have and lack for the work to fall on comparison alone, and is my version or the plainer classical one the better example?

Three misconceptions I expect: that upamāna is just analogy in the loose Western sense; that it collapses into inference from resemblance; and that what it yields is merely a word's meaning rather than knowledge about the animal standing in front of him. In what order will these actually arise, and where in the sequence should each be headed off?

Finally, what could the reader do at the close that would show the idea had landed, and what — the theory of pervasion, the five-membered syllogism, the full Sanskrit inventory — should the pandit openly decline to enter into?
