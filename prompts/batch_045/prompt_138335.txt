Me and my friend Marta got into this at a party and I've been going in circles for two days. She thinks slurs aren't derogatory in virtue of their meaning at all: the derogation is entirely in the use, so a slur and its neutral counterpart are semantically the same word with different social mileage. I hate that conclusion and I can't find the broken step. Here's her argument as charitably as I can put it. I'll call the slur S and the neutral counterpart N, because I'd rather not type either one out.

(1) Linguistic meaning is shared and conventional: if two competent speakers genuinely disagree about what a word contributes to what is said, then at most one of them is using the word with its linguistic meaning — the other is confused about the language, or speaking non-literally, or using a different word that just happens to sound the same.

(2) Take any slur S with a neutral counterpart N. There are speakers who use S and derogate the target group. And there are speakers who use S and don't: in-group reclaimer use (members of the target group calling each other S affectionately), careful reported speech by people who are explicitly anti-slur, and utterances by small kids or second-language learners who picked S up from a dictionary entry and meant nothing hostile by it.

(3) Both sets of speakers are competent, neither is speaking non-literally, and neither is making a mistake about the language. So by (1), the derogatory component is not part of the conventional meaning of S.

(4) Generalize: the same is true of every expression with a purported evaluative or affective component — "lazy", "cruel", "torture", "genius", "yummy", thick terms generally. For each one you can find competent speakers who use it with the associated attitude and competent speakers who use it without.

(5) So nothing is derogatory or laudatory in virtue of meaning. All evaluation lives in speaker attitudes on occasions of use, and the meaning of a word like "cruel" is just its descriptive bit.

(6) That's preposterous: part of knowing what "cruel" means is knowing that cruelty is a bad-making feature. That's exactly what makes it thick rather than neutral.

(7) Marta agrees with (6) being jarring and bites the bullet anyway. She says (5) is fine, what (6) really shows is that ordinary evaluative vocabulary works exactly like slurs — evaluation in the use, not the meaning — and that my discomfort is a hangover from thinking evaluation has to be in the semantics to be real.

So she accepts the conclusion across the board. To me that's the mark of an argument that proves too much, but "it proves too much" isn't a diagnosis. My money is on (4), the jump from slurs to thick terms, being the weak joint — but she says (4) is where the argument earns its keep and the real work is all in (1)-(3), and honestly I can't tell.
