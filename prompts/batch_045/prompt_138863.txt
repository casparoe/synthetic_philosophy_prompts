I need a second opinion on an argument about the centipede game that my study partner keeps defending. Every step looks sound to me and the conclusion looks unacceptable, and I can't locate the gap.

Set the game up as follows. A pot starts at 4 and doubles after every Pass. At each of twenty nodes the mover either Takes — receiving three quarters of the pot and leaving the other player one quarter — or Passes, handing the move to the other player. If the mover at the twentieth node Passes, the pot is split evenly and the game ends. Both players care only about their own money; neither can mis-execute a choice; each wears a monitor, known to both to be infallible, that displays to the other whether she is rational in the ordinary sense of choosing to maximize her own payoff given her beliefs; both monitors read rational, and each player sees the other's reading. So altruism, trembles, and doubt about the other player are excluded by stipulation rather than assumed away.

(1) At node 20 the mover Takes: three quarters of the pot there exceeds one half of it.
(2) At node 19 the mover knows that if she Passes, the other player faces node 20 and Takes, leaving her one quarter of a doubled pot — 524,288 — whereas Taking now yields 786,432. So she Takes.
(3) The same comparison holds at node 18, and at every node back to the first: at each node, Take beats Pass, given what the next mover will do.
(4) Nothing in (1)–(3) depends on the length of the game. So in a twenty-node centipede, and in a hundred-node one, the first player Takes at node 1 and gets 3; the second gets 1.
(5) Had both players Passed at every node until the twentieth, the player moving there would Take over a million and a half and the other would receive over half a million. Both can see this before play begins.
(6) So each player, before play, would pay a great deal to be replaced by an agent that does nothing but Pass at every node — provided the other player knows of the replacement.
(7) Suppose the offer is free. Replaced by such a mechanism, and with the second player informed, the second player's best play is to Pass at each of his own nodes until the last and Take there; the mechanism cannot deviate; and the replaced player ends with 524,288 instead of 3. So rationality recommends paying up to roughly half a million to stop being a rational agent, in a game where rationality is common knowledge.
(8) And the advice in (4) is advice no one who accepts it can profitably follow: the theory prescribes the one act that guarantees 3, while telling you that you would do better to arrange to be incapable of it.

My partner says this is all in order: rationality in sequential games is simply self-effacing, and (7) is just the familiar point that commitment pays. I think something fails in the induction, but reading (1), (2) and (3) one at a time I cannot say what.

Which step do you reject? I would rather keep the stipulations and keep (1) than accept (7) and (8), but I want to know the price. Specifically: if the gap is in the move from what happens at node k+1 to what the mover at node k should do, what exactly is that step assuming, and why does it present itself as mere arithmetic on the game tree? If we repair it by adding the missing assumption, does the argument then go through — and does it take with it, as my partner cheerfully concedes, the standard case for defection in the finitely repeated prisoner's dilemma?
