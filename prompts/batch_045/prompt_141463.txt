I'm 71, retired — protein crystallography, most of my working life — so I'm used to disputes where you can say in advance what would count as evidence. A year ago I joined a philosophy reading group at the library, and I've got myself into an arguement I can't settle, about something I actually watched happen from the inside.

About fifteen years ago I sat on the committee that moved everyone in my old department into the retirement savings scheme by default: you were in at a few percent unless you filled in a form saying otherwise. Take-up went from roughly half to nearly everybody, and people who had been putting it off for years came and thanked us. Nobody on that committee lost any sleep about it. The line we used was that we hadn't removed any option, nobody was made worse off in any material way, and getting out was one form.

Last spring I was asked to join a clinical study at the hospital where I volunteer. Same sort of institution, and the care they took was something else again — two conversations, a form I initialled on every page, a cooling-off period, and this is the bit that got me: they were visibly uneasy about paying participants anything much, on the grounds that a decent payment could pressure people into it. Yet this is the same hospital that now sends out clinic appointments with the date and time already filled in, which I'd bet moves more people than a fifty pound voucher would.

So in one corner of the same building, arranging things so that the lazy or busy or muddled path lands you in the outcome the institution wants is good practice, and in the other corner it's close to a scandal. I'd like the machinery, not the jargon: what has to be true about autonomy — or about what consent is supposed to be doing — for both of those to come out right at once? And which parts of that does the practice actually need, as opposed to the story people tell afterwards? I've got a feeling those aren't the same set, and that two quite seperate theories would license exactly this pattern, which is why I don't trust my own answer.

Here's where I keep getting stuck. I wanted a number. Suppose that for any default you could measure its "grip": the share of people who end up with the defaulted option, minus the share who would pick it if you genuinely had their attention for two minutes. The pension default presumably has a large grip; so does the pre-filled appointment. Now imagine an institution had to publish that number before rolling a default out, and drop any default whose grip was above some line. What would such a rule be committed to? As far as I can see the number only carries weight if there is a fact of the matter about what people would choose with their attention engaged, and that "would" is doing the moral work — not the fact that they were free to say no. Is that what the "you can always opt out" defence is quietly leaning on? And if it is, does the defence survive being said out loud, or does it turn into something much more demanding — something closer to what the consent people think they're already doing?

One more datum, since I said I liked evidence. When our committee made the default explicit in the enrolment letter — told people outright that we had enrolled them, why, and how to leave — take-up fell. Not back to the old level, but noticeably. A couple of my former colleagues took that as proof the default had been a trick all along. I don't think it proves that, but I can't say what it would take for that drop to be evidence either way.

So: what does this pattern of practice presuppose, where exactly is it underdetermined, is the consent case different in principle or only because of the relationship and the stakes, and what would have to change in both corners if grip were the test?
