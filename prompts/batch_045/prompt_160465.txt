hey — I've more or less come to accept the no-pure-time-preference line: the idea that shaving value off a benefit just because it lands later (as opposed to because it might not happen, or because the recipient will be richer, or because of diminishing marginal utility) is a form of arbitrary discrimination, and so the social discount rate should carry no pure component at all. I also think it bites longtermism harder than longtermists admit: if date-based discounting is arbitrary then its arbitrary in both directions, so the far future can't get any *extra* weight for being far, and whatever case there is for existential-risk work has to come entirely out of headcount and probability, never out of temporal position.

But I keep worrying I've built myself something unfalsifiable. So: what would actually count against this?

Things I want you to get at specifically:

- The Relay case I've been kicking around. Say an endowment is run by a series of 40-year custodians. None of them has any pure time preference — each treats a benefit in year 1 and year 39 of their own tenure identically. But each is partial, non-temporally, to benefits falling during their own custodianship, the way you'd be partial to your own projects rather than someone else's. Ten custodians out and benefits 400 years off are weighted at about a tenth of a percent, i.e. an effective ~2.4% annual decay, with zero pure time preference anywhere in the system. Is that a defeater for my view, or a separate phenomenon it never had a quarrel with? Would a committed defender concede it or wave it off?
- The general version of that: we don't treat it as irrational to weight the near and dear more heavily, and temporal proximity plausibly generates the same kind of relationship. Why is temporal partiality supposedly the one kind that's out of bounds?
- Does the view quietly depend on a metaphysics of persistence — on there being a fact of the matter that it's *you* who receives later — that I'd definately lose if I went reductionist about personal identity? That's the line of attack I'd personally find most moving, so push on it hard.
- Does the position have any distinguishing predictions left, or does zero pure preference plus consumption growth plus catastrophe hazard land on basically the same policy numbers as a modest positive pure rate? And if it does, is my view insulated into vacuity — and is that a problem for a view of this kind or just what correctness looks like?

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, be explicit about which ones a defender of the view would themself concede as counting against it versus which they'd dismiss as confused, and give me your honest read on how exposed the position really is. Please don't cite anything or point me at papers — I can't look any of it up, so argue it out in your own terms.
