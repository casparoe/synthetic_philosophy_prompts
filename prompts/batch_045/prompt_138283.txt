I've been sitting in on a graduate reading group on accuracy-first epistemology — the project of vindicating the norms on credence (probabilism, conditionalization, and the rest) from the single idea that credences are better the closer they are to the truth values, with no appeal to what credences are for in action. After last week's session two of the regulars, Mira and Dev, got into an argument they ended up asking me to referee, and I genuinely couldn't tell who had the better of it. Both were sharp and neither was being obtuse, so I'd like you to adjudicate the exchange as it was actually made rather than to describe the state of the literature. I know the basic framework, so you don't need to explain what a scoring rule is; where the algebra matters, show it.

Some common ground first, since they'd agreed on it the week before. Fix a partition into n cells; at the cell that obtains, the truth-value vector v is 1 in that coordinate and 0 elsewhere. A measure of accuracy assigns each credence function c a score at each cell. They used two: the quadratic (Brier) measure, score = 1 − Σᵢ (cᵢ − vᵢ)², and what Dev called the taxicab measure, score = 1 − ½ Σᵢ |cᵢ − vᵢ|. The exchange then went like this.

(1) Mira: The accuracy-dominance argument for probabilism is the cleanest thing in the area. On the quadratic measure, if c is incoherent then there is a coherent c* that is strictly more accurate than c in every single cell. So incoherence is ruled out without any pragmatic premise: it isn't that the incoherent agent will lose a bet or do worse in some decision problem, it's that in every possible world she is further from the truth than she had to be. And the argument rests on dominance, not on expected-utility maximization, so the standard complaint that accuracy-first epistemology smuggles in decision theory is misplaced — you never have to claim the agent maximizes anything.

(2) Dev: The conclusion is only as good as the measure, and closeness to the truth doesn't single one out. Take the taxicab measure, which is an equally natural reading of total distance from the truth values. It misbehaves: in a two-cell partition, if your credence in p is 0.8, then your expected taxicab score from reporting q is 0.8q + 0.2(1 − q), which is maximized at q = 1, not at q = 0.8 — 0.8 against 0.68 for reporting honestly. The measure rewards jumping to extremes; it isn't even proper. Never mind that nothing can be incoherent in a two-cell partition: the point is that the measure has to be justified on its own, before the dominance argument gets to run.

(3) Mira: Impropriety is irrelevant to a dominance argument. Dominance never mentions expectations, so there is nothing for propriety to do; you could run the argument on an improper measure. What you would need is a measure on which some incoherent credence function escapes domination, and the taxicab measure doesn't give you that — for any incoherent c, the coherent function closest to it dominates it. The quadratic measure is to be preferred because Euclidean distance is the natural geometry of closeness, not because it is proper, and the result is robust across reasonable geometries.

(4) Dev: It isn't robust, and here is the calculation, in a three-cell partition. Under the taxicab measure, any coherent d scores exactly dᵢ at cell i, since ½ Σⱼ |dⱼ − vⱼ| = 1 − dᵢ. Now take c = (0.2, 0.2, 0.2): at every cell its score is 1 − ½(0.8 + 0.2 + 0.2) = 0.4. For a coherent d to dominate it we would need dᵢ ≥ 0.4 at all three cells, hence Σdᵢ ≥ 1.2, which no coherent function can do. So that incoherent function is undominated and your recipe fails. And it is systematic, not a fluke of that number: the taxicab score of c at cell i works out to cᵢ + (1 − T)/2, where T = Σcᵢ, so an incoherent c is dominated only when T ≥ 1. The measure punishes credences that sum to more than 1 and lets credences that sum to less than 1 off entirely.

(5) Mira: I concede the arithmetic, but it gives you less than it looks like. It is a reason to reject the taxicab measure, not a reason to reject the program. A measure of accuracy has to make an agent's own credences score best by her own lights — that is part of what it is to measure closeness to the truth rather than something else, such as how decisive or striking a set of opinions looks. The taxicab measure fails that test precisely because it rewards extremism, which is the same defect that makes it unable to see what is wrong with (0.2, 0.2, 0.2). Restrict to strictly proper measures and the dominance argument goes through, so probabilism is still vindicated from accuracy alone.

(6) Dev: Then you have relocated the assumption instead of removing it. Strict propriety is defined in terms of expected accuracy, expectation is probabilistic expectation, and probabilistic expectation already presupposes the additive structure probabilism was supposed to establish — so the vindication is circular. And look at the shape of the dialectic: dominance was supposed to spare us the decision-theoretic machinery, yet the moment the measure is challenged you have to go back to expectations to defend the measure. Worse, strict propriety doesn't pick out a unique measure either: the quadratic, the logarithmic and the spherical measures are all strictly proper, and they disagree about which coherent function is closest to a given incoherent one and about how accurate various updating policies are. So you still owe an account of which of them measures accuracy, and the one that yields the norms I wanted in advance isn't an account.

(7) Jonas, who had been quiet until then: Isn't the moral larger than either of you is making it? The selling point of the program was that it vindicates the norms on credence without invoking what credences are for — that was the whole point of treating accuracy as a purely epistemic good. But if the choice of measure is carrying the argumentative weight, and the measure is selected partly by looking at which norms it delivers, then accuracy-first epistemology is doing exactly the consequentialist, pick-the-norm-by-its-outputs reasoning it was meant to be an alternative to — just one level up.

Please go through this move by move: for each of the seven, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and say briefly why. Check Dev's arithmetic in (4) and Mira's claims in (3) and (5) explicitly and tell me whether either of them gets something wrong. Then tell me who has the better of the exchange overall, and what, if anything, both of them are missing — including whether Jonas's parting shot lands on something real. I'm agnostic myself; my only hunch is that the live question is what constraints a measure of accuracy has to meet and whether those constraints can be stated without already assuming what the program wants to prove, but I may be mislocating the issue entirely.
