I run a student reading group on catastrophic and governance risk — about 16 undergrads, 90 minutes, half philosophy majors and half CS/policy people — and I want one session to be a game instead of a discussion. Personal reason this is on my mind: I'm also the treasurer-ish person on a student org whose constitution lets any one of the seven officers commit up to $300 without a vote, and last semester someone did exactly that on something the rest of us thought was a bad idea, and nobody in the room could quite say why the rule itself was the problem. What I want them to run into is the unilateralist's curse: when any member of a group can take a high-stakes action on their own, the action gets taken if *anybody's* private estimate is optimistic enough, so the group's effective decision is made by the largest optimistic error rather than by the median member — and adding more people makes the outcome worse, not better, because you're drawing more times from the tail. I don't want them to hear any of that up front, or even the name; the rules should do it to them.

The wrinkle I keep chewing on and want built into the design: in real groups the members aren't equally good at estimating, and they don't know which ones they are. Some people's private signals are mostly noise. My hunch is that this makes the curse worse in a specific and slightly depressing way — the person who presses is predictably one of the *least reliable* members, not just the most optimistic — and I want that to be visible in the room, not only in the numbers I show afterward. So give me a signal-generating procedure I can actually implement offline (index cards, envelopes, a deck of cards, or a printed spreadsheet — I have a printer and a bag of cheap candy, no real money, and I'd rather not use laptops), with at least two tiers of signal quality that participants can't tell apart, plus real payoff numbers and a scheme where the losses from a bad press are shared by the whole group while the gains from a good press go disproportionately to the presser, since that's how it usually works in real life — though tell me if that incentive skew is a mistake and why.

I also want them to get to rewrite the rules between rounds rather than only hear about fixes afterward. Two options I'd like on the table: anyone can publicly and irrevocably stand down (give up their own power to press) before a round starts, and the group can switch to a veto rule where the action happens only if nobody objects. My guess is the veto rule will feel obviously better in the room and is actually it's own failure mode — one nervous person blocks everything — and that the really nasty part is that this second failure is invisible: when a unilateral press goes wrong you get a disaster you can point at, but when a veto blocks a good action nothing happens and nobody gets credit for value that was never created, so groups drift toward veto rules even when the numbers say otherwise. If that's right, build in some way to make the forgone gains visible at the end (a running tally of points left on the table across rounds?) without me having to explain it first. Please give me: the rules and round structure, including at least one round played in small groups and one with all 16 so the group-size effect shows up; the materials and payoff scheme; a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto parts of the mechanism; what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (my bet is somebody slaps the button in the first five seconds and the round is over before it begins, or that everyone just shrugs because candy points don't feel like anything); and an honest note on which feature of the idea the game can't reproduce and I'll have to say out loud myself.
