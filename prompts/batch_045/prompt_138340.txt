I teach a first-year undergraduate course called Making History: Evidence, Method, and Doubt — about twenty students, mostly not history majors — and I have one ninety-minute slot to spend on why historical explanation feels so much more inevitable than it is. I can also get the same room for a twenty-minute slot one week later if your design needs a delay between a forecast and its recall, but I'd rather not depend on that. The room has a whiteboard and nothing else: no projection, no laptops, so everything has to run on printed handouts, index cards, envelopes and pens. What I want is a runnable exercise — a game or structured deliberation with rules, small real stakes (seminar credit is fine, money is not) and a debrief — that makes students collide with two things at once: hindsight bias, the way knowing an outcome silently rewrites your memory of your own earlier uncertainty, and the narrative fallacy, the pressure to impose agents, turning points and an ending on what was mostly a conjunctural mess. They should hit both before either gets named; I don't want the terms in the air until the last ten minutes.

My half-formed plan, which I'd like you to tighten, correct, or replace where it's weak: students receive a dossier of roughly fourteen dated dispatches from an invented crisis in an invented setting — a chartered trading post, a provincial water authority, an Antarctic research station, anything with no real-world counterpart, so that prior knowledge can't help and nobody can go and look anything up. Three of the items should be dead ends: leads that looked decisive at the time and led nowhere. Two should be counter-indications, pointing away from whatever eventually happens. Phase one is individual and sealed: each student writes a probability distribution over four candidate outcomes, scored with a proper quadratic (Brier-style) rule converted into credit, so that honesty about their own uncertainty pays and hedging does not. Phase two is in groups of four or five: each group is handed a sealed envelope containing what happened and writes a 150-word explanation of why it had to happen that way. Here I've been toying with a twist I can't quite calibrate: give half the room one outcome and half the room a different, incompatible one, and then reveal at the end that neither was the real one — the actual outcome was anti-climactic, the crisis simply petered out, no decision was ever taken. The thing I want them to walk into is that the demand for a climax doesn't weaken when the events don't supply one: the pressure comes from the explainer, not from the world. Tell me whether the two-envelope version or the false-outcome version earns the extra complexity, how to keep the reveal from feeling like a trick played on them rather than a result they produced, and what to do if a group guesses the twist from the sheer contrivance of the setup.

I also want two or three cheap, countable measures that turn your story is suspiciously smooth from a suspicion into a number students can compute on their own sheets — candidates I've been kicking around are a compression ratio (dossier facts against facts actually cited, and against facts cited with their valence reversed), a dead-end audit (what fraction of the dead ends were silently dropped, and what fraction were retro-fitted into causes), and a counterfactual gradient (rating four or five branch points for how close the world came to going the other way, once from inside the dossier and once after the reveal, to watch the gradient collapse). Tell me which of these are worth the minutes and which are busywork. Please give me: the dossier written out in full, with a private note on which items are dead ends, which are counter-indicators, and what an honest forecast distribution over the four outcomes would look like given that no single item should be a giveaway; the rules, the scoring rule and its credit conversion; a minute-by-minute timeline; the debrief, mapping specific moments in the session onto specific features of hindsight bias and of narrative pressure, and saying what participants should be able to see by the end that they couldn't see at the start; an honest note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, including the student who announces that it was all chance and therefore refuses to explain anything; and a frank statement of which feature of the problem the exercise cannot reproduce and I will have to supply in words — my worry is that the dossier arrives fully formed, whereas in real historical work the archive has already been filtered by what survived and by what somebody chose to write down, so the exercise may localize the distortion in the explainer when some of it lives in the record. Close the whole response with a single final line, and nothing after it, of exactly the form \answer{...}, naming in six words or fewer the one mechanic the entire session hinges on.
