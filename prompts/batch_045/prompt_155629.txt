I'm reading a popular book on complexity whose treatment of chaos comes down to this: a chaotic system is deterministic in its equations and random in everything that matters, because any uncertainty about where it starts is stretched exponentially, the stretching outruns any measurement we could make, and so the honest description of its future is probabilistic — and the probabilities behave exactly like the probabilities of a fair coin. My brother-in-law has taken this up and uses it for two quite different jobs. In arguments about climate policy he says that if the atmosphere is chaotic, then a projection of mean temperature decades out has no more standing than a forecast about coin flips; you cannot predict a chaotic system past its horizon, so the projections people argue about are noise dressed up as science. In arguments about free will he says the same result is a relief: determinism does not entail predictability, so even in a deterministic world our choices are not the sort of thing anyone could have foreseen, and that, he thinks, is all the room that moral responsibility needs.

I don't want a verdict on climate policy or on free will. I want to know how much work the coin picture can do. Specifically:

1. Set out the correspondence explicitly, a table being fine. In the coin case, what plays the role of the individual outcome, the probability, the independence of successive outcomes, the insensitivity to further information, and the thing that makes long-run frequencies stable? What corresponds to each of those in a chaotic system, and which items on the chaotic side have no coin-side counterpart at all?

2. Separate the disanalogies that matter from the ones that don't, and do it separately for each job. As far as I can tell, a chaotic system has at least three things a coin lacks: a state that more precise measurement would partly reveal, with each additional bit of precision buying a fixed increment of forecast horizon rather than an arbitrary one; statistical structure that stays put while the trajectory does not, in that the motion is confined to a bounded set whose regions it visits with stable frequencies; and a sensitivity to the present that is exploitable, since if small causes have large effects then small deliberate nudges do too. Is that the right list? And which of these, if any, carry the weight in the climate argument, and which in the free-will argument?

3. Say what the analogy quietly imports. A coin has no memory, no state to be measured better, no short-run structure that a clever observer could exploit, and probabilities that look intrinsic rather than expressions of ignorance about a fixed condition. Which of these imports is the book leaning on, and which is my brother-in-law leaning on? Are they the same one?

4. Tell me whether either conclusion survives once the imports are on the table. For the climate case: is it coherent to hold both that the trajectory of a chaotic system cannot be predicted pointwise beyond a horizon and that its long-run statistics — or the way those statistics shift when a forcing changes — can still be projected? If it is coherent, does the coin picture have any way of representing the distinction between predicting where the system will be and predicting the distribution it will wander over, or is that distinction exactly what the analogy erases? For the free-will case: does the argument need the unpredictability to be more than epistemic, and what is left of it if it is only epistemic? I have a suspicion that the second use depends on a slide from unpredictable to not determined, but I can't tell whether the book's framing is what licenses the slide.

5. If there is a better analogy available — a mixing deck, a smoke plume, a wheel you can watch for the first half-turn, something else — say what it gets right and what it in turn gets wrong.

For definiteness, take the target system to be something like a driven, damped pendulum or a simplified atmosphere: bounded and dissipative, with a small number of positive Lyapunov exponents, and with statistics that settle onto an attracting set.
