I am writing a chapter on sceptical scenarios in expected-value reasoning about the very long-run future, and I keep encountering an argumentative configuration for which I have no label. I do not need citations or a reading list; what I need is the shape of the thing and a definition I can use.

The first form it takes goes like this. Someone introduces the possibility that the world we inhabit is a computer simulation run by some other civilisation, and concludes that a project aimed at affecting the far future should have its expected value scaled down by roughly the probability that we are in such a run — because the run will probably be terminated before the future we are trying to affect arrives, or because the simulators will not permit the consequences we care about to accumulate. What I cannot name is what happens around that move. Nothing else in the calculation is re-derived under the hypothesis that has just been taken seriously. The size of the payoff is left at its pre-hypothesis value, even though a civilisation capable of running our world is presumably capable of running many like it, so that conditional on the project succeeding the number of beings affected may be larger rather than smaller. The location of the effects is left alone, even though on that hypothesis what we do here might matter primarily by influencing the simulators rather than by propagating through our own future, so that the hypothesis relocates the stakes instead of removing them. And no comparable scaling is applied to the near-term options the project is being compared against, even though the termination worry applies to them in kind — it is only that over a short enough window the accumulated risk is too small to notice. The hypothesis is consulted at exactly one point in the reasoning.

The second form is the mirror of it. In the same discussion, the hypothesis is used to deflate one high-stakes project and then, a few paragraphs later, used as a positive reason for a different expenditure — that if we are being watched, it matters what the run looks like to whoever is watching. Nothing is said about why the hypothesis is epistemically live in the first use and inert in the second, or about what makes it a reason-generator in one place and a reason-killer in another.

I want to be clear that I am not after the broad psychological diagnosis. There is a principled version of the asymmetry: the probability of termination plausibly accumulates with elapsed time, so it is entirely in order for long-horizon projects to be discounted more heavily than short-horizon ones, and a discount with the right time-profile is not the thing I am trying to isolate. What I am after is narrower: a hypothesis that is admitted into the probability term of one option's expected value and then withheld from the value term, from the alternative options, and from any use that would raise rather than lower the total.

So: does this configuration have an established name in epistemology, decision theory, or the literature on long-term priorities? If it does, I want it together with the conditions under which it applies. If it does not, I want it named and defined, with its edges marked. Either way, tell me how it differs from each of the things it resembles — ordinary motivated or confirmation-biased use of evidence, which is about which evidence gets sampled rather than about the asymmetric role one scenario plays inside a single calculation; scepticism of the Pascal's-mugging sort, which worries about the magnitude of small probabilities of enormous payoffs rather than about scenario-dependence; debunking arguments and the familiar objection that they generalise too far or undermine themselves, which is structurally close but seems to target a whole domain of belief rather than one term in one computation; the undercutting-versus-rebutting distinction among defeaters, which classifies an evidential relation rather than a pattern of deployment; and the first-order claim that a run would probably be shut down early, which is a substantive risk rather than a fact about how that risk is used.

Give me boundary cases as well: the principled time-horizon discount that is not an instance; a treatment that adjusts probability and payoff together under the hypothesis; a case in which the hypothesis is deployed only to raise the stakes; and the case where a reason for the asymmetry is explicitly offered but is a bad reason. Tell me what I would have to find in a piece of argument to diagnose the opportunistic version rather than the principled one. And finally: when the hypothesis is applied symmetrically, does the dampening of far-future fanaticism survive at all, or does it turn out to have been transferring the fanaticism somewhere else?
