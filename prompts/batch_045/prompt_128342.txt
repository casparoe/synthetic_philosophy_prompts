I'm writing the third chapter of my dissertation on the principle of indifference, and I want the chapter's central claim stress-tested before I commit to it in print. The claim is a version of the transformation-group reply: Bertrand's paradox does not refute the principle of indifference, it only shows that "choose a random chord" is an incomplete specification, and once the specification is completed in the right way the principle delivers a unique answer. The "right way," on my view, is to demand that the probability assignment be invariant under the symmetry group of the randomization procedure rather than under some arbitrary enumeration of cases. Concretely: if what produces the chord is something like a straw tossed at a plane with a circle painted on it, or a spray of parallel lines whose position is translation-invariant and whose orientation is uniform, then the distribution over chords is the unique rigid-motion-invariant measure on lines in the plane, conditional on intersection with the circle, and the probability that the chord exceeds the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle comes out at 1/2 — not 1/3 and not 1/4. I want to defend this as a general moral, not a one-off: indifference is a constraint on admissible priors given a symmetry group, and the classical paradoxes are what you get when people apply it while silently switching parametrizations.

What I'd like from you is an honest map of the exposure of that claim. Several worries are already nagging at me and I want them developed rather than listed. First, the maximum-entropy formulation of the same idea looks circular in a way that matters: differential entropy is not invariant under reparametrization, so maximizing it requires a background measure, and choosing that measure looks exactly like the choice the principle was supposed to make for us. Second, I've been constructing cases where the figure lacks the circle's symmetry and I'm not sure my reply survives them: replace the circle with a fixed ellipse (every ellipse is an affine image of the circle, so the problem can be pulled back to the circle, but the pulled-back question is no longer rotation-symmetric), or re-embed the same disk by central projection onto a hemisphere, where the measure invariant under the plane's rigid motions is not the measure invariant under the sphere's rotations, though each looks like "the uniform distribution over lines" in its own setting. Third, I've checked that the paradox is not an artifact of the continuum: the obvious finite analogues — two vertices chosen uniformly from a regular N-gon, a lattice point chosen uniformly in the disk, a lattice direction with a uniform point along it — converge on 1/3, 1/4, and 1/2 respectively, so I can't lean on finiteness to dissolve it. Fourth, I suspect that demanding more symmetry may demand too much: if invariance under the plane's rigid motions singles out a measure, what happens if we ask for invariance under the affine group, or the projective group, or some larger group still — does the same answer get selected more robustly, or does the demand eventually become unsatisfiable, so that indifference falls silent exactly when we push it hardest? And fifth, there is what I take to be the deepest worry: if the answer always arrives only after a physical randomization procedure has been specified, then the principle of indifference is really a piece of empirical physics wearing a priori clothing, and it has nothing to say in the cases that motivated me — cosmology, anthropics, prior choice in model selection — where no such procedure is available.

Please tell me what would count against my view, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about which of these a committed defender should concede as genuine defeaters rather than dismiss as misunderstanding the view. I especially want you to separate live worries from merely logically possible ones, and to say what my position predicts that its rivals — the "no fact of the matter" view and the "the principle is simply invalid for continuum-sized spaces" view — do not. Two further things: note where, if anywhere, I have qualified the claim into unfalsifiability (my suspicion is that "pick the symmetry group appropriate to the physical setup" can accommodate any observed distribution, and I want to know whether that is fatal or merely the price of a correct methodology). And finally, tell me which of my own worries you think I am overrating and which I am underrating; personally I find the background-measure circularity and the re-embedding cases most disturbing, and I'd rather be told that I have the ranking backwards than have my confidence confirmed.
