I'm a novelist, three books in, and the one I'm stuck on is about a woman — call her Ilse — who dies a third of the way through and who the other characters keep insisting is still around: her husband finishes a sentence in her cadence, her old piano teacher hears her corrections in his head, her daughter makes a choice she'd never have made before and everyone in the room recognises it as Ilse's. I got into all this through that Hofstadter line of thought, the one where a person isn't sealed inside one skull but is spread in varying amounts across a lot of brains, so that when someone dies whatever was running in the people who knew them doesn't simply go out. Lovely for a novel, useless for plotting, because I keep writing scenes where a character says "that was her" or "no, that was only you" and I have no idea what rule I'm applying. So I've been polling my own gut on cases, and here's where I land: (1) husband of fifty years, a year after her death, can still tell you what she'd have thought about a film neither of them saw and winces at a phrase she'd have winced at — yes, genuinely her, maybe thirty percent, and it fades; (2) a biographer who has read every letter and diary and scores exactly as well as the husband on the same test — no, zero, pure ventriloquism; (3) a former student who edits the way she edited and whose taste tracks hers on new manuscripts, though they hadn't spoken in twenty years — yes, but less than the husband even if you stipulate the behavioural match is identical, and I can't say what the difference is; (4) her left hemisphere, kept alive alone after the right is destroyed — her, full stop, no hesitation; (5) both hemispheres separated, each sustaining a person — neither one is her, both are heirs, and yet I don't want to say she died at the split; I want to say she's in both now and is in two places at once, which contradicts what I just said a sentence earlier; (6) a copy made from a full structural scan while she was alive, with the original dying in a crash an hour later — today I say yes, and more so than the student, which I feel slightly guilty about given (2); (7) her brother, who she half-raised, where I want to say she's in him about as much as he's in her and that it was already true before she died, which makes me think death isn't really what's doing the work here; (8) a system trained on everything she ever wrote that reproduces her judgements perfectly and is causally downstream of her in the boring ordinary sense — not her, not even slightly, and I'm definately more confident about that than about (6).

The only principle I've managed to write down that gets (1), (3) and (7) right is something like: the amount of her in you is the fraction of your dispositions that a complete causal-explanatory story of you couldn't account for without adverting to her, the stuff about you that only makes sense by pointing at her. That feels right about the husband and wrong about (8), where the story of the system does seem to have to advert to her and I still want to say zero, and it doesn't obviously say anything about (5) at all. Where's the flaw, and is there a repair — or a different account — that delivers most of my verdicts? If you can't have them all, tell me which you'd overturn and what that costs: I care most about keeping (1) yes and (2) no, and I'd surrender (6) first. Two more things I need it to do. First, the actual plot of my book: a procedure I invented where roughly a quarter of Ilse's memory-structures are transplanted into four different people while she's still alive, and she dies a year later — on your account is that one "I" located in five places, four successors, or one new "I" that didn't exist before, and can the percentages across different people add up to more than a hundred, i.e. can there be more of her in the world after she dies than there was while she was alive? Second, my mother, dead eight years, whom I catch myself "consulting" all the time: I want to say that's just me and not her, but I can't see how to hold that while keeping (1).
