I am seventy-four, and retired from a career in experimental physics — most of it, in one guise or another, spent on calibration: fitting curves to readings taken against known standards, and then arguing about how far beyond the last standard it was honest to extrapolate. Two years ago I joined a reading group, and this term we have been working slowly through Saul Kripke's *Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language: An Elementary Exposition* (Harvard, 1982). I am convening the session after next, and I would like it to be a session in which we do something rather than sit and discuss.

The group is eleven people, all retired, meeting for ninety minutes in a room above a library with a table, a whiteboard and nothing electronic — two members will not use anything with a screen, and I would rather the activity not depend on one. Six of us are scientists or engineers of some kind, there is a retired mathematics teacher, a retired solicitor, and three who read only novels and poetry. Most of the group have found the book exasperating. The mathematics teacher's line is that of course there is a right continuation, namely the simplest rule consistent with the data, and what more could anyone possibly want. Another member says, with some impatience, that this is sophistry of a sort we would never have tolerated in the laboratory, and that our instruments did not disagree about what their readings meant. I do not want either of those reactions answered at the end by me; I want the activity built so that they are what drives it.

There are three things I want the group to walk into, in this order, before anyone names them.

First: that no finite run of examples, however long and however well-behaved, settles what the next case is. Kripke's invented function is the sort of thing I mean — a rule that agrees with addition for every pair of numbers we have ever had occasion to add, and then departs from it at some point past everything we have actually done, so that nothing in my past usage singles out addition over that rival. I would like the group to arrive at that collision themselves, ideally by extrapolating privately and then discovering what the others wrote, rather than by being told about it.

Second: that the obvious escape hatch is not available, and this is the part I care most about. My own first instinct was to say that the rule is simply the simplest curve through the data. I have since come to think that this only relocates the difficulty: simplicity is measured relative to a vocabulary, and no finite run of examples tells you which vocabulary to measure it in either, so the challenge reappears one level up — a language with a primitive for the deviant function makes the deviant continuation the short one. If it can be staged, I would like the group to find this out by having their own criterion turned on them: handed a second, unfamiliar notation in which the continuation they rejected is the one with the shorter description, and left to work out what that does to the criterion they were relying on.

Third: the bit I cannot yet get hold of. Suppose we settle the rule, and settle the vocabulary, and all of us agree. There still seems to be something left over — that I *ought* to go on in one way rather than the other. In the laboratory we had a working substitute for that ought: the instrument is faulty, the calibration has drifted, the reading is wrong. I suspect the working substitute is all there is, but I want the session to end with the group having felt the residue rather than having had it described.

Please design the session for me. I need the materials (cards, envelopes, paper, anything printed in advance — I can prepare things at home but not spend money), the rules, the roles if there are to be roles, and a minute-by-minute outline for ninety minutes. No lecture at the front, and nothing named before the group has met it. I would also like your advice on one point of conduct: I would rather not deceive these people outright, but I am willing to withhold, and I would like to know where you think the line falls.

Then give me the debrief: which moment in the session is supposed to map onto which part of the argument, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — the mathematics teacher and the impatience about sophistry especially, and the risk that the whole thing collapses into a general shrug about meaning, which the solicitor will not accept — and how I should handle each. Finally, be frank about which part of the argument the activity cannot reproduce, and which part I shall simply have to say.
