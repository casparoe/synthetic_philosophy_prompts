I write a science-and-ideas column, and I am working on a long piece about who gets counted: the recent push to write cephalopods and decapod crustaceans into sentience legislation, the rapid growth of insect farming as livestock, the rules that govern laboratory use and pest control, and the researchers who now get asked by regulators to say which animals feel anything at all. What I have found in my interviews is that everyone has a verdict and nobody has a standard. Each specialist can tell me which creatures they would include, and why their rivals are being sentimental or chauvinistic, but when I ask what an account of the distribution of consciousness across animals would have to do in order to be worth acting on, the answers turn into anecdotes about octopuses. So before my next round of interviews I want the specification rather than the verdicts: what must such a theory be able to deliver, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, given that I am asking for adequacy for a particular purpose — good enough for a regulator deciding which animals fall under welfare law and a funder deciding which experiments to permit, not good enough to settle the metaphysics in the abstract.

To save you from writing a generic list, here are the places where I keep watching the conversation break down, and I would like you to turn them into requirements only where they really are requirements (tell me plainly if any of my cases is a bad demand):

- The anchoring dilemma, as I have been calling it in my notes. The only evidence where we have something like ground truth is human self-report, and every candidate marker is validated against that; but the markers that report-validated work licenses are tied to human hardware, so using them to rule on a creature whose nervous system is built on a different plan looks like we are measuring one thing and judging another. A theory needs some account of how it gets from our one calibrated case to creatures that will never answer.
- The discordance problem. Across taxa the markers refuse to line up: one group shows flexible motivational trade-offs but no apparent modulation by analgesics; another shows nociceptive sensitisation and protective guarding but nothing that looks like reversal learning or trade-off; a third looks inert under every behavioural test but has an unexpectedly rich neural repertoire. I want a theory to say what happens when the evidence profile is internally contradictory — whether some markers are privileged, whether there is a hierarchy, whether a single negative marker can veto — because a bare checklist does not seem to answer this and yet everyone argues as if it does.
- The ersatz-flexibility control. Slime moulds solve mazes, plants respond to damage with systemic signalling, a detached octopus arm keeps executing avoidance routines, and a simple reinforcement-learning agent will trade one cost against another. Whatever the theory says about bees or crabs, it has to say why these are not the same kind of case.
- The split-subject and interrupted-subject cases. Colonial and modular organisms where it is unclear whether there is one subject or many; an animal whose neural wiring is substantially rebuilt through metamorphosis; a creature that is unconscious right now but is the kind of thing that has experiences at other times. The theory has to tell me what it is attributing consciousness to, and over what interval.
- The distinction between being conscious at all, being conscious of this, having more or less of it, and mattering more or less. I keep seeing these run together in policy discussions, and I want to know which collapses are the theory's fault and which are the legislators'.
- The numbers. The categories where the science is most uncertain are also the categories with astronomical population counts, so a verdict of maybe, at roughly even odds, for insects and other small invertebrates is either the most consequential output the theory could produce or practically unusable. I want to know whether being action-guiding under that kind of uncertainty is a legitimate desideratum for a theory of consciousness, or whether I am smuggling in a decision-theoretic requirement that does not belong on the list.

Beyond the requirements themselves, three things would make this usable for me. First, mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are really preferences of a particular camp dressed up as constraints — I can hear when a neuroanatomist and a comparative psychologist are each defining the question so that their own evidence is the only relevant kind, and I want that exposed. Second, flag any subset of the requirements that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which one gives: if the list itself turns out to be internally inconsistent, that is the most interesting thing in the piece. Third, tell me which single requirement knocks out the largest share of the theories currently on offer, and why.

After that, and briefly, I would like a short scorecard — a few lines each, no table needed — for the four broad styles of view I keep meeting in interviews, described by their content rather than by their authors: structure-first views that tie consciousness to particular neural architecture; behavioural and marker-based views that work from what the animal does; views that require a specific functional organisation such as a global workspace or a self-modelling predictive architecture; and the pluralist, weight-of-evidence profile approaches that have become the unofficial method of official reviews. I do not want a bibliography or any citations, and I am not asking you to reconstruct the literature — I want arguments I can put to people across a table and see how they squirm, with brief sketches of how each style handles the hardest requirement on your list rather than a summary of who published what.
