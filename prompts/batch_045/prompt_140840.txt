I've just spent a long evening arguing with a friend about Kuhn, and I want to turn the stalemate into an exam question. Her position was that incommensurability is just relativism with better press, and that Kuhn's own list of criteria for theory choice — accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity, fruitfulness — gives the game away: if rival paradigms can be weighed against the same five criteria, they were never really incommensurable. Mine was that sharing a list of values is not the same as sharing a measure, since the criteria can be weighted differently, and even applied differently, once the kind-terms shift, so the list is compatible with the incommensurability thesis rather than a refutation of it. We went round in circles on whether "measured differently" collapses into "not compared at all", and I came away unsure whether my reply was a distinction or a dodge.

I'm setting the final for an upper-level undergraduate philosophy of science seminar: one essay question, answered in forty-five minutes, marked out of 25. Please write the question, a model answer of roughly 600–700 words, and a marking scheme that separates a First from a 2:1 by naming the moves each must contain.

The question should be unanswerable by anyone who has only absorbed the slogans — "paradigm shift", "gestalt switch", "no neutral observation language" — and should force candidates to do three things: (a) say precisely what incommensurability is a claim about (meaning, translation, comparability, or rational theory choice) and which of those is supposed to follow from which; (b) settle whether Kuhn's five criteria are consistent with his own thesis, and whether distinguishing values from rules does any real work, or is only a relabelling; (c) address a problem I'd like invented for the question — if two paradigms share no kind-terms, can they still be compared by what they let us do, the instruments, interventions and predictions they make possible, rather than by what they say? Candidates should have to say whether that is a genuine common measure, or whether it merely relocates the shared vocabulary into a language of manipulation and quietly concedes Kuhn's point (or collapses into a realism about entities that he would reject).

The marking scheme should also name the errors that cost marks: treating incommensurability as entailing incomparability, confusing it with the theory-ladenness of observation, or appealing to a neutral description of what a revolution "loses" without noticing what that appeal presupposes.
