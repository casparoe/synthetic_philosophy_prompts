I'm revising a paper in philosophy of biology and I want you to attack the view I'm defending rather than help me defend it. Call it the maintenance account, or MA: functions attach in the first instance to trait types in populations, and a trait type T's function is the effect E such that tokens of T are currently being retained in the population *because* they produce E, where retaining-because is a difference-making, selection-involving relation over a biologically relevant run of generations, so that if bearers of T stopped producing E, and nothing else changed, T would be on its way out. I like MA because it seems to keep the etiological account's goodies — historical grounding, a naturalistic kind of normativity, a real distinction between functioning and malfunctioning, and a principled way of excluding the effects that a pure systemic-contribution account can't exclude, since pumping blood also produces heart sounds and jostles the neighbouring organs — while also keeping the causal-role account's goodies. It handles co-option gracefully: on the usual telling, feathers stop having insulation as their function and acquire flight once flight is what maintains them. It gives a newly arisen beneficial variant a function straight away instead of making it wait for a completed sweep. And it explains why a molecular biologist who knows nothing of a lineage's history can still find out what a part is for by knocking it out and watching what happens to fitness over a few hundred generations. It also yields verdicts its rivals don't: exapted traits get *new* functions rather than accumulating obsolete ones, and vestigial traits — the eyes of cave fish, the human appendix, whatever its status turns out to be — have no function at all rather than a function they no longer perform.

Please separate things that have arguably already happened from merely possible or hypothetical ones, and be explicit about which objections a committed maintenance theorist should concede as genuine and which she should dismiss as resting on a misdescription of the case; I'd rather have the strongest version of what my opponents think than a friendly paraphrase. These are the worries I keep circling, and I'd like you to sharpen them, discard the weak ones, and tell me which are actually load-bearing. First, the passenger problem: a part that is now indispensable but became so through something like a neutral ratchet, where a dependency arises first and only later becomes required, so that removing the part is lethal even though the part was never retained *for* anything — MA appears to hand such a part a function for free. Second, relaxed selection: a trait whose maintenance effect lapses because the environment changed, so that on MA the function evaporates, even though we still want to say that the particular organ in front of us can malfunction. Third, spatially varying selection: the same allele maintained in one habitat and neutral or mildly costly in the next valley over, which seems to make the function of a part of an organism depend on where you draw the population boundary. Fourth, a hypothetical empirical finding I've been toying with as a foil: suppose that what in fact maintains most traits is a shifting mosaic of effects, the identity of the payoff changing faster than the trait itself does, so that there is no stable answer to the question of what the trait is maintained for over any interesting window; I can't tell whether that refutes MA, forces it into gerrymandered disjunctive functions, or is something MA should simply welcome. Fifth, the collapse worry: that retained-because-it-produces-E is just E-is-the-contribution-that-currently-pays, so that MA has quietly become the causal-role account it was supposed to beat — the move that would worry me most, since a view that collapses into its rival hasn't been refuted so much as revealed to have never been distinct.
