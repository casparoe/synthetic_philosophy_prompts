I should say at the outset that I have no training in philosophy, economics, or political theory: I left school at eighteen and have been working this out on my own in the evenings, mostly by arguing with myself. Below are the notes for an essay I want to write rather than a draft of the essay itself. I would like your comments on the argument, and I would like them as objections rather than as directions to the literature.

The notes

1. Nearly everything I have read treats openness to immigration as a single setting. I want to insist that it is three settings that can in principle be turned separately: who may enter and take work; what claims a resident has on the tax-and-transfer system; and who has a vote. The much-discussed tension between open borders and a generous welfare state seems to me to be a tension about the second setting only, and much of the confusion in the debate comes from assuming the three move together.

2. Even within the second setting, I think the tension is usually misdescribed. It is presented as a claim about poor people arriving and drawing benefits. I think the real mechanism is adverse selection at the point of entry: people move when the value to them of being inside the pool is at its highest, and the pool cannot price that event. That makes it a problem about the timing of claims relative to contributions, not about the income level of arrivals. If I am right, it has a consequence I have not seen anyone draw: the size of the problem depends on the shape of the benefit system, not on the number of migrants.

3. I also want an argument for limits that is neither fiscal nor cultural, because both of those turn nasty quickly. Call it the monitoring argument. Redistribution seems to be sustained by a widely shared belief that other people are not shirking, and that belief rests on cheap, local, hard-to-fake signals that residents use to judge one another's effort: accent, shared reference points, a common history of dealing with the same institutions. A fast inflow degrades those signals. Note that this does not say newcomers shirk; it says that long-standing residents lose the ability to tell whether anyone is shirking. So the externality is epistemic, and it is a function of the rate of arrival rather than the stock of foreign-born residents. It should bite hardest where entitlements are discretionary and means-tested, such as disability or unemployment support, and least where they are categorical and universal, such as pensions or child benefit. That gives a policy lever that is not a border: a country could buy a good deal of openness by restructuring its benefits toward universal categories.

4. The mechanism in note 2 suggests a remedy that does not exclude the poor, which is where ordinary entry fees or contribution minimums fail. Suppose that on lawful admission the state deposits a lump sum into a personal account in the migrant's name, financed out of general taxation; the account vests over a fixed number of years of residence and tax filing, and the unvested balance reverts to the pool if the person leaves earlier. Arrival then begins a period in which someone accrues a claim rather than one in which they draw on one, the state can price the residual risk by adjusting the size of the stake and the length of the vesting schedule, and nobody is turned away for being poor.

5. The objection that still bothers me: migrants typically arrive after somebody else has paid for their upbringing, so a receiving state imports the productive years and avoids the dependent ones. That is a gain for the receiving state and a loss for the sending one, and I think it is really an objection to recruiting skilled people out of poor countries, not an argument about whether borders should be open.

What I want from you

- Does the decomposition in note 1 survive, or do the three settings come apart under pressure? In particular, is access to the transfer system genuinely separable from entry, given that anyone physically present and working will make some claims on the system whether or not they are formally entitled to?
- Is the monitoring argument in note 3 actually a different argument from the familiar one about diversity lowering support for redistribution, or does it quietly reduce to that claim with an extra step bolted on?
- Does the vesting stake in note 4 solve the selection problem or merely rename it? What is the first thing a hostile economist would say about it, and can it be answered?
- Which of my premises is weakest, and what would a thoughtful defender of the opposite conclusion press on first?
- Finally, where does this argument turn on empirical claims I have simply assumed? Flag them, but do not try to settle them.

Please do not give me citations, bibliographies, or reading lists: I have no library access and I am not submitting this anywhere. I want to know whether the reasoning holds, and where it breaks.
