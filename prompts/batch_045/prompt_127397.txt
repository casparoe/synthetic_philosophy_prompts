For my jurisprudence essay this term I have been trying to pin down what H. L. A. Hart and Lon Fuller were actually disagreeing about in their 1958 Harvard Law Review exchange, and I have reached the point where reading the two papers side by side no longer helps: every time I think I have isolated the crux, the crux turns out to be about something else — sometimes about the definition of law, sometimes about what a judge should do with a valid but wicked statute, sometimes about what fidelity to a legal order even consists in. So I want to stop going in circles and test the two positions against a case: the trial of the major German war criminals before the International Military Tribunal at Nuremberg, which sat from November 1945 to October 1946 under the London Charter, charged twenty-two surviving leaders on three heads (crimes against peace, war crimes, crimes against humanity), convicted most of them with a few acquittals and a range of sentences, and was criticised at the time for criminalising aggression retroactively. Take these as given, and correct me only if one of them is plainly wrong: the victorious powers created the tribunal and its catalogue of offences by agreement in 1945; the defendants' conduct had been, in large part, authorised or required by valid enactments of the German legal order, and the superior-orders plea was handled under the Charter as a matter going to mitigation rather than as a full excuse; and where the tribunal answered the retroactivity charge for the aggression counts, it leaned on pre-war instruments, above all the Kellogg–Briand Pact of 1928 renouncing war as an instrument of national policy — a treaty that, as I understand it, said nothing whatever about individual criminal liability.

What I would like from you is a disciplined two-way analysis: the theory applied to the episode, and the episode turned back on the theory. On the first direction, I want the mapping done per count, because I suspect the two theories do not diverge uniformly across the trial. The war-crimes counts look like the easy case: there was pre-existing conventional and military law, and the interesting question is not whether the conduct was criminal but whether a soldier's duty under his own legal system could ever have shielded him — which is where the Charter's mitigation-only treatment of superior orders sits, and where I want to know whether either theory has anything distinct to say about excuse versus justification, a distinction I do not think either Hart or Fuller thematised. The crimes-against-peace counts look like the hard case, and the one that most closely resembles the grudge-informer situation the two of them actually argued about, since the pre-existing law was at best a treaty renouncing war rather than anything creating criminal liability; here I want to know what each position implies a tribunal should do and, crucially, should say — Hart's candid retroactive punishment against Fuller's claim that a system failing the internal morality of law never generated law in the first place, with Gustav Radbruch's post-war move as a third option in the background. Please also tell me where the theories are silent rather than forcing them to speak: for instance, both seem to be views about what a single legal system owes its subjects, and neither looks designed for a tribunal imposed by occupying powers on the officials of a collapsed regime, so I want to know how much of the episode each can reach at all.

The part I most want your judgement on is my own attempt to dissolve the argument. I have been defending the line that the debate looks conceptual but is really institutional: once you separate the legality question (were the Nazi-era enactments law?) from the adjudication question (what should a court, which must do something, do and say about conduct that was legal when done?), Hart and Fuller converge on the outcome and differ only over which cost is worse to bear — the candour of openly retroactive punishment, or the damage done to the enterprise of law by pretending the old law never bound — so that what looks like a dispute about the concept of law is really an empirical and institutional disagreement about how fragile a legal order is, and about how much expressive weight a court's official justification carries. I want you to test that: is the distinction between the legality and adjudication questions doing real work, or does it just relocate the disagreement somewhere it is harder to see? Does Nuremberg supply any evidence on the empirical question, given that it openly chose one style of justification rather than the other and that a functioning legal order did emerge in West Germany afterwards? And is there an asymmetry I am missing between the informer case and Nuremberg — the informer being a private person who weaponised a legal system, and the Nuremberg defendants being officials who acted inside one, arguably with a kind of fidelity that Fuller's own account should find pathological — such that the two cases pull the theories in opposite directions? I do not need citations, references or reading suggestions; I need the argument, and I especially need you to say plainly where my framing is confused or where I have made the two positions more different, or more alike, than they really are.
