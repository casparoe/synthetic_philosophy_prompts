I teach a graduate seminar on the ontological argument, and our last session was on the modal version. It ended with a student remarking that the whole enterprise is settled in advance by the dictum that existence is not a predicate. I do not think that is wrong exactly, but I want the seminar to be able to say precisely what it is that gets settled.

Lay out, as a menu for discussion, the distinct precise theses that line could be expressing: the grammatical point that existence is expressed by the quantifier rather than by a predicate of individuals; the second-order point that existence is a property of concepts and not of objects; the Kantian-sounding point that to say a thing exists is to posit it rather than to determine it further; the point that existence is not a perfection and so could not be recovered from the concept of a greatest being; and any stronger reading on which no existential conclusion could ever follow a priori from a concept alone. For each, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and say whether it is a claim about language, about concept-formation, or about what there is.

The part I actually care about: which of these readings, if any, engages the argument as it is really stated? The version we discussed turns not on existence being a property of God but on necessary existence being a positive property, where the relevant property is defined via essences rather than as mere existence in every world, and where the argument's machinery is designed to accommodate the platitudes above. Tell me which readings do real work against the positivity premise or the use of essences, which only restate something the modal argument already grants, and which reading the people who repeat the slogan in this context are most likely to be tacitly relying on.
