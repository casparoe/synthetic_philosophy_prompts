I'm teaching the Preface to Nietzsche's Genealogy of Morals (1887) on Thursday and I still feel like I don't have a clean way into the opening section. Here's the passage:

"We are unknown, we knowers, ourselves to ourselves: this has its own good reason. We have never searched for ourselves—how should it then come to pass, that we should ever find ourselves? Rightly has it been said: "Where your treasure is, there will your heart be also." Our treasure is there, where stand the hives of our knowledge. It is to those hives that we are always striving; as born creatures of flight, and as the honey-gatherers of the spirit, we care really in our hearts only for one thing—to bring something "home to the hive!""

Can you help me understand how the hive and honey-gatherer metaphors are supposed to explain why we remain strangers to ourselves? I've been toying with a reading where the point is that the very excellences that make someone a good "knower" of things (single-minded attention to the object, the habit of flying out and bringing stuff back to the store of knowledge) are exactly what prevent self-examination, because the self is not the sort of thing you can gather as honey for a hive. Does that track the text? Also, one of my students always objects that Nietzsche contradicts himself: he calls us "we knowers" in the first sentence and then ends by saying we are not "knowers." I want to tell her that "knower" shifts from "gatherer of knowledge about objects" to "one who possesses self-knowledge," and that the sting is that the first kind of knowing actually causes the failure of the second. But I'm not fully confident, expecially about whether the bit later in the section with the clock striking noon is just an illustration of the same self-alienation or adds a new claim about memory and experience. I don't need any secodnary lit, just a clear unpacking I can bring to class. Thanks!
