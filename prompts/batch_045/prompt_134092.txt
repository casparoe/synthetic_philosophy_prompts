I am a junior and the student member of the four-person committee that runs our state's History Day fair — two history teachers, our librarian, and me — and I have been asked to draft the memo we will vote on at Thursday's meeting. I would rather not hand in something that just applies the word pseudohistory and stops, so I am asking for help with the reasoning as much as the bottom line.

The case: a senior in my school has submitted an individual exhibit titled Mothers of Stone. Her claim is that the recurrence of large stone building traditions in Egypt, Mesoamerica, coastal Peru, and island Southeast Asia is best explained by contact with a single earlier seafaring civilization, and that archaeologists attribute it to independent invention mainly because of textbook inertia, grant incentives, and reluctance to credit outsiders. One panel of her board lays out the conventional dating; she argues that the dating itself presupposes what she is questioning. Her faculty sponsor has signed off, she has four pages of source notes, and nearly all of them are books and websites that already agree with her. She is a friend, and she has told me privately that if we suppress her project it will just confirm her thesis about who gets to decide what counts as knowledge.

Our rules say an entry must present a historical argument supported by evidence and must show how sources were used. They say nothing about whether the argument has to be true, or about what to do when a student's sources are all on one side of the question. Last year we disqualified a project for fabricated quotations, and the wording of that decision is now being cited as precedent for disqualifying anything we believe to be false.

What I need is a memo the committee can act on: a single clear recommendation — accept as submitted, accept on stated conditions with a deadline, move the exhibit out of the judged history category into exhibition-only, or disqualify — followed by a general rule of two or three sentences that we could apply next year to an Atlantis entry, to an ancient-astronauts entry, and also to a project whose conclusion is unorthodox but genuinely argued.

The things I want the memo to work through, because they are what we have actually been arguing about:

1. Whether the fair can judge conclusions at all without turning ourselves into an enforcement arm for current expert consensus. My own view is that we should be judging method, not verdicts, but I cannot say exactly which features of method we are judging.
2. The immunity structure I have noticed in her draft, and I want it described in a way I can defend to her face: she never says what would count as evidence against her; missing evidence gets treated as supporting evidence; she demands far more certainty from a conventional date than from her own; and her view seems to require that a large number of specialists be not merely mistaken but effectively complicit. I would like these turned into a test the committee can actually apply rather than an impression.
3. The precedent that worries me most. Amateurs in the nineteenth century dug up places that most scholars had classified as legend, so any rule keyed to contradicting present consensus would have silenced a real discovery. She will make this argument on Thursday and I do not have a good answer to it.
4. The opposite worry: a fair lends the school its name, judges' scores get read as endorsement, and middle schoolers walk through the exhibit hall. Is saying the point is the process, not the verdict, an answer to that, or a way of avoiding the question?
5. The option everyone has floated and nobody has defended: requiring her to reframe the exhibit as a second-order historical question — why lost-civilization theories have been so attractive, and what work they did for the people who believed them — which her material would mostly fit. Is that a fair condition with a deadline attached, or is it us declining to engage the claim she actually wants to make? If the memo recommends it, I need the exact language of the condition, because she will ask whether we would have rewritten the question of a project we liked.
6. Whether allowing hers commits us, in consistency, to allowing projects whose conclusions we find sympathetic but whose evidence is just as thin, and if not, what the difference is supposed to be.

Please also give me a short paragraph of what the committee should say to her out loud after the vote, and one sentence for next year's handbook. Aim for roughly eight hundred words, written to be read aloud to a room of teachers and a seventeen-year-old: no term of art I would have to look up, or if you need one, explain it in a sentence. I am less interested in a verdict than in a document that treats her position seriously and that we could still stand behind if her exhibit turns out to be right.
