You are a professional moral philosopher with expertise in normative ethics, population ethics, and the ethics of rescue. I am taking an advanced seminar on distributive ethics, and after class my study group got stuck on the question of whether the numbers count in rescue cases—the issue associated with Taurek about saving the one versus the many. To keep things sharp and not depend on exegesis, here is the exact claim I want assessed. In a pure rescue case, you can save either one identifiable stranger or five identifiable strangers; you cannot save both; you have no special obligations to any of them; all six would otherwise die; the lives saved are of equal length and quality; and the only difference is how many people are saved. The claim, call it N, is that the numbers give you no moral requirement to save the five: it is morally permissible to save the one rather than the five, and a fair procedure such as a lottery that gives each of the six an equal chance would also be permissible. Please treat this as the proposition to evaluate rather than as an occasion for exegesis; I do not want a bibliography, citations, or a hedge that the answer depends on your ethical theory as a way of avoiding a number.

My own credence in N is about 0.2, and my friend Priya's is about 0.7, and we both feel we are missing where the real disagreement lives. Please give me your own credence in N, decomposed as a rough probability for each of the following sub-questions, and add any further term you think belongs: first, the probability that the badness of a death, or the moral reasons generated by each person's need, cannot be summed or aggregated across different people to produce a stronger reason to save the many; second, the probability that, even if the numbers do not by themselves require saving the many, it is nevertheless permissible to rescue the one outright rather than being required to use a fair procedure that gives each of the six an equal chance, such as a lottery; third, the probability that the ordinary intuition that one ought to save the greater number in such cases is a reliable guide to moral truth, as opposed to being distorted by factors such as identifiability, scope neglect, or the vividness of an individual face; and fourth, the probability that N remains stable when the numbers are made much larger, such as one versus a million, or when small quality-of-life differences are introduced, so that the view does not collapse into absurdity at the margins. Assign a number and a one-line justification to each term, then show how they combine into your final credence in N, and tell me which single term you are least confident in and what argument or evidence would move it most.

Also, to guard against a purely verbal dispute, briefly note one way in which the numbers not counting could be true as a claim about what one is required to do while still being false as a claim about what one has most reason to do, or vice versa, and tell me whether that distinction changes your number.
