So: here's the package, numbered but in no particular order, nothing is a ranking of how confident I am.

(1) Moderate realism: trained networks really do have states with representational content. Good interpretability work *discovers* content rather than projecting it onto a system that's really just doing something else. The line that features are whatever we find it convenient to name them is false.

(2) Content is fixed by causal-informational role: a state represents P only if it carries information about P *and* the network's own downstream computation consumes it in such a way that the system's success counterfactually depends on it. That second clause is there to exclude read-in cases — where we train a probe that extracts P from the activations but nothing in the net was ever using P.

(3) Method neutrality: representations don't have to be linear directions. The linear representation hypothesis is a contingent, architecture-specific empirical generalisation, not part of what representation is. If a messy nonlinear feature plays the same role, it's as much a representation as a clean direction is.

(4) There is a privileged decomposition: at a given layer, some ways of carving up activation space carve at the joints and others don't, and that's a fact — not a function of what's convenient for us, or of dictionary size, or of the sparsity penalty.

(5) Deflationism about propositional attitudes: there's no fact of the matter about whether the network *believes* that the Eiffel Tower is in Paris. Belief ascription to nets is instrumentally useful and that's the end of it.

(6) Anti-essentialism about vehicles: nothing is essential to any given feature. Change the seed, fine-tune, distill — the same functional role gets implemented by a totally different pattern of activity. Only the roles survive.

(7) Most interpretability papers massively overclaim, because almost nobody checks the read-out / read-in distinction properly.

Two cases from our own toy setup, since I think they're where the pressure shows:

Case A — "shadow features". These are directions that every sparse autoencoder we train, across a range of widths, reliably finds; they're beautifully human-interpretable; if you clamp one hard, to values it never reaches on-distribution, you get coherent, on-topic behavioural changes; and when you ablate it within its natural range, literally nothing happens, and no downstream component's behaviour covaries with it on any input we've tried. My verdict: not a representation — it's a shadow cast by the real computation, a byproduct of compression.

Case B — the "gap-parity" feature. Small transformer, synthetic token task. There's a feature (only findable with a nonlinear probe; there's no single direction corresponding to it) that tracks whether the number of tokens since the last delimiter is even or odd. Perfectly correlated with gap-parity in-distribution, causally necessary on maybe 4% of inputs. Retrain with a different seed and the same role is done by something else entirely. My verdict: in the seed-1 network, that thing is a genuine representation of gap-parity.
