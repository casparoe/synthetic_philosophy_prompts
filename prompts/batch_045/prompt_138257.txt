For a graduate seminar in historiography that I am teaching next term, I need a specification rather than a survey. The topic is the standing of non-human causes — pathogens, climatic shifts, volcanic eruptions — in historical explanation, and the audience I have in mind is working historians trying to explain a particular episode: why a fiscal system collapsed in a given decade, why a colonial expedition failed, why an uprising spread in one province and not its neighbor. So the purpose of the theory is fixed in advance. I want a doctrine good enough to tell a working historian when a non-human factor earns a place in the explanatory narrative as something more than scenery, and to set a ceiling on how much explanatory work that factor may be allowed to do before the account tips into environmental determinism.

Write the desiderata. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark it as either non-negotiable or the preference of a particular camp.

Cover at least the following. First, the cases a theory has to get right: a large tropical eruption whose atmospheric effects produce a year without a summer and widespread harvest failure across Europe and New England; a mosquito- or water-borne disease that decides the fate of a military expedition in a colony; a drought followed by migration and then violence in a region that has weathered comparable droughts without violence; and two formally identical harvest failures, one produced by weather and one by a requisition or tax policy, where the historian's causal idiom plainly shifts. Say what each case is a test of.

Second, the distinctions the theory may not collapse: triggering versus standing condition; constraining versus enabling; being a cause versus being a selector of which social arrangements are stable under shock; explaining versus excusing, including the alibi problem in which an environmental factor is invoked to absolve agents, and the accompanying asymmetry whereby climate and terrain are recruited as excuses while pathogens are recruited as explanations.

Third, adjudicate two candidate requirements I have been toying with, rather than simply listing them. The substitution test: a non-human factor counts as more than background only if swapping in the nearest available alternative state of that factor — no eruption, a milder winter, a pathogen with a different transmission route — would have changed not merely the date or the body count but which options were live for the human agents deliberating at the time. The uptake condition: a factor counts only if it was registered at the time, however dimly, as risk, rumor, portent, or explanation by at least some participants. For each, tell me whether it is a genuine desideratum or a prejudice of one style of history, what it silently rules in and out, and whether it survives the cases above.

Then flag any pair of requirements you believe cannot be jointly satisfied — that is the part I most want — and identify the single requirement that does the most work in excluding the positions people actually hold. Close with a short table, no more than a sentence or two per cell, scoring four broad stances against the list: strict human exceptionalism; geographical or environmental determinism; a flat symmetric ontology in which germs, climate, and treaties are all actors on the same footing; and a middle view on which non-human factors supply constraints and affordances rather than agency.

Constraints: define every technical term you use; rely only on the cases as I have sketched them, with no citations, bibliographies, reading lists, or gestures toward specific studies; and write in American English. Aim for roughly 1,000 to 1,200 words, with the numbered requirements as the bulk of the answer and the table kept brief.
