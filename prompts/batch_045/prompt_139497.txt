I co-teach a graduate seminar on ethics and automated decision systems with a colleague in computer science, and we have hit a wall that I suspect is structural rather than a failure of imagination on anyone's part. For three weeks we have been circling the familiar incompatibility results — the trilemma linking calibration (the requirement that a given risk score mean the same thing whatever group the person who received it belongs to), error-rate balance (equal false positive and false negative rates across protected groups), and predictive parity — and our discussion now has a fixed shape. Someone observes that the three cannot hold together once base rates differ; someone proposes giving one up; someone else points out what that sacrifice costs, either leveling down or the deliberate degradation of predictions for the better-served group; and then we begin again. Nobody has moved anybody, and what strikes me as more diagnostic than the repetition itself is that the philosophers and the computer scientists in the room seem, without quite saying so, to be arguing about different things.

My own hypothesis — and I offer it as a hypothesis — is that the impasse is not at bottom a normative disagreement about which fairness property to sacrifice but a consequence of what I have started calling the conditioning-unit problem. The competing criteria are not defined over the same units at all: calibration is a condition on score-conditional cells, error-rate balance is a condition on cells defined by the actual outcome within a protected group, and the "treat similar cases similarly" style of individual fairness is a condition on pairs of individuals relative to a task-specific similarity metric. If you condition on different partitions of the population, incompatibility is more or less what you should expect, and some of the apparent conflict may be bookkeeping rather than clashing values. Two further hidden questions seem to me to be doing the rest of the work. First, an empirical and conceptual question about the target: in the case we keep returning to — a hospital allocating a fixed number of places in a post-discharge nurse-visitation program using a readmission-risk score — the recorded outcome is not a natural kind but a trace of what the institution previously did, so group differences in base rates are not simply a fixed constraint to be taken as given. Second, a formal question about the framing of the theorems themselves: they are stated for a single, static, deterministic score-thresholding rule applied to a fixed population, whereas our case has a hard capacity constraint, repeats monthly, and is plausibly performative, since who gets visited changes who gets readmitted. Several of my students have concluded from all this that the debate is a pseudo-problem, an artifact of a formalism that does not describe any real deployment. I am not convinced, not least because I cannot tell whether the incompatibility reappears in the dynamic setting in a new form or genuinely dissolves.

So I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict, and with reasons rather than a survey of the criteria. Is this a substantive normative disagreement about which property matters and about whether leveling down is ever acceptable; a verbal or conceptual disagreement in which the sides are conditioning on different partitions and so answering different questions under one name; a disagreement about the proper object of evaluation, the classifier versus the institution that deploys it; or a disguised empirical question about the construction of the outcome variable and the source of base-rate differences? Is my conditioning-unit diagnosis doing the work I think it does, or does the incompatibility survive once one has noticed it? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a theorem extending the impossibility to sequential, capacity-constrained, or performative settings; a distinction between the algorithm and the allocation procedure as objects of assessment; an empirical result about the measured outcome; or a change of question altogether? Please also tell me honestly whether my students' pseudo-problem verdict can survive scrutiny, since I have to tell them something next week.
