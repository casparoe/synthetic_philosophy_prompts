I'm a backend engineer with no formal philosophy background who has somehow ended up on my company's internal language-design guild, and I recently filed a bug report that a friend of mine in a philosophy PhD program tells me is actually a well-known problem in metaethics. Here is the setup. Our policy engine has a small rule language, and I added a statement-level construct for moral judgments: you write something like `wrong!(stealing)` on its own line and the engine records that the policy takes up a negative stance toward stealing. It works perfectly there. It breaks the moment it is embedded — inside a conditional, under a negation, as one arm of a disjunction, inside a question, in the body of a function that never gets called, or in a report of what somebody else thinks. In those positions the construct doesn't perform its job at all, since no stance is recorded, and yet programs that use it there still run, our inference rules still chain through them correctly, and nobody on the guild can say what the nested occurrence is contributing. My friend says this is the shape of an old embarrassment for views that treat moral sentences as expressions of attitude rather than as descriptions that can be true or false: the same sentence has to keep its content when it shows up unasserted inside a larger sentence, and it isn't obvious what an attitude is doing there, or why the larger inference still looks valid. I've picked up the vocabulary — expressivism, the Frege–Geach problem, quasi-realism, the cognitivist alternative — but I can't follow the papers, so I'd like to get at the problem through the only idiom I actually have.

Please write the document I wish I had submitted: a mock RFC / design doc proposing a fix, in the deadpan voice of an internal engineering committee, about 800–1000 words, with the usual sections (Summary; Motivation, framed as a bug report; Guide-level explanation; Reference-level explanation; Known limitations and unresolved questions; Alternatives considered) plus a short appendix of compiler diagnostics — invented error codes and messages such as one that fires on `if wrong!(stealing) { … }` complaining that no stance was performed and no value was produced. Keep it entirely in-universe: no named philosophers, papers, or citations, no winking at the reader, no footnotes explaining the joke. The humor has to come from the engineering being right about the real difficulty, so please get the substance of the problem correct rather than inventing a difficulty: the nested occurrence must retain whatever content the unembedded one had, or else the obvious patterns of inference through those sentences stop looking valid; and the fixes considered should map onto the real moves available — (a) give the construct a real value and make it an ordinary boolean expression, which solves everything at the cost of the original idea; (b) leave the semantics as stance-recording but desugar the embedded occurrences so that they talk about the state of a global commitment or plan store, recovering compositional behavior without admitting that the sentences are ever true or false; (c) accept that there are two distinct modes and stop pretending there is one uniform semantics. For each, the doc should notice what it has actually bought and what it has quietly assumed — in particular, whether the desugaring in (b) is a real semantics for the embedded occurrences or merely a story about what programmers are doing when they write them, and whether the resulting sentences can be treated as capable of being correct or incorrect in the way the ordinary ones seemed not to be. Please also have the doc flag the indirect case (reporting that someone else holds the moral view, where the writer plainly isn't taking up the stance) and a brief note under future work that the same failure shows up for other unasserted-looking expressions such as epistemic modals and taste predicates.

Two constraints on how it should land, and one thing I'd like appended. The guild is half people who have never thought about any of this and half people who will read it aloud to my philosopher friend, so it should be funny and self-contained for the first group while being fair enough that a metaethicist doesn't wince — exaggerate a real feature of the expressivist position, don't invent one, and make the straight-faced proposal follow the target's own logic to a place the reader recoils from rather than announcing that it is doing so. Then, separate from the RFC, add an afterword of 150–200 words in your own voice: where the compiler analogy takes liberties with the philosophical problem, which formulation of the problem the piece leans on, what a defender of expressivism would say the doc misrepresents, and — since I want something like a falsifier — the one consideration or counterexample that would make you change the framing or abandon the analogy altogether.
