I teach a 300-level seminar on rational choice — 16 students, one 75-minute session. By this point they can compute expected utilities and can read a simple causal graph (arrows, common causes, screening off). For the session I'm planning, they will already have read the classic Newcomb setup and the standard arguments for taking one box and for taking two. So I can't rely on naivety or surprise: they know the case, they know both answers, and half of them will have a position before they walk in. What I want instead is for them to run into the disagreement again in a form they have never seen, with real (small) stakes, a real predictor, and a second case that catches them out.

Design two games for that session, run back to back, with identical numbers and different causal stories, plus a short third vote.

Game 1 should be a genuine Newcomb setup staged with an actual predictor rather than a stipulated one. Suggested magnitudes, which you may adjust: an opaque box containing either 100 points or nothing, a transparent box always containing 1 point, points convertible into something trivial (a homework pass, a small prize). The prediction is made and sealed before the choice is offered. The predictor must be mundane — no near-perfect demon — and its accuracy must be honest and disclosed to the students in advance (say four times out of five), with the class able to inspect the mechanism afterwards. I'd like the prediction to be generated in the room from something the students do in the first five minutes without knowing why — a short risk-preference or "would you rather" questionnaire — so that the correlation is real rather than asserted, and so that anyone who has read the case and wants to fool the predictor has to do it blind. Give me the exact procedure, how the prediction is sealed and verified, and the text of a handout I could photocopy.

Game 2 must be presented to them as a separate problem, not as a variant of the first, and its relation to the first must not be announced. Same two conditional probabilities (0.85 / 0.15), same magnitudes (a state worth 100 points, an act worth 1 point), same "your act does not cause the state" structure — but now the state is a medical marker fixed by a common cause rather than a prediction of your choice: taking the pleasant option is strongly correlated with having the marker, and the marker is expensive. Make it real in the room as well, so that the correlation is something they can check rather than something I assert, and make sure the choice is private and the state is revealed only afterwards. The point is that the two games should be the same probability space and the same payoff matrix, and their verdicts should come apart.

Then a third private vote, before any discussion: in each game, would you pay some number of points, before the prediction is made or the marker is settled, for an intervention that would reliably make you the kind of person who takes the evidentially-favored option?

The debrief should map moments in the room onto parts of the dispute rather than explain the dispute from the front. I want it to make these visible: that both games license the same sentence — "the state is already fixed, so take the extra point" — and that following it costs roughly 69 points in one game and gains 1 point in the other; that nothing in the probabilities or the payoffs can account for the difference, so the difference must be located in causal structure; that what distinguishes the games is not "common cause versus no common cause" but whether the state is sensitive to the decision procedure you are running, as opposed to merely tracking a distal cause that also shapes what you want; and that a student who announces they will flip a coin has made the causal argument sound and simultaneously destroyed most of the expected value, which is a result worth showing rather than telling. Note explicitly where a student's allegiance should waver and what it costs them to stay consistent in either direction.

Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each: students naming decision theories within the first ten minutes and derailing the exercise; students who try to game the predictor; a student who refuses to play because the predictor or the setup feels illegitimate; a split vote that calcifies into two camps talking past each other; and the fact that an 85% predictor is not the near-perfect predictor of the case they read, which quietly changes the shape of the argument. I want private ballots recorded before any discussion, and I want to know what I should say to a class that demands to be told the correct answer.

Be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce. My sense is that it can produce choices but not a verdict, and that the metaphysics of "what the predictor is sensitive to," the idealization to a near-perfect predictor, and the fact that one-shot play with 16 students cannot show anyone the long-run difference between strategies all have to be supplied by me in words. Say which of these are unrecoverable and how little I can get away with saying.

Please lay the answer out as a facilitator's packet: the two rules sheets in photocopiable form, the payoff tables, a minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes, the ballots, the debrief with the mapping, the failure modes, and the limitations note. Assume no web access and give me no citations, bibliography, or reading list — I have the reading; I need the session. Please write in American English.
