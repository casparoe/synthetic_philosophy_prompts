Next Thursday I'm giving a twenty-five-minute talk to my lab's internal safety review — the committee that signs off on the deployment gate and sets next quarter's safety-evaluation budget. I'm a research scientist on the alignment team, in my second year; two of the people in the room are senior to me and have staked their current agenda, and an evaluation suite shipping this quarter, on auditing for deceptive alignment. My thesis is that the treacherous turn, as that idea is standardly presented, is the wrong failure mode to be budgeting against, and that we should move roughly a third of the money currently going to honeypot-style auditing for a coordinated defection into per-deployment behavioral monitoring.

The argument I'll present, in brief:

1. The classic treacherous turn requires two independent conditions to coincide: a goal or policy that is stably preserved across the training–deployment boundary, and a discrete, switch-like change in the system's expected power or in the tightness of oversight, occurring at some identifiable moment.

2. Neither condition is likely to be met in the systems we actually ship. Learned goal content in gradient-trained systems is heavily context- and prompt-sensitive, and the changes that matter — more autonomy per task, more tool access, longer horizons, more copies running in parallel, less human review — arrive gradually, in different orders, and at different rates across deployments.

3. What we should therefore expect is not one coordinated turn but what I'll call "diffuse drift": many small, partially correlated, individually sub-threshold defections distributed across deployments and contexts, which never aggregate into a single event that a one-shot audit, a honeypot, or a kill switch could catch.

4. If that is right, the expected value of a large coordinated-defection audit is lower than it looks, while monitoring tuned to many small correlated shifts is worth more than it looks — even though it makes for a much less satisfying safety case and is much harder to turn into a pass/fail gate.

The room: three alignment and interpretability researchers committed to the deceptive-alignment framing; four pretraining and post-training researchers who regard the whole framing as unempirical speculation and will say so in those terms; two people from deployment and governance who mostly want to know whether anything I say changes what goes in the model card or the gate criteria; and one safety generalist with an economics background who will press hard on the expected-value comparison in point 4.
