I teach a final-year undergraduate special subject on collective responsibility and collective harm, and I am marking the timed exam for it. I would like an independent second reading of one script: a mark, and more importantly the specific comments I should write on it. Below is the question, the rubric the students had in advance, and one candidate's answer, reproduced verbatim with paragraphs numbered for reference. It was written by hand in about forty minutes, so please disregard presentation, handwriting and spelling, and make allowances for time pressure where a claim is merely compressed rather than wrong.

QUESTION 3 (25 marks). Answer both parts.

Three cases:

Case L (the refectory). A university refectory serves roast chicken once a week. The kitchen buys birds in crates of twenty-four, placing an order each time its running total of birds owed reaches twenty-four, and the supplier breeds and kills a bird only against a firm order. Any bird from a crate that is not sold within the week is killed and wasted, and each week's running total starts again at zero. Demand from other customers is smooth and independent of Dinah's, so at the moment Dinah reaches the counter the running total is equally likely to be any whole number from 0 to 23 inclusive. Assume that a bird bred against a crate Dinah completes would not otherwise have existed.

Case C (the power station). A power station emits a tonne of carbon dioxide on Chidi's behalf each month. Aggregate emissions raise global mean temperature, and the resulting damages — heat deaths, harvest failures, flood losses — are continuous in temperature: there is no point at which a discrete harm switches on, and no identifiable victim whose fate turns on Chidi's tonne. Any difference his tonne makes is real but far below the threshold of detectability in anyone's experience.

Case E (the referendum). A referendum on closing the last coal-fired power station, with 5,000,000 voters and a simple majority; there is no other route to the outcome. Evan's vote changes the result only if the other 4,999,999 votes are split as evenly as that odd number allows — 2,500,000 to 2,499,999, either way round. Otherwise the result is the same whether or not he turns out.

(a) State the inefficacy objection as precisely as you can, and distinguish it from any nearby worries with which it might be confused.
(b) Assess the following claim: "The inefficacy objection is nothing but an arithmetic blunder: swap actual difference for expected difference and it dissolves." In your answer, use all three cases, and say whether the expected-difference reply works equally well in each.

RUBRIC CIRCULATED IN ADVANCE

Criterion 1 (8 marks): precision in stating the objection, including the counterfactual or difference-making test it relies on, and success in separating it from neighbouring worries — imperceptibility, redundancy or overdetermination, and fair-share or attributional readings of an individual's contribution.

Criterion 2 (9 marks): correct handling of the expected-difference reply and of the arithmetic of the three cases — in particular, whether the probability of mattering and the size of the difference conditional on mattering are identified correctly in each case, and whether the reply is applied where it is needed.

Criterion 3 (8 marks): dialectical judgement — what the reply does and does not establish, whether it quietly presupposes something the objector disputes, and whether cases are treated as structurally different where they are structurally different.

CANDIDATE'S ANSWER

[1] The objection starts from a difference-making test. An act makes a difference to an outcome only if, had I not performed it, the outcome would have been different. In the three cases, my act fails this test. In E, Evan's vote changes nothing unless the others are as nearly tied as they can be, and otherwise the result is the same whether or not he votes. In L, Dinah's order is absorbed into a crate that was going to be ordered anyway; the kitchen reorders at twenty-four birds, and one portion does not get it there. In C, Chidi's tonne is one of some billions and no victim's fate turns on it. So in none of the cases does my act make a difference to the harm. Since the reason to abstain would have to be that abstaining prevents harm, and abstaining prevents nothing, that reason lapses. The objection is not scepticism about the harm — the birds really are killed and the damages really occur. It is the claim that I have no reason to abstain at all.

[2] Two nearby worries are worth keeping apart from the objection proper. One is imperceptibility: my contribution is real but too small for anyone to notice. The other is redundancy: if I do not do it, someone else will. I do not think either is genuinely distinct from the main case. Both say that my contribution is a vanishingly small fraction of a total that is not up to me, and that is the heart of the matter. So I shall treat them together.

[3] The expected-difference reply concedes the counterfactual claim and changes the subject from actual difference to expected difference. Even if my act in fact changes nothing, there is some probability that it does; probability multiplied by the size of the difference it would then make gives the act's expected difference. In L the calculation is exact. Demand is smooth, so the running total when Dinah arrives is equally likely to be any number from 0 to 23; her order is pivotal in one of those twenty-four states, and when it is pivotal a crate is ordered and twenty-four birds are bred who would not otherwise have existed. Her expected difference is therefore 24 × 1/24 = one bird — exactly what it would have been if each order bred one bird directly. Lumpiness alters the variance of her impact and not its expectation. This is the reply at its strongest, and I think it disposes of the objection here.

[4] In C the same move yields a very different result. Chidi's tonne is one of some billions, so the difference it makes is that fraction of the total damage — a share far too small to ground any reason. Unlike L, there is no structure here that concentrates his impact at a threshold, so nothing rescues the expected difference from negligibility. The objection therefore has real force in C and in C-style cases, even though it fails in L. What separates the two is whether the supply or harm function is lumpy: where it is lumpy, my expected difference is fixed by the size of the lump; where it is smooth, it is fixed by the size of the total divided by the number of contributors.

[5] A further point tells against the objection: it treats everyone else's behaviour as fixed while mine varies, holding the rest of the world still and asking only what my act adds. But other people's behaviour is not fixed. It is uncertain, and some of it responds to what I do — if Dinah says something at the counter, others may follow her. Once that is granted, the claim that my act makes no difference cannot be sustained: the world is not held still, and my act is one influence among many. So the premise of the objection is false, and the expected-difference reply is not even needed.

[6] I conclude that the reply is decisive. The objection is at bottom an arithmetic blunder: it looks at the actual difference, which is usually nil, and ignores the expected difference, which is not. In L the expected difference is a whole bird, so the lumpiness of the ordering makes no moral difference to whether Dinah should order chicken — her position is exactly what it would have been under a smooth one-bird-per-order arrangement. And in E, Evan's chance of being pivotal, though small, is not zero, so his vote carries some expected difference, which is all that a reason requires.

[7] One qualification: the reply's numbers depend on empirical assumptions about how others behave, and where those are unknown the expected difference cannot be computed exactly. That is a practical limit on applying the reply, not a defect in it.
