I lead a small team that maintains a static-analysis pipeline. For every warning the tool emits — "this array index is always in bounds", "this mutex is released on every path", "these two pointers never alias" — we store a number between 0 and 1: our confidence that the claim is true. Nothing is proved at that stage. A claim gets settled only when somebody runs the full prover, which is slow and expensive, so the numbers exist mainly to decide what to spend prover time on first.

Last quarter we added an internal bounty program to keep the numbers honest. Anyone can buy a ticket on any unsettled claim at the currently posted number. Compound tickets are allowed too: "A and B" is priced at the product of the two numbers, "not A" at one minus the number, and so on. When a claim is finally resolved, tickets on it settle at 1 or 0. Numbers are revised by a batch job whenever new proof results land, and tickets can be bought and sold as they move.

A colleague on another team wrote a script that searches for bundles of tickets that cannot all pay off — a claim together with its negation, or a combination that a quick SAT pass over the relevant fragment shows to be unsatisfiable — and buys them whenever the posted numbers guarantee a profit no matter how the underlying claims are resolved. It has been consistently profitable.

He says this proves something general. Either our numbers satisfy the rules of the probability calculus, in which case they must already assign 1 to every claim that is in fact true and 0 to every claim that is in fact false, which is absurd, since computing those numbers is exactly the thing our pipeline cannot do; or they do not satisfy those rules, in which case they are incoherent and we should stop pretending the numbers mean anything. Either way, he says, a number that is not a probability is just a vibe dressed up as a number, and we should either prove everything or drop the numbers.

I do not want to drop them. They are genuinely useful for triage, and so far every bundle his script has found sits inside a fragment we could have checked cheaply ourselves. But I cannot tell whether his dichotomy is real, and if it is not, I do not know what standard I should be holding these numbers to instead. My questions:

1. Is the dilemma sound? If not, identify the assumption doing the work and say plainly what it costs me to give it up.
2. What should I require of the numbers instead? I want something I can write down as an engineering requirement and test automatically, not a slogan. Note that his script is a real program with a real compute budget and a deadline; it does not have unlimited time, and neither do I.
3. Should the number used for triage be the same number used to price the bounty tickets? We currently assume yes.
4. Concretely: do I pay the script out, ban it, or move its author onto the scoring team?

For context: every claim is decidable in principle, our prover is complete for the fragments it covers but slow, and I have no philosophy background, so please spell things out rather than naming positions.
