I sit on a small committee at work that decides which screening tests and preventive treatments our company health plan will pay for, and I also help moderate a long-running online support community for people with long-term conditions. Between those two things I keep running into the same annoyance: nobody can tell me what makes something a *disease* rather than just an unusual way to be, and the answer seems to shift depending on who is asking. From reading around on my own (I have no academic background, so please don't assume I know the technical literature) I've come away with three rough camps. One says it is purely a matter of biological fact: a condition is a disease when some part of the organism fails to do what that kind of part is supposed to do, at a level below what is statistically typical for the relevant reference class — which immediately raises the question of how you pick the reference class, since age, sex and population all change the answer, and how far down the distribution you have to be before it counts. A second camp says the biological facts never settle it and that the judgement is always, in the end, a value judgement about whether we disapprove of the condition. A third, hybrid camp says you need both: a genuine dysfunction *and* a harm. While arguing with myself about this I've invented two distinctions that I find helpful and would like you to use. The first is between what I call a *mechanical failure* (a part is broken and does not do what it is for) and a *fit mismatch* (the part works exactly as it should, but fits badly with the environment, diet or social arrangements the person actually lives in). The second is between *one-way* conditions, which would be a disadvantage under any environment you could plausibly imagine, and *two-way* conditions, which would be neutral or even advantageous in some environments and costly in others. I'd also like you to consider a third wrinkle I can't get past: some conditions look helpful over a short stretch of life and harmful over a whole lifetime, or the reverse, so I'm unsure whether "is it a disease?" even has a single answer independent of timescale.

Here is the list I want you to rate. For each item, give me a number between 0.0 and 1.0 for how much of the disagreement about whether it is a disease is, in your view, a normative or value disagreement rather than a factual one about biology; assign it to a tier (A = mostly factual, B = genuinely mixed, C = mostly value-driven); say which of the three camps above yields the cleanest verdict for that particular item; and tell me whether my mechanical-failure/fit-mismatch distinction and my one-way/two-way distinction actually do any work there or just collapse. The items are: (1) mild high blood pressure in someone who feels entirely well; (2) the hearing loss that is statistically normal for a healthy seventy-five-year-old; (3) being born unable to feel hunger, so that one must eat by the clock although every other function is normal; (4) an inherited near-absence of pain sensation with no other deficit, which leads to repeated unnoticed injuries; (5) a mildly raised blood marker picked up only by a new, very sensitive test, found in a fifth of apparently healthy people and shown by twenty years of follow-up to make no difference to any outcome; (6) infertility; (7) the restless, distractible style of attention and activity that gets labelled a disorder in children, in an adult whose job happens to suit that style very well; (8) obesity in a setting where cheap calorie-dense food is everywhere; (9) unusually fast wound healing that leaves stiff scar tissue and slightly reduced flexibility in a joint; and (10) being very short with no identifiable hormonal or genetic cause.

Please also give me one overall number for the whole set: across borderline cases like these, what fraction of the "is it really a disease?" dispute is a values dispute rather than a factual one? I'd like you to sort the ten into tiers as well, so I can see which cluster together and why, and to say briefly where my two home-made distinctions buy real clarity and where they fall apart. Please lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence — the overall fraction plus, in a line, which items come out most value-laden and which most factual — then give the table of ten rows, then whatever notes and caveats you think matter, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
