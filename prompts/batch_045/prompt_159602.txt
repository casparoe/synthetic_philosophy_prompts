ok so my study group is split on the centipede game and I think Ive basically cracked it but nobody can tell me whats wrong with my argument.

Setup as I understand it: two players alternate, at each node the player whose turn it is can either grab now (taker gets the bigger share, other gets less) or pass, which grows the total pile and hands the turn over; if the two of you somehow pass all the way to the last node you both get way more than either of you would have got by grabbing early.

My reading: backward induction is just iterated elimination of dominated strategies, so "take at move one" isnt a psychological prediction, its logic — as certain as modus ponens. The famous paradox is then really just the unhappy fact that rationality pays worse than irrationality: two people hardwired to always pass both end up richer than two rational people do. So the upshot is that rationality is self-defeating in some games, and the fix is simply to stop assuming common knowledge of rationality — put in a trembling hand (say, a 2% chance that any given player fat-fingers "pass" when they meant "take") and now its rational to keep going for a while instead of grabbing on move one, which is exactly what people do in the lab. Paradox dissolved: it was a artifact of an idealization, not a problem with backward induction. One guy in the group muttered that trembles are about execution mistakes rather than about what players want, but that sounded like nitpicking to me.

The bit I actually want checked is a variant I invented to prove the point. Same game, except that before move one each player may privately press a button, for free, that permanently locks them into "always pass"; nobody learns who pressed until the game is over. Obviously both players press, because being locked is precisely the irrationality you wished you had, and then both of you walk away with the fat mutual-pass payoff that rationality couldnt reach. So the original paradox is just a missing move in the game tree rather than anything deep about backward induction — right?
