hey — I'm the person who ends up writing the "how we do things here" docs at a ~60-person engineering org, three time zones, mostly async. I get two or three requests a week along the lines of "we should have a norm about X" or "can you write something that fixes the culture around Y", and I have basically no idea when putting something in the handbook does anything at all. I also can't enforce anything myself — if a rule needs teeth, a director or the team leads have to sign off, and they won't sign off on something they think is invented busywork. So quick, cheap and honest beats thorough every time.

I read a paraphrase of that Lewis account of conventions — roughly: a convention is a regularity in how people behave in a recurring situation where what mostly matters is that we match, where there's more than one way to match, and where everyone keeps doing it partly because everyone expects everyone else to keep doing it, and everyone knows that everyone knows. That clicked hard, because I think half of what I'm asked to "set" isn't that kind of thing at all. Can you turn it into something I can actually run?

What I need:

- a decision procedure I can work through in about fifteen minutes on a request: the questions in order, and what counts as a good enough answer to each one. Include an explicit place where it stops with a "no, there's nothing to set here", and a place where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts.
- a way to tell an already-live convention that just happens to be unwritten (where maybe the only right move is to write it down so the knowledge stops being private) from a genuine vacancy where nobody knows what to do.
- what to actually do when there are several equally fine options. I get the bit where the content is arbitrary and what matters is which one stands out — give me the concrete levers: precedent, analogy to something people already know, a flag day, getting a visible group to go first, whatever.
- a section on change, including conventions that have outlived the problem they solved: what it really takes to move one, roughly how long, how often it fails, and what a writen rule nobody follows does to the credibility of the next one.
- clearly flagged: the steps where judgment can't be removed and a human has to decide.

Then run it on three things actually on my desk:

1. "set a norm" that people reply in Slack within four waking hours.
2. a written rule everyone ignores: every PR needs a second reviewer, but in practice whoever's online rubber-stamps it or the author self-merges after a day.
3. the one I expect to hate: we're moving to trunk-based development and everyone complains about long-lived branches, so I want to announce that branches live at most a day and have it stick. I would definately prefer this to work by memo, and I suspect you're going to tell me it won't.

Be plain about which parts are the actual theory and which parts are you making up from how orgs really work — I'd rather see where the scaffolding ends than get a smooth checklist that isn't holding anything up. No citations or reading lists, I'm never going to read them. And if my framing is off — if some of this stuff isn't a convention in that sense at all, or the idea just doesn't stretch to cover handbooks, announcements and written rules — say so straight out instead of politely going along with it. I'd much rather be told the frame doesn't fit.
