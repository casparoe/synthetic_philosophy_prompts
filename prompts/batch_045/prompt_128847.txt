Got a job talk in three weeks where I'm defending a contractualist treatment of aggregation. The thesis: you may add peoples complaints together only when the burdens are jointly unavoidable — there was no way to spare any one member of the group without also sparing every other member — so each of them can be given a reason she couldn't reasonably reject, roughly "we couldn't have spared you without sparing everyone, and sparing everyone wasn't on the cards." I call it the linkage condition. I use it to make numbers count in the case the whole paper turns on (you can either keep one identifiable person from becoming paraplegic or spare a few hundred unidentified people from permanent but mild hearing damage), while still refusing to aggregate in plain headcount cases and without quietly becoming a utilitarian.

The department is four consequentialists who are somewhat EA-adjacent and think in QALYs and cost-effectiveness, one Kantian who thinks complaints are the wrong currency to begin with, one Scanlonian who's on my side but thinks any permission to aggregate is unstable and will collapse as soon as you press it, a decision theorist who'll want to know whether my condition is ex ante or ex post, and two philosophy-of-law people who mostly care whether any of this maps onto how standing actually works in tort.

Write me a Q&A prep sheet: eight or so objections in the order they'll really arrive, phrased the way each of these people would phrase it (the EA version of "why not just aggregate" is not the Kantian's), with a two-minute answer for each, the things I should just concede in the talk itself, the single reply that has to land or the paper dies, and the question I'm hoping nobody asks. My guess is the nastiest one is menu-dependence — that on my view whether aggregation is permitted depends on which alternatives happen to be on the table — but I want your read on where I'm actually weakest, including if it's somewhere I haven't thought of.
