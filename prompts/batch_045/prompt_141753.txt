We've got a reading group on causation this term — we did the Lewis-style counterfactual stuff first and we're about to start on the interventionist side of things — and I've been asked to write up a one-page statement of my own position so everyone else can have a go at it. I'd rather find out now whether it holds together than in front of nine people, so: audit, please.

Roughly where I am: (1) The basic causal facts are singular — about this event causing that one — not about event-types or about generalities. When I say the short circuit caused the fire, that's a claim about that fire on that night. (2) The closest-possible-world machinery in the Lewis-style account doesn't work for me; I don't know what makes one world closer than another and the miracle-versus-divergence business feels like a hack. What I like about the interventionist picture is that the other-things-equal condition gets pinned down by an actual manipulation instead of by a similarity ordering, and the preemption cases come out right. (3) But I'm a realist about all this: whether the short circuit caused the fire does not depend on what we care about, or on which variables we happen to write down. In my other life I fit models with DAGs, and I have this experience over and over where I add a variable to the diagram and an arrow stops counting as a cause — I'm convinced that's me learning something, not the causal fact changing under me. (4) Omissions and double preventions are real causation to me, not just a façon de parler: the nurse's not giving the antibiotic caused the patient's death, the guard sleeping through his shift caused the escape. (5) I like accounts that actually analyse their target rather than quietly using the target in the explaining of it. (6) Two shooters, simultaneous shots, either one on its own would have killed him, he dies: my verdict is that both shootings caused the death. Yet when I try to get that out of the intervention test I seem to get the opposite — hold the other shooter fixed at actually-fired, remove this one, he still dies, so no cause — and there's this no-difference-made-no-causation thought behind that test that I keep coming back to. (7) And I think it's fine that in one conversation we say the match started the fire and in another we say the presence of oxygen did; my friend says that's already me giving up on (3), and I think it's just about which true thing we bother saying.

So: which of these actually pull against each other, which only look like they do, and what's the cheapest repair in each case? Please rank them by severity rather than flagging all seven as equally bad, be specific about which numbered item would have to give and what that would cost me, and then say which single revision would clean up the most. No need for citations or a reading list — I just want to know whether I'm definately getting torn apart on Thursday.
