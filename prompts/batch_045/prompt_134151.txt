I'm putting together a short piece for a philosophy-and-film reading group on Villeneuve's Arrival, and I want to read it against Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility. The bit I keep circling: Louise's big choice — to have Hannah knowing how it ends — looks like the strongest possible case for responsibility (full information, no coercion, done for love), but on Strawson's picture it's also a perfect illustration of the regress: she acts as she does because she's become the kind of person who experiences time all at once, and that nature was given to her by the heptapods rather than chosen or self-made. So is the film secretly a pessimist tract, or does the fact that she can look back on the whole thing and still want it give you something Strawson's argument doesn't obviously allow — call it authorship by retroactive endorsement? Please press hardest on the question I can't settle myself: whether a nature you acquire (from an alien language, say) is any less of a problem for the regress than an innate one, given that in both cases you had no say in becoming that way. And tell me where the film resists or cheats the reading — the ending clearly wants her choice to count as hers, and I don't want to flatten the movie into a handy illustration. What, if anything, does Arrival add to this debate that the argument on its own doesn't give you?
