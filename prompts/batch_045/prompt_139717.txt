In my uni's effective altruism group chat people are always saying things along the lines of "suffering is the only thing that really matters" or "no amount of happiness makes up for one genuinely terrible hour of pain", and they use it to argue that our giving should go to things like severe pain relief or malaria nets rather than to anything that just makes already-comfy people a bit happier (I think this is what they mean by calling themselves negative utilitarians).

What I can't work out is what they could actually mean. My flatmate says it's just the boring point that a pound spent on pain relief does more good at the margin than a pound spent making someone who's already fine a bit happier, but that seems to be a claim about where money goes furthest, not a claim about what's good. When I said that in the chat, someone replied that no, they really do think a world with no suffering and no happiness is better than a world where everyone is mildly content — which looks to me like it pushes towards thinking we shouldn't have kids, or even that it would be fine to painlessly switch everyone off. Then everyone said that's just the "suffering-focused ethics is extinctionist" strawman again and got annoyed with me.

Can you lay out the different precise theses that slogan could be standing for — suffering counts more than happiness, suffering counts infinitely more, happiness counts for nothing at all, or something about whose experiences count in the first place — and say which are near platitudes, which are substantive but defensible, and which would actually land you with the conclusions my group chat is embarrassed by? And which reading do they need if they want the malaria-nets-over-happiness conclusion without the turning-everyone-off bit?
