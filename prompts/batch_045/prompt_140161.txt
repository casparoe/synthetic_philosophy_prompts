Here's my situation. I'm on the programming committee of the small university art gallery here, and there's a decision that's been chewing at me for weeks. The case first — I've made it up, deliberately unlike any real one I know, but it's the shape I need.

About twenty-five years ago a young artist, then completely unknown, struck up an acquaintance with a woman he met by chance on a train, and over the next eighteen months ran an entirely fabricated relationship with her under a false name: invented job, invented dying brother, letters from the "brother" that he wrote himself, a couple of staged meetings in borrowed flats. He kept everything — her letters back to him, the presents she gave him, her answering-machine messages, his own notebooks about what he was doing and why. Then he vanished. Two years later he exhibited the lot as an installation: her letters pinned in rows, her voice on a loop in a small dark room, the presents in vitrines, the notebooks mounted on a lectern where he is completely open about having done it to find out what it would feel like and to have something to show. It's a genuinely great piece of work by every standard we've got. People weep in it. It changed how a whole generation of artists thought about using documentary material. He's dead now, and the estate has offered us a version of it with a substantial grant attached, on two conditions: the whole thing goes up, notebooks included, or the money goes back and the show doesn't happen.

The woman found out when a friend went to the exhibition and recognised her handwriting. It wrecked several years of her life. She sued and lost — apparently nothing he did was actually illegal. She's in her sixties now and has become something like the work's advocate: interviews, a memoir, saying that the piece tells the truth about trust, that she's made peace with it, that it belongs to her as much as to him, that she'd be disappointed if galleries stopped showing it. Nobody appears to be paying her for any of this. Her children, who were teenagers at the time, want it gone and won't discuss it publicly.

One of the other committee members — a sharp person, not a philistine — argues for going ahead like this. Whatever was done to her was done a quarter of a century ago by a man who is dead. Putting it on the wall harms nobody who isn't already harmed, punishes nobody, deters nobody, and the one person with an actual grievance has thought about it far harder and far longer than we have and is asking us to do it. Meanwhile it's a masterpiece, and refusing to show a work because of how it came into the world is exactly the philistine reflex a university is supposed to be immune to. Turning it down doesn't protect her; it protects us from feeling bad. And, he added, you can't even say the badness is in the work: the badness is in the biography, the value is in the object in front of you, and keeping those two apart is the whole point of the distinction.

Something in this feels wrong and I can't locate it. Two things keep coming back. First, the piece works because a real person really trusted him, and there is no other way to get that effect — you couldn't stage it, you couldn't hire someone, you couldn't do it with her knowing, because the moment it's consented to there's nothing left to look at. So the thing we'd be celebrating isn't merely caused by what he did; it's made out of it, and it would be a worse work of art if he had been a decent person. Second, when I stood in that dark room with her voice on the loop, I wasn't only moved. I felt like a receiver of stolen goods — like I was being asked to take up a position towards her that I had no right to take up. My colleague can absorb both of those: he says that's just the biography bleeding through, and that I should get over it.

So: is he right? And more usefully, where exactly does the argument go wrong, if it does? I'd be grateful if you'd separate the question of whether it is a good work of art from the question of whether we should show it — do those really come apart here, and if so is the first question even the important one? I'd also like your view on what my discomfort in the dark room is evidence of: squeamishness I should grow out of, or something a person of decent character should listen to? Does her saying yes settle it, and does it change anything that her children say no? And then generalise a bit: what is it about this case that makes the detachment move fail, if it does — what feature is doing the work, and what would have to be different for his argument to be perfectly fine? I take it that a masterpiece whose maker was a cruel person but whose cruelty has nothing to do with what makes it good is a different matter, and I want to know exactly why. One last thing: tell me what we would actually be doing by showing it. I suspect there's a difference between not destroying something and actively staging and funding it, but I can't get the difference to do any work.
