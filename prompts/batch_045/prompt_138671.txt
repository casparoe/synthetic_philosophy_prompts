I'm in the final year of my philosophy degree, taking a seminar on the ethics of communication, and for the term paper I'd rather build something than pick a side again. The question I keep circling is whether lying — asserting what you yourself take to be false — is morally worse than merely misleading someone: implicating, insinuating, or letting a false belief stand — in cases where the intended deception and the resulting false belief are held fixed. Please act as a professional philosopher who works on the norms of assertion and on pragmatics, and draft the joint statement that two people who have argued about this for years could both sign: one holding what I'll call the asymmetry view (there is a genuine pro tanto difference between the two, not reducible to expected consequences, to the odds of being found out, or to how much work the hearer must do to recover the truth), the other holding the parity view (no stable moral difference survives once intentions and effects are matched; if anything the misleading is worse, because it is deniable, it recruits the hearer's own reasoning against them, and it leaves nothing quotable that the hearer can later hold the speaker to). Before drafting, brainstorm briefly — a dozen or so candidate sub-questions, in rough notes — and then keep only the four or five with real bite.

I want three cases of my own to do real work in the document, and I'd like you to tell me if any of them is rigged in favour of one camp and how to repair it. First: a university department announces in writing to all its graduate students that administrative staff answer only the question literally asked and never volunteer related information; the policy is genuinely common knowledge, everyone knows to read the answers suspiciously, and it is in fact followed. A student asks whether the thesis committee has approved the proposal; the administrator, who knows the proposal was rejected outright, replies that the paperwork reached the dean's office on 3 March. My question is whether the policy has abolished the misleading altogether, leaving only non-disclosure, or merely relocated it — and whether the asymmetry view can survive if it has. Second: five colleagues each contribute one literally true sentence to a hiring discussion, no individual asserts anything false, each knows the joint effect, and the committee is led to a false picture; is there something in the neighbourhood of a lie here that belongs to no one, and does the parity view need a story about collective speakers? Third: a speaker knows the hearer will draw the false conclusion only years later, after the speaker has left and correction has become impossible. I've also been toying with a shared measuring rod, which I'm calling the Audit Test: suppose the entire exchange is recorded and later played back to the deceived party, who is then told everything the speaker knew; would they have a complaint specifically about what was said, or only about what was left unsaid? Say whether each camp should accept that test as diagnostic of anything, and if not, what they'd put in its place.

Format the result as a numbered section of agreed claims — things both camps can sign, including about the pragmatics: that there is a workable distinction between what is said and what is implicated, that intentions and not just outcomes matter, that some misleading is worse than some lying — followed by a table of residual disagreements, with a column for the crux, a column giving the asymmetry camp's own preferred formulation, one giving the parity camp's, one for the case or argument both would accept as bearing on that crux, and one each for what each camp predicts that case will show. Where you think part of the dispute is merely verbal, say which part and why. Two further requests: separate clearly what is settled or near-universal among people who know this literature from what is genuinely contested, flagging any claim that rests on a stipulation of mine or on a contested reading of the concepts, and don't present a contested position as where the field has landed. And please don't append citations or suggestions for further reading — the value here is in the argument, not the bibliography.
