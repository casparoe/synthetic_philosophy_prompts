I'm a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy or game theory, and for the past few months I've been building a small agent-based simulation of the iterated prisoner's dilemma in my spare time, partly as an excuse to learn Rust and partly because I got sucked in after reading a popular account of the old round-robin computer tournaments. Payoffs are the usual T=5, R=3, P=1, S=0. The setup is roughly: a pool of about thirty hand-written strategies (tit-for-tat, always-defect, always-cooperate, a couple of grim-trigger "grudger" variants, some probabilistic and pattern-matching ones), random rematching each generation, a fixed 200 rounds per match, fitness-proportional copying into the next generation, no mutation, population of 100. With no noise the leaderboard looks like the story everyone tells: the nice, retaliatory, forgiving strategies cluster near the top. When I added a 2% chance that an intended move gets flipped in transmission, everything nice collapsed, and what ended up on top were the strategies that never forgive — mostly my grudger (cooperate until the other side defects once, then defect forever), with plain always-defect winning outright in some runs. I genuinely cannot tell whether I've discovered something or written a bug, and while documenting the README I realized the write-up quietly leans on a pile of claims I have never checked against each other. Before I post it anywhere, I'd like an audit.

Here is roughly where I've landed, as six commitments, none of which I'm especially attached to — and I'd rather you not ask me which one I'd drop, since finding that out is the point of the exercise:

A. In a genuinely one-shot prisoner's dilemma against a stranger — no repetition, no reputation, no mechanism by which my choice could be correlated with theirs — defection is the only rational play. I find the dominance argument airtight, and I don't accept any of the revisionary moves that try to rescue cooperation there, including the style of argument that says "we're both running the same decision procedure, so my move is evidence about yours."

B. The main explanation for cooperation wherever it actually shows up in nature and in human social life is direct reciprocity in the iterated game: the chance of meeting again doing the work, together with the standard result that once that chance is high enough, a wide range of outcomes including sustained mutual cooperation can be stable. Kin selection and group selection, I take it, are not needed to carry the explanatory load.

C. I read the tournament literature as supporting a strong and fairly general claim: nice, retaliatory, forgiving, non-envious strategies beat nasty ones, so cooperation is in an important sense easy, and my default expectation should be that cooperation emerges wherever agents interact repeatedly.

D. I also think that cooperation in the wild generally needs something that makes cooperators meet each other disproportionately — small groups, limited dispersal, spatial embedding, gossip and reputation — because in a large, well-mixed population with random rematching I'd expect cooperative strategies to be invaded once they become common. My own simulation only produces stable cooperation when I turn on neighbourhood structure, which is part of why I believe this.

E. Taking the noisy runs at face value, I'm inclined to conclude that in real-world settings, where misread signals and botched executions are everywhere, unforgiving retaliation is the robust core of any workable strategy and forgiveness is mostly a liability.

F. I'm unimpressed by the laboratory finding that a large minority — often around half — of subjects cooperate in supposedly one-shot experiments. I take this to show that subjects are confused, or don't really believe the interaction is one-shot and anonymous, or are importing habits that are adaptive in daily life. None of it, I think, bears on whether defection is the rational play.

What I want is a consistency check, not an introduction. Please go through these pairwise and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which only look like they conflict and dissolve once you state them precisely, and which are straightforwardly compatible — I suspect at least a couple of the conflicts I feel are just me using a word loosely, and at least one of the things I've been treating as settled is quietly incompatible with something else I've asserted. For the genuine ones, say which commitment has to give and what the cheapest revision costs me. Where it's relevant, tell me what I could change in the simulation to settle the question empirically rather than by argument — that's the part I can actually act on, and if you think my noisy result is an artifact of the setup rather than a finding, say so plainly and name the most likely cause.
