hey, I run the logic club at my school (its basically a math club that keeps drifting into philosophy). We get around 14 people, mostly 10th to 12th graders, one 50-minute block that's realistically 40 because everyone shows up late. Maybe a third of them are only there for the snacks but they'll play along if its a game. We don't use formal notation yet so keep it symbol-free, and no real money — I've got index cards, a whiteboard, some dice, and I can bring candy.

Anyway here's what got us stuck. We've been playing a lot of Hex lately, and someone pointed out that everybody in the room is happy to say a game of Hex can never end in a draw, and also everybody is happy to say they have definately no idea whether the first player has a winning strategy on the bigger boards — but apparently the second thing follows from the first plus some strategy-stealing move, and even after hearing that nobody feels like they now know it. So we ended up arguing about the general version: it sounds obviously right that if you believe something you should believe what follows from it, and it also sounds obviously right that you can't be blamed for failing to believe a consequence that nobody has actually worked out. I don't think both of those can be right, or at least they can't both be doing the work people want them to do.

Can you design a game or structured activity for one session where the group runs into that squeeze themselves instead of me explaining it? I want rules, materials, a payoff or scoring scheme built out of candy or points (ideally where people lose something real when their beliefs don't line up with what follows), a timeline, and how to actually run it. The thing I want them walking out with is that the problem is about what a perfectly rational person would believe, not about people being slow or bad at math — if the session just ends with everyone going "ha, humans are dumb," it'll have missed the point, so tell me how to steer it back if that happens. Also give me a debrief that maps the moments in the game onto the pieces of the puzzle, your honest guess at what goes wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and the part the game simply can't produce that I'll have to say out loud myself. I'd rather the name of the philosophical problem not come up until after they've played. Oh and if you can, include a version that still works if half the room is the snack people.
