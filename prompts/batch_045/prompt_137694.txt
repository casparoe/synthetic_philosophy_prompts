I convene a graduate reading group in philosophy, and next week I have a single ninety-minute seminar in which I must introduce David Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals. The group has already read a sympathetic exposition of Adams's thesis—the claim that a rational degree of belief in an indicative conditional should equal the corresponding conditional probability, so that the probability of 'if A, B' is P(B|A)—and they find it extremely attractive. They have not yet been told that Lewis showed this thesis cannot be sustained if the conditional is treated as a proposition with ordinary truth conditions. I was discussing this with a colleague in the mathematics department, who urged me simply to write down the four-cell Boolean algebra generated by two binary propositions and then run through all sixteen possible truth-functions for a putative conditional connective; but I worry that this will feel like a magic trick if I do not first make vivid why the equation is so compelling and why the result is not just another complaint about the material conditional. I am therefore not asking you to explain the results to me; I am asking you to plan how I should explain them. What has to come first, and why does that ordering matter? In particular, what must the group grasp about conditional probability, about the idea of a proposition as something with a truth value (or as a set of possible states), and about the partition into four cells, before the impossibility can even be stated without seeming like a sleight of hand?

I had been considering building the session around a single concrete case—a card drawn at random from a well-shuffled deck, with A the proposition that the card is a heart and B the proposition that it is a queen—but I am unsure whether that example should carry the whole explanation, or whether I need to move almost immediately to a generic four-cell diagram whose cells have variable weights p, q, r and s (where p = P(A∧B), q = P(A∧¬B), r = P(¬A∧B), s = P(¬A∧¬B)). Tell me which example or case should be the one vehicle, and say what it must have and what it must lack: must the two propositions be logically independent? must the cell weights be allowed to vary arbitrarily? why would an example with fixed numerical probabilities, such as the deck of cards, be inadequate at the point where the proof generalises? I also want you to specify the exact order in which I should introduce (i) the equation P(A→B) = P(B|A), (ii) the assumption that, if the conditional is a proposition, A→B must be one of the sixteen truth-functional compounds of A and B (or, more carefully, a proposition in the algebra they generate), (iii) the observation that any such proposition has a probability equal to the summed weight of whichever subset of the four cells makes it true, and (iv) the step where one sees that no subset of {p, q, r, s} can always sum to the fraction p/(p+q), no matter how the weights are varied. Where in this sequence should I first say explicitly that the result is not a refutation of the material conditional alone, and where should I generalise from the fixed four-cell case to Lewis's stronger conclusion that, if, for a single probability function, the equation is to hold for every pair of propositions and the conditional is still a proposition, then every pair A, B must be probabilistically independent—so that P(A∧B) = P(A)P(B) for all A, B, and hence, taking B to be ¬A, P(A) must be 0 or 1 for every A, leaving only degenerate, all-or-nothing credence functions?

Equally important is the misconception map. I expect the group to form at least the following misunderstandings: that the target is only the truth-functional material conditional; that the difficulty arises only for weird antecedents with probability zero, so that the equation can be saved for ordinary cases; that some alternative non-truth-functional conditional—a closest-world account on which 'if A, B' is true just in case B holds at the nearest A-world, for instance—could simply be substituted while keeping both the claim that the conditional is a proposition and Adams's thesis; that the upshot is that ordinary 'if'-talk is nonsense, rather than that one of a small set of jointly inconsistent commitments must be abandoned; and that the conditional probability P(B|A) is merely a rule for updating, so that there was never any hope of it being the probability of a proposition. Tell me whether I have characterised these correctly, whether I have missed a predictable misconception, and at exactly what point in the sequence each should be headed off. I also need to know what to leave out at this level and say openly that I am leaving it out: I take it I should not pursue the alternative of taking conditional probability itself as primitive (rather than as a ratio), nor the details of non-propositional approaches that represent the conditional as, say, a three-valued object or a random variable whose expectation is P(B|A), but tell me if I should merely gesture at these possibilities or avoid even naming them. Finally, give me one question or short exercise I can put to the group in the last ten minutes that would show me whether they have understood the force of the result—whether they can state the trilemma (that we cannot simultaneously treat the conditional as a truth-evaluable proposition, accept Adams's thesis, and retain non-trivial, non-degenerate credences) and say which limb they are inclined to reject—rather than merely reproducing the four-cell diagram or the sixteen truth-functions. Please write your plan in British English, and remember that the deliverable is a teaching plan: the sequence and its dependencies, the one carrying example and its required features, the misconception map with timings, the end-of-session check, and the declared omissions, not a direct explanation addressed to the students.
