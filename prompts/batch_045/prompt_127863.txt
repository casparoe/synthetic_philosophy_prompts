Someone talked me into this and I cant talk myself out of it. Setting it out with the steps numbered so you can tell me which one to cut.

(1) Condorcet's jury theorem: if each of n voters independently picks the correct one of two options with the same probability p > 1/2, then the chance the majority is correct goes to 1 as n grows, and for any error tolerance you care to name there is an n that gets under it.
(2) So a large enough majority is as near-infallible as you like on any question its members are even mildly competent about. The theorem puts no ceiling on how good the group gets; you just add voters.
(3) Now ask a large group, mildly competent on questions of this general kind, the question Q: are majority verdicts of this sort reliable? A large majority answers yes. Applying (2) to Q, that verdict is itself as near-infallible as you like. So we now have as much confidence as we want that (1) and (2) are true and that majority verdicts deliver.
(4) Nothing in (2) restricts subject matter, so run it again on whether the majority's answer to Q is reliable, then again on that, each round taking a small error rate and handing back one you can push as low as you like, and each round's conclusion available as a premise in the next.
(5) So from the modest assumption that the voters are 51% accurate and independent, plus arithmetic, you can get arbitrarily close to certainty that large majorities get it right.
(6) That has to be wrong. You cannot refine 51% accuracy into certainty by asking more people, not even when the thing you ask them about is how accurate they are.

I would rather keep (1) and (2) than swallow (5), and (3) looks like a straight instance of (2) — the theorem doesn't care what proposition is on the ballot. So which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? If the answer is that independence breaks, I want to be told exactly which votes are correlated with which and why; if the answer is that I am counting something twice, I want to know what the theorem's output is actually evidence about, as opposed to what I am treating it as evidence about.
