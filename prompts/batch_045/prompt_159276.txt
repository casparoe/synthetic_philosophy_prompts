I have been reading on my own, with no academic background, about whether all values can be put on a single scale and about what makes a choice hard. As I understand the three positions in that neighbourhood: the first says everything is comparable, so a hard choice is really either a tie or a case where we are ignorant of our own preferences; the second says some values are incommensurable, so that of two options none of better, worse, or equally good is true; the third says options can be comparable without being rankable — comparable, but neither better nor worse, and yet not equally good either. I can repeat all three slogans, but I cannot tell them apart in an actual case, and a friend I argue with says the third is just the first with extra steps.

So I want a pair of ordinary cases built to order. Case A should be one in which the three views give three genuinely different verdicts about the same two options: comparable-and-equal, incomparable, and comparable-but-unranked. Case B should be one in which all three collapse onto the same verdict. A and B must differ in exactly one respect, and you should tell me which respect that is. The verdicts have to be generated by the values and their relations in the case, not stipulated: writing something like suppose the options are on a par is exactly the failure mode I am trying to avoid.

Constraints. Ordinary life, the kind of choice an adult could actually face over a weekend; no lives at stake, nothing heroic, no rights violated, both options permissible. A paragraph each, no science fiction. Please avoid the stock examples — no career versus career, no Buridan's-ass or two-identical-desserts setups, no tragic dilemmas, nothing lottery- or trolley-adjacent. The hardness must not come from ignorance: the agent knows every relevant fact about the options and about her own tastes, so no she doesn't know what she really wants, no hidden information, no probabilities. The case must also survive fine-graining: even if the defender of the first view reindividuates values as finely as she likes, A has to stay unranked and B has to stay equal. And in A the two options should trade off goods that are different in kind but that a normal person would weigh against each other in the same breath — not two flavours of one good, and not two things so alien that nobody would ever compare them.

There must also be a practical difference between A and B, not merely a difference in how the agent feels. My own hypothesis is that it shows up after the choice: that in B flipping a coin is rationally fine and the agent's later attitudes are indifferent between outcomes, whereas in A something about deliberating, committing, or citing the choice as expressive of who one is becomes rationally apt or rationally required. But I am offering that as a hypothesis — if the real difference lies elsewhere, say where. Ideally the difference should be one that the first view cannot accommodate without changing its verdict about the options.

After the scenarios, please do the following: say what each of the three views says about A and about B and derive the verdict from that view's own machinery rather than labelling it; identify the single feature doing the work, and show what happens when it is removed, including whether the case collapses into a plain tie, into ignorance, or into incomparability — I especially want to know whether it collapses into the comparabilist's favourite redescription; and give the strongest attack a defender of the first view would make on A, with your honest estimate of whether A survives it. If the specifications cannot all be met together, say so and say which one has to go. No references or reading lists, please — I have no way to check them and I don't need them.
