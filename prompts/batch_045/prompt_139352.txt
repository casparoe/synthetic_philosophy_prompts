I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or economics — I picked up the idea of information cascades secondhand — and I'm putting together a short internal talk on why our org should collect written, anonymous assessments before anyone speaks in an architecture review. I'd like a worked example I can put on a slide for skeptical colleagues: realistic, concrete, and checkable rather than hand-wavy.

Please build me a scenario that meets all of the following at once:

1. An ordinary engineering decision made one person at a time — say, whether to split a monolith into services, or which of two vendors to adopt — where each person has seen the previous people's calls before making their own. Stakes a real team faces: months of work, not lives.
2. Every individual choice is rational given that person's own private read of the situation plus the public history. Nobody is lazy, sycophantic, deferring to seniority, or copying for its own sake; no bad-faith actor, no irrationality.
3. The team starts genuinely undecided, and no private read is better than a competent engineer's independent judgment — right maybe two times in three — so the outcome can't be propped up by an expert or an unusually strong signal.
4. The cascade nonetheless locks in on the wrong option.
5. With the same people and the same private reads, the group would get it right if the reads were aggregated before anyone saw anyone else's call — simultaneous votes, or written assessments collected first.

Then show that the case works: walk through the reasoning for two or three of the people with the actual odds, say exactly where the cascade begins and why, and show why the same group lands correctly once the sequence is removed. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, what happens if you take it out, whether anywhere the story quietly stipulates the outcome instead of generating it from the reasoning, and what someone who thinks open sequential discussion is fine would say against your example.

If those five requirements can't all hold together, or if I've misdescribed how cascades work, please say so plainly rather than handing me a story that fudges a step — I'd rather be corrected now than caught out in front of the room — and give me a rough sense of how confident you are in the analysis. Please write in American English.
