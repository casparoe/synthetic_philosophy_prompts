My roomate is doing an ethics module and has been going at me with this argument for about a week. Every step sounds fine when he says it out loud, but I can't accept where it ends up, so something must be slipping. Can you take it apart?

(1) What makes lying wrong, when it's wrong, is that you intentionally get someone to believe something false, and you do it so they'll act in a way that serves your purposes rather than theirs.
(2) Misleading does exactly the same thing. You can plant a false belief just as reliably by implying it, by answering a different question than the one you were asked, or by making sure somone overhears you, as by flat-out asserting it.
(3) So there's no moral difference between lying and misleading as such. The vehicle doesn't do the moral work; the false belief, and what you get out of it, do.
(4) And if the vehicle doesn't do the moral work, then whether you used words at all doesn't either. Turning a signpost around, forwarding someone else's old email with the date cropped off, quietly changing the room on the door list — all of that is morally on a par with lying to me about which way the station is.
(5) Which is mad. I owe people truthfulness in a way I don't owe them an untampered signpost. If you lie to my face I have a complaint against you that I don't have against whoever rearranged the building badly, even if both of us end up in Leeds instead of York.
(6) So (1) is false, the wrong of lying isn't just about producing false belief, and the door is open again to saying that asserting is special in a way that implicating isn't.

I'd most like to keep (2) and (5). I'm willing to lose (1), but if (1) goes I don't know what's supposed to replace it, and I don't want to end up back at "lying is bad because saying false things is bad."

Two cases I keep coming back to that I'm not sure any of his premises handle. First: telling the truth to someone who you know won't believe you, precisely because you know they'll take it as false and act on the opposite. Nothing false gets said and nothing gets implied, but words were used to land them on a false belief. Second: saying something true to the person you're actually talking to, knowing that someone else in the room will hear it, mangle it, and walk away believing something false. You didn't address them, but you knew.

Which step do you reject? Explain why it looked solid, what it costs to give up each of the alternatives you didn't pick, and show how your answer deals with those two cases.
