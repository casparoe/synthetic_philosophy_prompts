You are a professional philosopher who works on the ethics of armed conflict, and you also understand how a newsroom actually runs: you know the literature on the moral equality of combatants in detail, and you know what a copy desk looks like at midnight, what a translator working from a language they did not write in needs, and why a house style guide that requires a seminar to apply is a style guide that gets ignored. Before you write the procedure, spend a few paragraphs choosing its shape: sketch two or three ways of organizing it, and say in a sentence each why you rejected the alternatives. Then write it. Do not cite anything and do not suggest further reading — I need an answer that is self-contained, usable by someone who will never open a book on this.

I run the standards desk at an international news agency. We file in four languages, our clients include broadcasters inside every country whose forces and fighters we write about, and our corrections are published and read by the people who complain about them. Several times a week we write about people killed in a war: a patrol ambushed, a barracks hit overnight, a strike on a vehicle, a funeral, a memorial, an obituary of a veteran, a monthly casualty tally, a dispute over whether the dead included conscripts. The question that keeps coming back is how far our language may treat the two sides differently.

Here is the raw material I want you to operationalize. The traditional view — the moral equality of combatants, in the tradition of Walzer — holds, roughly, that a soldier on the unjust side is not a murderer for fighting, that combatants on both sides are equally permitted to kill and equally liable to be killed, and that whatever blame attaches to an unjust war falls on those who started it rather than on those who fight it. Revisionist critics such as McMahan deny this: on their account, whether a combatant may kill and whether they may be killed depends on the justice of what they are doing, so an unjust combatant is liable in a way a just combatant is not. I do not want my desk to adjudicate that dispute, and I do not think it can. But I also do not want to smuggle a verdict on it in through the adjectives.

The distinctions I think have to survive the simplification are these five, which I believe are routinely run together: (1) a person's status under the law of armed conflict — combatant, civilian, hors de combat; (2) whether a person is permitted to fight, that is, whether they wrong anyone by doing so; (3) whether a person is liable to be killed, that is, whether they have forfeited or lack a claim not to be attacked; (4) whether a person is to blame, or is excused, for fighting; (5) whether a dead person's life may be marked as a loss in the same way another's is — named, counted, commemorated. My working view is that the only part of the equality thesis an agency like ours can defensibly enforce is the fifth, together with a refusal to make our own findings on the second, third and fourth — and that this is enough to do real work on our copy. If that collapses under pressure, say so and give me something that does not.

One tool I already use informally, which I want repaired or replaced: I call it the swap test. Take the sentence, substitute the other side for this one, and see whether it still reads as something we would print. "Soldiers were killed in their bunks last night" passes; "the fallen gave their lives for their country" fails when I apply it to the other side's dead. I have found the test useful and unreliable in about equal measure, so I want to know exactly where it is decisive and where it misleads.

Please deliver the following:

1. A decision procedure, in six steps or fewer, in the order they are to be run. For each step: the question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to it, the conditions under which the procedure stops there with a no, and a stated default for when the question cannot be answered in the ten minutes we have. It must be applicable by an editor with no training and by a translator working in a fourth language, so avoid anything that depends on English idiom.

2. A ruling on the swap test: keep it, restrict it, or drop it, with the cases that show why.

3. Worked applications to three cases of the kind I actually get. First: an overnight strike on a barracks in a war our agency has not characterized, killing conscripts asleep in their bunks belonging to the army that crossed the border six months earlier. Second: a strike on a pickup truck that kills a local commander of a non-state armed group who planned and directed attacks on civilians in countries where we have clients, along with two passengers. Third: a weekend feature, timed to a memorial, on a man from our own country's forces who was killed in an offensive later shown to have been launched on the basis of intelligence that was wrong. Run the procedure on each and show the wording you would print. At least one of the three must come out against our current house style — tell me plainly which one does and why the procedure forces it rather than merely allowing it.

4. Verdicts on six hard cases, with an honest mark where the procedure stalls rather than answering: a child soldier carrying a rifle; a unit medic who is armed; a contractor driving a supply truck for a fee; an analyst who picks targets from a screen and never fires; a fighter who had surrendered and was then killed; a casualty line about a group one client designates as terrorist and another as a liberation movement; villagers who took up weapons for a single afternoon.

5. Marked escalation points: the steps that are questions of values dressed up as questions of fact, which must come to me rather than being settled at the desk, and what I should be asked to decide rather than to ratify.

6. Where the procedure will misfire: the kinds of conflict or copy for which it gives no usable answer, and the information it will cost us.

7. What would change your mind. Name the argument or consideration that would lead you to write a different procedure — for instance, if you think the epistemic case for symmetry is weaker than it looks now that combatants often have open access to documentation about their own side's conduct, or if you think most real wars are partly just on both sides and so the middle verdict swallows the procedure whole — and tell me the step in your own reasoning where you are least confident.

Be explicit, throughout, about which of the five distinctions above your procedure enforces, which it sets aside, and what each of those decisions costs us in the copy.
