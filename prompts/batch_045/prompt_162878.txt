I keep ending up in the same argument at dinner with a couple of friends who work on AI policy: everyone reaches for one of the big historical analogies — nuclear weapons, the printing press, the Industrial Revolution — and then uses it mostly as a vibe, like a way of signalling how worried they are rather than as something that actually licenses a conclusion. I want to test one of them properly, on one real case, and see whether it survives.

The case I want you to use is the peaceful-use settlement around fission: Eisenhower's Atoms for Peace speech to the UN in December 1953, the research reactors and fuel that got shipped abroad afterwards, the IAEA set up a few years later, and then the NPT bargain (signed 1968, in force 1970) where non-weapons states renounce weapons and accept safeguards in exchange for access to civilian technology plus a promise of eventual disarmament by the weapons states. Treat that as the concrete episode to analyze through the nuclear-weapons analogy for transformative AI. I want the mapping done carefully and explicitly: what counts as the dangerous affordance being gated, what counts as the benign benefit being shared, who the parties to the bargain are on each reading, what the verification mechanism actually consists of, and what the episode predicts about whether a bargain like that holds under pressure. One background belief I'm bringing in, which you should feel free to correct or discard: the leak in this regime was that the civilian infrastructure sits fairly close to the weapons-relevant step (enrichment, reprocessing), so sharing the good part inevitably transfers a lot of the bad part — the usual cautionary example is India's 1974 test drawing on material from a nominally peaceful programme, though I may have the details garbled, so don't build much on that specific case.

Then I want you to push back on the analogy rather than just apply it. I've been playing with a crude three-variable diagnostic for whether any technology supports an Atoms-for-Peace-style bargain: how leaky the transfer is (much weapons-relevant capability rides along with the beneficial capability), how much latency there is between the peaceful build-out and the dangerous outcome (years of visible construction vs. something that flips overnight), and how legible the activity is to an inspector (physical plant and material accountancy vs. something with almost no external signature). Score fission on those three and then score AI on them, and tell me honestly whether the triple is doing any real work or just relabeling what we already knew — and if it's the latter, what a better diagnostic would look like. I also suspect the three analogies are quietly doing different jobs: one supplies a menu of policy instruments, one calibrates how fast and how far the effects spread, one predicts the shape of the political conflict (states versus diffuse users versus labor and capital). Say which of those jobs the nuclear case genuinely licenses for AI, which one of the other analogies does better at the job the nuclear one fails at, and — this is the part I care most about — where the analogy is simply silent and any mapping would be forced. By the end I want to know which analogy, if any, you'd retire from this conversation, and what would have to be true of AI for you to bring it back. No citations or further reading needed, just the argument.
