I'm a first-year PhD student in history working on a manuscript tradition in which the same books were catalogued under several different names before anyone thought to compare them, and after an argument with a friend in the philosophy department I listened to a podcast episode on Kripke's *Naming and Necessity* to see whether it would help me think about my material. Here is what I wrote down from the host's main stretch, more or less in his words. "A name like 'Hesperus' is a rigid designator: it picks out one and the same object however the world might have been, whereas a description like 'the brightest thing visible in the evening' is not rigid, since in some other possible world some other object satisfies it. So when astronomers worked out that Hesperus and Phosphorus are both Venus, they were learning something necessary — because a rigid designator names the same thing in every world, and because if one thing is identical with another it is necessarily identical with it, there is no possible world in which the morning star and the evening star are two different things. Yet it is something we could only learn by going out and looking, and that is the necessary a posteriori: it kills the old empiricist idea that whatever is true of necessity can be known from the armchair. Exactly the same argument works for 'water is H2O' — imagine a planet just like ours whose lakes and rivers are filled with a different liquid with the same visible properties; once chemistry tells us the composition of our own stuff, we can see that the liquid on that planet is simply not water, so water could not have been anything other than H2O. And the upshot is that once a name has latched onto a thing, everything we go on to say with that name holds of the thing in every possible world in which the thing exists, which is why it is science, and not armchair reflection, that tells us how things must be."

What I actually want to use this on is my own case, and I can't tell whether it fits. Two early catalogues list "the Verona codex" and "the Ambrosian fragment," each named for the place where it turned up; in the 1920s a scholar showed that they are the two halves of a single book that was split up in the eighteenth century. Separately, nineteenth-century scholars introduced the name "the Codex of the Nine" by way of the description "the manuscript containing the nine Easter sermons"; it now looks as though no such manuscript ever existed, since the sermons were copied into three different books, and yet the name stayed in circulation and present-day cataloguers apply it to one of those three books, which certainly does not contain nine sermons. My friend says the first case is a textbook instance of the necessary a posteriori and the second is a case where the name simply fails to refer, and so every sentence containing it is false. I am not confident about either verdict. It seems to me that the book in question could perfectly well have been split differently, rebound, or left intact, and I don't know whether "the Verona codex" names the book of 1400 or the bundle of quires that happened to survive in Verona, which is the sort of question the podcast gave me no handle on at all. It also seems to me that Kripke's machinery was built for names of people and substances, and I can't tell whether it has anything to say about objects whose identity and persistence look as conventional as a codex's, where how long the thing exists and what counts as the same thing again seem to depend on our classificatory practices rather than on the object alone.
