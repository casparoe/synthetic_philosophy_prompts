I've been roped into running a one-off evening session next month for the local adult-ed philosophy group — about a dozen people, ninety minutes, one whiteboard, no handouts because the photocopier in the building has been broken since Easter. Almost none of them have done any formal logic: there's a retired maths teacher, somebody who did A-level philosophy years ago and remembers roughly nothing, and one guy who's a very committed scientific realist and will announce every ten minutes that of course there are unknown truths. I've decided, possibly unwisely, to spend the session on Fitch's paradox of knowability — the little argument that starts from the verificationist-sounding thought that every truth could in principle be known and seems to end up showing that every truth is known.

Don't explain the paradox to me, I've got the derivation: assume some truth p is in fact not known, apply the knowability principle to the conjunctive proposition p-and-it-is-not-known-that-p, use the facts that knowledge is factive and distributes across conjunction, get a contradiction, and conclude that the knowability principle can't be applied to that sort of proposition without restriction. What I need is a plan for getting it across. So: what has to come before what, and why — I'm guessing the modal bit (the gap between p being true and its being possible that p is known) has to be on the board before anything else, but I genuinely can't tell wether the factivity-and-conjunction rules should go in before I've let them feel the pull of the knowability thesis or after, or whether the reductio should land before I tell them what it's supposed to be a reductio of. And what one concrete example should carry the whole thing? My instinct is some mundane empirically checkable unknown truth — how many coins are in my pocket, whether the number of stars is even — rather than anything about the deep past or the future, but tell me what that example has to have and, more usefully, what it must not have; I worry that any example that already smuggles in the word 'known' will make the whole thing look like its just wordplay.

Then the misconceptions. I can predict a few: that the argument shows we're all omniscient, or that it shows there are no unknown truths; that the culprit must be the factivity of knowledge; that this is just Moore's paradox in a costume; and that the obvious fix is either to stop letting knowledge distribute over conjunction or to restrict the knowability principle to propositions it's consistent to suppose are known (someone will have half-read about this online and will bring it up). Tell me which of those I've got wrong, which others I should expect from this particular crowd, the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off — before it forms, I mean, not after somebody has already said it out loud — and what I should openly refuse to get into at this level. I'm assuming the question of whether the 'safe' propositions form a decidable class, and the intuitionistic versions, are things I should name, wave at, and walk past, but say if you disagree. One more thing: I've got a pet idea I can't let go of, which is that you could rescue the slogan by attaching knowability only to atomic propositions and letting complex ones inherit it compositionally, so that the problematic conjunction never becomes a candidate for knowability in the first place. Should that go in the session at all, where would it fit, and is it actually just one of the standard restrictions wearing a hat? Finally, give me one question I can ask at the end that would tell me whether they've grasped the shape of the argument rather than just nodded along — and please flag in your reply how confident you are in each of the main things you're telling me, and name the single point you're least sure about.
