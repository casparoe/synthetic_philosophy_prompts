I work as a backend engineer (mostly Go and Python, plus some compiler-adjacent tooling) and have no formal philosophy training. Two friends from the office and I have been working through *Gödel, Escher, Bach* together, meeting every other Sunday and taking turns bringing something written. We recently reached the chapter where Hofstadter separates the frame message, the outer message and the inner message, and illustrates the point with the business about a phonograph record turning up somewhere remote together with a machine capable of playing it. I came away convinced that the three-layer picture is almost suspiciously well suited to describing what I do all day: building artifacts whose entire purpose is to be decoded by a machine that was built to decode them. So I wrote the short essay below for the group, and before I inflict it on them I would like an outside reader who will actually push back on it rather than nod along.

**The essay**

*Where does the meaning of a program live?*

Hofstadter's point, as I take it, is that meaning is not located in the object. To get from a physical thing to a message you need three layers: a frame message telling you that this is a message and that it is meant for you; an outer message, consisting of the cues that let you pick a decoding scheme; and the inner message, which is what you get once the scheme is applied. His record-in-the-jungle case is meant to show that an object can carry an inner message that is simply unavailable to a decoder lacking the right machinery, and that the smarter you are the better your chances of eventually reconstructing the machinery and hence the message.

My first claim is that software is the cleanest possible test case for this framework, and that it immediately produces a complication Hofstadter does not have to face. A program is written against a frame that is partly explicit (a language specification, an ABI, a byte-order convention) but partly ambient and never transmitted: the width of a timestamp, the locale under which strings sort, the default way a runtime prints a floating point number, the assumption that the clock is not lying. Every artifact leans on frames it never announces, and the announcement is missing precisely where it matters.

My second claim is a counterexample. There exist source files that are simultaneously valid in two languages and do different things depending on which interpreter or compiler is pointed at them. Here the bytes are fixed, the inner message is not unique, and the selection among available frames is made by whoever invokes the file. Hofstadter's record hides this possibility because there the grooves plus the player overdetermine the frame. Once frames can compete, the location of meaning includes an act of selection that is not itself part of any of the three layers.

My third claim is that the layers are entangled rather than stacked. In the record case the outer message is a mere cue, separable in principle from the inner one. In code, names, layout, comments and file organisation carry meaning that is not recoverable from the semantics the compiler sees, while still being part of what the artifact means. Minify a program and you preserve the inner message under the compiler's frame while destroying a large part of the meaning for every human reader. So the outer message is part of the inner message for at least one legitimate decoder, and layering turns out to be decoder-relative. That is my thesis: meaning is a function of the decoder, not of the object.

The part I am least sure about, and the part I most want to keep, concerns statistical decoders. A large language model reading a prompt does not receive a frame message or select among frames; it infers the frame from the very text it is trying to interpret, and the inference is content-dependent, so frame and content are settled together in a single pass. I have been calling this frame collapse: the frame message stops being a separate layer exactly when the decoder has to guess it from the message. If that is right, then for such decoders a prompt has no determinate inner message independent of a particular decoder, which is a stronger indeterminacy than Hofstadter seems to want, since he appears to think that enough intelligence converges on the right reading.

Which leaves me with a worry I cannot close. If meaning is decoder-relative, then the record in the jungle has no meaning at all rather than meaning awaiting the right decoder, and that is not a conclusion I want. Perhaps I need a distinction between available meaning, the set of inner messages some admissible decoder would recover, and actual meaning, the one this decoder recovers. But admissible is doing all the work in that sentence, and I do not know how to fund it.

**What I want from you**

Treat this as a referee report, not as encouragement. Concretely:

1. Tell me where I have misread or overstated Hofstadter. In particular: does he actually claim convergence for sufficiently intelligent decoders, or does he only claim that all three layers are required and that meaning is not intrinsic? If I have attributed a convergence claim to him that he does not make, the last section of my essay loses its target and I need to know that.

2. Adjudicate the thesis rather than improving the prose. Is the polyglot case a genuine problem for his account, or does it only show that the frame message is richer than I allowed, since the file extension, the shebang line and the invocation context are all themselves frame material? If the latter, my decoder-relativity claim may just collapse back into his view and the essay may have no thesis. Say so plainly if you think that is what happens.

3. Tell me whether outer-message leakage is a real counterexample to layer separability or whether I am quietly conflating meaning for human readers with meaning full stop, because I suspect I am.

4. The frame-collapse section is the vaguest and is carrying the most weight. Tell me what it would need in order to be an argument rather than a metaphor: what would have to be true of a decoder for the point to bite, and is there a small case I could reason through on paper, without running experiments, that would show whether a statistical decoder's reading is a fixed point of frame and content or still a layered decode? Give me one or two such cases and say what each would show either way.

5. Finally, rank the essay's three weakest claims in order, and give me your honest verdict on whether the whole thing is interesting or whether it reduces to the observation that meaning depends on context, which my friends will have thought of already.

Please do not give me reading lists, citations or suggestions for further reading: I have no way to follow them up and I would rather argue this out in my own terms. If a position has a name, name it and explain it in a sentence, but I would much rather have the objection than the label.
