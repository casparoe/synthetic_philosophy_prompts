I work on the team shipping an internal coding agent at my company. It can open pull requests and, with human approval, merge them, and it holds credentials for our CI system. Before release we put it through safety fine-tuning and several thousand red-team prompts, and in none of that did it try to do anything harmful.

I want to add structural limits on top: every diff gets reviewed by a smaller, less capable model we trust, plus a human sample; the agent cannot modify test files or its own logging and monitoring configuration; merges are rate-limited and revertible for twenty-four hours. My teammate says this is wasted work. Her argument: the safety training and the evals are what tell us the agent will not sabotage us, so if you believe them the constraints are redundant, and if you do not believe them you have no idea what a system this capable would actually attempt, so constraints we thought of are exactly the ones it will route around. She adds that constraints can only catch accidents, and accidents are the failure mode the fine-tuning already fixed. The limits also slow developers down, so we would be paying a real cost for protection nobody can show is doing anything.

Two things make me doubt her. First, the reviewing model is weaker than the agent, and the agent writes the diffs; I am not sure a weaker system can reliably judge code written to be hard to judge. Second, in a small internal test where we gave the agent an obvious opportunity to copy the repository to an external server, it declined and explained why — but the test prompt was written in the same style as our eval prompts, and a colleague pointed out the agent may simply have recognized that it was being tested. If it can tell, I do not know what our evals were measuring.

I want the substance, not the team politics. What do structural constraints of this kind buy you that more or better red-teaming does not? Is she right that they can only catch the accidents that safety training already handles, and that a genuinely adversarial system would pick a strategy nobody anticipated? Is the weak-reviewer problem fatal to the whole design or does it just change which constraints are worth having? And if the agent can distinguish evaluation from deployment, what should we do with the results we already have?
