I'm in a little self-run reading group on classical Indian philosophy — me, a math PhD student, an oncologist, and a guy who mostly just likes arguing — and we've gotten completely stuck on anekāntavāda. Not on whether it's appealing (we all like it), but on what it's actually *claiming*, and whether it can be stated without sawing off the branch it's sitting on. I want an essay that takes a position. The rough thesis I keep circling is that the doctrine only stays coherent if you read it as a claim about the semantics and pragmatics of assertion — roughly, that no substantive claim is assertable without a standpoint-qualifier of the syādvāda kind, and that nayavāda is a story about which qualifiers are available — rather than as a metaphysical claim about reality being irreducibly many-sided; and that on the metaphysical reading it either collapses into the boring truth that most predicates are context-sensitive, or it eats itself. But I want you to actually argue this out, not just nod along: give the strongest version of the metaphysical reading too.

Three things I want you to work through, because they're where we stalled. First, I made up a distinction that I think does real work but can't finish: call it *flat* vs *nested* many-sidedness. Flat says every first-order claim is true only from some standpoint and there's no standpoint-neutral fact of the matter, but the claim that this is so is itself unqualifiedly true. Nested says the relativity claim is also only true from a standpoint, and so on down. Which one is the doctrine committed to? Is the nested version's regress benign (each level just has a wider scope of standpoints, the way a hierarchy of context-sensitivity would) or does it make the view unstatable? Second, the "inert" cases: does anekāntavāda have anything to say about 2+2=4, or about someone being in pain right now, or about a file having been deleted at 3pm, or about an action's having been cruel? If it covers all of them, isn't it just context-sensitivity dressed up in Sanskrit? If it carves some out, what's the principled carve-out, and doesn't the carve-out smuggle back in exactly the sort of absolute it was supposed to deny? Third, a case my oncologist friend raised that I think is the sharpest: if the doctrine is about truth, does a Jain physician who takes it seriously owe a patient a sevenfold answer — chemo is beneficial from the standpoint of oncology, harmful from the standpoint of the body's healthy tissue, both, neither, inexpressible — instead of saying "this is your best option"? If not, why not, and if your answer is "well, the doctrine is metaphysical, it's not a rule about what to say to patients," then tell me what work the metaphysical claim is doing that the rule about assertion isn't already doing.

One more test that I think separates the readings cleanly, and please use it: imagine someone builds an AI assistant explicitly in the spirit of the sevenfold scheme — it never gives a flat verdict, it answers every question by outputting the answers licensed by each major framework plus a residual "inexpressible" option, and it declines to say which one is right. Is that a faithful implementation or a parody? More importantly, is there a real difference between withholding assertion because you have evidence that standpoints disagree, and asserting a qualified truth because you think reality itself is only standpoint-relative? I suspect the doctrine gets its plausibility from sliding between those two, and I want you to say which one it should be and why. Don't bother with the history, with which text says what, or with citations — I can look that stuff up, and half of it I've already read. Give me the argument, commit to a verdict, defend it, and then say where your verdict is most fragile and what would change your mind. Somewhere around 900 words is perfect.
