Two philosophers have argued for years about what the MU-puzzle actually shows. One, the Internalizer, holds that any apparent case of jumping out of the system (JOOTS) is in principle recapturable as ordinary reasoning inside some larger formal system, and that the MU-puzzle looks dramatic only because the object system is too weak to state the elementary arithmetical fact that settles it. The other, the Escalationist, grants that any particular jump can be internalized after the fact, but denies that there is any single fixed system that contains them all: for every system offered, a further puzzle can be posed whose blocking feature that system does not find, and the capacity to see such features is therefore open-ended rather than mechanically bounded.

Draft the joint statement they could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

The object of the dispute. Alphabet M, I, U; sole axiom MI; four rules: if a string ends in I you may append a U; from Mx you may derive Mxx; any occurrence of III may be replaced by a single U; any occurrence of UU may be deleted. The question is whether MU is derivable. The familiar move is to stop deriving and count: appending U and deleting UU leave the number of I's unchanged, doubling doubles it, replacing III subtracts three, and a count that starts at one is therefore never divisible by three, while MU has none. That is a claim about the system which the system itself cannot express.

For the collaboration, generalize the object system to a family MIU(n, k): axiom MI; append a U to any string ending in I; from Mx derive Mx^n; replace any k consecutive I's by a single U; delete any UU; target MU. MIU(2, 3) is the original puzzle. Where some d greater than 1 divides k and is coprime to n, the count of I's is never divisible by d, so MU is unreachable; this covers (2, 3) and (2, 6) and (4, 3) with d = 3, and (2, 9) with d = 9. Where no such d exists the invariant gives out, and in at least some of those cases the target is reachable in a few steps — in MIU(2, 4): MI, MII, MIIII, MU. Re-derive whatever you cannot check; do not assume my bookkeeping.

Constraints on the collaboration.

Neither side may assert its governing premise. The Internalizer may not rest on the claim that cognition is computation or that a mind is a formal system; the Escalationist may not rest on the claim that they can simply see that a system is consistent or that an insight feels unrepeatable. Both must argue from the behavior of systems, from families of cases, and from derivability facts.

Separate weak internalization (for each jump there is some system that contains it) from strong internalization (there is one fixed system F, specified in advance of the family, which takes a description of the rules as input and outputs the correct verdict for every member, deriving the invariant rather than having it hand-coded per case). Say whether the dispute survives once weak internalization is granted by both.

Whenever an internalization is offered, the Internalizer must fill in a ledger: name F; say whether F is fixed or rebuilt per member; say whether the invariant is derived from the rule description or supplied; and say whether F also certifies the reachable members and produces derivations for them, or only ever returns a negative verdict. An F that can only say no has internalized very little, and both sides should say how much.

Both sides should agree on a matched-pair protocol that uses the members where the modular invariant holds and the members where it fails, in order to catch the failure mode in which a person or procedure reports unreachable across the board and thereby mistakes a bias for a jump. Specify the protocol and what each side predicts.

Deliverables.

1. A numbered section of claims both can sign: about the formalism and what the family actually establishes; about what stands in need of explanation; about whether the modest point that no system of this kind can fully see itself does any work here; about whether the modular condition is meant to concern the target MU alone or the full set of derivable strings.

2. A disagreements table with these columns: the crux; the Internalizer's formulation in wording the Internalizer would accept; the Escalationist's formulation in wording the Escalationist would accept; the case, test, or argument both agree bears on that crux; the Internalizer's prediction about it, recorded in advance; the Escalationist's prediction, recorded in advance. Cruxes I expect to matter: whether strong internalization is available for the whole family; whether generalizing from (2, 3) to (2, 9) or (4, 3) is one transferred schema or a fresh jump each time; whether a variant whose obstruction is not a conserved count at all but, say, a quantity that only decreases, or a condition on the arrangement of the U's, is handled by the same capacity or requires a different one; and whether the escalation move is a substantive possibility or merely a restatement of the Escalationist's premise. Add cruxes I have not listed, and do not force the ones I have.

3. A section identifying which parts of the dispute were verbal: jumping out as a phenomenological event, as a logical relation between an object system and a claim about it, and as a claim about the powers of a person or procedure are three different things, and both parties should say which of them each has been arguing about.

4. A ranking of the cruxes by how much settling them would move the debate, together with your judgment about which single one would end the dispute if settled and which would leave it substantially standing.

5. A brief closing note, in your own words and without appealing to authority, on what the puzzle was originally introduced to show and whether that reading helps either side.

Write it as a document both could put their names to. Where you think a formulation is vacuous, say so and supply a better one; where you think a crux is genuine but currently untestable, say what would have to be built to test it.
