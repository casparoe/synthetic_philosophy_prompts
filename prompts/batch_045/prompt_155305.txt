**The question, as set to the class**

Question 4. Daniel Dennett holds that we can adopt three stances toward a system: the physical stance (predict its behaviour from microphysics), the design stance (predict it from what it was designed or trained to do), and the intentional stance (predict it by attributing beliefs and desires and assuming it will do what it believes will get it what it wants). Two features of his account matter for what follows. First, attributions made from the intentional stance are not, on his view, mere projections: they can pick out *real patterns*, that is, patterns in a system's behaviour that any competent predictor could exploit for prediction and compression, and that are typically invisible from the physical stance. Second, Dennett allows that one and the same stretch of behaviour may be described from more than one stance at once, and that which stance we adopt is a matter of which is cheapest and most reliable for the prediction we happen to need.

Now consider a large language model M, prompted as follows: *You are a meticulous archive assistant. Answer only from the documents given you, and say when the documents do not settle a question.* Under that prompt M produces continuations that we would naturally describe as reporting what it takes to be in a document. Call the prompted system P. Note also that the very same weights, under a different prompt, would realise a system with quite different dispositions; call this feature of the case *prompt-relativity*.

Assess the following three claims:

(i) P is a genuine intentional system, because attributing beliefs and desires to P is by far the cheapest reliable way of predicting what P will say next; and the fact that P's competences were produced by training on human text is a design-stance fact that poses no threat to this.

(ii) Because P's next output is in principle a function of the weights, the tokenisation, and the context, there is in principle a design-stance derivation of the very same continuation; hence the beliefs we attribute to P are *merely heuristic* — a convenient shorthand, not a description of anything real.

(iii) Suppose that work in mechanistic interpretability eventually gave us a design-stance derivation of P's continuations that was as tractable and as reliable as the intentional one. Then the belief attributions we had been making would be shown to have been false all along.

In answering, you should address three questions explicitly: does the success of the intentional stance depend on the *failure* of the design stance? Is a pattern's being mechanically realised a reason to deny that it is real? And does prompt-relativity show that at most one of the prompted systems can be an intentional system?

**The candidate's answer, verbatim**

I take the three claims in order, and then say something about prompt-relativity.

(i) I accept this claim. On Dennett's own criterion, to be an intentional system is nothing over and above being a system whose behaviour can be predicted, cheaply and reliably, by attributing beliefs and desires and assuming a rough rationality. There is no further hidden requirement — not consciousness, not a biological history, not the right kind of causal connection to the world. So the cheapness-and-reliability point does all the work by itself, and P qualifies. Two things make the case easier rather than harder. First, the attribution is indexed to the prompted system: what we are predicting is the behaviour of P, the archive assistant, and not the behaviour of the weights under just any context. That is why the training history is not an objection. The design stance tells us how P got to be the way it is; the intentional stance tells us what to expect next. They answer different questions, and answering the first does not pre-empt the second. Second, this is why Dennett's position is standardly described as a mild or pattern-based realism rather than as eliminativism: he is not saying that beliefs are useful fictions, he is saying that they are patterns, and that a pattern can be perfectly real without being part of the microstructure.

(ii) The first half of this claim is right and the second half does not follow. It is true in principle that the continuation is derivable from the design stance: given weights, tokenisation and context, the output is fixed. But from the availability in principle of a lower-level derivation it simply does not follow that the higher-level description is a mere heuristic. That is exactly what the notion of a real pattern is invoked to deny. A pattern is real if it is there to be found, if it licenses prediction and compression, and if it is not an artefact of one observer's way of seeing; and the fact that the same events can also be captured exhaustively at the microphysical level shows only that the two descriptions are compatible, not that the higher-level one is a convenience. The word *merely* is not earned. This is also why the intentional stance is best understood as a fallback — something we resort to precisely when, and only when, the design stance is computationally closed to us, which for a network with hundreds of billions of parameters it certainly is.

(iii) I think this claim is correct, and it is the natural sequel to (ii). If interpretability delivered a derivation we could actually carry out, the intentional description would no longer be doing any predictive work for us. And that is not merely a change in our convenience, because the design-stance story is the causal story — the story of what actually brings the next token about. As between a description that cites the mechanism and one that cites a belief, the mechanism is the true one, and the belief attribution was an approximation that we needed only because of our own bounded computational powers. So the beliefs were never strictly speaking there; a successful mechanistic derivation would reveal them to have been, as (ii) put it, merely heuristic.

On prompt-relativity: the various prompted systems are not competing descriptions of one thing but different things, so there is no contradiction in attributing different beliefs to different systems realised in the same weights. It would be a mistake to suppose that the weights must secretly have one real set of beliefs of which the prompted attributions are approximations.
