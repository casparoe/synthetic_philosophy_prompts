I'm a novelist and I've got a problem that isn't really a plotting problem, it's been bugging me for two years. My next book is about a species that lives inside rock. The individuals never meet, never see each other, never touch anything they talk about — they're blind, and they communicate by scoring marks into clay tiles that get pushed along tunnels. So every one of them learns the whole language from the marks on tiles and from nothing else. And yet they coordinate: they shore up tunnels before collapses, they route water, they leave warnings that other burrowers act on correctly.

Halfway through the book a human character builds a thing — call it the reader — hooked to sensor arrays and trained on a huge corpus of the tiles, and after a while it starts cutting new tiles of its own that the burrowers treat as perfectly sensible. My narrator has to say one of two things and I need to know what I'm committing to: that the burrowers' marks are about the dark outside the rock and the reader's aren't, or that it's patterns all the way down for both of them. Two cases I keep circling: the burrowers have a word for the big predator that really tracks the vibration-pattern on their skin and not the animal, and they've been wrong about it for ten thousand years with nothing ever going wrong for them; and they've got a word for what's outside that's been handed mark to mark for a thousand generations without anybody checking it against anything.

So before I write myself into a corner — what would a theory of when a symbol is actually about something have to deliver for me to trust it? I want the requirements list, not the theories, and good enough for a narrow purpose: it has to let my narrator write a straight sentence about what the marks mean, or tell me she can't. Give me exactly five numbered requirements, one or two sentences each, no intro and no summing-up at the end. Each one starts with MUST or NICE-TO-HAVE and carries the case — from my world, or one you invent that's worse — that shows why it's on the list. Put an asterisk on the one you think rules out the most candidate theories. If two of them can't be had together, say so inside whichever item it bites and don't smooth it over. And one of the five has to be about what my human character could actually go do in the field: she's standing there with the tiles in her hands and she has to decide, so the theory owes her some test, not just a verdict.

Two things I don't want. No scorecard of the usual candidate theories — I'll do that myself once I know what to ask for. And no name-dropping: I'm writing in a cabin with no signal, so no papers, no quotes, no famous thought experiments I'd have to look up, no "as so-and-so argued". Just the spec. If it turns out the spec is definately unsatisfiable as a set, that's honestly the most useful thing you could tell me, because then the book writes itself.
