Hey — I run the physics club at my school, about 14 people, grades 10 through 12, and honestly only about half of them have taken physics past grade 10 (one or two are in it mostly because their friends are). We meet Thursdays after school in the physics prep room, nominally 90 minutes but really more like 70 once everyone stops messing around, and I have access to totally ordinary school junk: ping pong balls, cardboard boxes, a long shallow tray, coins, dice, index cards, markers, string, a few small weights, a kitchen scale, stopwatches, and maybe a hand-crank flashlight if I can find one in the lost-and-found. Last week I tried to explain Maxwell's demon to them — the little guy with the shutter between two boxes of gas who sorts fast molecules from slow ones and apparently gets free work — and it went badly in a specific way: someone said why not just build a better door, a smarter one, some kind of automatic flap that only lets the fast ones through, and I froze, because I'd read somewhere that the real answer isn't the door at all, it's that the demon has to write down what it sees and then wipe its memory clean to go again, and that erasing one bit costs something like kT ln 2 of energy dumped as heat. I sort of believe that but I couldn't make it feel like anything, and neither could the whiteboard diagram I drew.

So I want an activity where they actually *run* the demon instead of hearing about it, and I want the three things they come away having felt to be these: a shutter that opens at random achieves nothing, so sorting requires information and not just mechanism; that information lives somewhere physical — a notebook, a row of coins, whatever — and that's a resource you can run out of; and that the bill comes due when you reset that thing back to blank, not when you look at the molecule. My rough idea is two rounds. Round one is a free-for-all: teams play the demon with unlimited scratch paper over a tray of maybe 30 balls that differ in some way they can see (two sizes, say, standing in for fast and slow), and they sort like crazy and it looks totally free and they'll feel smug. Then round two I take away the unlimited paper and hand each team a fixed tiny memory — like six index-card slots, or six coins on the desk that have to be heads-up to be blank — and every time they want to reset a used slot back to blank they have to pay for it with a real physical action I can see: crank the hand flashlight a fixed number of turns, lift a weight, walk a token to a reset station at the back of the room. Points for sorted balls, points off for reset actions, and I want the scoring tuned so that the sorting profit gets visibly eaten and they start arguing about the ledger rather than the door. Please give me actual rules and roles (I'm imagining one person as the demon who can see the tray and one as the door-operator who can't, so information has to physically cross between them — maybe that's the thing that makes it land?), a materials list from a normal prep room, the payoff scheme in points, and a minute-by-minute timeline for the 70 minutes.

Three more things I'd really like. First: I bet someone will suggest the demon just never erases anything, keep the notebook forever, and I don't trust myself to answer that well on the spot. Second: one group will definately try to cheat by compressing their notes, like writing FFFSS instead of one symbol per ball, and I have no idea whether compression should actually help them or whether that's a trick — please tell me what to do with that if it comes up and what it actually shows. Third: I want them to construct the problem themselves and only hear the names at the very end, so no lecture up front and no one says entropy or Landauer until afterwards. Then give me the debrief — which moments in the game map onto which part of the argument, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (people peeking, people doing the reset half-heartedly, someone arguing the shutter is the problem), and an honest note about which feature of the real physics this just cannot reproduce and which part I'll have to say out loud myself.
