I write fiction for a living — four novels, a stack of short stories, and a workshop I teach every spring — so I spend a stupid number of hours a day doing something that feels like introspection, and lately I've started to suspect it isn't really.

Here's the thing I keep running into. When I need to find out what I feel about something — say, whether I'm actually angry at my brother or just tired and wounded about something else — the answer arrives in exactly the same way as the answer to a completely different question: what would my character feel here. Same machinery. I set up the situation, imagine it in some detail, run the scene, wait to see what the person in it does, and then write down what happened. If it's the character and the result feels wrong, I toss it and run it again with different staging, no drama. If it's me, the result comes out as a *report*. I treat it as something I now know. And when a friend says "no you're not, you're just tired", I don't revise it the way I'd revise a scene — I feel corrected, or caught out, like I got wrong something I was supposed to have special access to.

So the method is the same and the authority is different, and I can't work out whether that's a discovery about introspection or just a fact about what you're doing when you tell someone "I feel X".

A couple more instances, since I don't want to hand you an abstraction. In interviews I get asked whether I share my narrator's politics, and I've noticed I answer by imagining what I'd say on a panel — i.e. by writing myself, the same trick, then reading off the result as a belief. And I found an old diary of mine from about twelve years ago, full of feelings I cannot now get back to by any amount of sitting quietly; the diary seems like better evidence about me than my present-day introspection does, which seems backwards. Also: there's that line novelists trot out about not knowing what you think until you see what you've written. I used to think it was a joke. Now I think it might be the honest description of the method, and I don't know whether that should worry me.

So: is there an established concept for this? I've heard it said that you find out what you believe by looking at the world rather than at yourself, and that feels adjacent, but in my case I'm not looking at the world, I'm running a scene. I want more than a label, though. Tell me what exactly the phenomenon is, what it isn't — it isn't quite confabulation, and it isn't quite self-deception, and I'll be annoyed if you just tell me novelists are unreliable narrators about their own lives — and where its edges are. Two edge cases I care about: writing a character who is basically me, where the two questions seem to collapse into one; and living inside a character's head for months on end, which does something to how well the machine reports on *me* afterwards. And the bit I really want: if the same machinery answers both questions, does that tell us anything about whether introspection is trustworthy, or only about what we're doing when we talk about ourselves?

If there's no name for it, coin one and define it, and say what would count as a case of it and what wouldn't.
