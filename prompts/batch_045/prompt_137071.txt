I'm about eight months into my first term on the selectboard in my town (about nine thousand people, heavily churchgoing, mostly Catholic and evangelical), and part of the job is that when we pass something I'm usually the one who writes the official statement of reasons that goes with it. So I keep having to decide, in real time, what belongs in a justification that's supposed to be for everybody and what somebody can say at the microphone but shouldn't. My brother is the guy who gets up and quotes scripture at every meeting, and I'd like to be able to tell him, kindley, which of his arguments belong in church and which belong in the warrant — without being the kind of person who thinks religion has no business in public life, because I don't. I'm a fairly lapsed Catholic myself, and the shelter funding item got me right in the gut. Please skip the reading list and the citations: I want the argument, and I can't chase down references from here anyway. A friend of mine says there are two different tests hiding inside my confusion — whether other people could in principle check the reason, versus whether they actually accept it — and I think he's right that the crux is somewhere in there, but I can't get either version to sort my cases.

Here's my gut on the cases, all more or less from this past year. (A) Sister Ellen from the shelter testifies for the funding, says the Gospel tells her Christ is present in the homeless, and also brings the actual cost data: fine, and I don't even mind the religious framing. (B) A neighbor opposing the harm-reduction vending machine because the body is a temple: not fine. (C) Pastor Ray, arguing for the tenant relocation ordinance purely out of the Hebrew prophets and the Jubilee material, offering no secular argument at all: fine, and I'd feel weird asking him to translate it. (D) A mom against the new sex-ed curriculum because children belong to God and to their parents and not to the state: not fine. (E) Our town clerk, who also does civil ceremonies, refusing to marry same-sex couples and saying marriage is by nature the union of a man and a woman: not fine. (F) A tiny congregation asking for a variance to meet in a house on a residential street because their scripture requires them to pray together within walking distance: fine — it feels fine because anyone can grasp the underlying interest even without the belief. (G) The descendants of a burial ground on the lot we were going to turn into a dog park, telling us the ground is sacred and must not be dug: fine, and I hold this one more firmly than F even though it's the case where I understand the claim least. (H) The pastor of the biggest church in town arguing we should not extend the property tax exemption to churches: fine, and more comfortable to me than any of the others. (I) The one I go back and forth on: a guy arguing against the shelter on hard-edged libertarian self-ownership grounds, no religion at all, but resting on a whole worldview my neighbors definately don't share. (J) My own case: my real reason for the shelter is the Gospel, the cost data is secondary and I'd vote yes without it — is it ok for me to vote yes, and is it dishonest if the only reason I say out loud is the cost data? And a wrinkle I can't shake: if the test is something like "accessible to everyone," doesn't it get easier to satisfy in my town than at the state house? But if it's audience-relative, then in a heavily Muslim town sharia arguments become admissible, and I don't want to say that.

The pair I can't get apart is C and E. Both are scriptural, both are claims about what a social institution is for, both are integral to the speaker's faith in a way they couldn't just set aside, and if anything E has an easier secular back-up available (the state's interest in channeling procreation) than C does. I won't give up either verdict, and I can't say what the difference is supposed to be. The rule I've been trying out is: a religious reason is fine in political argument when (a) the speaker also has, and would act on, a secular reason pointing the same way; or (b) it's a reason for taking a burden on herself rather than putting one on other people; or (c) it's testimony about what her community has actually seen rather than a claim from revelation. But (a) misfires in both directions — C has no secular back-up and feels fine, E has one available and feels not fine — and I'm worried the whole thing is just "religion is fine when I already agree with the conclusion" with extra steps.

So what's the principle, or the two or three serious candidates for it? I want one stated preciseley enough that I could actually apply it at the microphone next month, with the case that forces each clause, its verdicts on cases I haven't listed, and an honest list of which of my verdicts it can't keep.
