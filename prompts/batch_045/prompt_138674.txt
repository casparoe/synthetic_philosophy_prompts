I'm writing a novel set around 1815 and one of my characters is a radical lawyer type who's steeped in Bentham — the essay Bentham never published, the stuff about "irregularities" of the sexual appetite. He holds a position I've had to make as strong as I can, because hes cleverer than me and I don't want to win the scene by cheating. As I've got it worked out, it has three legs:

1. An act between consenting people that causes no assignable harm to anyone isn't the law's business, full stop. You don't get to invoke "the corruption of society" or "the foundations of morality," because that just smuggles in a non-utilitarian standard and then counts the smuggled goods as if they were pain.

2. The distress other people feel at the mere thought or knowledge of what's done in private doesn't count, or counts for much less, when it exists only because of a prejudice that utilitarianism itself shows to be baseless. The pain is real, but it's manufactured. Otherwise any bigotry that hurts when it's frustrated gets a veto over what the law does.

3. And anyway punishing it is pointless — the appetite isn't deterrable, so you're inflicting pain for no gain.

Tell me what would count against this. Specifically:

- Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which leg each one actually bites. I suspect a lot of them only hit leg 3, which is an empirical claim he could just give up and keep the rest — is that right, or does losing leg 3 actually damage the others?
- Which ones would a committed Benthamite concede, and which would they dismiss as a confusion? I don't want my other character scoring points off a strawman.
- Leg 2 feels like the load-bearing move and the one I'm least sure about. Does it have any non-ad-hoc stopping point? If you can discount a pain because it's caused by a false or groundless attitude, what stops you from discounting any pain whatever — and if nothing does, does the view swallow itself, or is it saved by the fact that these pains are also caused by something the law can in principle stop manufacturing?
- Some of the distress isn't just "I'm disgusted by your act" but "I have to live in a society where this is openly practiced, and it changes what's taught and what's askable." That looks harder to write off as bare prejudice. Is he entitled to discount it too?
- Where does he get the resources to say what counts as genuine consent? Servants, students, soldiers, people who'd starve. Without some paternalism the view seems to have no way to see coercion, and with it he's back to something he's supposed to be denying.
- He treats several different irregularities together that a modern reader would separate. Does the argument have the same shape for all of them, or does it come apart somewhere — and if it does, is that fatal or just awkward?
- What does the view predict that its rivals don't? I mean observable things — what would my character expect to see or not see in the world that someone who thinks a shared public morality is part of what holds a society together would not expect? I can dramatize predictions; I can't dramatize footnotes.
- Honestly assess whether the thing has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if it has, whether that's a problem for a view of this kind or just what a view of this kind looks like when it's in good shape.

Two constraints: I don't want citations or a reading list — I can't check any of it and it won't end up on the page. And if a defeater is genuinely decisive but only visible with hindsight, flag it separately, because I can always put it in the narration; what I really need is one or two things that could actually be said out loud in a drawing room without anyone reaching for a book.
