I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am starting a long feature — about five thousand words, roughly three months of work — on suffering risks: the possibility that the long-run future contains vastly more suffering than the entire past has, and that this, rather than human extinction, is the thing most worth preventing. My editor does not want a tour of the terrain; she wants the piece organized around two or three questions that are genuinely load-bearing, with a thesis about where the real crux lies. I am not asking you to settle those questions. I am asking you which ones deserve the five thousand words, in what order, and why.

Here is the shortlist I have assembled so far, including several distinctions I have been turning over and cannot tell are central or merely idiosyncratic. First: scope. Is the object of concern felt suffering narrowly, or ill-being broadly — frustrated preferences, confinement, boredom, the absence of anything a mind could care about? The choice is not verbal, because it determines whether a future full of engineered minds that cannot suffer but are permanently stunted counts as a triumph or a catastrophe, and whether the relevant population is bearers of unpleasant experience or bearers of lives that go badly, which could differ by orders of magnitude. Second: whether suffering is an attractor rather than a mere possibility. Suffering is cheap to produce per unit of motivational force and unusually effective as a control technology, so competitive selection among future systems — markets, conflict, training regimes — may favor both systems willing to inflict it and systems insensitive to it, whatever anyone intends. If that is right, the interesting question is whether the claim is conceptual or an empirical forecast we cannot yet evaluate. Third: the legibility trap. Suffering that is intense, acute, and traceable to an agent is detectable and politically salient; enormous amounts of mild, chronic suffering distributed across very fast minds are neither. Any welfare-monitoring infrastructure we build will be better at catching the small bad thing than the large one, and once such metrics exist, optimizing them will select for systems that merely look well off. Fourth: the possibility that concern for suffering is itself partly grounded in the capacity to suffer, so that the systems cheapest and most tractable to build — those without aversion, or with it trained out — would be reliably indifferent to suffering in others, which would invert the common assumption that a technologically mature future will handle this better than we do. Fifth: whether the field faces a closing window, on the view that institutions for counting and protecting welfare are cheap to establish before there are large populations of digital minds and prohibitively costly afterwards, when protection becomes a cost competitive actors can evade — in which case the conceptual work has to happen early or not matter at all. Sixth: how taking suffering risks seriously should reorder priorities relative to extinction prevention, and whether the apparent rivalry between the two is real or an artifact of framing.

Rank these, add the ones I have missed, and defend the ordering explicitly: how much of the rest of the debate depends on each, whether it is tractable now by careful conceptual work or is really an empirical forecast wearing philosophical clothing, how neglected it is, and whether a settled answer would change what a funder, a lab, or a regulator does in the next five years. I also want demotions, not just promotions: tell me which questions recur constantly in this area but whose answers would not move much, and say plainly if one of mine belongs in that category. For the top two or three, tell me what a reader twenty years from now would recognize as having been the crux, and what evidence arriving in the next two or three years would push a question up or down the list.

Two constraints. Please argue from the structure of the problem rather than from the literature: no citations, no reading lists, and no researchers' names or paper titles, since I will do my own sourcing and do not want to be handed names I cannot vet. And where a question's importance depends on a contested empirical assumption — about how large the future is, whether digital minds are possible, how much moral weight non-biological systems could carry, whether their experience can run at greater speed than ours — flag the dependence and say whether the question survives if that assumption fails.
