Last night I spent several hours arguing with a friend about the torture-versus-dust-specks case, and the argument kept circling in a way that suggests we were each relying on a different unstated principle about how small harms combine. I am not looking for a survey of positions, a reading list, or any information about who has defended which view. I want the argumentative structure of the case, reasoned out rather than cited. If you introduce a technical notion — expected utility, lexical priority, person-affecting versus impersonal aggregation, and so on — define it as you use it.

Here is where we stalled. She granted the continuum argument: if one dust speck in one eye has any disvalue at all, then for any finite badness there is some number of specks, distributed one to a customer, that exceeds it; and she accepted that the number in the standard version of the case (3^^^3, or any equally astronomical figure) is large enough that no threshold short of torture-sized can absorb it. But she then refused the conclusion, on the ground that harms are not fungible across the boundaries of persons: a billion mild irritations in a billion different nervous systems is not one great suffering, and the intuition that it is, she said, is just the additive formalism doing the work.

I think my reply to her is sound but I cannot tell how much it really establishes. It is that the case is irreducibly interpersonal in a way that cannot be checked against our intrapersonal intuitions, because a single human life has a finite capacity for discrete discomforts. Even at one speck per second for a century of waking life you get roughly three billion specks — a lifetime of continuous mild annoyance, which on any additive scale is nowhere near fifty years of torture. So the example's force comes entirely from multiplying across lives, and the familiar move of asking whether *you* would accept a speck to avoid a speck, iterated, never reaches the relevant magnitude. Does that undercut her non-fungibility reply, or does it just relocate it?

The second crux is where I most want help. In the expected-utility formalism a certainty of a tiny harm for an enormous number of people and an astronomically small chance of an enormous harm for one person are treated by the same operation, and yet the two seem to come apart in intuition: plenty of people who will pay to eliminate a one-in-a-billion chance of catastrophe shrug at the specks, and my friend has the reverse pattern. Is there a principled difference between aggregating over chance and aggregating over persons? My thought is that weighting a single agent's outcomes by their probabilities can be supported by something like a long-run or representation-theorem argument that has no analogue when we sum across distinct people, so that the two stand or fall separately — but I am not confident, and I would like to see the strongest case for treating them as one problem.

Finally, I would like the shape of the choice laid out. As I see it, avoiding the aggregative conclusion means denying that a speck has nonzero disvalue, denying that disvalues add across persons, or positing a priority structure on which severity to the worst-off outweighs any number of lesser harms. Please say what each of these costs elsewhere — in particular what denying interpersonal additivity does to ordinary policy reasoning that trades small burdens on many against large burdens on few — and whether the priority option has an answer to a continuum argument aimed at its own threshold. One variant I raised and could not settle: if the harms are scaled down until each is genuinely sub-threshold, so that no one ever notices anything, the sum remains enormous while the total experienced badness is arguably nil. Does the aggregationist have to accept badness that is never experienced, and is that a cost or a consequence?

Tell me which of these disagreements is the real one, what would settle it, and where — if anywhere — my friend and I were simply talking past each other. Aim for something I could hand to her: rigorous, but written as argument rather than as summary.
