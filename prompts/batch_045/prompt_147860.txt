I'm a novelist, mid-fifties, on my sixth book, and I've written more death scenes than I can count, and Ive finally got to write the one I've been avoiding, which is my own, more or less. Not autobiographical exactly, but close enough that I can't fake it.

The setup. Miriam is 61, a literary translator, and she's been given a prognosis in months. Her daughter Ada is 34 and builds bridges; actual structural engineer, talks about load paths at the dinner table, it drives Miriam up the wall. Night, Miriam's kitchen, the kettle's just boiled and neither of them wants tea.

Miriam has decided, and has started telling people, that her death is not bad for her. Ada thinks it obviously is, cannot get Miriam to budge, and is furious about it in a way she's ashamed of.

What I need is that argument, as a scene. 900 to 1300 words of dialogue.

Rules:

- Nobody names a philosopher, an ancient text, or an -ism. I've poked around the standard material on this and I don't want it name-checked in the scene. They're a translator and an engineer; they'd talk about it in their own words. Build the arguments from scratch in plain language, out of the stuff in the kitchen if you can.
- Both of them have to be good at this. Miriam is not cold, not in denial, and does not get to win by being serene. She has to actually run the two moves that got me stuck: the one about there being no time at which both she and her death exist, so nothing can happen to her; and the one about how this is supposed to differ from the enormous stretch of time before she was born, which nobody counts as a tragedy for her. And she needs an answer when Ada says the two cases don't look the same, whatever the honest version of that answer is: people have projects pointing forward, the before-birth stretch doesn't cut anything short, and so on. She should concede something real early on, too, because I think she would: the dying is bad, the fear is bad, leaving an unfinished sentence is bad. She just doesn't think the being-dead is.
- Ada has to be more than "but you'll miss things." She has to say what kind of badness this is, if it isn't a bad experience, and she has to answer the who-does-it-happen-to-and-when question properly. I want her to actually pick a position on that, bad at every earlier moment, or at no moment, or at the moments she'd otherwise have been alive for, and defend the one she picks rather than gesturing at all three.

Then the bit I actually care about, which is my own idea and which I want to see survive contact with someone who knows this stuff. Here it is.

Take two versions of Miriam's life. Same length, same total amount of good in them, roughly the same number of good days, just arranged differently. In one, the good is front-loaded and the last third is thin, a slow narrowing. In the other it runs the other way: a thin start, then a late long swell. Now cut both off at 61 with the same disease. The usual way of counting says the loss is the same size in both, because the loss is the size of the slab you didn't get. But it doesn't feel like the same size, and I don't think that's just sentiment. In the second version the death lands in the middle of something that was going somewhere, and the life is a worse shape at the point where it stops. A bridge that stops mid-span is a different object from one that stops at the abutment, even with the same tonnage of steel in both. I want Ada to use this against Miriam: the badness isn't a quantity of missing goods, it's where the cut falls in the arc, and Miriam's accounting can't see that.

And I want the converse in there too, because it's the part that ought to worry Miriam. If the shape is what matters, then a long decline can wreck the shape, and some deaths, not good ones exactly, improve the whole. Which is a hell of a thing to say to your mother. Let Miriam find that one on her own and see if she can use it without being cruel with it.

One more move, because it's the only one I've found that might get Ada out of the nobody-is-there-to-be-harmed problem. Suppose the death also makes the earlier stretch of the life worse. Not by changing what it felt like at the time, but in the way a joke is worse if the punchline never comes, or a setup is worse if it doesn't pay off. Then there is a subject who exists when the harm lands, namely the earlier Miriam, and the harm is happening to her while she's alive. I want Miriam to fight this properly. I think her best line is something about confusing a change in how you rate a whole with something happening to a part of it, and maybe about double-counting, since those good years are now being counted twice, once as good and once as spoiled. Give her the strongest version of that you can.

Ending: don't let them agree, and don't let anyone cry and be forgiven. I want them to end up knowing exactly which sentence they disagree about. Miriam is allowed one small sour joke.

After the scene, 150 to 250 words from you, addressed to me, on:

1. Where each of them got off easy, meaning the strongest reply the other one had available and didn't make.
2. Whether my arc point is actually a separate point or just the standard counting view with extra steps. If it collapses into something standard, say so plainly, and say which thing.
3. Anything in the setup that's confused. Please don't be polite here. Two writer friends have told me the whole thing is beautiful and neither could tell me whether the argument works, and I'd much rather hear that my premise is muddled and why than get a nicely written scene built on a mistake. If my two lives don't actually come out the same size on the standard way of counting, or if worse-for-her and worse-as-a-shape aren't the clean distinction I'm treating them as, tell me straight and tell me what to do with the scene instead.
