I run a fortnightly "quant club" lunch at work — me and about 11 other engineers, 60 minutes, conference room with a whiteboard and a projector I'd rather not use, everyone's eating so nothing that needs both hands. Most of them are comfortable with probability, but two or three have never seen Bayes' theorem written down with symbols in it.

I want to run a session on updating when you *didn't* cleanly observe anything. Like: you get a flaky alert, or you glance at a dashboard out of the corner of your eye, and there's no proposition you're now certain of — your confidence in "the alert actually fired" goes from 0.6 to 0.8 and that's the whole of your evidence. I tracked down the name for the rule that handles this (Jeffrey conditionalization? probability kinematics? I've seen both) and I get the formula, but I can't make it *land*. Every time I explain it, people say "sure, obviously," and then go right back to acting as if they'd seen the most likely thing for sure.

So: design me a game.

What I want them to run into, before anyone names anything:

1. That "pretend you saw the most likely piece of evidence and do a normal update on that" is a genuinely *different number* from the weighted-average rule, not a rounding error. When I messed around with numbers myself I got a gap of about 6 percentage points and it definately wasn't enough to change anyone's mind — so please pick the numbers (the prior, the likelihoods, and how far the blurry observation shifts things) so the gap is big enough to be uncomfortable, like 10+ points, and show me the arithmetic for both answers so I can check it.
2. That the weighted average is really just "spread over everything you might have seen, weighted by how much you now think you saw each of those things." I want them to construct that in the room, not be told it.
3. That the whole move depends on the conditional probabilities *staying put* while the probabilities of the observations move. I want a round where that breaks. My ideas: the reason you're unsure what you saw is itself informative (you're squinting because the room is full of smoke, and smoke means fire), or the alerting system auto-remediates when it fires, so the observation changes what it's evidence *of*. Whichever mechanism you use, I want participants to be able to see that the rule they just worked out gives a silly answer there, and to say which assumption snapped.

Constraints on the thing itself:

- Cards, dice, chips, sticky notes. No laptops, no spreadsheet, printable on one page, playable while holding a sandwich.
- Some real (small) incentive to be *accurate* and not merely confident. I was going to use Brier scoring with a coffee gift card for the winner, but I'm not sure Brier is the right choice for making people care about a 12-point difference — tell me if something else works better and why.
- At least one round where people commit to a number under time pressure, so there relying on gut rather than arithmetic, and then get scored on it.
- Nobody says "conditionalization," "Bayes," or the name of any dead philosopher until the last ten minutes, me included.

What I need in the writeup: setup and materials, the rounds in order with a timeline (60 minutes, and people trickle in late), what I put on the board and when, the debrief mapping specific moments in the room onto specific parts of the idea, and honestly — what will go wrong. My prediction is that one of the stronger engineers announces the formula in the first five minutes and deflates the whole thing; tell me how to structure the rounds so that either can't happen or doesn't matter.

Also be straight with me about what a card game can't do: if there's some part of the idea — why the "conditionals stay put" assumption is the *right* one in the well-behaved cases, or what's even going on when your evidence isn't a proposition you could state — that no game is going to get across and I'll just have to say out loud, say so, and give me the one-sentence version to say.
