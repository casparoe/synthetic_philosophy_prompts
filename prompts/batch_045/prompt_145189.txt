My flatmate Dev and I got into an argument about this over breakfast, then again at the pub, and I've since wrote it out properly and now it looks like it proves something insane. Background: there's a working paper somebody dropped into a Slack group I lurk in, about compromise as a source of gains from trade between people with different values, and its central move is what the author calls the split-surplus lemma. The idea is that because everyone's returns to pushing their own favoured project are concave — doubling what you spend on a cause less than doubles the good it does — Jensen's inequality means a certain half of a shared pool beats a fifty-fifty gamble at all of it, so even a purely selfish, purely risk-averse agent does better to split than to fight over who wins outright. I buy that bit, and I've actually used something like it in negotiations at work. Dev then built it out into this:

(1) Concavity is a fact about how value behaves in a shared resource, not about who is right.
(2) So the surplus from splitting is there whenever two people disagree, and it grows with how concave the returns are and how far apart the two are — not with which of them is more likely to be correct.
(3) Disagreeing with another person about what to do with a shared pool is structurally the same kind of problem as being不确定 which moral view is true: in both cases you're deciding how to spread a fixed budget of effort across claims you can't fully verify.
(4) So the same concavity reasoning tells you to hedge under moral uncertainty: spread your effort across the live moral views instead of piling it onto whichever one has the highest expected choice-worthiness.
(5) And since hedging is spreading rather than probability-tracking, the slice a view is owed doesn't shrink just because you're confident it's false — a view you'd put at 0.1% still gets a seat at the table, because what's doing the work is the concavity, not the credence. He calls the resulting allocation the hedge ratio.
(6) Therefore: (a) a sensible person keeps a standing slice of her life for every moral view anyone takes seriously, however sure she is that it's wrong; (b) she never takes a high-variance moral bet, however large its expected payoff — no betting the farm on a long-shot intervention, no career gambles on speculative causes; (c) she never refuses to compromise with anybody, including people who want to do things she thinks are straightforwardly monstrous, since there's always some split that both sides prefer to open conflict.

(6) is obviously mad. I am not going to spend a fixed slice of my twenties honouring a moral view I think is garbage, and I'm not going to offer someone a compromise on something I think is wrong just because he'll take it. But I'd much rather hold onto (1) and (2) than give them up, (3) felt completely natural when Dev said it out loud, and (4) really does look like just (3) applied — hedging a bit under moral uncertainty seems like plain common sense to me. So: which step do you reject, why does it look fine when it isn't, and what does rejecting it cost? Do I actually have to choose between "compromise is often a genuine gain" and "don't give a standing slice of your life to views you're certain are false", or is there a version of the split-surplus lemma that survives?
