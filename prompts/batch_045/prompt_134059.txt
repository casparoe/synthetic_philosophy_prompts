hey — I run a fortnightly "no slides" session for about 12 engineers on my team (Thursdays, 90 minutes, everyone's already tired and slightly hostile, and we're a distributed systems group so anything that rhymes with quorum, consensus or bootstrap will land fast if you use it as a handle). The topic I want to do next is the thing where a system's rules include the rules for changing its own rules — someone told me there's a whole book about a game like this — and I have ran a sloppy 20-minute version of it with friends where it was fun but degenerated into pure trolling within about five minutes. What I actually want them to *feel* is something more specific that I can't quite name: that in a system that can amend itself there is always some part that can't be amended from inside, but that where that part sits is a choice and not a fact about the rules, and that the genuinely powerful move is usually to amend the thing that nobody ever classified as a rule at all — the score sheet, what counts as a turn, the fact that we're sitting in a circle. Can you design me a runnable session around that?

Constraints: 12ish people, 90 minutes, one whiteboard, index cards, no money or prizes, and no lecture up front — I don't want to say the words "self-reference" or "meta" until the debrief. My engineers will immediately try to break the game, so I'd rather the rules and payoffs make breaking it feel like winning than spend the session fighting them. Two moments I'd like engineered in if you can manage it: (1) at some point they should generate a proposal whose legality depends on its own outcome — my naive idea was a rule like "any amendment that passes by exactly one vote is void" and then it passes by exactly one vote, but do something better if you've got one — and I want them to notice the difference between that and a proposal that's merely unpopular or blocked, because I think the blocked-vs-undecidable distinction is the actual payload and I care more about it than about the paradox itself being cute; and (2) there should be something in the room that I introduce as "not part of the game, just how we're keeping score" that turns out to be the thing worth capturing, so the discovery is that the inviolate level was the unclassified infrastructure and not the document.
