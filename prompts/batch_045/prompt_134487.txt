Hey — I got drafted onto the board of a tiny family foundation last spring (the Halloway Fund, about $6m, we make one or two grants a year), and I've ended up way deeper into discounting the future than I ever expected. We've got two proposals on the table and I need to figure out whether I'm being reasoned with or just talked at.

Option A is the boring one: pay out roughly 3.5% a year forever to a local adult-literacy nonprofit, keep the principal whole in real terms. Option B is our youngest trustee's: let the whole thing compound for a hundred years and then make one enormous grant — at 3% real that's something like 19x today's money, so call it $110m of grantmaking power in 2125 instead of $200k a year now.

Our board chair, Marilyn, keeps killing option B with the same analogy: a foundation is just a household. A household doesn't eat its seed corn; a household is *allowed* to care more about its own kids than about great-grandkids it'll never meet; and a household knows that a dollar a hundred years out is worth a small fraction of a dollar today. So: perpetual payout, no century fund, and — her actual line — "we owe the future the interest, not the principal." The analogy is doing all the work: it's what licenses both the low payout and the idea that a beneficiary in 2125 counts for less.

Dev, who pushes option B, says the household frame *is* the disease. His version: distance in time isn't a moral reason for caring less, exactly as distance in space isn't. If you wouldn't discount a kid in another country because she's far away, you can't discount a kid in 2125 because she's far off. So pure time preference is zero, and the only thing that should move the number is the chance that nobody's there to recieve the money.

I think both of them are being sloppy and I can't say exactly how. A few things I keep circling on and want you to deal with:

The return is in dollars, but the thing we care about isn't. If the world keeps getting richer, a future dollar buys less welfare, so what we should be discounting might be welfare, not dollars — and once you do that it's not obvious which direction option B moves in. (Also: at 3% growth and 3% discount, isn't the century grant exactly as big as today's, in the only units that matter?)

We can't bind the 2125 board. "Saving for the future" is really a bet that whoever is in the room in 2125 will spend it well, and our own governing document will be read by people who don't know us. A household sort of has this problem too, but it feels different in degree.

Every year's board faces this same choice with the same reasons, so "compound now, give later" looks like a plan that nobody ever actually executes — unless we lock it in, which is the handoff problem again.

Some of the things we could fund now have a deadline: there's a tract of land that'll be subdivided within about five years, and once it's gone it's gone. Other things we'd know much more about in twenty years. I don't know whether that's an argument about time preference at all or just about which options expire.

And someone at a conference told me that if you're genuinely unsure what rate applies over 200 years, the honest thing is to discount the far future at roughly the lowest rate you can't rule out. That sounded like a party trick. Is it?

What I'd like, in this order:

**The mapping** (~250 words): a table of what corresponds to what in Marilyn's household picture — principal, kids, the mortgage rate, retirement, death, inheritance, "seed corn" — and what each of those is supposed to be in our case.

**Where it holds** (~200 words): the parts of the household comparison that genuinely carry over to a perpetual institution.

**Where it breaks** (~450 words): the disanalogies that matter for the decision in front of us — infinite horizon, impersonal vs. partial beneficiaries, dollars vs. welfare, growth, extinction and institutional risk, uncertainty about the rate itself — and be explicit about which ones are decisive and which are noise.

**What the analogy smuggles in** (~250 words): which features of a household the foundation doesn't have, and which of those features are quietly supplying the conclusion. I suspect "someone has to eat next year" is doing a lot of hidden work.

**A better comparison** (~200 words): if the household is the wrong object, what should we be comparing a perpetual foundation to — a dynasty, a trust, a nation, an ecosystem, something else — and does that comparison point anywhere different?

**What I should tell the board** (~300 words): once the audit is done, is there any pure time preference left over after growth, risk and uncertainty are subtracted out, and is whatever's left a moral judgment or just a fact about us? Then: A, B, or something else, and what I'd have to believe to change my mind.
