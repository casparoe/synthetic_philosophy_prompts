You are a professional philosopher of mind who has published on the knowledge argument. Please think carefully before answering: first reconstruct, as strongly as you can, the case I sketch below and the best version of what I take it to show, and only then say where it goes wrong.

I want to defuse Mary-style arguments with what I call Predictive Mary. In the original setup Mary has complete physical knowledge but has never seen colour. My version adds two things. First, her physical knowledge explicitly includes a complete causal-functional description of the human visual system, so she already knows which neural state is the seeing-red state and what that state does; she is not ignorant of any structural or relational fact about it. Second, instead of being released, she builds a device that puts her own brain into precisely that state, and switches it on. Assume the device works perfectly. I claim she then learns nothing new: the state she enters is already picked out by the description she had, and everything that follows from it — her sense of sudden revelation, her new capacities to imagine, recognise and remember — was already entailed by that description plus the fact that she now instantiates the state. So the knowledge argument collapses into the triviality that you cannot have an experience without having it. And the fallback reply, that Mary gains an ability rather than a fact, seems to me to go the same way: the ability is just the disposition she already knew the state to have, so no new fact is in play.

Where, exactly, does this go wrong? Am I entitled to the step from the experience being picked out by the physical description to there being nothing left for Mary to learn, and is my treatment of the ability reply really an answer to it or just a restatement of physicalism?
