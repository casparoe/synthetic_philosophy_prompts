I'm auditing a graduate seminar on relational conceptions of personhood, and one proverb is doing all the argumentative work in the room: the saying usually rendered in English as "a person is a person through other persons," sometimes "I am because we are," the line people reach for when they want to invoke Ubuntu. In a single session it was used twice, in opposite directions, and each speaker treated it as though it obviously settled things their way.

The case on the table was a woman with advanced dementia who no longer recognizes anyone and whose family has stopped visiting. The question was whether to withdraw an expensive intervention and move her to less costly care. The first speaker used the proverb to argue that her personhood has thinned as the relations that sustained it fell away: less is owed to her now than was owed before, and the resources may properly go elsewhere. The second used the same proverb to argue that our obligations are at their strongest exactly here, where she cannot reciprocate, hold up her end, or answer for herself, and that the point of the saying is that the relation must now be carried entirely from our side.

What I want is a disambiguation rather than a verdict on the case. Please lay out the distinct precise theses that sentence could be expressing, as a menu ordered from the weakest to the strongest, and for each one tell me:

- what work the word "through" is doing: causal enabling, metaphysical constitution, epistemic access to another mind, or the conferring of a normative status;
- whether it makes personhood binary or a matter of degree, and, if scalar, whether what is owed to someone scales with it;
- whose relations are supposed to do the constituting, and whether they must be actual or may be merely counterfactual or available — in particular what each reading implies when relations are absent through no fault of the individual, when they are absent because an unjust community withholds recognition from her, and when they are absent because she is now incapable of reciprocating;
- whether the relation must run both ways, or whether a one-directional relation, mine to her and unreturned, is enough to do the work;
- whether the other relata must themselves be persons, and what follows if the only relations left to someone are with a companion animal, with the dead, or with people who never knew her;
- whether "person" is a status one has or an achievement one becomes and, on the achievement reading, who is at fault when the achievement fails;
- and which of the two speakers' uses the reading would actually support, or neither.

Then sort the menu: which readings are true but too weak to settle anything, which are substantive and defensible, which are substantive but doubtful, and which are straightforwardly false. I especially want an honest answer on whether any defensible reading gets the first speaker his conclusion, and whether the readings that do get him there carry costs elsewhere that we should refuse to pay — for instance, that an unjust community could strip someone of personhood simply by refusing her recognition. Finally, give me your sense of which reading people normally have in mind when they repeat the proverb, since I suspect it is not the one that would carry an argument.

Please don't rest anything on scholarly attribution or on who has said what about the tradition; I'm interested in the conceptual space the sentence opens up, not in a reading list.
