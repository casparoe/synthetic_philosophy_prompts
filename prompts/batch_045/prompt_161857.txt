I am a novelist, and philosophy keeps finding its way into my books whether I intend it or not. The manuscript I am finishing turns on a decision made in its third section. My protagonist, Verity, has spent nineteen years reconstructing the surviving fragments of a forgotten composer's last opera; the work is, in the plainest sense, what her life has been about. She is offered the directorship of a logistics charity where, on the best estimates she can get, the same decade of her labour would prevent something like two hundred deaths. She refuses. Her brother, the novel's effective altruist, tells her that the arithmetic settles it and that staying with the opera is self-indulgence. I do not want the scene to be a stalking horse for a view I have half understood, so I read a recent popular book on ethics, which summarised the relevant part of Bernard Williams like this:

"Williams's objection from integrity is the objection that utilitarianism requires each of us to treat our own deepest commitments — the projects that make a life worth living — as one more entry in the global ledger of happiness, to be set aside whenever the sum would be better served elsewhere. Because such commitments are what give a person her identity, that demand alienates her from her own agency; and integrity, in the sense of sticking to your principles whatever it costs you, matters more than a marginal gain in aggregate welfare. The famous 'one thought too many' argument makes the same point from inside the agent's head. Since a utilitarian has to work out which act maximises welfare before she can act, a man who pulls his wife from a burning house rather than a stranger must think, first, that it is his wife, and then, as a separate step, that saving her is permitted by impartial morality; and the second thought cancels the love the first expressed, so the relationship is destroyed the moment morality is consulted. Williams's conclusion was that consequentialism is refuted — it cannot so much as permit us to favour the people we love — and that the moral life has to be rebuilt on personal attachments and partial concerns rather than on any impartial standpoint."

This is close to what Verity believes, and I would like it to be true, which makes me suspicious of it; but I cannot locate the mistake myself, and I have no way to check the sources.

Would you do the following?

Go through the passage sentence by sentence. For each claim, say what someone sympathetic to Williams would accept, what would make such a reader wince, and what is simply wrong — and, where it matters, what a defender of utilitarianism would say back.

I have a feeling the passage runs together two things Williams keeps separate: the objection from integrity and the one-thought-too-many point. If that is right, what exactly is the difference between them? Do they target the same theory, or the same feature of a theory? Does either depend on the other, and do they fail or survive together?

Then the scene itself. On the corrected version of the objection, does it vindicate Verity's refusal — give her something she could honestly say to her brother — or does it only explain and redescribe a refusal she was always going to make? I want to know whether the objection functions as a permission, as a complaint about what the theory demands of her, or as something else again. If it is a complaint rather than a permission, tell me what Verity can and cannot claim without misusing Williams.

A second character, minor but load-bearing, is a man whose deepest commitment — the project around which his whole adult life is organised — is a collection built on other people's ruin. He too refuses to be moved by the numbers. Does whatever Williams's account gives to Verity extend to him, or does the account have the resources to withhold it? I ask because if integrity turns out to be indifferent to the content of the projects, I need to rewrite him; if it is not indifferent, I need to know what does the work.

Finally: is there a difference between what the objection shows about what Verity may do and what it shows about what she must be thinking while she does it? The novel is written largely from inside her head, and the one-thought-too-many point looks to me like a claim about deliberation rather than about action, but I am not confident I have that contrast right, or that I know which reading the passage is implicitly relying on.

End with a single line beginning "Verdict:" that gives one of three labels — Substantially fair, Partly fair, Misleading — followed by a clause naming the single correction that matters most for how I write the scene.
