Hi — I need something I can circulate before our meeting next Tuesday. I sit on the Neuroprognosis and Life-Sustaining Treatment committee at my hospital (I'm the anesthesiology rep; the rest are two neurologists, palliative care, a neuropsychologist, a lawyer and a staff ethicist), and I've been auditing an evening seminar in philosophy of mind that has completely messed with my head, which is why I'd rather get this right then just sign off on whatever the device rep told us over lunch.

The concrete thing: we've been offered the chance to be an early site for a bedside consciousness monitor. It spits out two numbers. The first, the company calls the broadcast index: it perturbs the cortex with a magnetic pulse and scores whether the response dies where it started or spreads into a sustained, long-range frontoparietal pattern and lasts a while — basically the global-workspace picture turned into a number, where a high score means content got taken up and made available to lots of downstream systems (attention, report, memory, deliberate action) and a low score means it stayed local. The second is a local recurrence index, meant to pick up structured activity that persists in sensory and association areas without ever going global; in patients it's still basically a research output. We have a live case forcing the question: a 41-year-old man, eight months out from a hypoxic injury, eyes open, sleep-wake cycles, no reliable command following at the bedside, but a research fMRI last month showed what looked like reliable differential activity when he was asked to imagine playing tennis versus walking through his house. His broadcast index has been low on all four recordings; the local recurrence index came back high on two of them and nobody on the team can tell us what to make of that. His wife wants everything continued and says she'd accept withdrawal only if she were convinced he isn't experiencing anything at all. Two of our neurology people think low broadcast plus the behavioral picture is enough and that we're hurting him; palliative care wants us to say the device just isn't ready and go back to clinical judgment.

So: draft the committee's decision memo. I want it to recommend an actual policy — what role, if any, this monitor should play in recommendations about withdrawing life-sustaining treatment, with conditions attached (how many recordings, over what window, what corroboration, who can order it, what we tell families, how we document it), a timeline, and a rule for discordant cases like his where broadcast is low and local recurrence isn't. The bit I keep going in circles on, and that I'd like the memo to really dig into rather than wave away: even granting the global-workspace story, a broadcast score looks like it tracks availability to downstream consumer systems, and that isn't the same thing as there being something it's like for him. There are everyday cases where experience seems to run ahead of what gets taken up and used — you're briefly shown a grid of letters and it seems to you as though you saw the whole grid, but you can only name a few of them, and which ones you can name shifts depending on a cue that comes after the grid is gone. If that's anywhere near the right picture, a device built to detect ignition could read negative in a patient who is nonetheless having experiences, maybe experiences stuck in islands that never get broadcast at all — which is precisely the patient we'd least want to get wrong. But please take the other side seriously too, because the neurologists will say it and I half believe it: we already withdraw on bedside behavioral exams that are far crappier and that miss responsive patients all the time; a strict line about possible islands would mean treating every one of these patients effectively indefinitely; and continuing is its own kind of harm, not a neutral default we can hide behind. Please don't land on more research is needed — tell us what to do on Tuesday, and give reasons the people who disagree could actually answer.
