I teach a 300-level seminar on classical Indian philosophy and I'm writing the take-home final right now. We've just finished three weeks on Nāgārjuna — the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, the two truths, emptiness as the emptiness of svabhāva (intrinsic nature, own-being, independent essence, whatever you want to call it), and the four-cornered negation, the catuṣkoṭi: a thing is F, is not F, is both F and not F, is neither F nor not F, and all four corners get rejected. My problem is that every question I draft gets answered, in every past paper I could find, by writing emptiness-is-dependent-origination in big letters and then listing the four corners with a paragraph of mood music about the Middle Way. I want something that can't be done that way.

What got me thinking: a friend from the logic group sat in on the last class and afterwards said the tetralemma looks either redundant or just plain contradictory, and I couldnt give him a clean answer. His point was roughly that if corner one (x is F) and corner two (x is not F) are genuine contradictories, then corner three is false as a matter of pure logic and corner four is false as a matter of pure logic, so including them adds nothing — he could have rejected two corners and been done — and rejecting all four is just rejecting one and two, which is flatly inconsistent. So either Nāgārjuna is denying non-contradiction, or the four corners aren't really scoped over the same proposition, or the whole exercise isn't about truth-values at all. I'd like the exam question to put that fork to the students instead of to me.

Two other things I want it to do. First, force the scope question: make them run the tetralemma on something completely mundane — did the bus already leave, is the cat on the mat, did I lock the back door — and say whether all four corners get rejected there as well. If yes, they owe me an account of why this isn't just global skepticism with incense; if no, they owe me a principled statement of what marks the difference between the cases where the fourfold negation applies and the cases where it doesn't. Second, the regress worry that comes up in section every year: if none of the four is itself a position, isn't Nāgārjuna asserting something, and if he says he asserts nothing, isn't that the assertion? A good answer should have to do something with the idea that emptiness is itself empty rather than just changing the subject, and a weak answer that changes the subject should lose marks for it. One of my students has poked around in the Vigrahavyāvartanī, the text where he answers objectors one by one, so a reading that leans on the I-have-no-thesis-of-my-own move is fair game, but I don't want it to be required.

What I'd like from you:

- the question itself, as it would appear on the paper: one question, answerable in about 90 minutes or 1,200 words, phrased so students have to take a stand and defend a reading rather than discuss. Assume they have the root verses in front of them and no secondary literature — the question should be answerable and the answer gradeable without anyone citing anything, so please don't build in dependence on the Prāsaṅgika/Svātantrika split or on Tibetan doxography; a coupel of my strongest students have never heard those words and I don't want the weaker ones to have an excuse.
- define any Sanskrit term the first time it appears in the question (svabhāva, catuṣkoṭi, saṃvṛti and paramārtha if you use them).
- a model answer of roughly 800 to 1,000 words.
- a marking scheme out of 20, built around five must-hit points, with the marks for each and one line on how students typically get each one wrong.
- a short paragraph on what separates an A from a B: the specific moves an A makes that a B doesn't. And tell me what a C looks like. I strongly suspect that Nāgārjuna is pointing beyond logic, the tetralemma is really a meditative device, or emptiness is a mystery will show up, and I want to know whether that should cost marks and how to say so on the paper without sounding like I'm begging the question.

One last constraint: don't let the model answer get away with announcing that emptiness isn't nothingness and isn't a void. All of them say that, it's true, and it's worth about one mark.
