hey — I'm on the allocations committee of a small regranting fund (about $3M a year, five of us, we meet Thursdays) and our last decision has turned into an argument about decision theory instead of about grants, so I need a memo I can circulate to the other four before Thursday.

Two finalists left. A is boring and robust: a cash-transfer scale-up with actual RCT backing, call it 0.24 in the units B's spreadsheet uses, error bars you could drive a truck through but at least they're honest error bars. B wants $600k for an advocacy campaign aimed at a standards-setting process next year, and their entire case is one EV number.

Here's the bit I need you to actually engage, because it's the crux. B's model bins the outcome space by n = 1,2,3,...: bin n has probability 2^-n and payoff of size 2^n/n, with the sign alternating. The story is that the bigger the campaign's win, the more visible it is, and the visibility provokes a counter-mobilisation that scales with the win — so the big-upside bins are interleaved with comparably-scaled big-downside bins. Summed in descending probability, which is their ordering, the expected value is 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - ... = ln 2, so their headline number is +0.69. Our volunteer quant re-ran it: two good bins per bad bin gives about 1.04, one good per two bad gives about 0.35, and taking the positive bins first runs off to +infinity. Meanwhile the positive part and the negative part each diverge on their own, so this isn't an expectation we're uncertain about — it's the Pasadena game, there's nothing there to be uncertain about. Their defence is that descending probability isn't an arbitrary convention, it's the ordering their own model hands you, and everyone else is just relabelling bins.

What I want is a memo, 700-900 words, that (1) gives a bottom line — fund A and drop B, fund both, or fund B under conditions, with a timeline — (2) explains it in terms a smart non-mathy board member can follow without me at a whiteboard, and (3) gives the strongest version of whichever side loses rather than a strawman. The steelman I can't answer is this: at every finite truncation of that series, in the ordering they chose, B beats A — the partial sums dip to 0.5 at worst against A's 0.24 — and it beats A by more the further out you go, so rejecting B looks exactly like refusing to take a fat tail seriously at the point where all the value is supposed to be. If the memo's answer is just "the expectation doesn't exist, so ignore the project," I already get that from the resident skeptic and it doesn't tell us what to do with the money. Same for "it's Pascal's mugging with extra steps, reject the whole application class" — I don't want that either.

Two more things please. First, a standing rule for the fund we could actually apply next cycle — something a reviewer can run on a spreadsheet, not a seminar — for what to do when an applicant's case rests on a quantity that isn't defined. My own hack is to make applicants report the positive and negative parts separately, and when both blow up, rank by truncated expectation across a fixed range of horizons and require the ranking to be stable across them; tell me if that collapses, and where. Second, a short closing section headed something like "what would change our mind": the specific further analysis that would flip the recommendation, and where you think the memo's own reasoning is most likely to be wrong. Don't cite anything — none of us can check it and it won't help. thx
