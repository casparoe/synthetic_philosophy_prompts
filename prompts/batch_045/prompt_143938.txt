My reading group — a bunch of LessWrong-adjacent people who meet every other Sunday in somebody's basement — spent last session on Russell's theory of descriptions (you know, the present King of France stuff, existential import, the scope distinction between primary and secondary occurrences), and we ended up arguing about what Russell himself would say if you dropped him into a case he never saw. So I want to settle it by appeal to the man himself rather than by us shouting at each other.

Context, because it matters: a friend of mine does contract law and has recently been hired to sanity-check "smart wills" — bequests written as self-executing code. She keeps hitting clauses where the thing a definite description picks out is duplicated, missing, or specified in a way that isn't straightforwardly checkable against the world, and she asked me (the groups token philosophy person) what Russell would rule. Two of her cases, lightly anonymised:

(1) A clause leaving "the remainder of my estate to my only surviving sibling" — where the testator in fact had two sisters still living, neither of whom knew about the other. And a variant where the description fits nobody, but the testator very obviously had one specific person in mind and just described her wrong (she was actually a cousin, raised as a sister). Would Russell treat those two the same? Relatedly: he held that ordinary proper names are really disguised descriptions, so what does he do with a bequest to "my nephew John Smith" when there are two nephews by that name — or none at all?

(2) A bequest to "the person my late husband would have chosen to run the foundation," where no such choice was ever made or recorded. The satisfier is a counterfactual fact about a dead person's mind — and in 2025 her clients are starting to ask whether a fine-grained simulation of the husband could be used to settle it. Is that a new problem for his theory, or just a case where the truth value is determinate but practically unknowable? Would he have thought that difference was doing any work?

What I actually want: for each case, say what you think Russell would rule, why, and where his own machinery forces him into an ad hoc move or a revision. Assume he's been told the empirical facts but has read nothing in philosophy of language published after his death — no causal theories of reference, no modern semantics of context-sensitivity, none of that; he has to reason from what he actually had (existence plus uniqueness, scope distinctions, logically proper names and demonstratives, whatever he'd say about what's meant versus what's said). Also: would he even think this is a semantic problem, or would he say the real issue is legal drafting and policy — i.e. draw a line between what the clause literally says and what the law ought to do with it? And which of the cases do you think would genuinely make him most uncomfortable, as opposed to being something he already had the tools for?

I dont need citations or reading suggestions — half the point is to see how you handle a case Russell never saw, so please just reason it out rather than telling me who to go read. If it helps, imagine he's mildly annoyed about being teleported into 2025 and treats the whole thing as a puzzle to be solved with the tools in his pocket.
