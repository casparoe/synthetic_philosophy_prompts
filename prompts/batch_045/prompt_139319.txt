You are a comparative constitutional scholar and political theorist working on multilevel governance, with real command of the American, German, Swiss and EU systems. I would like you to answer in stages rather than in one pass. First, brainstorm the candidate concepts a specialist would reach for on hearing what follows — from federal theory, from EU studies, from public administration, from the literature on intergovernmental finance. Second, test each candidate against the three instances I give you, stating explicitly where it fits and where it strains. Third, tell me whether one of them is the concept, or whether the thing I am describing falls between them. If nothing established fits, coin a term: give me a one-sentence definition, a pair of near-identical cases that fall on opposite sides of the boundary, and a note on what the term should *not* be used for. I do not want a reading list, and I will not be satisfied with a bare label — the carving is the whole point. Please also correct any institutional background I have got wrong.

The background, as I understand it: in the EU the Union is supposed to act only so far as the objectives cannot be sufficiently achieved by the member states; in Germany the federation may use one of the shared legislative competences only where a federal rule is shown to be necessary, and the constitutional court is supposed to police that showing; Switzerland has a clause, as I remember it, telling the Confederation to take on only what the cantons cannot manage or what needs uniform regulation; the United States has no such clause and instead seems to work through money attached to grants, plus a rule that the centre may not simply conscript state officials. I work in the legal office of a subnational government, and what I keep watching is this: the same formally allocated power is worth wildly different amounts depending on which unit holds it, and the difference is not mainly money.

Three instances.

(1) Two units hold formally identical competence over, say, hospital planning. Unit A has a drafting office of about forty lawyers; unit B has four. A legislates continuously. B does one of three things in practice: it copies A's statute more or less wholesale — after which the comparative tables record that B has its own law; or it simply leaves the central framework provision standing unmodified, year after year; or it asks a central ministry for a model text. No competence has been transferred and the allocation rule has not changed, yet when the central framework lapses, A has a replacement in force within weeks while B formally requests that the central rule remain in place until its administration is in a position to legislate — and the extension is renewed twice. What differs is not legal authority but the capacity to originate a rule and then defend it once someone challenges it.

(2) When units lose a competence, they are compensated with a seat in the joint body that makes the central rule. But the low-capacity delegations cannot independently evaluate the central impact assessment or its several-hundred-page annex. They rely on the centre's own summary, or on whichever high-capacity unit's ministry has already costed the options, and they end up trading their votes for transition periods and side payments. The substitute for self-government is thus itself unequally available: the participation right is worth far more to the unit that can use it.

(3) Reversals run the same way. Where competences are formally returned, or where a scheme lets units opt into additional powers by meeting benchmarks, uptake tracks the same ranking every time. The units that decline are not refusing autonomy — they are declining an administrative burden they cannot carry. So the familiar claim that competences ratchet upward turns out to be true in one part of a federation and false in another, which is a worse situation than either the ratchet story or its denial describes.

My colleagues wave this off in two ways, and I think both are wrong but cannot say why. The first is that it is just the familiar fact that some regions are richer and some poorer; but equalisation transfers do not touch it, and the unit I have in mind is not poor, only small. The second is that it is just what everyone already discusses under the heading of units having different powers; but here the powers are formally identical, and that is precisely what makes the pattern invisible in the usual comparative tables.

So: does this configuration have a name of its own? How does it differ from each of the nearby things — unequal resources, formally differentiated status for particular units, the arrangement in which subnational bodies administer central law, the well-known claim that joint intergovernmental decision-making is biased toward the status quo, and the "earned autonomy" or benchmark-based schemes that are meant to remedy it? Is what I am describing a defect in the allocation principle itself, or only in the way the "cannot be sufficiently achieved" test gets applied — and why do the obvious remedies each seem to make it worse in a different way (transfers that the strong units can simply buy their way out of, capacity-building designed and supervised centrally, procedures that require a unit to petition the centre to be trusted)? Finally, is this specific to multilevel constitutional systems, or does the same structure appear wherever a formal power is granted uniformly to agents of unequal capacity?
