Hey — I write a science-and-ideas column for a weekend magazine, I'm filing tomorrow morning, and I've backed myself into a corner in the last third of a piece and need someone to find the loose board before I make a fool of myself in print. Please don't send me reading lists or citations or "as so-and-so argues in..." — I can't chase any of it before deadline and I'd rather you just reason from the case itself.

The setup is the old desert hitchhiker case, more or less as everyone runs it: stranded hitchhiker, a driver stops and says she'll drive him to town if he promises to pay her $1000 once they get there. Stipulate there's no repeat business, no reputation, no court, no phone signal, nothing he could use to bind himself — he's flying out of the country that night. Once they're in town the ride is already given, so handing over the $1000 buys him nothing, and if the driver can foresee that, she never stops for him. So he does better being the sort of person who pays. That's the fun bit my readers are paying for. Here's where I lose the plot, because my version of the argument keeps going and ends somewhere insane:

(1) The only thing that gets him into the car at the roadside is being someone the driver predicts will pay in town.
(2) The driver is a near-perfect reader of people: twenty years on that road, she's never been stiffed by somebody she misread. Acting won't do it, and neither will self-deception. The disposition has to be real.
(3) So before the ride, he has a decisive self-interested reason to acquire a real disposition to pay — even though, once in town, paying serves no purpose at all. Rational irrationality, whatever you want to call it.
(4) But the disposition has to be real in the strong sense that he can't switch it off once it stops paying. If it's the sort of thing he could set aside on reflection, the driver reads that in his face at the roadside and he stays in the desert. So what rationality tells him to acquire is not a conditional habit, it's a disposition to keep promises as such.
(5) Nothing in (1)–(4) turns on deserts, or money, or promises. Any case where your later self will be tempted to defect on someone who can read you now has this shape. So rationality tells you, case after case, to become the kind of agent who doesn't recalculate: to stop acting for reasons and act from unconditional dispositions instead.
(6) And it runs for belief too: if being visibly convinced of something gets you rides and halfheartedness won't pass the driver, then rationality tells you to acquire beliefs held regardless of the evidence.
(7) A standard of correctness that, whenever you consult it, tells you to stop consulting it, is incoherent. So something in (1)–(6) is broken.

The thing eating my afternoon: a guy I interviewed who builds reputation systems for gig platforms said the whole worry is avoidable, the driver doesn't need him to be irrational, she just needs a public ledger of who's paid before and plain self-interest does the rest. But the ledger only binds him if, in town, he cares about his next ledger entry — and on my stipulations he doesn't. Unless caring about his ledger entry is itself one of the dispositions he picked up at the roadside, which just drags us back through (4). So either the ledger does no work, or it works by making (4) true. I don't like either.

Take a position: which single step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what do I pay for rejecting each of the other live candidates? I'd most like to keep (1), (2) and (3) — those are the piece. If you make me give one up, tell me what the column then says instead. And don't hedge with "it depends on your theory of reasons" or hand me a menu; commit to one, say how confident you are, and say what would change your mind.
