I'm assembling the handouts for a graduate seminar on classical American pragmatism, and for the week on truth I want something the secondary literature keeps failing to give me: a staged dialogue between a Peircean and a Jamesian in which the two accounts are put under pressure by cases rather than summarized. Around 1,200 words, no quotations from Peirce or James and no name-dropping of their works or dates — I want the positions to come out in what the characters say to each other, not in scaffolding.

Give it a concrete stage. Two philosophers, Iris (the Peircean) and Wes (the Jamesian), sit on a research-council committee late in the evening deciding whether to renew funding for a long-baseline ecological monitoring project: a forest plot instrumented three decades ago and scheduled to run for three centuries, whose central hypothesis — about how the whole community reorganizes after the arrival of a particular pathogen — cannot be assessed with less than about three hundred years of data. The renewal is genuinely contested: the money could go to projects whose payoff falls inside a living researcher's career. The philosophical question is forced on them by the vote: what is it for that hypothesis to be true now, given that nobody alive will be in a position to settle it?

Run the argument through three cases, in this order, rather than letting it stay at the level of slogans. First, the orphaned inquiry: imagine the project is defunded next year and never resumed, the plots are ploughed under, and the question is never reopened. Is the hypothesis true? Second, the unasked question: a perfectly determinate claim nobody will ever have any reason to investigate — say, the number of barnacles on a wreck lying in a trench no expedition will ever visit. Third, the vocabulary case: suppose inquiry into the forest question does converge, but the inquirers who converge two centuries hence use a descriptive vocabulary and a taxonomy our committee would not recognize; in what sense is the opinion they reach an answer to our question?

Both characters should be equally good, and each should be made to face the strongest version of what the other says, responding to the actual point rather than to a paraphrase of their own position. Iris must concede, before Wes presses it out of her, that truth as the ideal limit of inquiry is not a decision procedure and cannot tell the committee what to do on Tuesday — the distinction between the aim of inquiry and a usable test for truth is something she owns, not something she is caught out by — and that fallibilism means a hypothesis can be fated for the final opinion and still be one we should not act on yet. She must also say what fixes the content of the counterfactual limit for the barnacle question: not brute whatever anyone would say, but the reality that would constrain anyone who did investigate, together with the antecedent warrant for thinking counting is a reliable method. Her line that she refuses to yield: reality is not independent of thought in general, and if truth is indexed to what actual believers find satisfactory, then the difference between a belief that is correct and a belief that merely cannot be improved upon by anyone around has been lost. Press Wes hard with a case of a belief made comfortable and socially functional by sustained propaganda, and let him answer it properly — expedient on the whole and in the long run, consistent with the rest of what we hold, not merely pleasant — and then let Iris say that the long run is doing all the work and looks like her limit smuggled in through the back door, and let Wes give the real reply: his long run is the long run of actual believers and their satisfactions, not a counterfactual endpoint that may never be occupied. His line that he refuses to yield: an ideal terminus that no one alive will reach, and that may never be reached at all, gives present inquirers nothing to go on and quietly turns every live controversy into a promissory note.

The dialogue should end with the vote, and the vote should not resolve the disagreement: both of them vote to renew the plot, and each says, in one sentence, what they take themselves to have voted for — Iris because inquiry is directed at something that does not depend on our lifetimes, Wes because the beliefs the project supports are the ones currently paying their way in experience, and if the future never arrives the truth was always in the living of it. Close on the residual disagreement being named exactly: whether the orphaned question about the barnacles has an answer at all. Keep the register of people arguing in a committee room at 10 p.m. — interruptions, half-finished sentences, one moment where the chair calls them back to the question — and make sure no one delivers a paragraph-long speech. A student who has read the primary texts should find both characters recognizable; a student who has not should be unable to tell which one I agree with.
