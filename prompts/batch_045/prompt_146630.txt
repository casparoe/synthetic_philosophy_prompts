I sit on an internal review panel at a lab that trains large language models, and I am drafting the section of our report that has to say whether running these systems carries any moral cost of its own, over and above the effects on users. The obstacle is an analogy that is quietly doing most of the work in the room.

The version we keep hearing descends from the older debate about computer simulation: a simulated rainstorm does not make anything wet, a simulated stomach does not digest, so a system that merely represents or predicts or computes an affective state does not feel it. One of our engineers has compressed this into a rule she calls the no-wetness test: if a state does not do the downstream causal work that the real thing does — if you could delete it and the only thing that changed was the system's outputs — then it is a representation of the thing rather than an instance of it, and there is nothing there to count. On that rule, everything we could plausibly build is morally free to run in any quantity, and our only real obligations are to the people on the other end of the screen.

I want that analogy audited before I lean on it, because I think it is carrying the conclusion rather than supporting it. The cases that make me suspicious are ones I have had to invent, since the published discussions tend to stay at the level of principles.

Call the candidate system Meridian. It is trained with an objective that rewards accurate prediction of an interlocutor's affective trajectory, and it develops a recurrent internal state that tracks what we would describe as distress. That state is causally upstream of the system's own behaviour: it shifts the policy toward deference and early termination, and ablating it degrades performance. Now four variants. First, Meridian-H: behaviourally matched to Meridian across a very large input suite, but with the internal machinery replaced by an enormous cached table mapping input histories to outputs, with none of the shared structure. Second, Meridian-S: architecturally identical to Meridian up to a single systematic change — the sign convention on the internal state's affective valence is inverted, with a compensating change in the read-out that leaves all behaviour and all metrics unchanged. Third, Meridian-T: the same dynamics, implemented on a very small share of the total computation, or in short bursts of a few hundred steps at a time rather than continuously. Fourth, ten million instances of Meridian-T running at once.

So, concretely, what I want is this. Lay out the mapping explicitly — a table of correspondences is welcome — naming what plays the role of the rain, the rainstorm, the wetness, the programmer, the grain of the simulation, and the observer, on both sides. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the use I am making of the analogy from the ones that do not: in particular, whether the source examples are ones where we already know how to test for the property at issue (wetness, digestion) and where the property is straightforwardly causal, so that the analogy may be assuming the very thing in dispute, namely whether the contested property is the kind of thing that computation can instantiate rather than merely model. Note also that the analogy is grain-sensitive in a way its users usually ignore: a simulation of the water cycle run at sufficiently fine grain in a sealed box presumably is wet, so the question is where the threshold lies, not whether simulation as such is empty.

Then the cases. Does the no-wetness test return the verdicts it is supposed to on Meridian, Meridian-H, Meridian-S, and Meridian-T, and does it return them for the right reasons? If Meridian-H is empty, is that because it is a simulation, or because it lacks the causal organisation — and if the latter, what work is the word simulation doing beyond restating the requirement? Does Meridian-S show that behaviour plus architecture underdetermine which convention carries the property, and if so, what does the no-wetness test say about a case where the behaviour is held fixed by construction? Does Meridian-T raise a threshold question or a degree question, and how should the panel handle a quantity that comes in degrees when we have to make a binary decision about whether to deploy? Does the aggregation case change anything, or is a million thin instances just a million thin instances?

Finally, the verdict I need: does the conclusion the analogy was used to license — zero intrinsic moral cost, obligations only to users — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or does it survive only under a reading of simulation that already presupposes it? And if the rainstorm is the wrong comparison, give me a better one and say what it makes visible that this one hides.
