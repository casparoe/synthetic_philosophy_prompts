Here is the pattern. A resident I'll call Mrs. H. has moderately advanced Alzheimer's. Years earlier she signed a directive refusing a ventilator and a feeding tube. She now seems, as far as anyone can tell, contented; she enjoys music, she smiles at her grandson, she does not appear to be suffering. If you asked her now whether she wanted to be kept going, she would probably, in the moment, say yes. Nobody on the unit treats that as relevant. The refusal is treated as still hers, and the language people use is the language of authority: it is her decision, it is not ours to second-guess, she was entitled to speak for herself then and we are only carrying it out.

Now flip the content. Another resident, Mr. A., filled out the same kind of form and indicated that he wanted everything done — ventilator, tube feeding, the works. His daughters and the staff treat that as advisory at best. The language switches: we have to think about what is good for him now, we cannot just follow a piece of paper, a person who is not in that situation cannot really know. (A nurse once told me — I have no idea whether this is legally accurate and I do not want your answer to rest on it — that on these forms the "treatments I refuse" half is the part that really binds and the "treatments I want" half mostly does not.)

What I keep noticing is that the earlier self's authority seems to run in one direction much harder than the other. It is strong when the earlier self said *do not do this to me*, and weak when it said *do this for me* — even when the two were written by the same person, on the same day, in the same frame of mind, about the same future condition. And the same asymmetry shows up outside medicine entirely. My friend and I make pacts: "if I start texting my ex again at 2 a.m., take my phone and do not give it back" is a pact everyone honours, and feels obliged to honour; "if I start skipping morning practice, come over and drag me out of bed" is treated as a joke I am allowed to release you from, even though it was made in the same cool hour with the same foresight about my own weakness.

So: does this asymmetry have a name, or a literature, or is it a name that still needs coining? I am not asking you to hand me a label; I want the carving. Specifically, please address the following, and do not stop at the first one that seems to fit:

1. Is this just the familiar asymmetry between a right to refuse and a right to demand — the thought that a person's claim against interference is stronger than any claim to be provided with something? My reason for doubting that: the precommitment cases involve no rights and no one's body, and I think the asymmetry survives there. Test whether it actually does.
2. Is it the standard distinction between deciding by substituted judgment and deciding by best interests? I do not think so, because the switch between the two styles of justification seems to happen *inside* cases where everyone agrees the standard is what the person would have wanted. 3. Is it really the doing/allowing distinction, or an act/omission effect, in disguise — overriding a refusal being a thing we do, ignoring a request being a thing we merely allow? Give me the control case that would settle this: what would the asymmetry look like if both options were omissions, or if the earlier instruction concerned something with no bodily intrusion at all?
4. Could it be entirely about which error is worse, i.e. an artefact of defaults and risk-aversion? Propose a case where the content of the instruction is held fixed but the default is reversed, and tell me whether the asymmetry flips, persists, or dissolves.
5. Boundary cases I want handled either way: an instruction that includes its own cancellation clause ("if I ever reach this point and seem happy, ignore what I wrote"); a *request* that the later self is actively resisting ("make me take my pills even when I say no"); and a directive written by someone who has had no experience of the condition they are legislating for.
