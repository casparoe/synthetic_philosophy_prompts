I'm writing the final for my upper-level epistemology seminar (third years, they've all had the intro knowledge-and-justification course, and the last three weeks were on self-knowledge: the KK principle in its Hintikka-ish form, Williamson's argument against luminous conditions in Knowledge and Its Limits (2000), the safety / margin-for-error premise, that whole cluster). Last year I set something like "explain the anti-luminosity argument and say whether it refutes KK" and got thirty near-identical papers: recap the fine-grained series, recite "no non-trivial condition is luminous", conclude KK is false. All true, all regurgitated. So this year I want a question where merely having the argument by heart isn't enough, without it turning into a question about who said what in the literature — assume they've got the core material and nothing else.

The crux I keep circling is this. As we teach it, the argument only hands you a failure somewhere in the borderline region of a series: a case where the condition C holds but the subject isn't in a position to know that C holds. So a KK defender has an obvious retreat — concede that KK fails at the margin, insist it still holds in the good cases, i.e. when you know p with a decent margin of safety you're in a position to know that you know p. A colleague of mine thinks he can kill that retreat in one move, which he calls the re-start: don't run the argument on the condition "S knows that p", run it on the family of conditions "S knows that p with a margin of at least m", for larger and larger m. Anti-luminosity then gives you, for each m, a case where S knows p with margin ≥ m but isn't in a position to know that. So however much margin the retreat demands, there is a case where knowledge-with-that-margin isn't luminous, and the retreat is dead. I don't buy it — it looks to me like the move quietly changes what the subject has to be in a position to know in the consequent (knowledge-with-margin-m, not knowledge simpliciter), so it may not touch the retreat at all — but I can't quiet say what the retreat would have to look like for his move to bite, or whether there's a repaired version of it that does. There's a related thing I want them pinned down on too: KK is normally stated as an actual-knowledge principle, but luminosity talks about what you're "in a position to" know, and I have a suspicion the dialectic slides between the two somewhere and that the slide is doing work in both his argument and in mine.

So here's what I need from you: one exam question, 45 minutes, about 700 words of expected writing, that forces them through exactly this — question that a student who has only memorised the argument will fail, and where the gap between an A and a B is whether they can locate where the re-start move does or doesn't land and say what would have to be true for the retreat to survive. Give me the exact wording as I'd paste it onto the paper (set the retreat and the re-start move up in the question itself, since the re-start is my colleague's invention and isn't in any reading), then a model answer of roughly 600-700 words, then a marking scheme out of 25: the specific moves an A has to make, what a B gets right and where it stalls, what a C looks like, and for each must-hit move a one-line note on how students typically botch it. Please make sure the question can't be answered by summarising Williamson — if your draft lets someone score full marks without engaging the re-start move, tell me so and fix it.
