ive got an essay due for a bioethics seminar and I want to try reading Kazuo Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go through the nonidentity problem — not the boring "is human cloning wrong" version. The setup: Kathy, Tommy and Ruth only exist because the donation programme exists, so on a person-affecting view nothing the programme does to them makes them worse off, which is obviously teh wrong verdict and the novel never once lets anyone think it. I want a reading that separates two things people mush together: the act of creating the donors, and everything done to them once they exist (Hailsham's guardianship, the rumours about deferrals for couples who are really in love that turn out to have never been true, Tommy being pushed away from his art, Miss Emily's admission near the end that the guardians' real project was showing the world that clones had souls and were more than their donations). The second batch doesn't look identity-affecting at all, so the nonidentity problem shouldn't shield it — my question is whether the book's moral weight actually sits there and whether the book notices the difference, or whether Ishiguro is deliberately making the creation itself the crime.

The part I want you to fight with: the novel adds a variable no textbook case has. The donors are supposed by everyone in the book to be copied from living "originals", and Ruth spends a long stretch hunting for hers. If Kathy is a copy of somebody who already exists, does "she wouldn't have existed otherwise" do the work Parfit needs it to do? I've been playing with a label: call an act identity-shielded if avoiding its bad outcomes would have required the victim not to exist, and identity-exposed otherwise. Is that anything more than person-affecting vs impersonal in a new hat, and does it come apart when you run it through copies? Give me three or four scenes, say which side of the line each is on, tell me where the reading is strained or where the novel resists it, and finish on what the book adds to this debate that the standard cases (the girl whose child would be better off if she waited, the deplete-vs-conserve policy) can't get to.
