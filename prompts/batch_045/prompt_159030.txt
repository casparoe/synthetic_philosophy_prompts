I'm on a small team building a reasoning system that keeps explicit credences over mathematical and computational claims and can spend compute to settle them. Our epistemics lead has been ruling, case by case, on which of three buckets a claim belongs in: open, meaning a live credence that is bettable and updatable; deferred, meaning no standing credence, just run the computation when the claim matters; excluded, meaning no credence and no attempt to settle. Her rulings so far:

1. The ten-thousandth digit of π — deferred. It never gets a credence.
2. Whether a 300-page proof posted online, which nobody here has checked, is correct — open, with the credence moving as partial checks come in.
3. Whether a program she wrote halts within 10^9 steps — deferred; run it with a step bound. Whether it halts at all — open, and she allows that it may stay open indefinitely.
4. Whether P equals NP — open, with no compute allocated to deciding it directly; the credence may move on indirect evidence, analogies, and expert opinion.
5. The Gödel sentence of the formalism the system reasons in — excluded. She will not let the system take any position on it, even a careful one.
6. What a frozen copy of the system's own weights would output on an input it has already been given — open. She refused to let the system settle it by symmetry, even though the code is identical and running the copy is cheap.

Next week she rules on a new case, and I want to predict her rather than argue with her. The system is asked, in advance, to price a claim about its own future output: that its answer to a query arriving tomorrow will contain a certain string. There are two routes to settling it. It can simulate itself — identical weights, identical code — on tomorrow's query now and read off the result; that is cheap and, as far as anyone can tell, reliable. Or it can prove a theorem about its own source code, which requires assuming that the proof procedure running the proof is sound. Note also that the system's beliefs about its own future behavior are themselves among the inputs to what it outputs.

Give me the rules she could be running that fit all six earlier rulings, what each implies about the new case, and which you would bet on. My own read is that at least three fit and come apart here: a threshold-on-the-cost-of-settling rule, which should send the claim to deferred; a rule against ever letting a reasoner settle a claim whose truth depends on its own credences or outputs; and a rule about claims on which taking a position changes the outcome, which should send it to excluded alongside the Gödel sentence. But I cannot tell which she is running, or whether these are really three rules rather than one.

Add a final row labelled best guess, with your prediction in the verdict column and a one-line reason in the rule column. Don't cite anything; I know the literature.
