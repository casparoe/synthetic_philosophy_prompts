I write literary fiction, and I have come to a standstill over a single chapter that no amount of craft on my part seems able to settle, because the objection raised against it is not, in the end, an objection about craft.

My manuscript's narrator, Anne, has been blind from birth and, until she was thirty-one, lived in one room whose only furnishings were books, supplied to her in Braille. She acquired her entire vocabulary from a dictionary and a set of encyclopedias, and she corresponded with no one. She has never seen color. In the closing chapter she writes a long passage describing a red coat laid over the back of a chair in borrowed afternoon light — a passage built, I should admit honestly, out of everything the encyclopedias told her about red: that it sits nearer to orange than to green, that it is the color of blood, of stop-signs, of foxes, that it has the longest wavelength of the visible spectrum, that it is used to signal danger, that certain painters mixed it from madder root.

My editor's report is blunt. No one who has never seen red, it says, means anything by the word red; the passage is a machine stitching other people's sentences together; and it should either be cut or reassigned to a sighted character. My own feeling is that this proves too much, and I would like to know whether it does before I cut two years of work.

Three things complicate it for me, and I would be grateful for your help with each.

First, I have been toying with a device. Suppose Anne is given an instrument that converts the dominant wavelength of whatever she points it at into an audible tone, so that she can in some sense hear that the coat is red and the chair is not. My worry is that this changes nothing: a tone is no more red than a word is, and she would merely have one more symbol to look up in a table. But I cannot tell whether that worry is deep or merely stubborn.

Second, my early readers weep over the passage. They are sighted, they have seen red, and they plainly take something from it. I do not know whether their understanding can lend her words a content they would not otherwise have, or whether it only shows that a reader can always supply the missing thing for himself.

Third, my editor raises no objection at all when Anne uses words like electron, justice, or sincere — terms for which nobody, sighted or otherwise, has the kind of acquaintance he seems to be demanding in the case of red. I suspect he owes me an account of those, and I suspect that whatever account he gives will either rescue red or ruin half the language.

So: is the chapter defensible? What exactly is missing from Anne, if anything is — and is it missing from the rest of us as well? And what would I have to change, in the scene or in the conceit itself, for the passage to be something other than a machine stitching other people's sentences together?
