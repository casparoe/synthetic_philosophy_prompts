I convene a fortnightly decision-theory reading group, and after last week's session two colleagues — Julian (in my own department) and Marta (an económica who sits in with us, and who is much more impatient than I am with idealizations) — stayed behind and got into an argument about the force of Good's theorem, the result that for an agent who updates by conditionalization and then maximizes expected utility, a costless observation has non-negative expected value, evaluated ex ante. The question they were actually fighting about is whether the theorem underwrites the sweeping claim that one can never be made worse off by free information, and so whether there is any distinctively epistemic reason ever to remain ignorant — something that isn't just a cost of looking or a defect in the looker. Tea ran long and I was asked to referee; I said nothing, partly from cowardice and partly because I really couldn't tell where to land. I'd like something in writing, since I may use the exchange as a teaching case in a graduate seminar next term on the value of knowledge. Please don't treat either of them as a foil: every move below is one I would have made in that person's position. Their exchange, as closely as I can reconstruct it:

(1) Julian: The theorem tells us that ignorance has to be paid for, and there are exactly two currencies: either the information wasn't really costless — it took time, attention, money, or it foreclosed options you'd rather have kept — or you're planning to mishandle it after you get it. Neither currency buys a norm of ignorance. So anyone who declines free information faces a dilemma she should have to answer: either she is paying a cost she hasn't put on the ledger, or she is counting on herself being irrational later. What we don't get is an intellectual analogue of 'sometimes the right thing is to stop looking.'

(2) Marta: The second horn of your dilemma isn't a diagnosis, it's a description of the human situation, and that's why the dilemma is too quick. Take the case clinicians actually worry about: a genetic test for a late-onset condition with no treatment and no preventive measure available. The patient who tests positive gains no option she lacked; what she gains is a state that reliably degrades her subsequent practical reasoning — she becomes depressed, she stops making long-term plans, she makes worse medical and financial decisions than she would have made in ignorance. For her the information is not free in any sense that a ledger written from the inside can represent: as a matter of psychological fact she is worse at getting what she wants once she has looked. Telling her that a perfect Bayesian in her position would be fine is like telling a man with a broken leg that a perfect walker wouldn't fall.

(3) Julian: You're conceding too much to the ledger. If she now foresees that looking will corrupt her later reasoning, two things follow and neither is mysterious. First, the package 'look, then reason badly' is dominated by 'look, then reason well,' so what she has discovered is a known defect in her own future practical rationality, and the rational response is to repair it or to arrange matters so she can't act on the corrupted reasoning — commit now, delegate now, burn the bridge now. Second, if neither fix is available to her, then the information isn't costless for her: there is a handling cost, and once we price it, the theorem's own computation tells her not to look. Both routes confirm the theorem. Neither yields a reason for ignorance that isn't either a cost or a defect.

(4) Marta: But now you've made the theorem unfalsifiable by construction: every apparent counterexample gets reclassified as a hidden cost. There's a class of cases the reclassification can't reach, because the problem isn't how well she reasons, it's the assumption that information moves beliefs and leaves concerns alone. Call it the Sealed Letter case. At fifty a woman opens a letter her mother left her and learns that the father she idealized — and whose reputation she has spent her career defending — was a police informer. Nothing in her reasoning is impaired: she updates impeccably and acts impeccably on what she learns. But the learning reorganizes what she cares about. She no longer cares about the family name; the projects that gave her later life its shape collapse; she becomes someone with different ends. Before opening, she would have paid to know. After opening, she would give everything to unknow, and she can't. By the only lights available to the person who has to live the rest of it, she has been made worse off by the information — and Good's theorem cannot so much as formulate that verdict, since it can only compare lotteries under one fixed utility function defined over the whole tree.

(5) Julian: It can formulate it; it just doesn't return the verdict you want. 'Worse off' is being used in two voices in your last sentences — the ex ante voice of the woman deciding whether to open the letter, and the ex post voice of the woman who wishes she hadn't. Only the first is a voice in which anything is chosen, and in that voice she isn't worse off. And if you insist that the later self's complaint must count now, the way to honor that isn't to abandon the theorem but to write it into the utility function: if I care about what my future self will care about, or about the integrity of my concerns across time, that is already a term in my current utility function — call it a continuity-of-value term — and once it is there the theorem applies again and returns exactly the answer you dislike: for such a person, some information has negative expected value. That is the 'hidden cost' move you object to, except that now we can see what the cost consists in — the risk of becoming someone whose ends you presently have reason not to want.

(6) Marta: Then the theorem can't do the work you gave it in (1). If every harmful piece of information can be absorbed as a term in the utility function — a handling cost here, a continuity-of-value term there — then the theorem is a bookkeeping identity: a costless observation has non-negative expected value, where anything that could make an observation bad for you is re-described as making it not costless. Read that way it never touches an actual choice, so it can't establish that there is no such thing as a reason to remain ignorant. Read the other way — as a substantive claim about agents like us, who do sometimes reason badly and whose concerns do sometimes change under what they learn — it is false, as the clinic case and the Sealed Letter show. So the theorem is either vacuous or falsified, and either way it doesn't license the polemic you started with.
