I am giving a paper in six weeks at a closed roundtable on the democratic legitimacy of the European Union, and I would like you to build me a Q&A preparation sheet. The paper is twenty-five minutes, the discussion is forty, and the audience is small enough that I will hear from nearly everyone.

The audience, in roughly the proportions that matter. Four EU public lawyers: one works specifically on the division of competences, implied powers and the flexibility clause; one works on withdrawal and treaty amendment; two are generalists who will default to institutional-design talk. Two political theorists: one works inside the input-legitimacy versus output-legitimacy framework and is broadly sympathetic to problem-solving accounts on which a union earns its keep by solving problems member states cannot solve alone; the other is a republican or discursive theorist for whom legitimacy requires an identifiable constituent power and genuine, unforced contestation of the basic settlement. One former permanent representative who thinks in terms of Council bargaining, the credibility of commitments, and the integrity of the single market. One analyst from a Central European think tank who worries about a two-speed union and about exit threats being used asymmetrically by the states that can afford them. The chair is a comparative constitutionalist whose instinct will be to redescribe whatever I say as a category mistake.

Everyone in that room already knows the standard reform menu: empowering the European Parliament, lead-candidate selection for the Commission presidency, transnational lists, a right of legislative initiative, the citizens' panels, the familiar argument that there is no European demos. My paper opens by setting that menu aside as at best incomplete, and I have replies ready for the three predictable objections that the UK's withdrawal proves exit works, that ordinary treaty revision is always available in principle, and that what I am describing is just differentiated integration under another name. Please do not spend the sheet on any of those; I need what survives them.

Here is the thesis. The deficit is not primarily one of authorization, and it is not cured by shortening the distance between the voter and the decision-maker. It is a structural property of a certain kind of delegation. The Union exhibits what I call delegation asymmetry: inside the field that has been delegated, decisions are taken by qualified majority and are enforced by a court whose doctrines of direct effect and supremacy make them legally binding on national administrations and courts; at the boundary of that field, change requires unanimity plus ratification, which is to say it requires something very close to a political miracle. And the content of the delegated field has come to include a substantive policy orientation, not merely a set of procedures: an open and undistorted competition regime, an independent state-aid and merger control system, a price-stability mandate, free movement and the four freedoms read as directly enforceable rights. That orientation is now insulated from ordinary electoral reversal in the member states.

From this I derive what I call the reversibility condition: a delegation of sovereign powers is democratically legitimate only if those who delegated them can recover some specified part of them through an ordinary, anticipated, non-existential political process. Article 50 is not such a process, because it is all-or-nothing and therefore unusable against a single policy; a mechanism that can only be operated at the cost of national catastrophe is not a mechanism of ordinary contestation. Treaty revision under Article 48 is not such a process either. So, on my account, the Union fails the reversibility condition, and this failure, rather than the weakness of the European Parliament, is the hard core of what people gestured at when they complained about a democratic deficit.

The remedy is a competence-return procedure with four moving parts. First, a trigger available to national parliaments as a strengthened successor to the subsidiarity early-warning mechanism, or to a transnational citizens' initiative at a high threshold. Second, a mandatory and public competence audit: what the Union currently does in the field, who gains and who loses if it stops, what the internal-market externalities are, and what it would cost other member states. Third, a decision by reversed majority: the field reverts to the member states unless a supermajority of the Council affirmatively votes to retain it, so that the default is contestation rather than permanence. Fourth, an anti-cherry-picking rule: reverted competences come back to the member states jointly, and re-transferring the same field later requires the full amendment procedure. I also concede up front what I take to be the deepest political obstacle: the ever-closer-union language in Article 1 and in the preamble to the Treaty on European Union makes reversibility conceptually unthinkable, so the procedure requires amending the teleology itself, and I argue in the final section that the Union has to choose between being an ever-closer union and being a reversible one.

What I want from you is the following.

Ten or so objections, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, each attributed to a member of the audience and phrased the way that person would actually phrase it: the competence lawyer talking about implied powers, effet utile and the flexibility clause; the credibility-of-commitments official asking why anyone would ever bind themselves to a single market that can be unbound at will; the republican theorist asking what reversible delegation has to do with constituent power; the Central European analyst asking who in practice will hold the trigger and against whom; the chair asking whether my condition, stated generally, does not condemn independent central banks, constitutional rights, devolution settlements and every other deliberately hard-to-reverse delegation inside the member states themselves.

For each objection: a two-minute reply in spoken register rather than essay register; a note on what I should concede immediately rather than have dragged out of me; a note on what I must not concede because the argument collapses without it; and one line on where the reply is soft. Ordering matters: I want to know which objection opens the second half of the discussion and which ones only arrive once the lawyers and the theorists start arguing with each other rather than with me, and I would like one bridging line I can use to pull the conversation back from the lawyers' institutional details to the claim about reversibility. I also want an honest assessment of the weakest joint in my own position, distinguishing the one I should volunteer in the talk from the one I should prepare to defend but not advertise.

Finally, include the objection I should hope nobody asks: the single question that, if it lands, shows the whole apparatus misfires rather than merely needs repair. And where an objection turns on a point of EU law or treaty text, flag that I should verify it rather than asserting the point, so I do not go into the roundtable repeating something I have wrong.
