I'm a science-and-ideas reporter working on a piece about what should happen when a community's memory collides with the archival record, and I've realised my gut verdicts don't line up with each other. Tell me what principle I'm secretly using, or should be.

My verdicts. (1) A Midwestern town's plaque says the land was bought outright from the Ojibwe in 1854; the treaty and deed records show no sale, just removal. Plaque comes down, no hesitation. (2) A family in Lyon remembers great-uncle as having hidden a Jewish family for nothing; notarial records show he was paid, and paid well. I don't think the memory gives way — I think it's a true memory that got retold as a story about virtue. (3) An Aboriginal Australian community's oral tradition puts a massacre at a particular waterhole; the colonial record for that district is silent for that whole decade — silent about the massacre, but also silent about the community existing at all. Memory stands. (4) A village remembers a famous novelist visiting on 12 June 1878; his published letters and hotel registers put him in another country that June, and nothing local was written down at the time. Confabulation — drop it gently. (5) A village remembers a wartime massacre of 300 of its own people; exhumations turn up 12. The number gives way, but I resist saying the memory was wrong in the way the plaque was wrong. (6) After a dictatorship falls, the secret police's own files name the village's beloved schoolteacher as an informant; everyone who knew him says impossible. My gut: the file doesn't get to decide. But I won't say he was innocent either.

Two things bug me. Cases 3 and 4 look structurally the same — the record says nothing — and I come out opposite ways. And in both 1 and 6 the document was produced by power, yet I side with it in one and against it in the other.

The rule I keep half-forming: trust the record when it speaks against the interests of whoever made it, and trust the memory when the silence of the archive is exactly what the powerful needed. But that gets case 6 wrong, and I don't trust it on case 4 either. I've also been toying with two distinctions that may be doing no work: memory held in practice — a procession, a place-name, a gravesite — versus memory held only in propositions; and what I've started calling counter-archival memory, where the content of the memory isn't a rival factual claim but a refusal of the record's jurisdiction at all.

So: give me one principle, or two candidates, stated precisely enough to grind out verdicts, with the case that motivates each clause. Then tell me which of my six verdicts it can't accommodate and wants me to drop. And two cases I haven't listed, that I genuinely go back and forth on: a community's memory that has hardened into a monument and a museum exhibit, with nobody alive who remembers the event firsthand — is that still memory with a claim on us, or just a false assertion with better architecture? And a memory that exists only because somebody in 1970 read the archive wrong — a memory manufactured by a record.
