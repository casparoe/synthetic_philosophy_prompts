I am writing a novel set in London in the winter of 1786, about a small circle of legal reformers who are drafting, in private, a model penal code they hope never to see enacted; much of the book is argument in rooms, and one chapter is a pamphlet that exists only inside the fiction. The pamphlet's author is a young barrister named Edmund Vavasour, the most rigorous utilitarian in the circle and the least willing to flatter an audience, and I need him to defend a position his colleagues find both chilly and convenient: that once a legislator has satisfied himself that a private sexual practice between consenting adults harms no assignable person in body, property, or reputation, the circumstance that the practice is regarded with widespread revulsion is not merely outweighed by the case for repeal but should be struck out of the calculation altogether, so that the opponents of repeal are left with nothing to weigh and not simply with less to weigh. I want the argument to be Benthamite in its machinery rather than in its slogans: pleasure and pain as the only ends; punishment as an evil that must be justified by the mischief it prevents and by that alone; the mischief of an act divided into what befalls assignable individuals and the further alarm and danger that radiate outward from it; and conduct that touches no one but the agent left to the moral or popular sanction rather than to the law. My recollection of what Bentham wrote when, and of what he left in manuscript, is unreliable, and I do not want chapter-and-verse citation or any apparatus of scholarly reference: argue from the framework, as a pamphleteer of that decade could have argued from it, in a way that a reader who knows only the general shape of his hedonistic utilitarianism could assess.

The device I want Vavasour to build the case on is a rule I have not seen anyone propose, which he calls the test of corrected opinion: a pain of disapproval enters the legislator's ledger only if it would survive the removal of every false belief about matters of fact held by the person who feels it. The neighbors' revulsion, he will say, is built out of errors — that the practice spreads disease, that it dissolves the household, that it is a species of fraud — and a pain that exists only because of an error is not a pain the law should buy off by prohibition, since prohibition ratifies the error and removes the occasion for correcting it, whereas repeal lets the pain expire on its own as the error does. Please have Vavasour press this as far as it will go and then meet the two cases I intend to have his enemies raise at him in the next chapter. First: a neighbor whose revulsion rests on no false matter of fact at all, but on the sincere and settled judgment that the practice is base — if such a pain counts, the test does no work and any majority can veto any reform by feeling strongly enough about it; if it does not count, the test appears to assume what is in dispute, since whether the practice is base is precisely the question. Second: a case in which the distress is triggered by visibility rather than by the act, so that it could be averted by concealment instead of by prohibition — a result Vavasour should either embrace or block, and I would like to see which, and on what principle rather than by assertion. He should also make one concession, in his own voice and not grudgingly: the rule must be shown to cut against his own side somewhere, and in particular his own indignation at the existing law, and at the suffering it causes, has to pass the same test he imposes on the neighbors, and it may not.

The chapter after the pamphlet is a reply from an elderly Anglican moralist in the circle, and I would rather Vavasour answer the real objection than a version of it I can knock down for him. The moralist's claim is that a people's sense of the sacredness of its own morals is itself a public good — a fund of restraint on which the enforcement of every other law depends — so that a legislator who strikes that sense from the ledger whenever it can be traced to a correctable error is sawing off the branch he sits on, and buying a small present relief at the cost of a general indiscipline. Vavasour should answer that within his own terms: either by arguing that the fund is not depleted, or by admitting the depletion and pricing it, or by denying that the test has the corrosive scope the moralist attributes to it. The register throughout should be that of a pamphlet meant to persuade intelligent opponents — pointed, concrete, willing to concede a point in order to keep a larger one — rather than a seminar paper; aim for something in the neighbourhood of seven hundred to nine hundred words, and let the argument be one a hostile reader could reconstruct well enough to attack.
