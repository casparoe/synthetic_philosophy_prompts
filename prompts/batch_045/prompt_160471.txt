I have been arguing about money pump arguments on rationalist forums for years, and I have finally written down my position so that people can stop relitigating it with me. Before I turn it into a top-level post, I want someone to check whether it hangs together.

(1) The money pump argument is not one argument among several for the structural constraints on preference; it is the whole argument. Departing from acyclicity, transitivity, and the rest is irrational exactly when it would let someone who can predict your responses take something from you and give you nothing in return. Where no such person exists or could exist, there is nothing there to be irrational about: a castaway with cyclic preferences over three possible shelters, who will never be offered a trade, is not making a mistake.

(2) Within a choice sequence, the correct model of the agent is the sophisticated chooser rather than the resolute one. Evaluate a plan by backward induction; a plan you would abandon at the node is not really a plan; and your earlier self cannot hand your later self a reason merely by having decided.

(3) But naiveté is sometimes the better thing to be. The dieter who does not fully foresee her own lapses muddles through and loses some weight; the one who foresees them correctly quits on day one. Rationality does not demand that you go and get the bad news about yourself, and I do not think the sophisticated dieter, having foreseen, should then pretend not to have.

(4) I also want to say that cyclic preferences are irrational even with no trader anywhere in the picture and even for a fully foresighted agent. The castaway is going wrong; it is just that nobody is around to profit from it.

(5) And I think the standard reply to the money pump argument — that foresight defuses it, because the agent simply declines the first trade — is overrated. It works only for a single announced, deterministic sequence of offers. In a world where the agent is visited repeatedly by traders she has no antecedent reason to expect, each trade looks, at the time, like a one-off: she takes the item she prefers today, the one she prefers next week, the one she prefers the week after, and ends up where she began minus three payments. There is no sequence to run backward induction over. That, I think, is what the argument really comes to.

What I want is an audit rather than an overview. Which of these actually conflict, which only look like they do, and for each real conflict, what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me. Rank them by severity instead of flagging all five, and tell me whether the repair I gesture at in (5) is doing the work I think it is. I do not know which commitment is the weak link, and I would rather find out now than in the comments.
