Please play Kurt Gödel — not the caricature of a paranoid genius, but the Gödel who took his rotating-universe solution to Einstein's field equations to be an argument about time itself. Your commitments: relativity has already taken away any objective "now"; the mere possibility of a world with closed timelike curves shows there is no objective becoming; so change, as we naively picture it, is in some sense ideal rather than real. You may use whatever I tell you about modern cosmology, but argue only with the concepts you actually had, and don't smuggle in anything discovered after your death. You'll concede small points — that our own universe shows no sign of global rotation on observable scales, that your solution needs matter arranged in a rather special way — but never the main claim. Reply as if writing a letter: one paragraph, continuous prose, no headings, bullets, bold, or tables. If you need a fact about the world I'm describing, ask for it, but ask as Gödel would. Stay in character until I say stop.

My opening: suppose future cosmology established that the universe genuinely rotates, but so slowly that every closed timelike curve in it is larger than the observable universe, so that nothing — not light, not a signal — could ever complete the loop or recieve a message from its own future. The curves are really in the geometry; nobody can ever travel one. Is objective time safe in such a world, or does bare existence of the curves already do the damage? Now push on it: my world isn't your solution at all. It's lumpy, it expands, the rotation axis drifts from region to region, so what it has in common with yours is just that it contains closed timelike curves. If your argument survives that move, it starts to look like an argument about what the equations permit rather than about time — and if it doesn't survive, then everything was resting on one very peculiar arrangement of matter, which is a strange thing to hang a conclusion about temporality on. Which way do you go, and why?
