Hey — I'm a longtime lurker of the old SSC-adjacent rationalist scene (data engineer by trade, not a philosopher), and our Discord book group has been grinding through luck egalitarianism for a few weeks now. I've noticed something annoying: my gut verdicts on individual cases are far more confident than any principle I can actually state out loud, and when I try to state one it either collapses into mush or gives verdicts I hate. So I want to do reflective equilibrium on myself and I'd like you to do the principle-extraction part. Here are my verdicts, all of them pre-theoretic and some of them probably inconsistent:

Two loggers, same job, same wage, both knew the risks, one gets crushed by a tree that fell the wrong way and the other walks away. I think the injured one is *owed* compensation, not charity — it's a claim, not a favor. Two poker players, same stakes, one loses his rent money and then loses his apartment and then loses his job. I think he's owed nothing, and I'm fairly confident even though the downstream consequences are grim. I can't fully say why these two differ, and that's the pair that bothers me most — both are voluntary gambles taken for expected benefit. Someone born with an expensive congenital condition: obviously owed. Someone who deliberately cultivated a taste for vintage champagne and now says he can't flourish on tap water: owed nothing. But a person whose religion requires a more expensive diet or housing near a synagogue: I do think they're owed something, and I realize that sits badly next to the champagne guy. The drunk driver who ends up paraplegic: I want to say we owe him stabilization and rehab but *not* income replacement, and then I immediately suspect I only believe that because I want to deter drunk driving, which feels like the wrong kind of reason. An eighteen-year-old who declines a free, cheap preventive treatment and develops the condition at thirty-five: I think his claim is weaker but not zero, and I don't know how to price the seventeen years of not being sick. Parents who refuse vaccination on religious grounds and whose kid ends up with permanent damage: the kid's claim is full and unconditional; I have a vague sense the parents owe something but no idea what the state may do to them. And here's the case I think actually breaks the framework: a couple conceives knowing there's a substantial chance of a serious hereditary condition, the child exists with that condition, her life is well worth living, and had they waited a cycle a *different* child would have existed. I think she's owed care and I don't think the non-identity fact changes that at all — but I also can't see how a view built on "brute luck made her worse off than she'd otherwise have been" gets there, since there's no such comparison available.

My tentative principle, which I'd like you to test against those cases and repair rather than replace wholesale: brute-luck inequalities are unjust and option-luck ones aren't; for outcomes that come from an actual gamble, don't ask what the person chose, ask what coverage they would have bought in a hypothetical ex ante market where they know their risk type and their own temperament but not whether they personally draw the short straw. I think that gets the logger right and the gambler right, but I genuinely don't know what it says about the drunk driver (whose recklessness is itself partly a product of his genes and upbringing — and if I take that regress seriously it looks like no choice is ever option luck at all, which I don't want) or about the IVF kid, and I suspect it gives the champagne guy more than I want unless I add a clause about tastes being within the person's control. Please also tell me whether my logger/poker asymmetry can be saved by any clause that isn't ad hoc — socially useful risk, bodily integrity being special, failure of compensating wage differentials, the employer's benefit — or whether I should just overturn one of the two verdicts. And one last thing: I'd like you to keep clear throughout which parts are settled background that anyone in the field would grant, which are live disputes where competent people disagree (including whether the whole brute/option framing survives the regress objection or the Anderson-style complaint that it's humiliating to the badly-off), and which are just your own view. Don't smooth a contested call into a consensus one, and say plainly which of my verdicts your best principle forces me to drop.
