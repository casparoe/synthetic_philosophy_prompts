I'm retired now — thirty-odd years in experimental physics, mostly precision measurement: low-noise amplifiers, lock-in detection, calibration chains, and the endless business of working out how big your error bars actually are. A couple of years ago I started sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the local university, and we've landed on Timothy Williamson's attack on *luminosity* in *Knowledge and Its Limits* — the claim that for any non-trivial condition, there are cases where the condition obtains but you aren't in a position to know that it does — and on what that's supposed to do to the KK principle. As I've got it: KK says if you know *p* you're in a position to know that you know *p*, which needs the condition *knowing that p* to be luminous, since it says whenever that condition obtains you can tell that it does. I follow the argument in words, but I want it in my own vocabulary before I decide whether I believe it, so:

Translate the anti-luminosity argument into the vocabulary of measurement and instrumentation. The words I actually think in: signal and noise, resolution, decision thresholds, hysteresis, false alarms versus misses, ROC curves, calibration against a standard, and the metrology rule of thumb that whatever you calibrate an instrument against has to be several times more accurate than the instrument itself. Give me the argument in those terms — what is the gradual case-series in your vocabulary, what is the *margin for error* requirement, what is the conclusion — and then give me the residue report, which is the part I really want:

1. What maps cleanly. I have a hunch that *you can't calibrate an instrument against itself* is doing real work here, and I'd like to know whether that's the whole argument or just an analogy that happens to look like it.
2. What only goes through with distortion, and where the translation quietly changes the claim. My talk of error rates is statistical and about repeated trials; I suspect your *margin for error* is a claim about a single case and nearby possible ones, which is not the same thing at all. Say plainly where I'd be kidding myself.
3. What won't come over at all. In particular: does anything in my vocabulary express *being in a position to know*, which seems to be about what a subject could work out without recieving any new information, and modal or normative in a way no spec sheet captures? And is that residue a genuine blind spot in how I think about instruments, or a sign the philosophers' notion is puffed up and should be deflated?

Two extras, since you'll have thought about them more than my group has:

- The argument walks down a series of cases one imperceptible step at a time, and Williamson apparently insists that's related to but not the same as a sorites. I want to know whether the gradualism is load-bearing. My state spaces are mostly discrete — photon counts, yes/no triggers, digital readouts with a gap between levels. Does the argument still bite if the cases come in chunks with a real gap between them, or is it a sorites in disguise that needs a continuum to get going? Give me a straight answer about whether the discreteness of my world makes me immune, and if it doesn't, show me the discrete version.
- Applied case: am I luminous about my own error bar? When I say this reading is good to ±0.3%, am I claiming something the argument says I can never be in a position to know? That feels exactly like the sort of self-knowledge the argument should kill, and if it doesn't kill it I want to know why not.

Write for someone fluent in labs, noise budgets and instruments but not in epistemology jargon, define anything you have to use, and don't smuggle your own favourite theory of knowledge in and call it a translation.
