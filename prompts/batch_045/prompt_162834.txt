I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about comparing populations of people, going by gut. Welfare below is on a scale where 0 is the point at which a life stops being worth living to the person living it and 100 is a great life.

1. 1,000 people at 100 vs. those same 1,000 plus one extra person at 90, nobody else affected: adding her is fine — not worse, maybe a touch better.
2. The same 1,000 at 100, plus one extra person at 2 (glad to be alive, but only just; nobody else affected): feels worse to me, or at least not better. I cant settle which.
3. 1,000 at 100 vs. ten billion people each at welfare 1: the second is much worse. I know there's a well-known argument that 1 and 2 plus transitivity force this on me, and I'd rather drop something than swallow it.
4. The 1,000 at 100 vs. those 1,000 plus one person at -5: clearly worse, and worse by more then the -5 suggests — the one bad life looms larger than the arithmetic.
5. Two populations made up of entirely different people, 1,000 at 100 vs. a different 1,000 at 101: better, but only barely — and part of me says not better at all, since nobody is better off.
6. 1,000 lives that each net out at 100 but each contain one month of agony, vs. 1,000 smooth lives at 100, same totals: the first is worse.

The thing I keep coming back to is: add up welfare, but only count a life once it clears some bar, and count anything below zero extra heavily. That seems to handle 1, 3, 4 and 6, but I can't see what it says about 2 or 5, and I suspect it falls apart somewhere.

Is there one principle that gets all six? State it precisely enough that I could apply it myself, say which case motivates each clause, be honest about which of my verdicts it can't accommodate, and give me its verdict on a case I have no feel for at all: 1,000 at 100 vs. 2,000 at 100, no overlap, everyone equally well off.
