I'm writing up my position on the epistemology of conspiracy theories for a graduate reading group in epistemology, and I want the position audited before I commit to it in print. Below is the set of things I take myself to hold. Most of them are individually fairly popular, but the set has felt unstable to me whenever I try to write it down as a single view, and my hunches about which pairs are the load-bearing ones have been unreliable — so please audit the whole set rather than starting from my guesses, and if you think it actually hangs together, say that plainly rather than manufacturing a problem.

1. Definitional neutrality. "Conspiracy theory" is for me just the label for an explanation of some event of public interest that cites a covert, coordinated group as a significant cause. It carries no built-in epistemic defect, and I reject the pejorative definition on which a conspiracy theory just is an explanation that it is irrational to believe.

2. Particularism about warrant. I don't think there is any epistemic property shared by all and only conspiracy theories in virtue of which they are prima facie unwarranted. Warrantedness is a function of the evidence in the individual case.

3. Self-insulation as a genuine defect. Even so, I want to say that conspiracist reasoning characteristically exhibits a real structural vice: the theory can absorb an apparent refutation by extending the conspiracy to whoever produced it, so that the total evidence looks much the same whether the theory is true or false. I want this to be a criticism of the reasoning even when the theory happens to be true — a true conspiracy theory can be believed for defective reasons.

4. Rejection of the leakiness argument. I reject the argument that large conspiracies are unwarranted because they inevitably leak. The premise that our information environment reliably surfaces defection is exactly what the conspiracy theorist denies, so appealing to it begs the question, and the historical record contains large conspiracies that held for years.

5. Scale sensitivity. But I do want to say that the number of people who would have to be in on it matters epistemically, and that this is not just confidence in the press or in official inquiries in disguise: even in a thoroughly captured information environment, the more participants a theory requires, the more antecedently demanding it is.

6. No default privilege for official accounts. That a government or an agency has said so is not by itself much of a reason to believe, and appeals to institutional authority carry little weight in either direction.

7. Independent-institution convergence. On the other hand, that several institutions with different institutional interests, different funding sources and different career incentives each investigated and none found the thing is, for me, strong evidence against it.

8. Higher-order deference. If a community of inquirers I take to be competent and honest has examined a theory and rejected it, and I cannot locate the flaw in my own first-order case, that disagreement lowers my confidence: it is evidence about my own reliability, not merely about the world.

9. Locally raised priors. Members of groups with a documented history of being deceived by a particular institution — the Tuskegee syphilis study is the case I have in mind — are entitled, epistemically and not merely politically, to a higher prior for conspiracies involving that institution. This changes what it is reasonable for them to believe, not only what it is reasonable for them to say or to demand investigated.

10. A case verdict. Where a single leaked document conflicts with the official timeline and the leak's provenance is unverified, I think the correct response is to suspend judgment about the larger story and treat the discrepancy as a reason for investigation; I don't think it is yet confirmation of the larger narrative.

What I want from you: go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how severe each tension is, ranked rather than listed as equals; which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection and exactly why they only look like tensions; and for each real tension, the cheapest revision that would restore consistency and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set. If the cheapest revision turns one of my commitments into something I would probably not want to hold, say so. Then tell me which single revision, if I could only make one, would clean up the most.

Finally, be explicit about your own confidence. Give a rough confidence level for each verdict you hand down, and single out the one claim you are least sure of, with the reason you are unsure.
