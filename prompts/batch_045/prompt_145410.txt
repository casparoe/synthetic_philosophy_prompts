"Lewis tried to analyze causation as counterfactual dependence: c causes e iff, if c hadn't happened, e wouldn't have happened. This is now just taken to be refuted by preemption cases — two kids throw rocks at a bottle, the first one shatters it, but the bottle would have shattered anyway because the second throw was on its way, so there's no counterfactual dependence even though the first throw plainly caused the shattering. It's also circular, since which counterfactuals come out true depends on what the causal laws are, so you can't use counterfactuals to analyze causation. Woodward's interventionism fixes both problems by dropping possible worlds in favor of actual manipulation: X causes Y iff you can wiggle X and thereby change Y while holding everything else fixed, full stop. Because the view is about what you can do rather than about what would have happened, preemption cases simply don't arise for it. And that makes it basically a souped-up version of the old manipulability/agency theory, with the consequence that there's no causation where nobody can intervene — so it can't make sense of the Big Bang causing the cosmic microwave background, or a black hole's mass causing the orbit of a nearby star. Either way it doesn't matter much: for anything an engineer actually does, interventionism is just the obvious common-sense view and the counterfactual machinery around it is philosophy tax."
