What we did in section (this was on the handout, so the students had it too):

Russell's analysis of a singular definite description: 'The F is G' is true iff
∃x(Fx ∧ ∀y(Fy → y = x) ∧ Gx) —
three conjuncts doing the work of existence (something is F), uniqueness (at most one thing is F), and predication (that thing is G). The description itself is an incomplete symbol: it doesn't contribute an object, it gets eliminated in favour of quantifier structure. For negation Russell gets two readings by scope:
 - wide scope: ¬∃x(Fx ∧ ∀y(Fy → y = x) ∧ Gx)
 - narrow scope: ∃x(Fx ∧ ∀y(Fy → y = x) ∧ ¬Gx)
So on the wide-scope reading 'the present King of France is not bald' comes out true, and the unnegated sentence is straightforwardly false rather than lacking a truth value.

The question:

Imagine a variety of English, call it English-2, that is exactly like English except that the plural definite article carries an extra requirement: 'the Fs' can only be used when there are at least two Fs. Call this the MANY requirement. Suppose we Russell-analyse plural descriptions using a plural variable X ranging over pluralities, and suppose we write the narrow-scope reading of a negated sentence by attaching the negation to the final 'all of them' clause, so it reads 'not all of them G'd'.

(a) Write out a Russell-style analysis, in this plural-variable notation, of 'The Fs are G' in English-2, and say in a sentence each what the conjuncts are doing. (5 points)

(b) Consider 'The students who took the make-up exam didn't pass', in English-2, in the following situation: Anna is the only student who took the make-up exam, and she passed; Ben took the exam at the regular time and failed; Cara took it at the regular time and passed. Give the readings you get from the scope distinction, write each one out in the notation, and say for each whether it is true in this situation. (10 points)

(c) Does the MANY requirement cause any trouble for the Russellian claim that a description does no work beyond the quantifier structure it's eliminated into? Answer in three or four sentences. (5 points)

And here's the answer:

(a) ∃X(∀x(Fx → Xx) ∧ |X| ≥ 2 ∧ ∀x(Xx → Gx))

The first conjunct links the description to the predicate F: anything that is F is in the plurality, so X is fixed by F the way the uniqueness clause fixes the single object in the singular case. X stands for the maximal plurality of Fs, which is what the description refers to. The second conjunct is the MANY requirement from the question. The third is the predication clause, like the last conjunct in the singular analysis. Since the description is analysed away into a quantifier over pluralities, this is still Russellian: it contributes quantifier structure rather than an object.

(b) There are two readings. Wide scope: ¬∃X(∀x(Fx → Xx) ∧ |X| ≥ 2 ∧ ∀x(Xx → Passed(x))). This is true in the situation described: there is only one student who took the make-up exam, so no plurality of two or more satisfies the MANY requirement, and the negation of the whole thing is therefore true. Narrow scope: ∃X(∀x(Fx → Xx) ∧ |X| ≥ 2 ∧ ∀x(Xx → ¬Passed(x))). Here the negation only applies to the last clause, so this says there were at least two students who took the make-up exam and none of them passed. In our situation that isn't met either, since there was only one student. But it would be wrong to call this reading false. The MANY requirement isn't part of what the sentence asserts; it's a presupposition of the plural article, like the existential presupposition that Strawson discusses for singular descriptions. So the narrow-scope reading isn't false here, it's infelicitous, it has no truth value. And that shows Russell's eliminative apparatus needs to be supplemented with something like Strawson's notion of presupposition once you move to plural descriptions: the quantifier structure handles the assertion, and the presupposition handles the MANY requirement.

(c) It causes a bit of trouble. The MANY requirement is a condition on when you're allowed to use the plural article, not a condition on what the sentence says, so it can't really go inside the quantifier structure at all; it has to be stated separately, as a rule about the admissibility of 'the Fs'. That's why in (b) I treated it as a presupposition instead of as one of the asserted conjuncts. The quantifier part handles who did what, and the use-condition handles MANY. This is different from uniqueness in the singular case, where uniqueness genuinely is part of what is said, since 'the F is G' says that there is exactly one F.
