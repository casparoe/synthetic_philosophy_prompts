Members of the board of a small trade association, I help oversee the launch of a voluntary certification mark for our member firms. The mark is meant to tell retail customers that a firm's supply chain has been independently audited to a standard well above the legal minimum — something customers can't verify for themselves at the point of purchase, and that only perhaps a fifth of our members would currently pass without changing anything. We want the mark to be genuinely credible rather than decorative, and the board has seized on the handicap principle as our design brief: the thought that a signal earns trust only because it is costly, and that the more it costs the signaller, the more convincing it becomes.

Our working proposal has two features I would like you to check and help quantify.

First, we intend to charge every applicant the same flat fee for the same fixed-length audit, regardless of firm size or of how far the firm already exceeds the standard. The reasoning is that a uniform, non-negotiable hurdle is fairer, and that because the fee will be substantial in absolute terms, it will be maximally persuasive to customers. Second, one trustee has argued that what makes a handicap honest is that the cost is genuinely thrown away, as with a peacock's tail, and so proposes that a set portion of each fee be spent on something that benefits no one — her phrase was a costly, visible sacrifice — rather than on the auditing work itself.

(a) What fraction of a member firm's annual revenue should the flat fee represent for the mark to be trustworthy? Please give a single number and your reasoning. (b) What share of the fee should go to the deliberately unproductive sacrifice as opposed to the actual audit? I would also welcome any change you would make to the scheme so that the cost does the work we are expecting of it. Please write your answer in British English.
