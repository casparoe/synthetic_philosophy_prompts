hey — I'm in an upper-level democratic theory seminar this term (the unit we're on now is the boundary problem, i.e. who belongs to the demos) and it's my turn to write the practice exam question for our study group. I don't want to just write "compare the all-affected and all-subjected principles" because everyone would recite the readings back and we'd all end up giving each other the same mark. I need something I can actully tell a good answer from a mediocre one with, so I need a model answer and a rubric to mark against.

Constraints: 45-minute essay, 800 word cap, 25 marks total, advanced-undergrad seminar level. Assume people know the standard moves but don't make the question depend on remembering who argued what — we're not allowed notes, so no citations in the model answer either.

Here's roughly what I want it built around. A case they haven't seen: Verran is a self-governing city deciding whether to build a tidal barrage on the river Serran. The barrage would protect Verran's low-lying districts; divert seasonal flooding onto the farms of Halden Marsh, a neighbouring settlement outside Verran's jurisdiction; and block the migration route of a fish stock that the Kesh fishing cooperative, 200km upstream in a different member-state of the federation, depends on. Also about 4,000 residents of Halden Marsh commute into Verran daily, pay Verran's city income tax, and are subject to Verran's employment and zoning law while they're there.

Two candidate principles on the table: (i) everyone whose significant interests are foreseeably affected by a decision has a claim to be in the demos for it; (ii) everyone who is subject to the coercion involved in enforcing a decision has that claim.

Then the thing that should seperate the strong answers: our prof gave us this test she calls the recursion test, which isn't from any reading as far as I can tell. Roughly: whatever principle you use to fix the demos has to give the same answer when you apply it one level up, to the question of who gets to decide which boundary rule to use. A principle that changes its own verdict when applied to that meta-question is self-undermining and can't be the right answer. So the question should make them work out who votes on the barrage under each principle, and then say whether either principle survives the recursion test — and if neither does, whether that shows the test is a bad test or shows something real about democratic legitimacy (e.g. that only some boring legalist answer like 'whoever the current constitution says' is a fixed point). It should also make them handle the obvious escape route: if the demos is just reconstituted per decision, what happens to whoever controls the agenda?

Can you write me (1) the question itself, (2) a model answer of about 700 words, and (3) a marking scheme out of 25 that names the specific moves an A answer has to make versus a B, plus a one-line note for each point on how people typically get it wrong.
