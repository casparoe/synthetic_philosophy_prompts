I am leading a single seventy-five-minute seminar on the argument from illusion and the disjunctivist reply to it, for first-year undergraduates in a large introductory course on knowledge and reality. Almost none of them have studied philosophy; their only prior contact with the thought that perception might not put us in touch with the world is pop-culture brain-in-a-vat imagery, which they will arrive treating as both obviously unanswerable and slightly silly. They will not have read anything, and I want the session to work without a single proper name or historical attribution in it.

What I want from you is not the explanations the session should contain but the plan for delivering them. Please set out:

1. The order in which the components must be introduced, and what depends on what. In particular: why the possibility that we are always hallucinating is the wrong door to walk in by; where the claim that a hallucination can be subjectively indistinguishable from a veridical perception has to come relative to the step that infers a common kind of awareness in both cases, and relative to the conclusion that even in ordinary perception we are immediately aware of something mind-dependent; and how much of the pull of the argument has to be secured before the disjunctivist denial of that common-kind step can be made to sound like a substantive move rather than a verbal dodge.

2. The one case that should carry the entire session, what it must have and what it must lack, and why each of the stock candidates fails or succeeds at that job: a straight stick that looks bent in water, a geometric illusion figure, a mirrored or lensed room, a vivid hallucination as of something close at hand, an artificially generated environment indistinguishable from a real one.

3. The misconceptions to expect, in the order in which they will form, and the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. The ones I anticipate are: that the argument is an argument for scepticism about the external world; that disjunctivism is the view that hallucinations do not occur, or that they are not really experiences; that disjunctivism is direct realism under a new name; that the disjunctivist must deny that a hallucination can be subjectively indistinguishable from a perception; and that the mind-dependent item the argument introduces is a private mental picture inspected by an inner eye.

4. One question, or a pair of questions, to put to them at the end, such that a correct answer shows the disagreement has landed and a confident wrong answer reveals which of the misconceptions survived.

5. What I should explicitly decline to settle in seventy-five minutes, and say aloud that I am declining — including whether the argument from illusion is in fact any good.
