Hi — I'm running a 90-minute session of an upper-level undergrad seminar on probability and decision theory, 14 students, and I want a proper in-class activity built around Dutch-book style arguments. They've seen the basic setup already (you post your prices, I take whichever side I like, if your prices are incoherent you lose for sure) and most of them can do the algebra. What they can't feel, and what I want them to feel, is the gap between three things that all sound identical when you say them fast: (i) a book where you lose for sure on every outcome, and I can show you that on the board before anything is resolved; (ii) a set of bets where you lose on every branch of the decision tree but nobody can tell, at the moment the bets are made, that this is one of the losing branches — the case where the stake has to be adjusted later depending on what happens; (iii) a book where you lose with probability 1 but not with certainty. I've seen (ii) called a 'Dutch strategy' and (iii) called a 'Czech book' — if those aren't the terms that come to mind for you, don't worry about it, and please don't hand me a reading list or citations: I can't check them and neither can the room.

The bit I actually care about, though, is throwing a spanner into it. I want them to run into the place where the argument stops working and work out for themselves what it was relying on. Concretely: instead of posting a single price per proposition, some of the bettors are allowed to post a spread — a highest price they'll buy at, a lowest price they'll sell at — and to pass when my offer lands inside the spread. And the betting has a floor and a ceiling: a smallest stake you can place (one chip, everything in whole chips) and a cap on how much total exposure I'm allowed to take on. So an incoherence smaller than one chip's worth can't actually be exploited, and a 'sure' loss that requires me to risk more than my cap is not a sure loss I can deliver. My hoped-for punchline is that the classic argument quietly needs point-valued prices, both sides tradable at the same price, and stakes that can get big enough — and that once you relax those, what survives is a graded, resolution-relative claim, not 'be a probabilist or else'. I'd much rather they discover this by being unable to run the book they just wrote on the board.

So: rules, materials (index cards, poker chips, a die, no real money, nothing to prepare in advance beyond what you tell me to write), a timeline with rough minutes, and roles — I'd like most of the room playing at once rather than two volunteers at the front. The rules and payoffs should be arranged so they hit (i), (ii) and (iii) as discoveries: say a first round where the sharp-price bettors get taken for a loss they can see coming, a second where the spread-posters can't be touched by any single book and the room has to argue about whether that means they're in the clear, and a third where something that looked safe gets got anyway. Then a debrief that maps specific moments onto the distinctions, your honest guesses about what will go wrong the first time I run it (with fixes), and a frank note on what the game can't reproduce — my guess is the move from 'you'd lose for sure' to 'you're being irrational' is one of those, but tell me what else. No bibliography, no citations, no 'as Ramsey says'; I won't be able to verify any of it and I'd rather have something I can actually run on Thursday.
