I'm moderating a half-hour Q&A after a lecture by a visiting scholar who defends what she calls audience-relative textualism: the claim that the correct method for interpreting a statute is whichever method the legislature that enacted it would have expected courts to apply, so that the authority of textualism rests on contingent empirical facts about a given legislature's drafting practices and expectations rather than on notice, democratic legitimacy, or the separation of powers. She opens by quoting from Holy Trinity Church v. United States the familiar rule that a thing may be within the letter of the statute and yet not within the statute because not within its spirit nor within the intention of its makers, and she argues from it that textualists and purposivists have both been looking for the justification of a method in the wrong place.

Draft me eight to ten questions for the session. I want at least one on whose expectations are supposed to count when a statute enacted in the 1930s is applied to facts nobody then envisaged: the enacting legislature's or the current one's. I want one on how the expectations of a multimember body are to be aggregated when its members expect different methods, or have no expectations at all. I want one pressing the charge that the view is purposivism in disguise, since an appeal to what the legislature would have wanted courts to do is an appeal to a counterfactual legislative attitude. I want one on whether, on her account, Congress could switch the correct method by statute, say by directing courts to consult committee reports. And I want one on a municipal ordinance barring vehicles from a public park as applied to a motorized wheelchair, where text and purpose diverge but where the narrow absurdity carve-out she concedes may not clearly reach.

For each question, anticipate the reply she is most likely to make and supply the follow-up that makes it harder to rest on: expect something like legislators know that courts read text, so they expect textualism on the aggregation point; something like my view concerns expected method rather than expected outcome on the purposivism charge; and something like a legislature may choose its interpreters' methods only within the limits of whatever anti-delegation rationale you accept on the statutory-override point. Close with one question about what empirical finding would make her give the view up.
