ok so, an analogy I keep running into in the AI-governance corners of the rationalist sphere, and I've started to suspect it's doing more work than it admits.

People here say things like: the system prompt is legislation, RLHF is case law, whoever writes the model spec is a lawgiver, therefore design decisions are political acts and we should either (a) demand for them the same contestability, legibility and democratic input we demand of actual law, or (b) just own that every default is coercion and stop pretending it's neutral. It descends from the old Lessig line that code is law (his four regulators thing: law, norms, markets, architecture), and it inherits the Winner-style move where a design settles a political question in concrete rather than in statute — low overpasses that keep buses off the beach, the mechanical tomato harvester and the migrant workforce it was built for.

What I want is an audit. Start with the correspondence table: what plays the role of the statute, the legislator, the enforcer, the sanction, the appeal, the jurisdiction, the repeal, the subject of the law. Then where it breaks. My guess is that the load-bearing disanalogies are about authoriship and discretion rather than about coercion: law has a deliberative author, is addressed to subjects, gets enforced ex post by agents who can be merciful or corrupt, admits interpretation and hard cases, and you can break it; code — and a trained model's behavior even more so — has no clear author, attaches to a place or a system rather than to a person, is enforced ex ante by the same causal structure every single time, does no equity, and removes exit instead of pricing it.

There's a cut I keep making that I want you to use or replace: law-shaped constraints (they prohibit, they're addressed to you, they leave the option on the table and then punish you for taking it) versus terrain-shaped ones (they just close the option off, like a wall or a river). If that's the wrong axis, say so and use a better one.

Then the actual question: which of (a) and (b) can the analogy carry, and for the one it can't, where is the conclusion actually coming from? And for the case people mostly use it on now — behavior that was trained rather than written, weights that came out of gradient descent on a corpus, not anyone's decision — is there a better analogy? My partner suggested precedent and common law, someone else suggested something closer to a price or an ecosystem, and I trust neither.

Give me the table, the disanalogies seperated into ones that matter for these two conclusions versus ones that are just noise, a verdict on each conclusion, and a better analogy if there is one. At the end, tell me how confident you are in each of your verdicts and single out the claim you're least sure of — including whether you're quietly pattern-matching the standard objections to Winner because that's what this topic's training data looks like.
