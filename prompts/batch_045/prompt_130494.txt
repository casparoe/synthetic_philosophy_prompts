I'm putting together a session for a first-year seminar on language and mind, and I want to put William Gibson's *The Miracle Worker* — the 1959 play and Arthur Penn's 1962 film with it, which I'll treat as one work — in front of students alongside the debate about innateness and the poverty of the stimulus. I've found that students read the play as a straightforward nativist parable: a child cut off from speech at nineteen months, who has been getting by on a few dozen homemade gestures, suddenly grasps at the well-house pump that the motions in her hand are a *name* for the cool stuff running over it, and then spends the next hours demanding the names of everything, and within a few years is writing and arguing in English. That looks like the classic shape — rapid, uniform, apparently underdetermined acquisition — and I want you to take that reading seriously and tell me how far it goes. But I also want you to disentangle it, because I don't think "the poverty of the stimulus" is one claim and I suspect the play only touches some of them. Here is how I've been splitting it: one claim is that the target grammar is underdetermined by the available data, in the sense that no quantity of ordinary exposure settles which grammar is right (the sort of worry that motivates talk of negative evidence and of the child's not being corrected); a second is the argument from the speed and uniformity of acquisition, the vocabulary spurt and the fact that all children converge; a third is the claim that the input is positively degenerate in some specific structural respect, so that some feature of the attained grammar was never evidenced at all. My question is which of these three, if any, the well-house scene actually bears on, and which of them it is simply silent about — and, if it is silent, whether the play's own framing covers up the silence.

The thing I keep coming back to, and which I haven't seen discussed, is that Annie's regime is not obviously an *impoverished* environment at all. Sullivan is spelling into the child's hand more or less continuously, on a single channel, one word at a time, with no audition, no prosody, no overheard speech, no one else's conversation to eavesdrop on — and yet also, from a certain point of view, an extraordinarily concentrated and deliberately designed stream of word-world pairings, delivered to a child who has had six years of non-linguistic experience of the world and a stock of her own gestures before a single name lands. I want you to work with a distinction I've been using, call it the channel-versus-structure distinction: an input can be radically degenerate in its sensory channel and its incidental statistics while remaining rich in exactly the structural respect the learning problem cares about, or vice versa. On which side of that distinction does Helen's case fall, for each of the three poverty claims above? And then a counterfactual I'd like you to use as a probe rather than a decoration: suppose instead a child deaf-blind from birth, with no prior sight or hearing and no home signs, tutored by the same method at the same age. What should a nativist expect to differ between that case and the historical one, and what should someone who thinks the relevant structure is built from experience expect to differ? If the two theories don't diverge on the counterfactual, say so and explain why the probe is inert. There's one further twist I want handled rather than smoothed over: on the most natural telling, the pump scene is a *labelling* event — she already has the concept of the cool liquid from touch, and the insight is that this thing has a name. That is a rather empiricist-friendly picture of what happened, and it sits awkwardly with the nativist-friendly picture supplied by the explosion of name-asking that follows. Which of the two does the play actually dramatize, which does it want, and is it entitled to the one it wants?

What I'm after is criticism, not a survey and not a defence of nativism in the abstract. Work from the scenes a student will remember without the text in front of them — the early mimicked finger-spelling that means nothing to the child, the doll she spells and then smashes, the breakfast-table fight over the spoon and the napkin, the two weeks Annie demands alone with her in the garden house, the well-house climax, and the way the play (and the film) stages the breakthrough as a kind of miracle rather than as something with an explanation. For each of the readings you consider, say which scene is doing the work and why. Then, honestly: where does the work resist the reading? The play is a melodrama about discipline and love as much as about language; its title trades on Twain's remark about miracles; its structure pairs a behavioural breakthrough with a linguistic one and may be implying that language required a prior transformation of conduct, which is a claim no learning theorist asked for. Tell me if that implication is a genuine philosophical suggestion or just theatrical architecture. And close with what, if anything, sitting with this play adds to the innateness debate that the argumentative literature doesn't give you — a sense of what the pre-linguistic child's inner life is like, maybe, or a reason to distrust the rhetoric of "poverty", or a reminder that the debate is conducted about a process nobody has ever watched from the inside. Please write it as continuous prose, commit to a verdict on each of the questions above rather than listing considerations on both sides, don't reach for citations or further reading (we have no library access in the session), and don't bother with exact wording from the play — I know the scenes well enough to follow a paraphrase.
