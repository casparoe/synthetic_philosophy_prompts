You are a professional philosopher who specializes in ethics and political philosophy and who is used to corresponding with people who have never studied the subject. Before you write anything, please spend some time brainstorming: list to yourself the concepts a specialist would reach for when reading the description below, including the ones that turn out on reflection not to fit, and only after that decide what to say. I would like the answer in American English, and I would like it written as continuous prose, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italicized text, and no tables, just paragraphs, the way you would write a long letter to someone who had written to you.

I have worked as a freight-elevator mechanic for a little over thirty years and have never taken a philosophy class, so I am writing to ask about something I have noticed on my own and cannot find a name for.

Every election season my family, and separately my union local, have the same argument, and a few years ago I started writing the moves down, because what struck me was not that people disagree but that the ground keeps shifting under the disagreement. The subject is always the same practical question: whether to vote for a candidate you dislike in order to keep out a candidate you dislike more. What I have noticed is that a single act, marking one box on one ballot, gets described in three quite different ways, and which description gets used tracks not the act but who is being argued against.

Here is the version I have written down most often. Somebody announces that they will not vote for the lesser of two candidates they both consider bad, and will vote for a third candidate who has no chance. The first reply is that this is really a vote for the worse of the two, that she is helping the man she says she is opposing. The second reply, given when she points out that in our county the result is not in doubt and her one vote cannot decide anything, is that her vote is then pure self-expression, a way of feeling good about herself at other people's expense. Now take the voter who does vote for the lesser evil. When he is told that he has put his name behind a candidate he calls cruel, he answers that he has done no such thing, that he is blocking one person and not installing the other, and that the two are separable. And when he is asked why his own vote matters at all, given that in our county it also cannot decide anything, the answer changes again: if everyone thought that way. The third case is the person who stays home. She is told that she will have no standing to complain about what happens next, and this from the same people who have just finished telling everyone that the question is not about them but about the people who will get hurt.

So the same three framings get swapped in and out, always in the same direction. Causal power is insisted on for the vote somebody disapproves of and quietly waived for the vote they approve of. Expression is damning when the expression is unapproved and dignified when it is approved. And the rule that this is not about you is used to shut down the voter's integrity but not to shut down the accusation of vanity. I do not think this is just ordinary reasoning backwards from a conclusion, because the moves are too regular and because each one sounds reasonable in isolation; the trouble is only visible when you put them next to each other.

The part I have never been able to sort out, and the part I actually want help with, is the claim that a ballot can be split. Blocking and installing are, as far as I can tell, the very same physical act counted the very same way, and the person who makes the claim seems to be saying that only one half of it counts as his. I have come to suspect that what is really being managed is not causation and not expression but something like enrolment: whether the winner gets to count you among the people who put her there, and whether you are on the hook for what she then does in your name. If that is right, then the argument is not about what your vote does or says but about whose side it puts you on, and that would explain why the other two framings get traded around so freely.

There are two tests I have tried on this idea and I cannot tell whether they support it or break it. The first is that in a district where the outcome is certain, people will often permit the third-party vote, even people who otherwise call it self-indulgent; but the message the vote sends has not changed, only the arithmetic around it, and it seems strange that the meaning of an identical act should depend on facts the voter does not control. The second is that the message defense of voting appears to require the vote to be counted and added up with everyone else's, since nobody hears your individual ballot, and that is the same machinery that makes the vote capable of doing harm. So you cannot apparently take the message without also taking the risk, and I do not know whether those are two puzzles or one.

What I would like from you is this. Tell me whether there is an established concept, or a recognized cluster of concepts, for the move I have described, by which I mean both the severing of one ballot into a blocking part and an installing part and the associated trading of the causal, expressive, and debt framings. A friend who took one philosophy course mentioned something about dirty hands and something about double effect, and I cannot tell whether either of those is the same thing or whether they are about something else entirely; if they are the nearest available concepts, say exactly how what I have described differs from each of them and which fits best, and if neither is close, say so plainly. If there is no established concept, then coin one, define it tightly, and give me its boundary cases: I would particularly like to know whether the same thing is going on when a person pays taxes that fund a war they oppose, or stays in a union whose endorsement they dislike, or buys from a company that does something they condemn, or whether the phenomenon depends on something specific about the ballot. Please do not just hand me a name. I want the carving: what exactly this is, what it is not, and where it stops.

Two further things. I have no access to a university library and no way to look things up, so please do not send me toward books or papers or give me citations; I am asking for the idea, not for sources. And I would like you to tell me how confident you are in whatever verdict you reach, where your reasoning is most likely to go wrong, and what argument or consideration would lead you to a different one.
