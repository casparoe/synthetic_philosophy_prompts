Our department is revising how diagnoses are handed to patients, and I want a philosopher's verdict on the argument I've been making for what I call the bookkeeping model.

The background, as I understand it: Hacking's looping effects show that when we classify people, the people classified can come to know the classification and change in response to it, so the kind we set out to describe is a moving target — unlike the kinds of the natural sciences, where what is classified does not react to being classified. My conclusion is that because the target moves, any claim that a diagnosis picks out a stable underlying dysfunction is unverifiable in principle, so the only honest nosology is an explicitly administrative one: group patients by their current reported complaints for the purposes of care planning, service eligibility and audit, assert nothing about hidden causes, and thereby stop looping, since a label that is a mere entry in a ledger rather than a hypothesis cannot feed back into the people it sorts.

A colleague replies that this is just the old descriptive, theory-neutral nosology in new vocabulary, and will loop just as hard. I don't see why, if we genuinely make no causal claim — but I'd like you to press on both steps: is the move from "human kinds loop" to "no claim about an underlying condition is verifiable" sound, and is a category that announces itself as cause-free really insulated from looping, or does it simply loop by another route? If my argument fails, say precisely where it fails rather than substituting a view of your own.

Answer in exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, with no preamble and no closing summary.
