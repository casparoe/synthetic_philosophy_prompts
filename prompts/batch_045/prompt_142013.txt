in my archives & power seminar we got into a fight about absence as evidence and I want an actual number, not 'it depends'. Case: a colonial district office's surviving papers for 1890–1913 (outward correspondence, monthly returns, a register of petitions received) contain basically no first-person statements by the local peasantry — just officials summarising what people supposedly said. My prof floats something she calls the registration ceiling: a bureaucracy can only file a claim it already has a category for, so a silence can mark a category mismatch rather than an absent event, and the two are indistinguishable once you're reading the finding aid. One wrinkle: there's also an index of petitions that were rejected, with a one-line reason each, but the petitions themselves are gone.

My credence is about 0.6 that this silence was produced mostly at the moment of recording (what officals chose to write down, or were able to write down) rather than by later loss, weeding, or decay; my classmate says 0.2. Give me your own number decomposed — how exhaustive this kind of paperwork realistically was, whether documents of that sort would have survived if they had been created, whether the rejected-petitions index cuts one way or the other, who actually did the weeding and on what principles, and whether the silence even has a single location — with a number and one line each, then how they combine. Then tell me which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, what single diagnostic would move it most, and which of us is probably wrong about where the disagreement lives.
