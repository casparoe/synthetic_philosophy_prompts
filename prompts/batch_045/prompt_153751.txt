I'm giving the eight-minute opening remarks at the public launch of a digitized archive next month and I'm completely stuck on how to get through it without saying something false, so I'd really appreciate a hand.

The collection is the surviving intake ledgers, case files and superintendent's correspondence of the Marchmont County Home for the Aged and Infirm (1898–1962) — basically a poorhouse — and it's being released online in the building itself, which is now a community centre, with a small exhibition in the hallway. The room is going to be uncomfortably mixed: the two state archivists who did the cataloguing and built the finding aid; maybe forty local volunteers who transcribed the ledgers over the winter; descendants of people who were in the Home, some of whom only found out about an ancestor because the finding aid went up and some of whom turn out to be descended from the trustees instead; the county commissioners and the foundation officer who paid for the scanning; and a bunch of neighbours who came for the sandwiches. The officer's draft press release says the project is "finally giving a voice to the voiceless," and my problem — I've been going round about this with my sister, who transcribed four hundred pages of the ledgers and says I'm overthinking it and that people just want to hear their grandmother's name — is that that sentence is false in a way I can't repair with a nicer adjective. There are no voices in the boxes. Every document in the collection was written by an official about a resident; there are no letters, no diaries, no oral histories, and no photographs of residents at all (the glass plates are all buildings, staff and boards of trustees). On top of that, the county's out-relief records — the aid given to people in their own homes, i.e. everybody the institution never held — were pulped by the county clerk in 1971 during an office move, so the collection makes it look as if the Home was the only relief that ever existed, which is an accident of what survived rather than a fact about the past. And the archivists, for perfectly good professional reasons, kept the Home's own 1903 filing scheme as the arrangement of the digital collection, which means residents are searched for under headings like "deserted," "no visible means of support," and "feeble-minded" — so any query anyone runs is a query in the superintendent's vocabulary. the funders apparently love that it's "authentic to the original order."

What I want is for the speech to be built out of the different kinds of silence in a collection like this, spelled out so a room of non-specialists actually gets it: the thing that was never written down in the first place; the thing that was written and later destroyed or weeded out; the thing that exists but that nobody in practice can reach; and the hardest one, the thing that is physically there in the file and still isn't the person's — a line in an intake ledger reading "no means of support, husband at the woollen mill, one child" is a record about her rather than by her, and I want the room to feel that the existence of a document can get mistaken for the presence of a person. Coin or pick one plain, non-jargon phrase for that mistake and use it twice. The distinctions have to do real work in what the speech allows itself: it can't promise the descendants that the archive tells them who their grandmother was, it can't perform the recovery of voices that aren't in there, and it also can't be a lecture on archiving as violence delivered to the two people who did the work, to the volunteers, and to the woman who paid for it, in the building where they're all standing. There's one moment where I want to say a single resident's name out loud — say a woman admitted in 1911 who shows up in three documents across fourteen months and then in the burial register — and the speech should decide, out loud, whether saying her name is a recovery or a second taking, and then either say it or refuse to. Eight minutes, spoken, no slides, one small joke at the very most, please don't give me a reading list or citations since I have no library access and I'm not going to attribute anything to anybody — I just need sentences I can actually stand up and say.

After the speech, add a short note (150 words or so) on how confident you are that the distinctions you built the thing around are the right ones to build it around, which single line of the speech you're least sure about, and where you'd expect a working archivist or an actual descendant to push back hardest.
