You are a philosopher of language who works on the semantics and pragmatics of derogatory language, and you are careful, patient, and unwilling to let a well-sounding sentence do argumentative work it cannot do. Please think before you write: first brainstorm, in whatever order they occur to you, as many distinct precise claims as the sentence below could be expressing, and only then sort them, evaluate them, and decide which of them the people who actually repeat it are committed to. Do not settle for the first reading that occurs to you, and do not rescue the sentence by charitably turning it into a thesis nobody who utters it has in mind.

I am a high school student, and I help run a discussion group at my school where we invite people to talk about things that came up badly in the school week. Last week a classmate used a slur for a racial group — not addressed at anyone, just quoted from a song he was playing out loud — and when a teacher told him to stop he said: "It's just a word. Words only have the power you give them." I have now heard that exact sentence do three different jobs, and I want to know whether any of them survives being made precise.

The first job was the one my classmate used it for: to move the responsibility for the offence off the speaker and onto whoever was offended ("you chose to be upset, not me"). The second job is a policy claim some older students made: that our school's approach — the word is banned, and any utterance of it is treated as a serious disciplinary matter — is self-defeating, because prohibition and squeamishness are exactly what keep the word charged, so we should stop treating it as forbidden. The third job is one that several of my friends who belong to the targeted group make: they use the word with each other, and they say that doing so "takes its power away," which sounds like the same idea but may not be.

Two things I have noticed and want you to deal with rather than dodge. First, in the boring sense everything is "just a word," so if the sentence has any content at all it must be a claim about what words contain or what speakers do with them, not about what a word is. Second, one person in the group supported the sentence by appealing to the slogan that the meaning of a word is its use; I suspect that gets things backwards, since use looks like a pattern in a whole speech community rather than something any individual speaker can opt out of, so "we can just decide it no longer means anything" does not seem to follow. Tell me whether I am right about that.

Here is what I would like, as a numbered menu I can bring to the group:

1. The distinct precise theses the sentence could be expressing, ordered roughly from the most trivial to the most radical, with each stated so sharply that you could imagine evidence settling it. Please cover at least: readings on which the derogatory force is part of what the word semantically encodes; readings on which it arises instead from what a speaker does by using it; readings on which offensiveness is simply a matter of hearers' reactions or of community conventions; and readings on which the sentence is really a normative claim about who may use the word rather than a claim about meaning at all.
2. A verdict on each reading: is it a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, a substantive but contested one, or something false or incoherent? If a reading is an empirical claim about how words in fact work, say so and say how loosely we would have to hold it.
3. For each reading, whether it would actually support job one (the hearer, not the speaker, bears responsibility), job two (stop treating the word as forbidden), and job three (in-group reclamation drains its power). I especially want to know whether any reading supports all three, because my guess is that the reclamation claim is doing something quite specific — something about who has standing to use the word — that the classmate's use cannot borrow.
4. Whether the sentence helps at all with cases where the word is merely quoted rather than used, such as in a history lesson or a linguistics example, and how those cases bear on the readings.
5. Which one or two readings you think people who say this sentence in a school corridor are actually intending, regardless of whether they should.

Please do not give me a general essay on slurs, and please do not spend your answer summarising the academic literature on derogatory language; the object here is one sentence and what it can be made to mean. Criticism of the sentence is welcome, but only after the menu is on the table.

Finally, end your reply, after everything else, with a single machine-readable line in exactly this form:
