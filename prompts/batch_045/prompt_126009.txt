I'm writing a paper on what the mid-century smoking and lung cancer controversy can tell us about theories of causation, and I want a disciplined two-way case analysis rather than a survey of the two families of theory. I already know the popular version of the story: association is not causation, a well-known list of considerations for strengthening an inference, a distinguished statistician who insisted the inference was not yet warranted, and an eventual official report that stated the causal conclusion. Please don't spend words on that. What I want is the mapping: what each theory says the participants were actually disagreeing about, which features of the episode each one explains, which it misdescribes or is simply silent about, and what the episode in turn does to the theory.

Here are the facts as I intend to take them. Correct any you think is wrong — I'd rather be corrected than given a fluent answer built on a mistake.

By the mid-1950s, case-control work and then a large prospective cohort of British doctors had produced a strong association with a clear gradient in amount smoked. Randomized assignment of human beings to smoke was not an option: the evidence base was observational, supplemented by animal work, histopathology, dose-response patterns, and, later, follow-up of people who quit. R.A. Fisher, in letters and articles around 1957 and 1958, argued that the association did not license the causal conclusion; my understanding is that he floated a common-cause story on which a constitutional or genetic factor disposes a person both to take up smoking and to develop bronchial carcinoma, and separately raised whether inhaling rather than smoking as such was the operative variable — paraphrase and correct me as needed. By 1964 a US Surgeon General's report stated the causal conclusion, and litigation and regulation followed. Later evidence I want in play includes cohort quit data, geographic and tax variation, public-place smoking bans, and the low-tar episode: machine-measured yields fell, smokers appear to have compensated, and the expected reduction in risk largely failed to show up.

The questions:

1. Under a counterfactual treatment, what exactly is the counterfactual in dispute? Distinguish the population-level claim about rates from the individual-level claim about a particular patient, and say what similarity of worlds is being invoked in each case, given that the nearest non-smoking worlds for a given person are ones in which their constitution or their whole life-history is different. How does the theory dispose of Fisher's common cause in principle, and does it have anything to say at all about how one could ever come to know that the common cause is absent?

2. Under an interventionist treatment, specify the variable set and the intervention variable. Is smoking the variable, or a stand-in for a family of more precise variables — pack-years, tar delivery, depth of inhalation, age at initiation, duration, time since cessation — and does the answer change which causal claim is true? What invariance is asserted, over what range and against what background? Then the part I care most about: can interventionism say anything about a claim nobody ever intervened on? I have been working with a notion I call a surrogate intervention — something that is not an intervention on the variable of interest but is supposed to stand in for one: migration between populations with different rates, occupational cohorts, tax and ban variation, quit cohorts, and the low-tar case as one where the intervention was aimed at the wrong variable. Is there a principled line between these and interventions proper, or does admitting them cost the theory whatever distinguished it from a counterfactual or broadly evidential account?

3. Which features of the episode does each theory explain, and which does it miss? In particular: is the famous battery of considerations better reconstructed as evidence for invariance under interventions, as evidence of counterfactual dependence, or as neither? Which theory is better placed to explain why the demand for a single specific cause was a wrong turn for a multifactorial disease? And is the sociology of the episode — industry funding, a manufactured controversy, professional norms about when to assert causation — merely outside both theories, or does one of them quietly presuppose an evidential community it never models?

4. What does the case do to the theories? My suspicion is that both were built around general causation and that the real pressure point is actual causation, which is exactly what tort law wanted: whether smoking caused this plaintiff's cancer when asbestos and radon were also in play, and the rough heuristic of a doubling of risk. Does the episode support deflationism about actual causation, or a two-tier view on which interventionism handles the type-level claim and something counterfactual or proportional handles the token-level one? Is that hybrid stable, or does it concede that interventionism was never a complete account?

5. One counterfactual about the episode itself: if human trials had been feasible in 1950, does interventionism predict that the dispute dissolves quickly? Consider long latency, noncompliance, and rare outcomes. If the honest answer is that a trial would have been slow and hard to read anyway, does that show the theory is conflating the meaning of a causal claim with the best evidence for it?

A compact correspondence table up front — what plays the role of the causal relata, the modality, and the evidence in each theory — would help me check the mapping. Where a theory has nothing to say, say so rather than forcing it to speak.

As you go, mark how confident you are in each substantive claim: separate what is standard in the literature, what is contested, and what is your own reconstruction. Then single out the one claim you are least confident about and say what would change your mind. Also flag any of my factual premises you think are shaky instead of accommodating them.
