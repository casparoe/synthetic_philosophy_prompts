I am writing a paper for a graduate seminar on personal identity, and I keep losing my grip on one move in Hofstadter's treatment of how a self can persist in other people, so I want a number and I want to see the machinery that produces it. Here is the proposition I want your credence on, stated as precisely as I can manage: that one and the same I, the single first-person subject that is you or me, can at a given time be realized partly in its original brain and partly in the brains of other living people, who do not merely resemble, imitate, or remember the original person but literally house some of her, so that part of what it is for her to go on is a matter of going on in them. I am not asking about an uploaded copy or a transplanted duplicate. The case I want assessed is the one Hofstadter is actually interested in, where what sits in the other brain got there slowly, by years of conversation and exposure, so that it was grown there rather than delivered there, and where it is present at low fidelity, perhaps a few percent of the original pattern, more in some people than in others. I also want the claim read literally rather than sentimentally: not that people influence one another, which nobody denies, and not that we speak loosely about someone living on in her children or her students, but that the very same person is, in some robust and non-metaphorical sense, partly located in a brain that is not hers, and is so while she is still alive and fully functioning.

My own credence is about 0.3. Someone else in the seminar puts his at about 0.75, and I would like you to tell me where you think that gap most likely lives. Give me your own number, but give it to me decomposed into the sub-questions it depends on, with a rough probability and one line of justification for each, an account of how they combine into the final figure, a statement of which term you are least confident in, and a statement of which single consideration or finding would move the number most. The terms I want you to include, along with any others you think belong, are these: whether facts about personal identity are facts about degree of pattern-preservation at all, rather than all-or-nothing facts about an organism or some brute further fact; whether one pattern realized in several substrates at once counts as one person, as several overlapping persons, or as no person at all, which is the branching problem in its awkward form; whether the causal and functional integration that a single mind seems to require can be achieved across brains, given that the bandwidth available between two brains is many orders of magnitude below the bandwidth within one, and given what is known about split-brain patients about how much intra-cranial integration a single mind actually needs; whether a partial and degraded copy, of a few percent, can carry identity at all as opposed to resemblance or causal influence, and whether identity even admits of degrees; whether persistence requires that there be a subject for whom the copied patterns are first-personally available, when the patterns running in a friend's brain are not introspectively available to him as a second viewpoint but only as dispositions that shade his own single stream of experience; whether provenance matters, that is, whether a copy that was grown in someone by exposure has a better, worse, or merely equal claim to be the original person than an intrinsically identical copy that was transmitted all at once; and whether the question has a determinate answer in the first place, or whether it dissolves into a stipulation about how far the word I can be stretched before it stops doing work.
