I teach a graduate seminar on scientific credit, and I would like you to argue for a position I am not yet convinced of.

The familiar multiples — Newton and Leibniz on the calculus, Darwin and Wallace on natural selection — are standardly used to deflate the individual discoverer: if a result was "in the air," the reasoning goes, credit for it is close to arbitrary, a matter of who happened to publish first. I want the reverse argued, using a distinction I will stipulate.

Call a discovery content-inevitable when, had its actual author not existed, some identifiable contemporary would have reached essentially the same proposition within a few years. Call it practice-inevitable when that alternate's version would also have carried the machinery by which the result actually entered scientific practice: its notation, its proof or experimental techniques, its canonical examples, and the problems it was first aimed at. Most celebrated multiples, I suspect, are content-inevitable but practice-contingent.

Please argue that for discoveries in this third class our priority-based institutions of credit — eponymy, prizes, patent priority, the norm of citing whoever published first — track the wrong thing: they reward being first to a proposition, when what did the historical work was the construction of a transmissible practice. Argue further that the upshot is not the abolition of credit but its redistribution, toward those who made results usable: the notation-builders and expositors, and the "losers" whose formulations were better suited to uptake. Then defend that redistribution against the two objections I find hardest — that practice-building is visible only in hindsight, so such credit could not be administered in real time, and that a practice-contingent result is really several discoveries rather than one, so there is no single item of credit to redistribute.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the thesis you will defend — and then give the reasoning. No preamble, and no restatement of my question.
