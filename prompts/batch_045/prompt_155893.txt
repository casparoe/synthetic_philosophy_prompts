I run the weekly reading group for the grad students in our department, and two of them went at it last week over a draft paper, and since I'm moderating the follow-up session I have to get clear on who actually landed what.

The draft is about outcome luck: does someone deserve more blame when a careless act of theirs happens to cause something bad than when the very same careless act causes nothing? Jonahs thesis is that the intuition that the outcome matters is an artifact of how the standard pairs are written, and his evidence is two cases he made up himself (he calls them intuition pumps, Dennett's coinage, which he reminds us of every single session).

His first case is a matched pair. Ada is a hospital courier who leaves a crate of blood products unattended in a corridor for forty-five minutes while she goes for coffee, which is against protocol. In one version nothing happens; in the other a thief walks off with the crate and a patient dies because the blood wasn't there. Jonah's variant is the same pair, except that in the second version the patient dies at that same moment of something unrelated and the crate was never needed, so whether Ada left it made no difference at all to the death. He tried it on about a dozen people and says they still hold Ada more to blame in the second version. That's move (1): the extra blame isn't tracking her causal contribution to the death, so it's contamination from there being a death in the story at all — an artifact of framing, not a verdict about her.

Simone's reply, (2): the variant no longer varies the thing in dispute. What separates the standard versions is that the risk Ada imposed got realized, and once you cut the causal chain you have deleted the candidate difference-maker, so both sides predict that it makes no difference, and a case like that can't tell them apart. A match that is never struck can't show there's no fire. She adds that his dozen readers may simply not have noticed the death was unrelated, and that "more to blame" in his question is slippery anyway.

Jonah then says fine, hold the causation fixed and vary only the packaging, (3): in one wording the second version ends "a patient died because the blood wasn't there," in the other it ends "the hospital recorded one additional death that night, later attributed to the missing crate" — identical facts, less vivid — and people's ratings drop. If the verdict tracked what she deserves, it wouldn't move when you swap the nouns around.

Simone gets the last word, (4): that shows first-pass reactions are noisy, not that they're aimed at the wrong thing; the verdict that should count is the one a reader gives after seeing both wordings side by side with the mismatch pointed out, and Jonah has no way to separate "contaminated intuition" from "intuition plus measurement noise." She also says his standard for a clean intuition is one no intuition anywhere could pass, so the argument is rigged. And she points at a third case of his, involving an autonomous delivery drone and an encrypted audit log, and says nobody has an intuition about that setup at all: readers are deriving an answer from principles and he is then calling the output an intuition, which is just an argument in a Halloween costume.

Can you referee this for me? I'd like the answer as exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no preamble and no summing-up at the end: one item for each of moves (1) through (4), saying whether it lands, half-lands, or misses and the one reason why, plus a fifth item on whatever it is that both of them are missing.
