I would like help reconstructing the implicit metaphysics of an institutional practice that I think quietly takes a stand on a question most of the people operating it would deny having an opinion about: how a person persists through time.

Start from this practice, which I will describe rather than cite, since the legal details vary and are not what I am asking about. Assume a jurisdiction of the following shape. A competent adult may record instructions that are to govern what is done to her later, at a time when she is no longer competent: a living will refusing ventilation or assisted feeding in specified circumstances, a durable power of attorney naming a surrogate, and — in mental health law — the so-called Ulysses arrangement, in which someone with recurrent mania or psychosis authorizes in advance the very treatment she knows she will later refuse. Two features of how such instruments are in fact handled strike me as doing philosophical work.

The first is the direction of authority. The earlier instruction overrides the contemporaneous, sometimes vehemently opposed wishes of the later patient: the woman with advanced dementia who is, by all reports, contented and says she wants to go on living; the manic patient who feels magnificent and demands discharge. It overrides them *because* the later patient is judged incompetent. Yet a competent patient may revoke any such instruction at any time, for any reason or none at all, and that revocation is treated as conclusive even when it contradicts everything she earlier said she valued. So the power to speak for the person tracks a capacity ascribed to a stage, not the intensity, coherence, or sincerity of that stage's desires.

The second is the fallback. Where no instruction exists, surrogates and courts are standardly directed to apply a substituted-judgment test — decide as the patient herself would have decided, on the evidence of her prior values — and to retreat to an objective best-interests test only when those prior values are simply unrecoverable. The best-interests test is treated as a regretted substitute, not as the default.

Now two further things, which I take to be the genuinely diagnostic part.

The first is a contrast *within* the law's treatment of earlier and later selves. In property and contract, the earlier self can bind the later self's assets across very long stretches of time — a forty-year mortgage, a use-restriction attached to land long after the person who agreed to it has sold it and moved away — but she cannot bind the later self's body or choices in the same way: courts refuse to compel personal service, and no one may contract into servitude, however freely. The earlier self's authority over the later self's *stuff* is nearly unlimited in duration; her authority over the later self's *person* is not. Medicine appears to run this backwards. A living will or Ulysses directive is precisely an earlier self's control over the later self's body, at its most intrusive — whether to be sedated, restrained, fed, or left to die — and it is honored at a time when the later self is objecting. Is the medical practice an exception to a principle the rest of the law holds, or are the two practices drawing a distinction I am misdescribing?

The second is a reform I want you to evaluate rather than assume is coherent. Suppose a hospital system adopts what its authors call a stage-relational policy. Once a clinician certifies that a patient has crossed a threshold of psychological discontinuity from the person who executed her directive, the directive is no longer to be enforced *as her own contemporaneous consent*; it is to be enforced as a constraint inherited from a predecessor — the way an easement or a conditional bequest binds someone who never agreed to it — and the later stage is to be registered as a distinct welfare subject whose current experiences receive their own independent weight in the decision, tallied alongside rather than beneath the predecessor's instructions. Call this the temporal-parts reform.

My question throughout is: what does the existing practice presuppose about persistence — about whether the person is wholly present at each moment of her life (endurantism, as I understand the term) or has temporal parts, with persistence being a matter of earlier and later stages standing in the right relations (perdurantism)? Two sub-questions matter especially. Does the practice *strictly require* one of these, or is the metaphysics underdetermined in the way I suspect, with the real work being done by some normative view about autonomy or practical identity that could be bolted onto either? And does the practice harbour an internal inconsistency — I have in mind the way it seems to use bare biological or organismic continuity to establish that the directive is *hers* at all, in cases like advanced dementia or the permanent vegetative state, while using psychological continuity and narrative to establish *which* stage's values are authoritative? My lay sense is that those are two different theories of personal identity, and I want to know whether the practice is entitled to both.

**1. The practice restated** — about 100 words. Say back to me, in your own terms, the feature of the practice you take to be doing the metaphysical work, so I can see whether we are looking at the same thing.

**3. Strict presupposition versus comfortable fit** — about 200 words. Separate what the practice cannot make sense without from what merely sits well with it. If several theories of persistence license the same rules, say so explicitly and say what would defeat the underdetermination.

**4. Two reconstructions compared** — about 300 words. **5. The contract and property asymmetry** — about 200 words. Explain whether the law's willingness to let an earlier self bind a later self's assets indefinitely, but not her person, undercuts or supports whatever you reconstructed in section 2, and whether medicine really is an exception.
