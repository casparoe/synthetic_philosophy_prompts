I am a PhD student in philosophy, and I am revising a paper in which I argue that the question "why be moral?" is not one question with several competing answers but a family of questions that come apart as soon as one stops holding the agent's circumstances fixed. To sharpen the argument I have built a small testing apparatus, and I would like you to work through it with me.

The setup. Take Glaucon's challenge in Book II of the Republic: the ring of Gyges, which confers invisibility and therefore impunity, together with the paired portrait of the perfectly just man universally believed to be unjust and the perfectly unjust man reputed to be just. The claim is that anyone with the ring would act unjustly, and that anyone in the just man's predicament would come to think justice worthless — from which it is supposed to follow that what we call justice is prized for its consequences rather than for itself.

My apparatus. I treat the ring not as a single case but as a set of knobs. Turning different knobs strips the agent of different contingent features that an answer to the challenge may be covertly relying on. Call an answer's *piggyback set* the list of features of the agent and her situation that the answer must hold fixed in order to get any leverage; call a ring specification *severance-complete* for a given answer if it removes every feature in that answer's piggyback set. My working thesis is that no familiar answer survives a ring that is severance-complete for it.

The variants. Each includes the baseline power of undetectable action.

R1. Impunity only. Actions are undetectable and unsanctioned. Nothing else changes.
R2. Affective severance. As R1, plus: while the ring is worn the agent feels no guilt, shame, or anticipatory dread, and is perceptually insulated from what she does — those she harms never appear to her as suffering.
R3. Causal inertness. As R1, plus: what the wearer does has no causal consequences at all; the world unfolds exactly as it would have had she acted justly, and she knows this.
R4. Universalization. As R1, except that every other agent also has a ring, and this is common knowledge.
R5. Character drift. As R1, plus: repeated unjust use progressively rewires the wearer's motives, so that she ceases to care about justice and ceases to be pained by her own injustice; she foresees this in advance.
R6. Mnemonic severance. As R5, plus: she retains no memory of what she did under the ring, nor of having used it; the post-ring person is, from the inside, the person who never acted.

The answer-types. Please treat these as types rather than as ascriptions to particular authors.

A. The prudential-contractarian answer: injustice is instrumentally bad for the agent even under impunity — uncertainty about detection, the costs of vigilance and of managing accomplices, exposure to retaliation by other undetectable agents, and the loss of the gains of cooperation.
B. The constitutivist answer: the demands of justice are constitutive of agency or of practical rationality, so the question cannot coherently be raised from a standpoint outside them; the ring confers power, not exemption.
C. The psychic-order answer: justice is a well-ordered condition of the agent's own motives, and that order is intrinsically better for its possessor regardless of detection, reputation, or outcome.
D. The concession answer: if an agent really could be severed in these ways and knew it, there would be nothing left to say to her — but that is a reason to doubt that the case is a genuine counterpossibility for creatures like us, or that we ever wanted a reason that binds every conceivable agent.

What I want from you.

1. Rank R1 through R6 by how severe a challenge each poses to the claim that there is reason to be just — that is, by how much dialectical work each does against a defender of justice. Give the result as a tier list, with a one-line justification per tier and an ordering within tiers.

2. For each answer-type A through D, state its piggyback set in the form "cannot survive R__," and assign a fragility score from 0 to 10, where 10 means the answer loses all leverage the moment any knob is turned up. Then rank the four answer-types by overall resilience across the variants.

3. A worry about my own thesis, which I want you to assess rather than flatter: a severance-complete ring may be self-undermining. Strip away sympathy, memory, causal efficacy, and social embedding, and what remains may no longer be the kind of thing the original question was about; on that reading the variants are not progressively harder versions of one test but progressively emptier ones. Rate this objection from 0 to 10 for dialectical strength, and say which of your rankings in parts 1 and 2 would have to be revised if it succeeds.

Please keep the whole reply under about 900 words, use the labels I have given, and do not spend words on literature, on bibliography, or on summarizing the Republic.
