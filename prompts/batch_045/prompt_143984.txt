I run a monthly no-slides lunch thing at work for about 18 people — half backend engineers, half support and design — 60 minutes, in a room with a projector I'd rather not touch. We ship a customer support chatbot, and every few weeks someone says in standup something like "it obviously doesn't understand anything, it's just predicting tokens," someone else says "how is that different from you," and the thread dies. I want to do a session on what we're actually doing when we use behavior as the test for whether something has a mind, but I don't want to give a talk, and I definately don't want to just rerun the parlor game everyone's already heard of — I tried a straight version of that once and two people just answered "bot" to everything and it was over in ten minutes.

Constraints: no lecture up front, and I don't want anyone to say the name of the famous test out loud until the last ten minutes. What I want people to feel rather than be told is (a) that a behavioral verdict is a property of the *pair* — the interrogator's probes plus the channel the entity actually has available — not a measurement of the entity on its own, and (b) that the criterion fails in two opposite directions at once: it can wave through something that's pure surface, and it can lock out a perfectly real mind that can't use the channel. A couple people on my team are extremely terse in chat and one writes like a poet, which I suspect is going to matter and I don't yet know how.

Some mechanics I've been toying with — steal, combine or replace them freely: a relay layer that strips typing-speed and latency tells and normalizes spelling; interrogators wagering confidence under a scoring rule that punishes confident wrong calls harder than it rewards correct ones; one participant assigned to be a real human being who is only allowed to answer in five-word sentences or only in the form of questions (an actual mind, in the room, made unreadable by the format); and a second round where the same answers get re-judged with the questions stripped out. I need roles, materials, a minute-by-minute timeline for 60, the payoff scheme written out concretely, and a debrief that maps specific moments onto (a) and (b). Also: what will predictably go wrong. I'm already expecting the know-it-all who says "bot" every time, the restricted human who gets bored and breaks character, and somebody quietly getting offended at being classified as a machine. And be honest about what the game can't show — my guess is the big one is that no finite transcript can rule out a really enormous lookup table, so whatever we run in an hour can't even in principle make the point about surface vs. understanding; tell me what I have to supply myself at the end and how to do it without the last ten minutes turning into a lecture. I'm an engineer with no philosophy background, so no vocabulary I won't have, but don't water it down either. Bonus if you can land the room on how the same style of criteria shows up when people argue about animals, or about patients who can't move or speak — that's where I actually want us to end up.
