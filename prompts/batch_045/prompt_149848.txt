hey — im trying to wrap my head around something and wanted your take.

when someone has major surgery, theyre usually put under general anestesia. Often they also get a drug that paralyses their muscles so they dont move during the operation. The anestesia team then has to figure out whether the patient is really unconscious. Since the patient cant move, wince, or try to sit up, the usual signs that a person is awake arent available. They look at things like heart rate, blood pressure, sweating, or some monitor of brain activity, and they try to dose the drugs right so the patient isnt aware. Every once in a while — maybe 1 or 2 cases in a thousand, i think — a patient later reports having been awake: they heard stuff, felt pain, and were terrified because they couldent move or signal anyone. When that happens, hospitals treat it as a serious harm, apologize, and often provide psychological help.

heres the bit that puzzles me. We act as if the paralysis is risky precisely because it can hide consciousness, not because it makes you unconscious; the paralytic itself doesnt stop you feeling pain or being awake. And we trust brain-based monitors, when we have them, as evidence about whether anyone is home. But we also know the monitor isnt perfect, and in the end we often rely on the patients later report to know for sure that they were conscious.

So: what theory of consciousness does this practice presuppose? More specifically, against the backdrop of the hard problem — the puzzle of why all this physical processing in the brain is accompanied by any subjective experience at all — what must the practice assume about what consciousness is, about its relation to behavior, and about its relation to brain activity? Does it strictly presuppose that consciousness is something over and above any observable behavior, so that a totally paralyzed body can still have experiences? Does it rule out a behaviorist view on which being conscious just is being disposed to behave in certain ways? Does it require a specifically neurobiological view, or would any view that ties consciousness to brain activity, or even a dualist one, license the same practice? And is the practice philosophically underdetermined: are there several different theories of consciousness that would all say yes, worry about hidden awareness and use brain monitors, even though they disagree about the hard problem? Finally, if we replaced the implicit view with a straightforward behaviorist one — consciousness is just behavior or the disposition to behave — what exactly would have to change in how surgery, paralysis, and monitoring are done? And if we went the other way and adopted a view where the felt quality of experience is an extra ingredient that doesnt just fall out of brain activity, would the practice need to change at all, or is it already neutral on that?
