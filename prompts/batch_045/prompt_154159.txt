I read a magazine piece about patients with pain asymbolia — brain damage leaves them able to report where a pain is and how strong it is, and to say that it is pain, while being entirely unbothered by it — and I've been turning over ever since what exactly is supposed to be the bad part of pain. My gut verdicts, case by case:

(a) The asymbolic patient: nothing bad is happening to them. I wouldn't push morphine on them. The damaged tissue is bad for them, but in the way a silent infection is bad, not because of how it feels.
(b) Me with a broken arm: obviously bad.
(c) The burn of hot chili, the ache of a long climb, the sting of a too-hot bath: I seek these out, would pay to repeat them, and afterwards don't wish I hadn't felt them. Not bad, or bad but worth it — I genuinely can't tell which of those I think.
(d) A soldier who doesn't notice a leg wound until the firefight ends: no verdict, I go back and forth.
(e) Pain during a procedure I consented to knowing exactly how much it would hurt, and would consent to again: still bad while it lasted.
(f) A friend who has trained himself over years — genuine stoic discipline — not to mind pain: I still want to say it's bad for him, and more bad than I want to say about the asymbolic patient, even though both report not minding. This is the one I keep reversing on.
(g) Someone who enjoys pain with no achievement or afterwards story attached, a masochist: I find myself wanting to say it's still bad for them, and that I'd rather not be the one causing it, though I can't say why.
(h) A friend who can't stand the sound of people chewing, or the feel of a certain fabric, to the point of real misery: something bad is happening to them, even though nothing is damaged.
(i) A made-up case: someone's wiring is crossed so that tissue damage produces a sensation they like, and they go looking for injuries. I don't think anything bad is happening to them, and I don't think that changes when the tissue damage is real.
(j) Another made-up case: the dislike is intact but there is no felt sensation at all — a pure, objectless aversion switched on and left running, with nothing to feel. Bad, I think.

My tentative rule is that pain is bad to the extent that the person is averse to it at the time. That gives me (a), (c), (h), (i) and (j) cleanly, and it seems to fail on (e), (f) and (g). So repair it if it can be repaired, or replace it with something that returns as many of the verdicts as possible — and tell me which of my verdicts your account cannot accommodate, which one you would overturn, and why that one is the cheapest to lose.

Two cases I haven't settled: a deer with a broken leg thrashing in a ditch, which can't report either way, and a hospital drug that strips the unpleasantness out of pain while leaving the sensation exactly as it is, offered as a routine to every patient. What does your account say about each?

Finally, tell me what would change your mind about which of my verdicts to give up, and where you think your own reasoning is most likely to be wrong.
