I convene a fortnightly film-and-philosophy evening for my university's philosophy society, and the room is always split roughly half and half between physics students who have taken a first course in quantum mechanics and literature students who have taken no science since school. Last night we screened Everything Everywhere All at Once, and afterwards I had a long argument with a friend who is finishing a doctorate in theoretical physics, which I have not been able to settle. Her position was that the film is essentially popular Everett — a multiverse of branching, equally real outcomes — and that its emotional resolution is a genuine, rather moving answer to the worry that many worlds makes nothing matter: Evelyn chooses to be kind in the one branch she finds herself in, and the film vindicates her against the everything-at-once nihilism of Jobu Tupaki. I don't think either half of that survives contact with the details, and I would like a reading I can put in front of the group in a fortnight, one that takes both the film and the interpretation debate seriously rather than using the film as a poster for one camp.

Three things I want pressed. First, the verse-jumping mechanism: it requires branches that have already diverged to remain causally and epistemically accessible to one another, whereas what makes something a branch in the decoherence picture is precisely that it has become dynamically isolated and no longer interferes with its siblings. On the face of it the film's ontology is closer to something like modal realism — a plurality of concrete, equally real worlds, none privileged as the actual one — than to Everettian branching, except that in the standard modal picture the worlds are causally sealed off from one another and here they visibly are not. Second, the branch points in the film track human decisions: Evelyn stays or leaves, and there is a world for each. Decoherent branching is driven by the amplification of microscopic events and mostly has nothing to do with anyone's choices, so I suspect the film is quietly running a picture on which agency generates worlds. Third, and the part I care about most: the film's branches look amplitude-flat. No world is presented as weightier, thicker or more probable than any other, and that is exactly the limiting case in which Everettians have the hardest time recovering anything like the Born probabilities, because there is no measure over branches for anybody to be uncertain about. My friend says the film's answer to nihilism is untouched by this; I think the reverse, that flat measure is precisely what makes it hardest to say that what I do here matters, since the number of worlds in which Evelyn is kind and the number in which she is not are both enormous, and neither counting branches nor weighting them seems to give you a reason to care about this one. I have started calling this the flat-measure worry, and I would like to know whether I have stumbled on something real or simply confused myself.

So: give me a reading of the film through the three interpretations — Everett, Copenhagen and Bohm — that is honest in both directions, one that neither reduces the film to an illustration nor bends the physics to fit it. Where does the physics illuminate a scene, and where does the film get the physics wrong badly enough that I should not use it with students? I want you to take up two images in particular: the everything bagel, and the silent sequence in the world of two rocks. Is the bagel best read as a superposition of everything, as a collapse into an indifferent state, or as something that fits none of the three interpretations? Does the rock world do philosophical work about what is left when you delete the observers, or is it just an affecting silence? On Bohm, where there is one occupied configuration and the rest are empty waves, the other Evelyns are causally and emotionally load-bearing in a way that empty branches cannot be — which makes me wonder whether the film is committed to something Bohmianism specifically rules out, or whether there is a defensible Bohmian reading on which Evelyn's actual life is the one occupied trajectory and the borrowed lives are a moral rather than a physical resource. Close by saying whether the film adds anything to the debate that the arguments alone do not; my suspicion is that the interesting claim is about the phenomenology of living as if one branch mattered, but I cannot make it precise. Two constraints on the answer, please: write it in British English, and keep it free of formalism — no equations, no bra-ket notation — without condescending to the physicists in the room, since I would rather you named the distinction you are relying on than smoothed it over. And I want to know where you are standing: mark your confidence in each major interpretive claim as high, medium or speculative; keep claims about the physics separate from claims about the film and from guesses about what the directors intended; and tell me plainly if you are reconstructing a plot detail from memory and might have it wrong. Finally, if my framing is confused — if it is a category mistake to read a film about family and grief as making claims about the measurement problem at all, or if the flat-measure worry as I have stated it is not a problem anyone actually faces — say so directly instead of complying politely. I would much rather be corrected before I stand up in front of forty people than after.
