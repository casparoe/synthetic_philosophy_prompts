Stuck on an argument my decision-theory reading group came up with. It seems to prove way more than any of us believe, but I cant find the bad step.

(1) Your credence in H just is the odds you count as fair: you would pay p dollars for a ticket that pays 1 dollar if H is true and nothing otherwise, and you would also sell such a ticket for p dollars.
(2) If your credences violate the probability axioms, some set of bets each of which you count as fair will together lose money in every possible world.
(3) Being disposed to accept a package you yourself can see loses money for sure is a defect in you, not just bad luck, so it is irrational.
(4) So rationality requires probabilism: credence 1 in every logical truth, 0 in every contradiction, and credences in H and not-H summing to exactly 1, for every H whatsoever.
(5) Whether some unsolved claim in arithmetic follows from the axioms is settled by those axioms, so (4) requires me to already have extreme credence in it; and a bookie who computes faster than I do can always assemble the book.
(6) But be logically omniscient is not a norm I accept: nobody can meet it, and it is not what we are praising when we call someone rational.
(7) So one of the steps above is wrong.

My friend says it does not matter that no such bookie exists, since the argument is really about the shape of your dispositions rather than about being exploited. I would rather keep the core of (4), that incoherent credences are defective, than swallow (6). Which step do you reject, what does rejecting it cost, and does the Dutch book argument for probabilism survive once that step is gone or does it just end up assuming what it was supposed to show?
