For thirty-eight years I worked as an experimental physicist, most of it on detector calibration and background estimation, which meant that I spent my professional life producing and defending numbers of a particular kind: efficiencies, confidence intervals, significances quoted to two figures. I was trained, without ever being explicitly told so, to think of these as long-run relative frequencies — what would come out if we could run the experiment again and again. It never occurred to me to ask what the numbers meant. Since retiring I have begun reading philosophy, and I find that my unexamined allegiance is one option among three, and that all three of them appear to fail at precisely the point where I now need one of them to work.

The occasion is this. I sit, as a lay member, on the board of a regional flood authority. We must decide, once, how to spend a sum that is large enough to do one of two things and not both. The decision turns on a number: the chance that the ageing tidal barrier in the Carron valley will be overtopped at least once in the next twenty years. One hydrological model, calibrated against ninety years of gauged records, puts it at thirty-five per cent. A rival model, structurally different and fitting the same record equally well, puts it at twelve. The two disagree not because one has worse data but because they disagree about the shape of the tail — how the frequency of extreme flows behaves beyond the range of anything ever recorded. No extension of the historical record, however long, could settle that disagreement, and the climate of the next twenty years is not the climate the record was drawn from. The event, moreover, will happen at most once; the barrier will be replaced at the end of the period whatever happens.

So I am left with a number that seems to be about one unrepeatable stretch of river, produced by a method whose official justification is in terms of repeatable ones, and contradicted by an equally well-fitted alternative. And whatever we decide, someone will afterwards ask whether the number was right.

I would like an essay that takes up three questions and answers them.

First: what proposition, if any, does a statement like thirty-five per cent assert here? Is it a claim about the valley, a claim about our evidence, or a claim about a merely hypothetical sequence of valleys? Second: could there be a fact of the matter as to which of the two models is correct in the tail, and if so, what kind of fact would it be — the sort we could ever get evidence for, or only the sort we could be entitled to assert? Third, and for me the crux: I have come to think that what a working scientist needs from a probability claim is not exactly truth but auditability. A usable interpretation should explain how such a claim could be shown to have been wrong — or vindicated — by evidence we could in principle have had, without presupposing that the case can be repeated. I would like you to test the three interpretations against that requirement as well as against their more familiar difficulties, and to say which of them, if any, survives it.

Finally, I would like a practical verdict, not a survey: tell me what I should say to the board about what the number entitles us to do, and how the decision should be recorded so that the retrospective question — was it right? — has an honest answer. Please weigh the strongest objection to whichever view you defend, and do not conclude that each interpretation is useful in its own domain; I have read enough to know that is the available escape, and it is the one thing I do not want. Argument rather than bibliography, please: the journals are beyond my reach now, and it is the reasoning I am after.
