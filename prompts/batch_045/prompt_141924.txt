I have designed type and taught a lettering workshop for about fifteen years, and there is a thing I keep running into that I don't have a word for. A few years ago I gave thirty students the same instruction: draw an 'a' -- any 'a', in any material, any size, any degree of abstraction. What came back included a marker scrawl that was essentially one continuous stroke; a shape built from three disconnected dots and a bar; a stencil 'a' with the counters bridged; a photograph of a shadow; a cookie; and several perfectly conventional letterforms in wildly different styles. No two of them shared a single measurable feature -- no common stroke count, no closed bowl in every case, no common number of terminals, no skeleton they all contained. Yet every person in the room identified all thirty instantly and without hesitation, and -- this is the part that interests me most -- they also agreed, to a surprising degree, about which ones were *good* a's, which were merely acceptable, and which were ugly, lazy, or wrong. So there seems to be both a boundary (in or out) and a standard (better or worse), and neither one appears to run along any feature I can isolate; any feature I propose, I can draw an 'a' without.

Three further observations make me think I am not just looking at a fuzzy category. First, the identity seems partly borrowed from its neighbors: a stencil 'a' shown on its own was read by most people as a broken 'o' or a 'c', but placed in a sentence set in the same stencil face it was read as 'a' without a pause; and when I redraw the 'o' or the 'e' of a family, the 'a' often has to change too, or it stops looking like itself. Second, there is an asymmetry in how the capacity is acquired: I can recognize and grade an 'a' I have never seen before, in a script style invented yesterday, but show me a letter from an alphabet I don't read and I get neither the recognition nor the quality judgment -- I can't tell a good one from a clumsy one at all. Third, a friend who works in machine learning says this is just a classifier trained on examples, and my objection is that her classifier's boundary moves when the training set moves, whereas the judgments in my workshop were stable across people and did not seem to be up for negotiation -- someone who insisted that a perfectly ordinary double-story 'a' "isn't really an 'a'" would strike us as confused rather than as having different tastes. A philosopher at a dinner party, when I described all this, said "family resemblance," but as I understand it that idea needs overlapping similarities chaining the cases together, and here I cannot find any overlaps at all; another said "essence" and "Platonic form," which felt like relabeling rather than explaining.

So: is there an established concept -- in philosophy, in psychology, in typography, anywhere -- for this exact thing, and if there are several nearby ones, which fits and where does each one break against the cases?
