Been chewing on this for a couple of weeks and I want an audit, not a survey. At an ACX meetup a few weeks ago someone in my reparations reading group (we're doing US slavery/Jim Crow mainly, plus a couple of colonial cases) keeps leaning on one analogy, and I think it's doing a lot of unearned work but I can't localize where. The analogy: a country is like a dead man's estate. You don't choose your ancestors, you just inherit; and when you inherit you take the assets together with the liens against them — you don't get to keep the house and disclaim the mortgage. The obligation rides along with the asset rather than with anybody's guilt, so he uses it to get three conclusions at once: (a) nobody has to establish that any living person is blameworthy, (b) the obligation runs from whoever currently holds the assets, not from the traceable descendants of named wrongdoers, and (c) since basically everyone in the country is a beneficiary, the bill should come out of the general tax base rather than being itemized against the specific banks, universities, and firms you can actually show profited.

So: give me the correspondence table explicitly — heir, decedent, asset, lien, creditor, executor, the date-of-death valuation, whether we're inside or outside any limitations period, adverse possession / good-faith-purchaser if it bites — then the disanalogies, then the part I actually care about: sort the disanalogies into the ones that are load-bearing for those three conclusions and the ones that are noise. Things I keep circling on. (1) Probate has a definite death, a definite asset set, and a valuation date; a polity has none of those, and the 'assets' (a legal order, a currency, a labour market, the fact that your degree is from a university that's been rich for two centuries) aren't the sort of thing that can be handed over or divided. (2) A lot of the inherited advantage looks positional rather than a thing: the house's value is a function of the neighborhood's rank, the credential's value is a function of the institution's rank, and you can't transfer those to the losers without destroying them — which makes me suspect the estate picture quietly models the harm as a chunk of stuff that got moved when maybe the harm was a shape. (3) Estate liability is capped at what you recieved: the heir pays out of the estate, not out of his own pocket. That seems to cut both ways in a way he hasn't noticed — it caps the total bill, but it also means that if you can't show the living heir was net enriched the claim is zero, and with the non-identity stuff (no slavery, different pairings, different people, no determinate fact about how much he'd have had) I'm not sure you can. (4) The case I made up and can't get out of my head: suppose the advantage had been handed out by a rigged annual lottery that pulled, say, 12% of the population into the good slots of a queue, and the slots were heritable — except they pass to whoever currently occupies them, so today's slot-holders aren't descendants of the original winners at all, while the victims' descendants are still in the bad slots. There's no identifiable asset and no identifiable transferor, the estate story collapses, and yet my sense that something is owed doesn't move at all. Every time I try to taboo 'owe' the argument evaporates, which is probably a clue.

And this is where it stops being academic, because the estate framing looks structurally committed to a one-time closing of accounts, whereas if the true picture is 'the plumbing is still misdirected' or 'this is a continuing trespass, so the limitations period hasn't even started running', you'd want something flow-shaped instead: ongoing payments, or instruments whose payouts track the gap for as long as it persists (baby-bond-style accounts at one end, some kind of share that pays a fraction of the gap rather than a lump sum at the other). Does the analogy actually entail the lump-sum conclusion, or is he just importing it because probate is a discrete event? Be committed: tell me whether the analogy survives, which of the three conclusions it can really carry, where you think it's actively misleading, and what the better analogy is if there is one — I've got 'diverted river', 'continuing nuisance', and 'unjust enrichment / constructive trust' rattling around and no way to pick between them. And please mark clearly which parts you'd defend without hedging and where you're extrapolating past anything you'd actually stand behind.
