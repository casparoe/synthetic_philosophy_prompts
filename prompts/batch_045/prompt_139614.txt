I am writing up a position paper for a reading group on the philosophy of the infinite, and before I commit myself in print I would like an audit of whether the set of views below is jointly stable. Here is where I find myself.

(1) Actual infinities are coherent and, as far as I can tell, metaphysically possible. Hilbert's hotel is not a reductio of the actual infinite; it is a correct description of what an infinite arrangement would be like. A full hotel with one room for each natural number can take in a new guest, and indeed infinitely many new guests, by reassignment, and the even-numbered rooms house exactly as many guests as the hotel does as a whole. The general lesson I take from this is that the failure of our finite heuristics is no evidence of impossibility. I also do not want to lean on the abstract/concrete distinction anywhere in this neighbourhood: if a story is coherent for numbers, it is coherent for rooms, guests and lamps.

(2) Cantor's criterion is the right one: two collections have the same number of members just in case their members can be correlated one-to-one, and the intuition that a proper part must be smaller than the whole has to be surrendered. On that basis I take Cantor's theorem to be a genuine discovery about quantity, not merely a result about formal systems: there are strictly more real numbers than natural numbers.

(3) At the same time, I hold that there is no determinate answer to the continuum question. I am attracted to the thought that all subsets of the naturals is not a completed totality with a definite number of members — that it is indefinitely extensible, and that the hierarchy of sets is in some sense absolutely infinite and never finished.

(4) I hold that there could not have been infinitely many days before today. A series cannot be completed by adding one member at a time, and the past is just such a series. An endless future, with infinitely many events still to come, is another matter, and I have no objection to it.

(5) I am a B-theorist about time. All times are equally real; there is no objectively moving present; earlier and later differ only in the relations they bear to a standpoint, not in any metaphysical feature of the world.

(6) I deny that a supertask — infinitely many operations completed within a finite interval — is so much as possible, and I take the standard cases to show this. If a lamp is switched on and off at a half, three quarters, seven eighths and so on of a minute, there is no answer to whether it is on at the end of the minute. If ten balls are put into a vase and one removed at each of those halving steps, whether the vase is empty at the end depends on which ball is removed at each step and on nothing about the vase. My reason for denying the possibility is that the descriptions fail to determine an outcome, and I take that failure to be a defect in the supposition rather than a gap in the story.

What I want is a consistency audit rather than a survey of the literature. Several of these strike me as pulling against each other, but I cannot tell which of the pulls are real, and I would rather you do the discriminating than confirm my hunches. So: which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look as though they are? Rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad. For each one, say precisely what would have to be given up, what the cheapest revision would be, and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set. Where a pair is only apparently in tension, state the distinction that dissolves it. Finally, tell me which single revision would do the most work in making the whole package stable.
