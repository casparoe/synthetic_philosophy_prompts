I run the consultation and engagement team at a unitary authority in England. My officers take in several hundred deputations, petitions and written submissions a year, and increasingly the difficulty is not volume but a recurring judgement call: what to do with contributions whose grounds are religious.

The governing thought, as I understand it, is that a public body should be able to justify what it does by reasons that all its citizens could reasonably be expected to accept, and that this is meant to be a restraint on the state rather than on conscience. What is not obvious to me is how far that restraint reaches back into what we will accept, record and weigh when it comes *from* the public. My team has to rule on this in the room, in a couple of minutes, with no training in philosophy and no opportunity to consult; the ruling has to be one a non-specialist can apply consistently, it has to survive being read back in writing by the Monitoring Officer or, occasionally, by a judge; and where the procedure runs out it must hand the decision to a named person rather than pretend to a mechanical answer.

I would like a written procedure we could actually run, and I think it needs to distinguish at least three stages rather than give one verdict: whether a contribution may be accepted and entered into the record at all; whether it may be counted as a reason bearing on the decision; and whether it may appear in the authority's own statement of reasons for the decision.

There are some wrinkles I want the procedure either to use or to tell me are the wrong distinctions. First, I do not think you can tell from the words alone whether a reason is religious, since it seems to depend on why the speaker holds it. Second, some reasons have a religious ancestry but now travel free of it, and I am unsure whether the ancestry should matter. Third, asking someone to restate their reason in a shared idiom is not costless, and the cost falls unevenly — on people with less fluency in the majority vocabulary, on communities whose reasons are not detachable from their cosmology, and on anyone who would have to misdescribe why they believe what they believe to be heard at all. Fourth, there is a question I cannot settle about who authors a reason: if an officer or a councillor supplies the shared surrogate on the speaker's behalf, the reason that ends up counting is no longer theirs. Fifth, I want to know whether restraint is symmetric — whether a comprehensive secular doctrine gets the same treatment — and whether it bites equally on reasons that happen to support what the authority intended to do anyway.

Please also say plainly whether we should translate contributions for the record or require speakers to translate to be heard, and why.

Then apply the procedure to three cases. (1) A deputation against a proposed crematorium whose stated ground is that cremation is incompatible with the resurrection of the body; the speaker volunteers that she has no other objection and will not invent one. (2) A joint submission against a pavement-licensing extension from a mosques' council, whose ground is the prohibition on alcohol, and a residents' association, whose ground is noise and litter; the two reach the same conclusion and the officers disagree about whether the conclusion can be carried by the residents' reason alone. (3) A secular humanist society's submission that a faith-based adoption agency should be excluded from commissioning, grounded in a comprehensive claim about autonomy and religious formation, with an explicit refusal to offer a narrower reason. At least one of these should come out somewhere I or the councillors will not like, and I would rather you said where than softened it.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do.

Please write in British English. I am not looking for a reading list or citations: I need something my officers can hold in their hands.
