You are a curriculum designer who has actually run hands-on formal-systems workshops for strong teenagers, not someone summarising a book from the outside. Before you settle on anything, spend a little while brainstorming two or three genuinely different ways the session could be built, and then tell me in two or three sentences which candidates you discarded and why — I want to see the shape of the design space, not just your favourite point in it.

I help run a five-day summer intensive for mathematically strong rising high-school seniors: 16 participants this year, one 90-minute block on Tuesday morning, plus an optional 25-minute follow-up slot on Wednesday, no computers in the room, nothing but index cards, markers, scissors, tape and a couple of dice. Last year I ran the MU puzzle as a live game and it went beautifully — the students could *feel* the difference between moving inside a system and reasoning about the system — but the session I built on top of it, on how a formal system can talk about itself, degenerated into me talking at them for forty minutes. They came away able to repeat that code numbers can encode statements about code numbers, with no felt sense of why the indirection does any work. So this year I want the self-replication slot to be the thing that fixes that, and I want it to be typogenetics-flavoured: strings of letters get "translated" into enzymes, and the enzymes edit other strings.

Please design a stripped-down variant I can actually run. I have read the original typogenetics material and I do not want to use it: there are too many amino acids to keep in working memory while fifteen people are shouting at each other, and I'd rather have a tiny system with a codon table that fits on one side of an index card and can be executed by hand on paper strips. Two design constraints I care about, which are my own additions and not part of the usual setup, so please engage with them rather than quietly reverting to the standard version. First, a **quarantine rule**: an enzyme translated from a given strand may never be applied to that very strand — it can act on any other strand in the pool, including a numerically identical *different* copy. My hope is that teams will first try to build a strand that copies itself directly, hit the wall, and then discover on their own that they need a machine that copies *whatever it is handed*, together with a description of the machine handed to it as data. If you think that rule makes the puzzle trivial, or impossible, or that the failure it induces is pedagogically fake, say so and give me the variant you would actually run instead. Second, a **noise phase**: once at least one team has a working replicator, run three or four more rounds in which I roll a die and flip a single base somewhere in the pool, and score teams not on who replicated first but on whose lineage is still replicating at the end. The thing I want them to run into is the tension between describing enough machinery to get yourself copied and staying short enough to survive being copied badly — a self-description is also a target, and I want them to discover that trade-off by losing to it, not by hearing a sentence about error thresholds.

What participants should come away seeing: that "X copies itself" is always shorthand for "X is copied by machinery that X also encodes, and that machinery has to be able to take X as data" — the same indirection that makes quines and self-referential sentences possible. Nobody should hear the words quine, Gödel, diagonal, or fixed point until the debrief, and the debrief should map specific moments in the session (the failed direct attempt, the moment a team realises it needs an intermediate strand, the moment the noise kills a long replicator) onto specific parts of that idea, including why the direct attempt *cannot* work. Give me the minute-by-minute timeline with materials, the rules as a table, three predictable failure modes with fixes (someone hand-copies a strand instead of going through an enzyme; one team finishes in four minutes and flattens the room; a team finds a degenerate replicator that exploits a loophole), and be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce — self-reproduction of this kind is much closer to self-replicating machinery than to incompleteness, and I would rather know exactly which parts I have to supply in words, and which false takeaway a bright student might walk out with, than have the activity oversell itself. I have been around the rationalist blogosphere long enough to know how loosely "self-replicating" gets thrown around, which is most of why I want them to earn the concept instead of inheriting it.
