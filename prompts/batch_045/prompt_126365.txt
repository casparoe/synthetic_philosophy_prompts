You are a professional philosopher who also writes comedy — picture someone who could place a piece in a magazine's humor column while getting the decision theory exactly right, and who understands the difference between a joke that insiders laugh at because it is *accurate* and a joke that is merely about nerds being socially awkward.

I'm emceeing the last-night cabaret at a small summer school on rational agency that I help run. Last year's offerings were almost entirely variants of "the decision theorist takes one box and walks away from a guaranteed thousand dollars," and I'd like to do better this time: I want a single joke, tellable aloud in under a minute, that would land for a room of graduate students and a handful of faculty who know causal, evidential, and updateless decision theory well, and that would at least still make situational sense to the two or three partners and children who wandered in. The punchline should turn on the actual mechanics of updateless decision theory rather than on generic nerdiness. For the purposes of the joke, here is the characterization I want you to work from: the updateless agent does not condition on what it has observed and then ask what to do; it scores whole policies — mappings from observations to actions — by their expected utility computed with respect to its *prior*, i.e. from behind a veil of ignorance about which situation it will turn out to be in, and only then feeds its actual observations in as inputs to the policy it has already selected for the entire ensemble of situations. Two consequences you should feel free to exploit: the agent gets the benefits of precommitment without visibly paying for them, and it will sometimes do something that looks flatly pointless given the evidence right in front of it, because what matters is how the policy performs across situations it can already see it is not in.

Here is the setting I'd like you to use; I invented it for the occasion, so please build the joke around it rather than around Newcomb's problem proper. A pop-up restaurant called *Ensemble* has the following arrangements. Before seating you, the maître d' runs a high-fidelity simulation of you and, on ordinary nights, seats you only if the simulated diner paid the bill. On Thursdays there is a "transparency special": the prediction is printed in small type at the foot of the menu — it might read *your simulation paid* or *your simulation stiffed us* — and on Thursdays the house seats everyone regardless of what the prediction says. There is also a "policy window" by the coat check where, for a two-dollar fee, you may file in advance any policy you like, and the house will simulate that policy instead of you; the restaurant's lawyer maintains that this makes the whole arrangement consensual. A causal decision theorist, an evidential decision theorist, and an updateless decision theorist have come to dinner together on a Thursday. What I'm after is a joke in which the updateless agent's relationship to the policy window, or to the line at the foot of the menu, is what makes the thing funny — and in which the other two diners' differing failures are part of the comedy, not an excuse to lecture the audience.
