Someone handed me this at the Sunday meetup and I've been chewing on it for a week and cant find the seam. It's supposed to show that infohazards are a confused concept. Interested in where it breaks.

Setup so we're using the same words. Call an option *credence-gated* if you only have it while you're ignorant of something — truthfully testifying that you don't know where the hostages are, being able to swear under oath that you never opened the file, genuine suspense about a spoiler. Call a channel *sealed* if no one else can tell whether you queried it and *auditable* if they can. I suspect sealed-vs-auditable is going to matter somewhere but I can't make it do any work.

The argument:

(1) Good's theorem, the value-of-information one: for a single agent with a fixed utility function and a fixed option set, letting a free signal arrive before you choose has non-negative expected value — the expectation being taken with your prior, over the possible messages.

(2) The proof is a dominance argument and it doesn't care what the message says: whatever act you were going to perform without the signal is still there for you after you see it, so you can always just ignore the signal and do that. Nothing in the proof turns on how informative the signal is, or on whether the channel is sealed.

(3) So the "expected" in (1) isn't doing any real work. The conclusion is stronger than it looks: there is no realization of the signal after which you are worse off than before you looked. You can't regret looking, for any message whatever.

(4) Applied to the interesting case: a genuinely dangerous piece of knowledge would be a counterexample to (3), since learning it would leave you worse off in the world where you learn it. So there are no infohazards for the agent doing the learning. Talk of infohazards has to be either confused or really a claim about what *other* people do once you know.

(5) But (4) is preposterous. There are plenty of cases where learning a thing leaves you worse off in exactly the sense (3) denies: you get the genome result and now you can't honestly fill in the insurance form; you read the method paper and now you could build the thing if you went off the rails; the letter tells you the kidnappers already know your address. People who worry about this stuff are not making an arithmetic slip.

I want to say (3) is the broken step and that everything downstream is just a misreading of (1), but I can't cash out why "you can always just ignore it" is a cheat. The act really is still available, isn't it? My vague sense is that *available* and *worth the same* are being run together, but I'm not sure which way the substitution goes, or whether the averaging step in the proof is doing something I'm ignoring.

If it helps to know what I'd rather keep: I'd keep the theorem and drop "infohazards are confused." But I also want to know whether, once (3) is repaired, the correct reading of (1) still causes trouble for (4). Does the single-agent, non-strategic framing carry all the weight there, or is there a second problem hiding in it? And are credence-gated options a real category the theorem genuinely can't see, or am I inventing a taxonomy to protect a conclusion I already liked?

Also, because I've read one too many confident forum posts that turned out to be one guy's take: please keep separate what's actually settled (the math, the proof, what the theorem is standardly taken to establish) from what people who work on this really disagree about, and mark the places where you're guessing or where you think there's no consensus. If you give me a diagnosis of (3), say how sure you are that it's the *only* place the argument slips.
