The question:

Dennett calls the self a "centre of narrative gravity," and he insists he is not denying that there are selves — his claim is about what kind of thing a self is, not that there aren't any. Parfit, on the reading we did, is a reductionist: there is no further fact of personal identity beyond physical and psychological continuity, and identity is not what matters in survival. Both of them reject the Cartesian picture of the self as an inner witness or a thing in the head.

Question: what, if anything, does Dennett's narrative view add that Parfit's reductionism doesn't already give you? Then apply your answer to this case. Maria has advanced dementia, no longer recognises her family, and cannot tell you anything about her own past. Her family keep an accurate, detailed "life story" file at her care home, and the staff use it to interpret what she does and to keep her calm. Is the person described in that file, in Dennett's sense, her self? Say why. About 700 words.

The answer I'm looking at (I fixed one spelling mistake, otherwise it's as written):

Dennett's phrase "centre of narrative gravity" is meant to replace the Cartesian picture. On that picture there is an inner witness, a little person in the head watching the show — Dennett calls this the Cartesian theatre, and he thinks nothing like it exists. His replacement is that the self is an abstraction, the way a centre of gravity is an abstraction. A centre of gravity is not an extra lump of stuff inside the body, it is a point you can calculate, and it is useful even though you would never find it on the operating table. In the same way the self is not an ingredient of the brain and not a homunculus receiving reports; it is the point around which the stories we tell about a person cohere. And those stories do not have to be told by the person herself: a biography, or a family's account, does the same job. So the self is something like the hero of the novels we write about ourselves and each other, and because that hero is an abstraction and not a thing, this also explains why we cannot settle questions about when exactly a self begins or ends by looking for some self-stuff — there isn't any to find.

Where this goes beyond Parfit is that Parfit is only asking about identity over time. His reductionism says there is no further fact of personal identity over and above physical and psychological continuity, and that identity is not what matters in survival. That is a claim about what the facts are. Dennett's claim is about what kind of object a self is in the first place, at a time as much as over time. You can agree with Parfit that there is no further fact about whether you survive and still want to know what you are tracking when you talk about somebody's character, their projects, what they would do next. Dennett's answer is that you are tracking an abstraction fixed by a narrative, not a chain of connected experiences. So the narrative view adds an account of what we are doing when we take the intentional stance towards somebody.

Now Maria. Since the self just is the story, her self now is wherever the story now is, and that is the file in the office. The staff use it to predict and interpret what she does, it is accurate, and there is nothing else in the neighbourhood that could do that job. So yes: the person in the file is her self, in exactly the same sense that any of us has a self. And there is no deeper fact that could overrule this, because there was never anything more to a self than a story somebody is telling. In fact this shows what Dennett really means: strictly speaking there are no selves. An abstraction is not a real thing, it is a way of talking, a useful fiction, so what exists is the brain, the behaviour and the document, and the self is like the hero of a novel, which is to say it doesn't exist. This is basically Parfit's conclusion by another route — no further fact, and in the end nothing that the fact could be about.
