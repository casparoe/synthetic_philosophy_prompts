My old tutor runs a blog where she rules on hypothetical atonement cases — "is this penal substitution, and is it ok?" — and I'm trying to work out what she's actually running underneath. She came to all of it through David Lewis's paper on penal substitution; her gloss is that once you grant the Christian story, its doctrinal terms get filled by whatever plays the right role in that story, and what plays the role turns out to be penal substitution however much the label upsets people.

Her verdicts so far:

- a friend pays a convicted man's court-ordered fine, the court accepts it: yes, penal substitution, nothing spooky about it
- a father takes his son's flogging, both willing, judge agrees: yes
- a random volunteer with no connection to the murderer serves his life sentence, both willing, judge agrees: no — "a travesty, not an atonement"
- a clan chief is executed in place of his clan after the massacre they committed, and he volunteers: yes
- the substitute is punished but the people he substitutes for never hear about it and never repent: yes, the substitution happens anyway; whether they get anything out of it is a separate question
- the lawgiver who fixed the penalty himself steps down and takes it: yes, very much yes
- a substitute who is coerced into it: no

The case she hasn't ruled on: a stranger with no prior tie to the convict, but the court formally transfers the conviction onto him by a public legal act of imputation, so that from then on he counts as the convict for the purposes of the sentence. He consents, he is punished publicly with the crime explicitly denounced, and the convict is released and genuinely remorseful.

Will she say yes? More usefully: what rule is she implicitly running, what other rules fit her verdicts just as well, and which further case would best tell those rules apart? My own guess at the crux is whether the substitute has to be really united with the wrongdoer beforehand, or whether the transfer itself can constitute the union — but I'd rather hear what else fits, including anything that makes her verdicts look a bit wierd than she thinks.
