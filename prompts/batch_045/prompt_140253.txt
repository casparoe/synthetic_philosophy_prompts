I'm putting together the spring issue of a tiny photocopied satire zine that circulates around our department (mixed crowd: some market-design econ people, some political philosophers, a couple of bioethicists, plus partners and friends who only read it for the jokes), and I need a centrepiece of about 700–900 words. I have a specific thing in mind: a promotional brochure from a fictional consulting firm — something bland like "Continuity Advisory" or "Frame & Form Partners" — that sells repugnance mitigation. Their product is not to change transactions, only their descriptions. They take exchanges a society currently blocks or stigmatises (selling a kidney, commercial surrogacy, disaster-time price gouging, vote buying) and re-engineer the framing until the repugnance dissipates: a "naming ladder" that walks a sale up through compensation, reimbursement of expenses, gift, reciprocity, participation; and a three-way "Repugnance Stress Test" that runs the identical transaction under money framing, gift framing and a clincial/therapeutic framing to see which one clears review. I want it formatted like a genuine brochure — short mission statement, three or four before/after case studies with the old name struck through and the new one in bold, an FAQ, and a fee schedule priced by how much repugnance has to be laundered, billed in some invented unit. Voice is flat, confident, management-consultant deadpan: no winking, no narrator stepping in to tell the reader any of this is bad.

The reason I'm asking you and not just doing it myself: the jokes have to come from actually getting the philosophy right, because half my readers are the people being sent up and I'd rather they laughed than winced. So build the copy quietly out of the real machinery — the idea of blocked exchanges, the gap between repugnant and harmful, the way the "gift" redescription can leave the actual person worse off than an honest sale would, the thought that repugnance is mere squeamishness if it's purely frame-sensitive but is tracking something real (vulnerability, duress, who ends up doing the selling, what the altruistic framing quietly asks them to eat) if it isn't, and motivation crowding-out. Nobody in the brochure should state any of that; it should all be dramatised, and someone who's never thought about commodification at all should still feel the discomfort by the last panel. Two more requirements: one case study has to break the firm's own method — my favourite candidate is a city that hires them to make vote-buying acceptable and discovers that the two framings the client needs ("the vote is a duty we would never price" and "here is your civic participation stipend") cancel each other out, leaving the firm's fix transparantly worse than what it replaced; and the brochure must never notice this failure. No footnotes, no real names of living people, invented in-house experts quoted as testimonials. I was arguing with a friend about whether this kind of relabelling is just hypocrisy or whether the social meaning really does shift when the description shifts, and I genuinely can't tell — so don't resolve it for the reader either, just let the firm crawl far enough out on the branch that we can hear it cracking.
