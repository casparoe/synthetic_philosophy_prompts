I wonder whether there is a name for a pattern I have started noticing at work, and — if there is not — whether you could help me pin it down precisely enough that I could use the word in a committee paper without being laughed at.

Some context. After a reorganisation last spring I was asked to chair the underwriting committee of a medium-sized mutual insurer. I am an accountant by training rather than a statistician, so I spend a good deal of my week in meetings where intelligent people argue about how much to charge for risks whose likelihood nobody in the room can put a number on. Over the past year one particular shape of argument has recurred often enough that I want a handle on it, and nothing I have found on my own quite fits. I should mention that I have no access to a university library from here, so I would be grateful for an answer built out of the distinctions themselves rather than a list of things to go and read.

Four instances, real but tidied.

First. One of our senior underwriters habitually prices an unclear risk at the pessimistic end of whatever range we can agree on. But he only does this when the same agenda also contains a risk of the same general kind whose rate we do know well. Take the unclear item off the agenda and bring it back a fortnight later on its own, and he will price it at the middle of the range without any apparent discomfort. The risk has not changed between the two meetings; the company has not learned anything; only the presence of the other item has changed.

Second, closely related. If I hand him a number — any number, including one I have visibly invented on the spot — the padding disappears and he prices at the number. He does not pad *known* rates, only missing ones. So whatever this is, it is not a general taste for caution about how much we might lose.

Third. When the unclear quantity happens to fall in the area where he is our designated in-house expert, the caution reverses: he will take the unclear risk in preference to a clear risk of the same mid-range value. My colleagues and I checked his track record out of curiosity, and his judgements in his own area are no better calibrated than his judgements elsewhere. So the reversal is not tracking his actual reliability.

Fourth, and this is the part I cannot find discussed anywhere: the reversal has nothing to do with his own expertise either. It depends on who else is at the table. Put an outside consultant in the room and he retreats to the pessimistic end even in his own field — and he does this even when the consultant's information has nothing whatever to do with the question being decided, and even when there is no opportunity for him to consult them. Remove the consultant and he is comfortable again. It is as though what he is avoiding is not the missing number but the visibility of the missing number.

My questions. Does the first pair of observations have an established name? I have a vague memory of a well-known puzzle about drawing coloured balls from an urn whose composition is unknown, which seems to be in this neighbourhood, but as I understand it that puzzle concerns how people rank the two options, not the ranking changing depending on what else happens to be on the table at the time. Does the third observation have a name of its own, or is it the same phenomenon in another guise? Is the fourth the same family at all, or is it ordinary social embarrassment wearing the costume of a judgement about uncertainty — and if the two readings cannot be separated by armchair argument, what observation would separate them? I have been using the private label "expert exemption" for the third, which I assume is already taken; and if no single concept covers all four, I would much rather have a short taxonomy with its boundary cases marked than one borrowed label stretched over everything.

Please answer in British English.
