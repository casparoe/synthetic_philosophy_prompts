I run a fortnightly "games, choice and proof" workshop for first-years, mixed maths and philosophy, about 16 people, 80 minutes, one room with moveable tables. I can get printed boards, two colours of counters, index cards and a whiteboard. Almost none of them have met a non-constructive existence proof before.

I had a long argument with a friend last night about the strategy-stealing argument. She says it's a parlour trick: it tells you the first player wins at Hex and hands you nothing you can actually use, so what has it shown. I kept saying that's the whole point — you find out *which* player without ever getting your hands on a strategy — but I couldn't make her feel it, and I think the only way to feel it is to play. So design me the session: rules, materials, a timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the steps of the argument (the supposition that the second player has a winning strategy, the throwaway opening move, the "pass" you make when the stolen strategy calls for the cell you already took, and the premise that an extra stone of your own colour can never hurt you). No lecture up front and nobody says "strategy stealing" until the last ten minutes — they should run into the move themselves.

The bit I want you to work out rather than look up, because I invented it on the way home: a variant — call it Stint Hex — where each player has a fixed budget of stones, fewer than half the board, must place one every turn, and looses if the budget runs out before they've connected their sides. In that variant the throwaway move costs something, and I want the group to discover that by trying to rerun the argument on it, not by being told. If who actually wins Stint Hex isn't obvious, say so honestly instead of inventing an answer. There's a second beat if time allows: a free stone of your own colour, placed by someone else, is a gift in Hex — get them to find a game where the same gift is a curse.

Finally: what goes wrong the first time I run it. I expect people to slide from "the first player wins" into "I can name the winning opening move", and someone to object that finite games are determined anyway so the existence claim was never the news — how do I handle that, and what does the activity just can't show them that only I can say out loud?
