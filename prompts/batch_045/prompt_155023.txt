Me and two friends got into a long argument after game night last week and I've been chewing on it since. Dev was describing this open-world RPG he'd sunk like forty hours into, where you travel with a companion character who reacts to your choices and remembers what you've done; he said he'd sold her into slavery in-game for a stat bonus and then, weirdly, felt bad about it for days. Noor said he'd done something genuinely wrong, not just something that made him uncomfortable. Dev: (1) she's code, there's nobody there, no consciousness and no suffering, moral status requires being the kind of thing that can be harmed, so there's no duty to her — the only real duties in the neighborhood are indirect ones, like whether being casually cruel in a game makes you worse to actual people. Noor: (2) grant no consciousness; still, after forty hours that character has a unique history and behavioural profile that no one else's copy has, and if he deletes the save, a specific thing that will never exist again stops existing — we treat the burning of an author's only manuscript, or the extinction of a species nobody will ever observe, as a loss, so there can be loss without a subject. Dev: (3) loss without a subject is either aesthetic value, which needs possible appreciators and here has none, or just his own sadness, which is a fact about him; and if uniqueness alone generated duties then every unrepeatable arrangement of matter would be morally loaded, which is absurd. Noor: (4) it isn't uniqueness alone, it's that he stood in a relation to it, and separately — reasons can be internal to a practice: inside the game it's true that she trusted him and he betrayed her, and he has a reason not to of the same shape as the reason not to hang your queen in chess, a reason generated by the role; and practice-internal reasons aren't fake, since promising is practice-internal and promises bind. Dev: (5) promises bind because of reliance and expectation in actual persons, whereas the chess reason only binds given that you want to win, and "wanting to play well" doesn't include not-betraying — so the practice-internal story gives him nothing unless you sneak in a moral premise. Noor: (6) maybe, but then he has to explain the asymmetry everybody feels: most people think gratuitous virtual sexual abuse of a child character is morally worse than virtual murder, even though both are equally pixels and equally internal to the practice, and the only thing that seems to generate that asymmetry is something about what the action expresses or does to the agent — which is his own indirect point, meaning the indirect stuff is carrying all the weight rather than being a minor footnote. Dev: (7) that asymmetry is fully explained by effects on the player and on the community around him, no duties to the character needed, and he'd add that if anyone ever builds NPCs with the right internal architecture the direct duty just switches on; the disagreement is only about when the architecture is rich enough, not about principle. Noor: (8) but "the right internal architecture" is always inferred from behaviour, never observed, even for humans — we don't have direct access to anyone else's consciousness — so if an NPC's conduct were best explained by something like dread, refusing that inference there while granting it to humans is just substrate chauvinism. Dev said the best explanation of the NPC's conduct is weights and gradients, and that in the human case we have independent reasons — shared biology, evolution, people's reports — and then we all got tired and went home.
