Hey — I run a small monthly fiction workshop, 9 or 10 novelists, two hours, someone's living room. Half the drafts right now are time-loop stories and they all use the loop the same cheap way: character relives a day, works out the lesson, gets out. I want one session that ruins that move for them.

So: design me something we actually do in the room that makes them feel the weight of Nietzsche's eternal recurrence — not the live-so-you'd-repeat-it poster version. Rules, materials, a timeline, and a debrief. No lecture up front, and I'd rather not say the name until afterwards.

Two things the exercise has to force into the open. First: the recurrence is identical. No memory carried over, no doing it better on the second pass, no arc. I want them to find out for themselves how much of what makes their own plots work depends on quietly smuggling that in. Second, the bit I keep chewing on and can't write my way out of: if the recurrence is literally identical, then the reaction to it returns too. Whatever verdict someone reaches — I can affirm this, I can't, I'll try — is itself part of the loop, so it isn't a decision that settles anything once and for all; it's either an eternal feature of the loop or it never happens, and from inside there's no telling which. I want that to land as something they've run into, not as a paradox I announce.

What I want them walking out with is the difference between enduring a life and affirming it, and why that difference kills the redemption arc. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run it (somone will try to out-clever the setup, and at least one person will treat it as therapy), and be honest about the part of the idea the exercise can't produce and I'll just have to say out loud.
