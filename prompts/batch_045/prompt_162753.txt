I co-run a fortnightly formal epistemology reading group — about 14 people, mostly first- and second-year grad students with a few advanced undergrads, one 90-minute session, a room with a whiteboard and dodgy wifi, so nothing that needs laptops (phone calculators are fine). We've done ordinary conditioning and Jeffrey conditioning and everyone is comfortable with Bayes, but nobody has really worked on updating on conditionals — the Judy Benjamin problem, the paratrooper dropped somewhere in a grid who gets a radio message about a conditional probability and has to figure out what to now believe. I want a session where they *feel* that the sentence she hears doesn't by itself determine the update, and I'd rather they collide with that by trading bets with each other than by me announcing up front that there are competing rules. My tentative scenario: a territory cut by a river (north/south) and a road (east/west), priors 0.45 for north-east, 0.05 for north-west, 0.25 for south-east, 0.25 for south-west, and the radio message is: "if you are north of the river, the chance that you are west of the road is three in four." I picked those numbers because I think they make the candidate answers come apart violently — I get roughly 0.5 for the probability of being north if you hold the north/south marginal fixed and just redistribute inside the northern half, versus something like 0.23 if you minimise relative entropy from the prior subject to that constraint, and a third, different answer again if you sloppily apply the 3:1 likelihood ratio to west-vs-east across the whole grid — but please sanity-check my arithmetic and swap in better numbers if they make the divergence starker or hand-computable. The extra wrinkle I'd like built in is *protocol-dependence*: half the room gets an envelope saying the operator always broadcasts that one sentence and it's simply a true constraint on the territory, the other half gets an envelope saying the operator had a fixed menu of three candidate conditionals and picked uniformly at random among the ones true of your actual cell, and you know the menu and the randomisation — same sentence heard, and I want them to discover that the right posterior is different, and that the sentence alone doesn't tell you which situation you're in. Please design the menu and the numbers so that the protocol-B answer is computable by hand as a reweighting by message probability and really does differ from the other two. Rules, materials, chip payoffs or some proper scoring rule that doesn't need real money, a timeline, and the staged reveals.

Then I need the debrief: map specific moments in the session — the trades that got accepted, the ones nobody would take, the moment the two envelopes get compared — onto the different updating rules and onto why the content of a conditional message underdetermines the update. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm guessing: people will insist the message must raise the probability of being west generally; someone will refuse to bet on the conditional at all; one guy who half-remembers the problem from a survey paper will blurt out the answer; and trading may be thin if everyone's numbers happen to be close) and how to handle each without me just giving the answer away. Be frank about what the activity can't reproduce — my sense is that it can show them that the rules differ but can't adjudicate which is normatively correct, and that bets measure betting dispositions rather than credences, so risk-aversion and ambiguity-aversion will masquerade as a genuine disagreement about updating — and say what I'll have to supply in discussion instead. Also: please say how confident you are in the parts where you're doing something substantive (the numbers, the protocol-B computation, the claim that the two protocols really come apart), and single out the one thing you're least sure of.
