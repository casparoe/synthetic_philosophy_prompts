Hey — I need a short research proposal, about two pages, for a one-semester independent project (a thesis chapter, call it 6000 words) in a moral and social philosophy seminar. No citations or reading list, I'll fill those in myself; what I want is the framing.

The topic is norms about standing out: the Scandinavian "law of Jante" / tall poppy syndrome type on one side, and the American-Dream-ish habit of celebrating the successful on the other. Every time I try to set the two up against each other I get told the contrast is about how much inequality a society tolerates, or about whether success is seen as earned, and neither of those feels like it's the actual thing going on.

My half-formed idea, which you should throw out if it's dumb: maybe the disagreement isn't about the moral value of success at all but about its epistemic value — about how much deference, credibility, or attention succeeding at something is supposed to buy you. On that reading, cutting down the tall poppy is a kind of testimonial hygiene: a device for stopping "she succeeded at X" from sliding into "she's worth listening to about Y." The American norm would then be the opposite bet, that success does carry information worth deferring to.

If that's the right shape, the real questions look different from "is envy justified." Two I keep circling:

1. What is the object of the levelling norm, exactly — the successful person's self-conception (arrogance), her status-seeking behaviour (boasting), the inequality as such, or the deference other people are inclined to hand her? I think these come apart in cases, and I think which one you pick changes whether the norm is defensible.
2. Whether these norms are self-defeating in a way that matters. You can only enforce a norm against standing out by keeping very careful track of who is standing out, so a levelling culture has to keep ranking people in order to stop people from ranking people. I want that sharpened into something with a determinate answer: is it a genuine instability, or just a cute observation with no teeth?

What I need in the proposal:

- one precise question that actually has an answer, even if you have to narrow or replace mine to get there
- why the usual ways of setting this up — envy vs admiration, desert and luck, relational equality, the "politics of resentment" story — leave it open
- what I'd do week by week-ish over about 14 weeks, given I have no experiments, no fieldwork and no archive
- the two or three possible answers, and which considerations would decide between them
- what would count as the project failing, and honestly where it's definately too big for one semester

Two more things. My supervisor is going to ask "isn't this just a paper about envy", and I want a paragraph in the proposal that answers that properly rather than defensively. And please don't let it turn into a sociology or social-psychology proposal — I can't run a survey, and if the deliverable is just "someone should test this empirically" the project has failed. The thing I recieve at the end should be a conceptual result someone could disagree with.
