Our little reading group — two molecular biology postdocs, three logic/CS people, me doing a masters in something in between — got completely derailed last week and I've been turning it over since. One of the CS guys pulled up Hofstadter's typogenetics from Gödel, Escher, Bach and argued that the DNA–Gödel isomorphism isn't just a cute illustration but a proof of principle: bases are alphabet symbols, duplets or codons are numerals, the cell's translation machinery is arithmetization, enzymes are theorems, a self-replicating strand is the Gödel sentence, and viability in an environment is truth. From that he concludes that any self-replicating system rich enough to encode its own replication machinery — a self-improving AI, a gene drive, von Neumann style nanotech, whatever — has a built-in blind spot: there will be true facts about its behaviour that aren't derivable from its own specification, so a self-contained formal safety case is impossible in principle and we should stop pretending verification-based alignment work could ever bottom out. He also used it against the biology people: if a code needs an interpreter, then meaning isn't in the chemistry, and reductionism about the cell is dead.

The biology side pushed back, but mostly by saying the analogy is decorative and that Hofstadter only ever claimed a structural isomorphism, which I dont think really engages with his argument. Meanwhile I've been messing around with a modified version of the game and it's made me less sure either side is right. Call it reversible typogenetics: (1) I drop the rule that you can go from strand to enzyme but never back, and make the duplet-to-operation map a bijection instead of many-to-one, so the code is genuinely effectively invertible the way a Gödel numbering is supposed to be; (2) I let some enzymes edit the translation table itself, so the code is self-modifying rather than fixed by fiat; (3) I add a negative operation — an enzyme that fires only when a specified subsequence is absent — plus a self-erasing enzyme that deletes the strand that produced it. My instinct is that even this doesn't get you incompleteness, that what it actually gets you is a fixed point in the style of Kleene's recursion theorem (a strand can be made to produce any computable transform of itself, replication being the identity case) and then undecidability at the level of which strands are producible at all, which is a limitation on a would-be verifier rather than a limitation inside the replicator. But I can't tell what in the picture is supposed to be provability, what is truth, and what, if anything, plays the role of consistency — I half suspect viability under selection is quietly doing the work of truth, which would put the semantics outside the system entirely and break the setup in a different place than he thinks it breaks.

So can you audit the thing properly? I'd love an explicit correspondence table — what in Gödel's construction lines up with what in the cell or in typogenetics, including the rows where nothing lines up — and then a judgment about which disanalogies are load-bearing for his conclusion and which are harmless noise. Does the degeneracy of the real genetic code matter, given that a Gödel numbering has to be injective and effectively decodable? Does the absence of a provability predicate and a standard model in a cell matter more? Is syntax plus self-reference even enough, or do you need enough arithmetic to represent derivability — the completeness of Presburger arithmetic always trips me up here? Does reverse translation plus a self-editing code plus negation change the verdict, or does it just relocate the limit? And if the isomorphism won't carry the conclusion, is there a better one that captures whatever true thing he's reaching for — quine, recursion theorem, von Neumann's constructor, halting undecidability — and does that better analogy support any of the practical pessimism about verifying self-replicators, or only a much narrower version of it?
