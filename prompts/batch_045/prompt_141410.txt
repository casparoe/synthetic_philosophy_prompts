so I work at a tiny nonprofti that runs after-school programs, and every quarter I send our main funder a report with attendance numbers. I've done it nine times now, I pull the numbers straight off the sign-in sheets, and one has never come back wrong. Two days before this quarter's deadline a board member who has been angling for more control over the program emails me: these numbers do get audited occasionally, and if there's an error the funder could pull the whole grant, and it will be traced back to whoever signed off. Then, in the next line: do you actually *know* the numbers are right?

Here's what bugs me. He didn't give me a single new reason to think they're wrong. I already knew the funder cared about accuracy, I already knew I'm the name on the sign-off, and he didn't point at a gap in how the sheets get filled in or a place where the numbers could have drifted. But after reading it I honestly can't say "yeah, I know they're right" anymore, and I'm now thinking about burning two days re-verifying every sheet, which means cancelling three site visits.

Two more things make it weirder. First, last quarter the exact same kind of numbers felt like total knowledge to me, and if a coworker had said "relax, the funder never really checks," I'd have been *even more* comfortable saying I know. So whether I know seems to slide around with what's at stake, in both directions, with nothing about the numbers themselves changing. Second, this board member obviously wants me to doubt myself — he wants the sign-off moved to him — so the stakes thing feels like a maneuver a person can run on purpose to get me to hand over a decision. But the consequences he named aren't invented; they'd be exactly as real if a friend had raised them out of kindness.

So what I want to know: is what he did a legitimate way to make me stop being a knower, or is it just pressure dressed up as an epistemic point? How should I think about whether to say "I know" here and whether to actually spend the two days? And is there a real difference between a stakes-raiser who brings new evidence that I might have erred and one who only makes vivid a risk I'd already priced in — because those two feel totally different to me and I can't say what the difference is.
