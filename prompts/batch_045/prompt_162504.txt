I am finishing the last volume of a family novel that runs from 1946 to 2024, and I have got into an argument I cannot settle with my own publisher. The marketing deck assigns every character a cohort label — Silent, Boomer, X, Millennial, Z — and speaks as if those labels explain the characters: why one of them hoards, why another cannot buy a house, why the youngest treats irony as a first language. A critic at a festival went further and told me that cohorts, not families, are the real subject of my book whether I meant it or not. I do not know whether that is a deep remark or a piece of journalistic shorthand, and before I let it affect how I revise, I would like the specification rather than the verdict: what would a theory of generational cohorts have to deliver for terms like Boomer, Millennial and Gen Z to name real kinds rather than convenient slices of a timeline? Please write the desiderata, each with the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and please fix the purpose as follows: a theory good enough to license the explanatory and predictive claims that novelists, critics and pollsters actually make with these labels — that a character born in 1981 will differ in some specifiable way from one born in 1978, that a cohort shares a structure of feeling, that knowing someone's cohort lets you infer traits you have not yet measured.

Here is the provisional list I have been carrying around, which you should feel free to cut down, split, or replace, and, above all, sort into what a theory genuinely must do and what is only a prejudice of one camp. (1) Adjacency: a boundary that puts two people born eighteen months apart in different kinds while they are distinguishable in no other respect, and whose location shifts by three or four years depending on which organisation prints the chart. (2) The age–period–cohort tangle: because cohort is just period minus age, any measured difference between cohorts is compatible with an age effect or a historical-moment effect, so I want to know what extra assumption or research design a theory must supply to break the tie — the concrete case being the familiar findings about Millennial narcissism and delayed adulthood, measured when the oldest Millennials were in their late twenties. (3) Permanence versus trait-change: nobody stops being a Millennial at forty-five, but the traits once used to define Millennials were age-linked snapshots; contrast adolescence, a kind you exit. (4) Reference before description: Gen Z was named and is discussed now, while the experiences supposed to characterise it are still unfolding, so the label's reference seems settled while its content is not. (5) Birth date versus exposure: a child raised entirely inside an older cohort's media world, or an immigrant arriving at twenty-two, separates the two, and I want a theory to say which one it is tracking, plus what formative-years window it commits to. (6) Projectibility: whether cohort membership is supposed to license inferences to properties nobody has measured yet, or is merely a summary of properties already measured. (7) The sieve problem: what stops the grid from being sliced ever more finely — Zillennials, geriatric Millennials, a cohort per year, a cohort per month — given that proposed mid-1970s cohorts have been floated and failed to stick. (8) Composition: whether apparent cohort differences reduce to cohort size, education mix, urbanisation or labour-market crowding, which are demographic causes and not shared experiences. (9) Co-presence: cohorts are made by, live among and reproduce with other cohorts, with nothing like the isolation that separates biological species; say whether that blocks kindhood or merely distinguishes generational kinds from others. (10) Scope: whether the grid travels beyond the anglophone West, when the marker events used to define it are local ones. (11) Imposed versus owned labels: cohorts are named from outside, sometimes long after the fact, as with the retrospective names applied to earlier generations, and members may reject the name; say whether self-ascription is a requirement or a red herring. (12) The cyclical picture: the popular theory that assigns each cohort a recurring archetype and repeats a four-part round roughly every eighty years — is a fixed cycle a desideratum or a liability?

Beyond sorting that list, three things matter most to me. First, mark which requirements are non-negotiable for any theory that wants to call generations real kinds and which are negotiable preferences of particular camps. Second, flag any subset that looks jointly unsatisfiable — my own suspicion is that sharp boundaries, adjacency-indistinguishability and identifiability of cohort effects cannot all be had at once, and that permanent membership sits badly with labile defining traits, but I would rather you found the real tensions than confirmed mine. Third, tell me which single requirement does the most work in eliminating candidates, since I suspect one of them quietly decides the whole question. A table is welcome. If you have room, score briefly — a few lines each — three candidate accounts: the view that cohorts are administrative or journalistic conventions useful for charting, the view that they are real causal-exposure kinds grounded in shared formative experience, and the cyclical archetype view; but keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases, since the criteria are the part I cannot write myself.
