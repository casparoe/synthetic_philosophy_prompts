Last night my friend and I argued for three hours about whether the demandingness objection refutes consequentialism, and we finished exactly where we began, which makes me suspect that whatever we were disagreeing about was not what we thought. The shape of it: I gave the ordinary case (I can redirect a fair share of my income to interventions that avert serious harm, and the reasoning seems to have no natural stopping point short of substantial sacrifice) and claimed the theory must therefore be false. She replied that all I had shown is that the theory is revisionary, that revisionariness is a cost only if the revision is not otherwise warranted, and that I had not yet given her a reason to give anything up. When I said the worry is not really about quantity but about what is left of the agent's own projects, she said that is a separate complaint, about character or integrity rather than about rightness, and that the two do not stand or fall together.

I think there is a third thing I was reaching for that neither of us named: the demand seems to apply to deliberation itself. Every act of weighing the demand, checking whether a cheaper intervention would do more good, or deciding how much of one's life to hand over is itself something one could have spent on the outcome instead, so at every order there is no non-arbitrary point at which "do the best you can" settles into an answer. Call it second-order demandingness if you want a label.

Is this one substantive dispute, or are we answering different questions under a single name: one about the criterion of rightness, one about how much revision a moral theory is licensed to demand of common sense, one about what it is to be guided by a theory? Is my regress a genuinely new objection, the old one in disguise, or a muddle about the costs of optimizing? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a distinction, a formal result about optimizing under deliberation costs, or abandoning the question in this form?
