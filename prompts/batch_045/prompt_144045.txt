You are a philosopher of punishment who also works on the ethics of speech, association, and informal social sanction — the territory where penal theory meets the philosophy of language and the ethics of boycotts. Please think carefully and at length. Before you defend any single reading, brainstorm: give me a list of at least six candidate theories of what a practice like the one below might be doing, including some you will later reject, and say which features of the practice each candidate explains and which it leaves unexplained. Only then eliminate and reconstruct. Where you are unsure, or where your answer depends on a contested empirical claim, say so explicitly rather than smoothing it over.

I need this because I have recently become responsible for administering a practice whose implicit commitments I cannot get clear about. Last spring I was elected to the council of my professional society, and because I had been loudly in favour of it, I was put on the committee that has to turn our new accountability policy into operational rules. A colleague of mine went through the process last year — she lost a plenary slot, then a book contract, then most of her collaborators — and the experience is the reason I care about getting the theory right rather than merely getting a document passed. We have to bring draft rules to the membership in six weeks. So please do not just tell me the policy is wise or unwise. I need the reconstruction: what view of punishment, of harm, and of meaning it only makes sense under, which rival views it quietly rules out, and where different parts of it seem to be pulling in different directions.

Here is the practice, described as concretely as I can. Our policy, like most in this family, has these features:

1. It is triggered by harm, defined entirely by its impact on members of the community. There is no codified list of prohibited acts, and no mental-state requirement: the target's intentions, and whether the statement was a joke, a quotation, or a retraction of a view they no longer hold, are declared irrelevant to whether a harm occurred.
2. Sanctions are graded but open-ended. The mildest outcome is a private letter; the most severe is permanent exclusion. There is no maximum, no fixed duration, and no point at which anything counts as served. Sanctions are officially revisable upward if the member's response is judged inadequate.
3. There is no statute of limitations. Statements from a decade ago, including ones already addressed and apologised for at the time, can be reopened and aggregated with new material.
4. The outcome is not described as punishment. There are no findings of misconduct and no penalties; the document speaks of conditions of accountability, and the society's public defence is that it is simply exercising its own freedoms of speech and association, as any private body may.
5. There is a restorative track, but admission of the harm as characterised is a precondition for entering it, and the member has no standing to contest the characterisation of what they did — no adversarial hearing, no cross-examination, no appeal on the merits.
6. Meanwhile, in every other context, the society insists that context and intent are decisive for what a statement means and whether it is offensive — and in its public statements about criminal justice it endorses decarceral, restorative, reintegrative alternatives to punishment.

Here is what I want to know.

First: is this punishment at all? The official framing in (4) looks like it presupposes a fairly specific view — something like the claim that only hard treatment deliberately imposed by an authorised body, and labelled as such, counts as punishment, so that uncoordinated or disclaimed social withdrawal cannot be. Is that view defensible, or does it collapse under the fact that we have an organised procedure with graded outcomes? And note the awkwardness that our sanctions are imposed by a body that disclaims punitive intent while the severity of the outcome is largely set by how many outsiders happen to pick the case up. What does a practice whose severity is fixed by that kind of contingency presuppose about proportionality, and can any theory that cares about proportionality survive it?

Second: reconstruct the positive theory. The open-endedness, the irrelevance of intent, the aggregation across time, and the fact that the object of the sanction seems to be what the person is like rather than what they did, all look to me like they fit some theories and rule out others. Does the practice strictly presuppose a non-retributive aim — denunciation, incapacitation, or general deterrence — or can a retributivist explain it too, say by treating the sanction as tracking character or as forfeiture rather than as proportional desert? Does the absence of a statute of limitations in (3) presuppose something about the persistence of the self over time, or about character as an enduring object of appraisal, and what would have to change if we held instead that punishment addresses a past self that the present person may no longer be?

Third: the intent question in (1) versus (6). Elsewhere we treat meaning and offence as intention- and context-dependent; here we treat them as fixed by uptake. Is there a coherent position that holds both — for instance, by separating the question of whether harm occurred from the question of culpability — or does the policy, by jumping straight from impact to sanction without a culpability stage, commit itself to something like strict liability? Say what strict liability would presuppose here, and whether that is consistent with the rehabilitative aims in (5).

Fourth, and most useful for my committee: distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and flag where it is underdetermined, i.e. where two or more theories would license exactly the policy we have. For each underdetermined point, tell me which additional rule we could add to discriminate between the theories. Then, for at least three rival views we might adopt instead — a retributive view with fixed tariffs and time limits; a pure freedom-of-association view on which there is no official process at all, only individual withdrawal; and a genuinely restorative view with reintegration and an endpoint — tell me concretely what in the practice would have to change.

Finally: is our official anti-carceral rhetoric in (6) actually in tension with running the harshest apparatus the society has, or does scepticism about state punishment positively recommend informal sanction of this kind? I genuinely cannot tell, and the committee is split on exactly this point.
