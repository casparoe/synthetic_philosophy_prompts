I work in machine learning, on the end of the field that trains policies against human preference data: we collect pairwise comparisons from a pool of annotators, fit a reward model to them, and then optimise a policy against that reward under a KL penalty or some equivalent trust region. I have no formal training in philosophy or welfare economics, and my grasp of expected utility theory is whatever I absorbed by osmosis. I'd like you to translate a particular result and the argument around it into the vocabulary I actually use every day, and then tell me honestly what the translation loses.

The result is Harsanyi's aggregation theorem. As I currently understand it — please correct me where I have it wrong, since I'll be reasoning from your version — it says something like: if each person's preferences over risky prospects satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, and society's preferences over those same prospects do too, and we additionally impose a Pareto condition (if everyone is indifferent between two prospects, society is indifferent; and if everyone weakly prefers one and someone strictly prefers it, society strictly prefers it), then the only possible social ranking is the one induced by a weighted sum of individual utilities, with each person's utility taken as cardinal but identified only up to a positive affine transformation. The weights are not pinned down by the theorem. There is a companion picture in which an impartial observer, placed behind a veil of ignorance and assigning equal probability to being any one of the people affected, ranks prospects by average utility; and a well-known exchange in which Harsanyi defended that average against a maximin alternative associated with Rawls.

Three things I want.

First, the translation proper. Map the argument onto: annotators and the population they are drawn from; a latent per-annotator utility and the reward model I fit to it; the affine non-identifiability of each annotator's reward and the normalisation steps I apply before combining them; a single scalar objective as linear scalarisation of a multi-objective problem, with maximin, lexicographic and Nash-style objectives as the alternatives; the training distribution over prompts and over annotators as the distribution the impartial observer takes an expectation against; and the KL budget as whatever limits how far the policy can push. Do this without quietly importing assumptions from my side and calling them translation: if the mapping only works because I have added something, say so.

Second, the residue report. Which claims of the theorem and of the average-versus-maximin argument translate cleanly; which translate only after distortion, and what exactly had to be bent; and which have no expression at all in my vocabulary. In particular I want your view on the normalisation step: my suspicion is that when I rescale each annotator's reward before averaging — by its variance, by a fixed KL budget, by whatever — I am making exactly the interpersonal comparison of utility that the philosophical debate spends its time arguing about, and that I get away with it because it looks like a numerical detail. Is that the whole story, or is something else going on?

Third, a case the translation has to handle, because it is the case I am actually in. The set of people over whom the aggregate is taken is not fixed independently of the policy. The annotator pool is drawn from today's population; the policy I deploy changes which users arrive, which ones stay, and how many there are, so the population that experiences the outcome is partly a function of the policy itself, and its size is policy-dependent too. Tell me where this goes in the ex ante construction. Does the impartial observer take an expectation over a distribution of identities whose support the policy being evaluated changes? Does the theorem's conclusion survive if she does, or does the argument simply presuppose a fixed population — and if it presupposes it, was that an assumption people argued about explicitly, or a quiet one that shows up only when you try to implement the thing? I'd also like to know whether my vocabulary has resources here that the philosophical framing lacks, or whether my framing is just blind to the problem in a way that makes it look tractable.

Please write in British English, and keep the translation honest rather than flattering: the point of the exercise for me is what fails to survive, not what does.
