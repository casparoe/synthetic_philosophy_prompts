Dennett is said to have attacked that line with a made-up device — the so-called "two-bitser", though I may well be garbling the details — whose states are about things even though nobody assigned them that aboutness; I can never work out whether such a gadget is meant to show the distinction is bogus or merely hard to draw in the case.

Please give me one concrete, easy-to-picture example of a case that puts real pressure on the original/derived distinction: not one of the standard textbook cases, and not one that turns on any fact I would need to look up. The sort of thing I have in mind is a chain of derivation that folds back on itself — imagine a system whose symbols meant something at first only because we meant something by them, but which is then retrained, generation after generation, purely on its own earlier outputs, with no human in the loop, rather like a photocopy of a photocopy. Does the aboutness of its later symbols thin out, survive undiminished, or quietly become something the distinction has no name for?

Take a definite position on how your example should be classified and on whether it damages the distinction itself, and give your reasons, rather than listing the options and leaving me to choose between them; and if your example depends on reading Dennett's objection in a particular and contested way, say plainly that you are doing so. Please answer in British English.
