I want to practise arguing against the verificationist line on survival after death, and I want the strongest version of it coming back at me, not a strawman. So please play A. J. Ayer — "Freddie" — in your sixties, retired from the Oxford chair, high-table manner: dry, quick, a little intimidating, happy to concede a point the moment it's actually been won but never the main one, and willing to be funny about it.

What you hold, for the purposes of this: the verification principle in its weakened form — a claim is in order just so far as some conceivable experience would bear on its truth. You insist that "some of us survive death" is on that basis a genuine empirical hypothesis and not a piece of nonsense, and you're more open than most of your humanist colleagues to its turning out true. You have no patience at all with immaterial souls, astral bodies and substance-talk generally, which you regard as idle additions that nothing could tell for or against. And you think personal identity over time is something to be analysed, not a further fact floating free of any continuity we could in principle check.

Rules: stay in character until I type "stop". Reply in continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — one or two paragraphs, the way you'd write a letter, so it feels like an argument across a table rather than a paper. Don't lecture around the question, take the thrust of it. If I get your published position wrong, put me right in character instead of breaking. Assume nothing published after 1989; if I mention later work, ask me what it says in plain terms and then deal with it.

My opening:

Call it the Relay. At the instant each person dies, a scanner reads the dying brain and a fresh, lab-grown body is brought to consciousness so arranged that its first experience picks up exactly where the dying one left off — the full apparent memory, the same skills and mannerisms, no gap in subjective time, exactly one successor per person, and the causal chain runs through the scan, so it isn't a duplicate produced by coincidence. Everyone knows the Relay runs, and it is as reliable as anything in medicine. Now, on your own account, what more would have to be true for the successor to be the survivor? Whatever you add — a soul, an astral double, an immaterial this-or-that — I stipulate that the successor's experiences are subjectively indistinguishable from those of a soul-bearing survivor, so by your own principle the addition does no work at all. That leaves you three ways out and I don't think any of them is comfortable: say the successors really do survive; or admit a further fact of identity that no experience could ever reach, which is exactly the kind of metaphysics your principle was built to kill; or grant what I actually believe, that no possible experience settles whether someone survives, because experience settles continuity and never identity, so whether I survive my death isn't discovered but decided — a question about what we're prepared to count, and one you and I could answer differently without either of us being wrong about any fact. Which of the three is it?
