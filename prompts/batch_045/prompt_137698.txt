I'm a PhD student in philosophy finishing a paper on derived intentionality — the conditions under which an artifact counts as a subject rather than as an instrument someone uses — and I want to use Tom Riddle's diary in *Harry Potter and the Chamber of Secrets* as a case study, read against the Einstein's-brain thought experiment collected in Hofstadter and Dennett's *The Mind's I* (1981), where a preserved brain keeps up a conversation and the suggestion arises that the person might be located wherever the right information-bearing structure is, possibly a book.

The complication I want the reading to sit with is host-dependence. The diary is causally inert on its own: it forms no aim it can execute without recruiting someone to write, and its internal states seem to have content only derivatively, borrowed from Riddle's memories — on an etiological or teleological account of content it is a consumer of content, not a producer. But that is arguably just the condition of any book: a book acts on the world only by being read, and a book is exactly the artifact that thought experiment offers as a candidate person. So: is the diary's dependence on Ginny a reason to demote it to a tool, or is it the dependence every book has, and therefore no objection at all?

Second, keep the intentional stance separate from consciousness attribution. Predictively the diary is a gift to the stance — treating it as an agent with aims is what lets anyone anticipate what it will do next — but the diary's behavioural repertoire is also very narrow, which is where Dennett's own worry about cheap tricks bites. Predictive payoff is one thing; whether there is something it is like to be the diary is another. Say which the novel gives us grounds to assert, and note where the narrative slides between the two, since the diary is destroyed at once as a person and as a thing.

Third, the later books reveal it to be a horcrux, a shard of a soul, so canon eventually settles what the thought experiment leaves open. Does that retroactively dissolve the interest of the earlier evidence, or does it show that readers — and Harry — were tracking stance-relative facts all along, and that the soul-talk is doing no independent work?

Give me a reading that is faithful in both directions: don't reduce the diary to an illustration, and don't stretch the philosophical view to fit Rowling. Close with a one-sentence bottom line beginning "Verdict:" stating whether the diary is best read as a subject, an instrument, or a continuation of Riddle, followed by one sentence on what the case shows that the bare thought experiment does not.
