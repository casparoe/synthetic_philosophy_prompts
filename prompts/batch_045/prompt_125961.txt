I'm a PhD student in early modern philosophy, and I'm writing a paper on Berkeley's so-called master argument. In trying to reconstruct it in a form that a当代 skeptic can't simply wave away, I have produced a version that seems to me to prove far too much, and I can no longer see where the damage is. I'd be grateful for a diagnosis.

The material I start from is the challenge Berkeley issues in the Principles, where he invites you to conceive of trees standing in a park or books sitting in a closet with nobody by to perceive them, and then points out that the attempt defeats itself, since in trying to conceive of the objects as unconceived you conceive them. My reconstruction:

(1) Reflexive step. For any object x, if I try to conceive of x as existing unconceived, then in so trying x is conceived by me. So I never succeed in conceiving of x as unconceived.

(2) Framing principle (weak). If a subject cannot perform the act of conceiving that p, then that subject cannot so much as frame the thought that p — cannot assert it, entertain it, or take it to be a possibility.

(2*) Modal principle (strong). If a subject cannot conceive that p, then p is impossible (or, on a weaker variant, the supposition that p is empty).

(3) So no one can frame the thought that anything exists unconceived. [1, 2]

(4) Substitution schema. Nothing in (1) turns on the verb conceive in particular. For any intentional verb V whose content can be about states of the very kind V denotes — imagine, represent, think of, believe, desire — the same reflexive structure arises: in imagining x as unimagined I imagine it; in believing that nobody believes p I make the content false; in desiring that no one desire a thing I desire it. So the reasoning of (1) generalizes: nobody can frame the thought that there are unimagined things, unrepresented things, unthought-of things, undesired things.

(5) Applying (2*): it is impossible that there be unimagined, unrepresented, unthought-of things. That is, everything that exists is at every moment of its existence an object of my actual, occurrent mental activity.

(6) That is false on grounds independent of anything Berkeleyan: I spend a good part of my life in dreamless sleep, and the overwhelming majority of what I take to exist is never occurrently before my mind.

(7) A colleague points out that the failure is in (2*) and that Berkeley needs only the weak framing principle. But the weak version generates its own trouble: if (1) and (4) are sound, I cannot frame the thought that there are things nobody is thinking about — yet framing that thought is exactly what I appear to do in step (6), and in ordinary talk about sleep, unread books, and unvisited rooms. The argument then convicts me of failing to think thoughts I manifestly can think, which is a worse result than the false conclusion in (5), not a better one.

(8) Second horn. Substitute perceived for the verb and apply the apparatus to spirits. Berkeley holds that minds are not perceived, that ideas are inert, and that an idea can resemble nothing but an idea, which is why we have at most a notion of a mind rather than an idea of one. But if inability to conceive is what disqualifies a claim, then the unperceived spirits Berkeley needs — God above all, who perceives everything and is perceived by nothing — fall to the same argument. If instead the reflexive step is restricted to things of which we can have ideas, it seems to establish only that we can frame no idea of mind-independent matter, and not that matter cannot exist unconceived.

What I would most like to keep is (1) and (4): the reflexive structure strikes me as genuine, and some contents in that family really are unsatisfiable (nobody believes that p, and p is true is a content no one can truly believe). If I can keep (3) as well, so much the better, since my thesis is that the argument does real work for Berkeley. That leaves (2)/(2*), the step from (1) to (3), or the substitution itself as the place to give ground.

Please tell me:

(i) Which step you reject, stated precisely — is the failure in the reflexive step, in the substitution schema, in the two principles, or in an equivocation or shift that occurs between steps?

(ii) Why that step looked sound enough to survive casual scrutiny.

(iii) What each available rejection costs. I want the alternatives priced, not just the one you prefer: what does Berkeley lose if the reflexive step goes, what does he lose if the substitution goes, and what does he lose if only (2*) goes?

(iv) What a defender of the master argument should say to stop the generalization in (4). What feature distinguishes conceived from believed such that the reflexive structure might be benign in one case and not in the other? A supervisor of mine suggests that the difference is that in the belief case the attitude and the content concern the same relation, whereas the master argument must quietly assume that an object's existing just is its being conceived, which would make the argument circular. I am not convinced by this, since Berkeley presents the argument as a proof of his principle rather than as a corollary of it, and in any case I don't see how the circularity charge would explain why (4) feels compelling. Assess that suggestion, and tell me if the real distinction lies elsewhere.

(v) What the strongest surviving version of the master argument looks like after the repair, what it can establish, and what it cannot.

(vi) Whether the second horn in (8) is a genuine difficulty for Berkeley, or an artifact of the way I have set the argument up.

I don't need references or a reading list — I have the secondary material at hand. What I want is the argumentative diagnosis, at the level of the steps I've numbered above.
