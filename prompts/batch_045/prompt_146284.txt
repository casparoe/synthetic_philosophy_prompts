I am putting together the closing session of an undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of biology, on the question of what life is, and I would like to end it with a poem that does philosophical work rather than decorating it. Specifically, I would like a sestina.

The formal conceit: the six end-words should not be abstractions but six borderline cases — fire, virus, mule, spore, colony, and one case of my own devising, which I will call the selvage. The six stanzas should each take up one candidate mark of life in turn: metabolism; reproduction; heredity with variation, that is, evolution by selection on heritable differences (one familiar working definition in astrobiology runs: a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution); bounded individuality or compartmentalization; the problem of dormancy; and, last, the selvage. Which mark a stanza is interrogating has to be legible from the stanza itself — no titles, epigraphs, or glosses. The case-words should migrate through the line-positions in the ordinary sestina rotation, so that no case ever comes to rest twice in the same place; I want the form itself to carry the point that no ordering of the marks settles anything.

The cases should fail the marks for the reasons they actually fail them: fire metabolizes and grows but accumulates no heritable variation, and its boundary is a front, not a membrane; a virus is metabolically inert and borrows another cell's machinery; a mule is indubitably alive and cannot reproduce; a desiccated spore — or a cryptobiotic animal, or a frozen embryo — is alive throughout an interval in which nothing metabolic is happening, so that the list of marks cannot be a list of current activities; a colony or superorganism, an ant colony or a siphonophore, puts pressure on where one individual ends and another begins.

The selvage is the case I most want handled carefully, and it is the one a model cannot have read about. It is a laboratory lineage of peptide coacervate droplets. It takes up a supplied feedstock and chemically transforms it so as to maintain its own organization; it divides by shear-fragmentation; the composition of a droplet biases the composition of its daughters, so over months the lineage accumulates heritable variation and adapts to a changing feedstock; it has no membrane, its boundary being a liquid-liquid phase separation, and no nucleic acid, no code, no translation apparatus — its heredity is compositional, as a prion's is, only far richer. It was designed: the first droplet was assembled by chemists from purified parts, and everything since has been division and selection. It survives complete desiccation, after which it can be re-instantiated from a stored specification of its composition, so the lineage passes through an interval in which nothing living exists and its continuity across that bottleneck is informational rather than material.

It therefore satisfies nearly every mark going, and the hesitation about calling it alive is not the hesitation we have about fire. I want the poem to locate that hesitation exactly. The candidates are: that heredity without a code is not heredity; that a designed origin disqualifies it, which would make life a historical property one can forfeit by being made; that self-maintenance paid for by a supplied feedstock is not autonomy; that a thing whose lineage can pass through a specification is a product rather than an organism, however much it evolves thereafter.

Give the poem its volta beginning in the fifth stanza, at dormancy, and completing in the sixth, at the selvage: the move from asking which mark is necessary and sufficient to asking what work we want the word to do, since the physiologist's question, the astrobiologist's question, and the question of what may be owed to a living thing are not the same question, and one list of marks can serve all three only by going vague. The envoi should carry all six words, two to a line, and should leave the choice open — neither "life is just a cluster concept, so nothing is at stake" nor "there is a real essence we have not found yet" — while leaving the reader knowing precisely what is at stake in it.

Unrhymed, line lengths flexible but with a pulse underneath; it will be read aloud, so it must work on the ear, and no line should be wrenched out of shape to land an end-word. Technical terms are welcome where they earn their place. Someone who knows this literature should find the poem right about the cases and the marks; someone who does not should still find a poem.
