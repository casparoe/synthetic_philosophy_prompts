Hi — I'm the (reluctant) chair of the animal care committee at a mid-sized medical school. We review somewhere around 180 protocols a year: mostly mice and rats, a couple of rabbit projects, one pig lab, and — after we absorbed a small primate center last year — some macaque work. Our meeting is two hours once a month and we get through maybe twelve protocols, so I've got roughly ten minutes of discussion each, and the room is me, two working scientists, a veterinarian, the facility manager, and two lay members (a retired teacher, and a guy from a local animal advocacy group who is actually pretty reasonable and very good at spotting when the rest of us are hand-waving).

Here's my problem. Everyone agrees the committee is supposed to do a harm–benefit analysis. Nobody can tell me how to actually do one across species. The guidance we operate under amounts to: think about the animals' pain and distress, think about the value of the research, use fewer animals, use anesthesia, don't repeat work. Fine as far as it goes, but it completely falls apart exactly where the decision is the comparison. Is 240 mice better or worse than 12 macaques? Is "we will learn how this circuit works" a benefit that can justify a thing, or only a benefit if somebody can say what it's for? Does it matter that the pigs we use are, on every test anyone runs, smarter than the dogs at the shelter down the road? And what do we do with the crayfish people, where nobody can tell me whether the thing we're measuring is pain or just a reflex?

I've been reading around in animal ethics — the utilitarian stuff and the literature on speciesism — and the thought I keep coming back to is the one about marginal cases: if a protocol would obviously be unacceptable were the subjects humans at the same or lower psychological level (infants, people with profound cognitive disabilities), then whatever is doing the justifying work isn't their level, and I want my committee to have to say out loud what it actually is. But I don't want a procedure that just says "weigh all sentient interests equally," because that would ban essentially everything we review, nobody on the committee believes it, and a procedure that outputs something we won't do is not a procedure, it's a gesture.

So please write me an actual decision procedure we can run, in order, in about ten minutes, on a real protocol, and write into the minutes. Concretely:

- The sequence of questions, and for each one, what counts as an adequate answer from the investigators — what evidence, in what form — and what counts as a dodge. I especially want the dodge list; we get an enormous amount of "the literature supports it" and "this is standard practice in the field."
- The points where the procedure stops with a hard no, and the points where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts — and how to mark that handoff in the minutes so it's clear we made a judgment and didn't just forget to check.
- A way of doing the interspecies comparison that doesn't pretend we can convert mouse suffering into human units and doesn't just throw up its hands either. My rough idea: instead of comparing animals to us, compare each animal to the human we'd be willing to use if we had to use humans of comparable capacities — a kind of capacity-matched stand-in — and then make the investigator defend the gap between that stand-in and the actual animal. Improve on it or replace it if it's broken.
- A device I've started wanting: some kind of standing register of the capacities we ascribe to each species we see, so we can't treat pigs as cognitively fancy when we're arguing they suffer and as cognitively simple when we're arguing the research is necessary. I don't know what to call it or how to maintain it, but the inconsistency is real and I've caught our own minutes doing it.
- How to handle taxa where sentience is genuinely uncertain, and how to handle "replacement" cases where the replacement lowers the individual but multiplies the count and weakens the science at the same time.

Then run the finished procedure on three cases, showing the minutes entry for each: (1) a curiosity-driven mouse study — receptor X, social behavior, no application anyone can name, moderate but real harm; (2) the macaque-to-mouse swap I mentioned, where the mouse model is acknowledged to be worse; (3) our breeding colony, which produces a steady surplus of animals who are killed without ever being used in anything. I have a feeling at least one of these comes out as a no to something we currently approve, and I'd rather find out here than in a meeting.

Two last constraints. The checklist itself has to be runnable by a retired teacher and a veterinarian who have never read any philosophy — put the theory in a separate section addressed to me, not in the steps. And tell me plainly where the procedure will misfire, including the place where the advocacy guy will push and be right.
