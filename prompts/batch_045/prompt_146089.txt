I am writing a paper for a postgraduate seminar on duties of rescue, and I want to use Ursula Le Guin's 1973 short story "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" as the case study. The reading I need is of the story against Singer's drowning-child argument: the shallow pond, and the strong principle that argument is used to establish — roughly, that if we can prevent something bad from happening without thereby sacrificing anything of comparable moral significance, we ought to do it. I can summarise the story and the argument myself, and I do not want either summarised back to me, nor any passage quoted. What I want is for the two to be put into genuine contact.

The reason I think the pairing is worth something is a structural point I have not seen made anywhere. Singer's passer-by has two options: wade in, or walk on. Le Guin's citizens have three. They can stay and accept the bargain, knowing what it costs; they can free the child, at the price the story stipulates, namely the withering of the whole city's happiness; or they can walk away alone into an unknown country, helping no one, and in particular not helping the child. It is the third option that interests me, because it has no obvious place in a duty-of-rescue framework. It is not rescue, since the child's condition is unchanged. It does not look supererogatory, since the walkers-away are presented less as saints than as the only honest people left. And it is not mere omission, since it costs them everything they have. I have been calling it an unprofitable refusal: declining to go on accepting a benefit that you cannot bring yourself to end, at no profit to the victim.

So I would like four things addressed. First, whether a broadly Singer-style position can make room for unprofitable refusal at all — as a duty, or as something rationally admirable. If consequentialism is forced to count walking away as either pointless or a dereliction, then the story may be dramatising a moral response that the argument cannot so much as register, and I would like that said plainly. If it can make room, I want the mechanism: character, integrity, collective action amongst the departures, effects on the practice itself — and whether that mechanism smuggles in more than Singer can afford.

Second, the cost structure. Singer's argument earns its demandingness from iteration: there is always another child, and the sacrifice does not become morally comparable until one has given to the point where further giving costs as much as it helps. Omelas replaces that long slope with a single cliff. Is that an unfair test — a case where the principle falls silent rather than a case it fails — or does the cliff expose something? Notice that the walkers-away appear to hold both that the price of rescue is too high to be required and that taking the benefit is nonetheless not open to them. Can a defender of the strong principle accept either half?

Third, scope. Singer's principle is framed around prevention; Omelas is a case of production and maintenance, sustained daily by a practice the citizens could withdraw from. Does that difference mark the boundary between duties of rescue and duties of justice, such that the story is not really about rescue at all? I keep being told that it is a case of complicity and that a weaker argument therefore suffices, and I am unpersuaded; I would like the objection taken seriously and then tested against the ending, where the walkers-away do not protest, organise, or attempt to free anyone.

Finally, where the work resists the reading. The dependency is magical rather than economic, whereas our own relation to distant suffering is statistical; the story refuses to say what becomes of those who leave, or whether the child is even rescuable; and Le Guin credited William James's 1891 essay "The Moral Philosopher and the Moral Life", not Singer, for the central idea. I want an honest separation of what is doing philosophical work from what is stage-setting, and a closing claim about what the story adds to this debate that the arguments alone do not.
