I serve on the seven-member board of a private foundation that runs a rapid-response fund for early-stage work on pathogen surveillance and countermeasures. Under our current bylaws any single trustee may authorize an emergency grant of up to $200,000 without consulting the others; in practice about a third of our awards go out that way, often within 48 hours of a proposal arriving. Two episodes have forced a bylaw review. In the first, one trustee authorized funding for a group proposing to synthesize and characterize a horsepox-like orthopoxvirus in a facility with containment well below what our own guidelines would require; he had read the full application and judged the risk acceptable, while four of us, shown the same materials afterwards, judged it reckless and would have blocked it. In the second, another trustee used the same clause to push $180,000 to a sequencing lab for work the board would almost certainly have approved anyway, which makes us wonder how much speed the clause actually buys.

Several trustees have converged on a structural worry: because each of us can act alone, a grant goes out whenever at least one trustee's own read of the proposal says go, even if most of us would have said no — and that exposure grows both with the number of people who can act alone and with how much of the downside falls on people who had no say. The review subcommittee's proposed fix is: two trustees required for any award above $50,000; any single trustee retains the power to block; 72 hours' notice to the full board with objections logged, waived only where the applicant can document a hard external deadline.

Before we vote I need a memo to circulate. Please draft it as a board decision memo: a bottom-line recommendation (specific rule, threshold, and time frame) followed by the reasoning, including an honest treatment of the two arguments that pull hardest against it.

1. Our holdout trustee argues that declining does not prevent the research, it only transfers it to a funder who will ask fewer questions, so the relevant comparison is not funding versus not funding but funding under our conditions versus funding under someone else's. If that is right, restricting unilateral authority may buy much less than it appears to.

2. The trustee who proposes a grant has read the full application; the others would be voting on a one-page summary inside 72 hours. Requiring agreement may therefore hand the decision to the least informed members, and it is not obvious that errors of uninformed blocking are safer, in expectation, than errors of informed enthusiasm.

The memo should also say whether the asymmetry between unilateral blocking and two-person authorizing is principled or merely cautious, and whether the calculus changes given that we are separately considering expanding the board to nine trustees. Please reason from the structure of the situation rather than from the literature; do not cite sources.
