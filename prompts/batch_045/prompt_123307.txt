So here's why this is on my mind: I just handed in the manuscript for my first book, a short trade book on statistics, and it's got maybe 300 checkable claims in it. I went through and verified every one, and I'd still put money on each of them individually. But I also wrote the usual line in the preface about how any mistakes left in the book are my own, and I meant it — I'd bet good money there's at least one error somewhere in there too. My partner says that's just modesty. I say it's an actual inconsistency and it's bugging me. Someone told me this thing has a name, the preface paradox, and that it has a sibling called the lottery paradox, and I've skimmed a bit about both but the writeups feel like they're circling something without grabbing it.

About me: I'm an applied statistician, that's the day job — A/B tests, multiplicity corrections, family-wise versus per-comparison versus false discovery error rates, calibration curves, posterior thresholds, decision rules under asymmetric loss. I'm fluent in all of it, I use it weekly. I have essentially no philosophy.

So: restate both paradoxes in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, including the standard moves people make in response — the threshold idea where you count as believing something once your credence clears some bar, the principle that rational belief closes under conjunction, and the move of junking flat belief altogether and going graded. Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want: what translated cleanly, what only translated because you fudged it, and what has no counterpart at all in how I talk. My vocabulary has probabilities and decision procedures but no attitude called belief that anyone could call me inconsistent for having. Please don't quietly substitute high probability for belief and then call the result a translation — if that's what you're doing, say so.

One wrinkle I want built in, because I don't see it in anything I've skimmed and I think it matters: my checks were not independent. Every claim in that book was verified through the same pipeline against the same two sources, so if one claim is wrong the others are more likely to be wrong too — the errors are correlated, not independent draws like lottery tickets. That seems like it should change the arithmetic on the conjunction somehow, and I can't tell whether it makes the paradox weaker, stronger, or just a different animal.

Give me exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, no intro and no conclusion, in American English: (1) the lottery paradox in my vocabulary, (2) the preface paradox in my vocabulary, (3) what mapped cleanly, (4) what you had to fudge and what couldn't be said in my vocabulary at all, (5) the correlated errors version and your verdict on whether the residue is a hole in my framework or a sign the philosophy is thinner than it sounds.
