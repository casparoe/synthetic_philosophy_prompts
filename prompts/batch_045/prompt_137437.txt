Two people in a reading group I sit in on got into it after the session last night over whether the poverty-of-the-stimulus argument for innate grammar is basically dead. I have no idea who won — I flip depending on who is talking. Rough reconstruction, I'm numbering the moves so they're easy to respond to (I've probably garbled bits of it):

(1) Mira: the argument was always an argument from 1960s ignorance about what's actually in the input. Linguists eyeballed child-directed speech, couldn't see how the data could pin the grammar down, and concluded the rest must be built in. But we can check now: train a domain-general learner on raw text, no hand-coded rules, and it picks up long-distance dependencies and agreement across intervening junk — the stuff that was supposed to be unlearnable. Whatever you think of the theory, the empirical premise is dead. The stimulus was never that poor.

(2) Ted: that conflates behaving correctly on text with ending up with the knowledge the kid has. The argument was always about specific generalizations. Take structure-dependence: when kids learn to form yes/no questions they move the auxiliary belonging to the main clause, not the first auxiliary in the string — *is the man who is hungry ordering dinner*, not the version where the auxiliary inside the relative clause gets moved. Nobody models that contrast, nobody corrects it, and the wrong version looks like the easier one if all you're doing is tracking word order.

(3) Mira: this is the unfalsifiability problem in a nutshell — whatever a learner does, you say "that's not the real knowledge." Give me one observation that would count against the nativist claim. And it's worse than that: any learner needs a bias. Brains aren't blank slates, but neither is a neural net; without some prior nothing is learnable at all. So "the data didn't determine the grammar" is true of every induction ever, and it can't possibly distinguish a dedicated language faculty from domain-general machinery.

(4) Ted: I'll grant the second half — the argument as usually stated doesn't do that work. But you're sliding between two claims. First: children converge on a much narrower set of generalizations than the input allows. That's empirical. Second: the best explanation is a species-specific, domain-specific faculty. That's a theoretical leap, and I agree the argument alone doesn't establish it. Your "every learner needs a bias" line is a truism that never touches the first claim, because the first claim isn't just "something innate is involved."

(5) Mira: but what the generalizations are depends on what you count as the input. Prosody, joint attention, the situations the kid is in, the fact that speech to children is simplified and repetitive — draw the boundary around "the data" differently and the poverty mostly evaporates. You're picking the boundary that pre-decides the answer.

(6) Ted: sure, but the boundary can't swallow everything — at some point you're counting the meanings the child is supposed to recover as part of the signal, and then the empiricist wins by definition. And some of the relevant evidence isn't subtle, it's absent: no child is handed a list of impossible sentences, and yet children still converge on the same impossible ones.

(7) Mira: absence is evidence too. If you're tracking a distribution and a form never shows up, that's a reason to downweight it; you don't need anyone to correct you. The old learnability result people cite assumes you have to pin a language down exactly from a space of candidate languages, which nobody thinks learners are doing.

(8) Ted: then you've located the innateness in the hypothesis space, which is my point. Absence can only be evidence if your candidate generalizations are constrained ahead of time. The real disagreement isn't innate versus learned, it's about what the constraints are and where they come from.

(9) Mira: that's a bait and switch. If "innateness" just means "the learner comes with constraints," the thesis is uninteresting and everyone always accepted it; if it means the constraints are a language-specific module, then nothing you said supports it.

Then Ted tried a thought experiment. Two learners, same child-directed corpus. One comes with a built-in constraint that operations on auxiliaries are structure-dependent; the other is a plain sequence learner with far more parameters and far more data. Both ace the standard tests. Then you test them on a sentence type they never saw questions built from: the first generalizes immediately, the second does something that tracks surface order. Ted: that shows the constraint is doing work that no quantity of data substitutes for. Mira: that's rigged — build the constraint in and of course the system generalizes; leave it out and all you've shown is that that architecture was wrong, which is just the nativist claim wearing a lab coat.

On the way out someone also threw in the case of kids whose parents only had improvised home sign and who ended up with something more grammatical and consistent than what they were given — or so the story goes — and nobody answered it before we got kicked out of the room.
