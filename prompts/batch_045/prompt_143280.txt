I rely on the outside view constantly in my own work. I run a small team that does data migrations for clients, and before any project I ask what fraction of comparable migrations finished on schedule, rather than letting the team talk itself through its own plan and produce a date. A colleague in our strategy group has become convinced that this practice is empty, and gave me the argument below. I think it proves too much, but I cannot locate the exact error, and he is not budging. His stipulations: call a forecast *singular* if it is produced by reasoning about the details of the particular case, and *distributional* if it is produced by locating the case in a class of cases and reading off that class's outcome frequency.

(1) A distributional forecast yields a number only once a class has been fixed; and any case belongs to arbitrarily many classes, which yield arbitrarily many different frequencies.
(2) So a distributional forecast must be preceded by a judgment about which similarities matter for the outcome — that is, by the very kind of singular reasoning it was supposed to replace.
(3) Whatever singular reasoning fixes the class also fixes the answer; the frequency thereafter only reports back what was put in.
(4) Hence a distributional forecast has no evidential force of its own: it cannot correct a singular forecast, since any error in the singular reasoning at step (2) is transmitted straight through to it.
(5) Therefore the outside view — the thesis that distributional forecasts are to be preferred to singular ones — is false, or at any rate empty.
(6) But the form of the argument is entirely general. Any application of a statistical generalization to a particular case — trial-level base rates applied to a patient, component failure rates applied to a particular bridge, actuarial tables applied to a particular driver — requires judging which class the particular case falls under, and that judgment is singular reasoning about the case. So by (4) and (5) none of them has evidential force either, which is absurd: medicine and engineering are not empty.
(7) So something in (1)–(5) must go.

I want to keep (2): it seems clearly right that class selection is a substantive judgment and not a mechanical matter.
