I have no academic background in linguistics or philosophy — I work as an actuary, and I got into this reading on my own after my daughter started talking — and over the last couple of years I've drifted into a position about the poverty of the stimulus argument that I'd now like stress-tested. I want to know what would count against it, not what counts against nativism generally; the object here is my view.

The view has two parts.

The first is deflationary about the argument's content. As I read it, the argument from the poverty of the stimulus, run honestly, can only ever establish something thin: that human language learners are not blank slates, that they bring some bias toward certain hypotheses over others. And that thin conclusion is, I think, compatible with nearly everything the argument's opponents want — learning driven by statistics over the input, no dedicated language faculty, no rich innate grammar of the sort peoplepicture. If the argument is only ever going to deliver that, it isn't delivering the Universal Grammar that it's standardly wheeled out to support.

The second part is a claim about the debate itself. At the level where the argument is actually conducted, I don't think the two sides make differential predictions. Whatever children are observed to acquire, each side can accommodate after the fact: if a child acquires something for which the input looks too thin, the anti-nativist says the input was richer than assumed, or the learning was easier than assumed, or the child got indirect evidence in some form; and there is, I claim, no shared way of measuring how rich the input is or how large the relevant hypothesis space is that could settle which redescription is right. So I've concluded that the argument is close to evidentially inert as usually run, and that the dispute is stalled for a structural reason rather than because either side is short of data. I want to be clear that this is a view about the argument, not about innateness: I'm genuinely agnostic about whether there's a domain-specific language faculty.

What would count against this? I'd like the defeaters ranked, fatal at one end and merely awkward at the other, and I'd like them separated into (a) things that would refute the thin reading, (b) things that would refute only the stall claim, and (c) things that would just embarrass me.

Some things I specifically want you to take up, because they're where I feel most unsure:

Observations that would break the symmetry I'm claiming. I have in mind cases where learners reliably fail to entertain a hypothesis that the input would have supported — the standard structure-dependence examples, e.g. the passive pairs where a rule stated in linear order would wrongly predict that The dog is believed's owner to be hungry is fine, and the parallel case in yes-no question formation, where children are said essentially never to front the first auxiliary they hear rather than the one in the main clause. Is that empirical claim about children actually well established, or is it folklore? And crucially: could the systematic absence of an error be explained by something other than a constrained hypothesis space — processing limits, memory, the way comprehension experiments are built — and if so, how would I tell the explanations apart?

Cases where learners produce structure that wasn't in what they heard. I've read that deaf children exposed only to an inconsistent gestural system with no settled grammar ended up with something considerably more grammatical than what they were given, and people treat this as evidence for innateness. How strong is that, really, and how much of it turns on facts about the specific situation that I can't check? More generally: what would a case of this shape have to look like to be decisive rather than merely suggestive?

Comparisons of data budgets. Systems trained on text now produce grammatical-looking output, and people argue about whether that refutes the argument. My own sense is that the comparison only bites if the system sees input comparable in quantity and kind to what a child sees, and that architectural differences make the comparison hard to interpret either way. How much weight should a non-specialist put on this? Is there a version of the comparison that would actually be informative?

Formal learnability results. I've heard that there's a classical result in learning theory to the effect that languages can't be identified from positive examples alone under a demanding criterion of success, and that this is sometimes cited in the argument's favor. Does it bear on the empirical question at all, or is it a result about an artificially hard standard that tells us nothing about children?

Then, the things I can't supply myself:

What would someone who holds my deflationary view have to concede as counting against it — not the things nativists say about it, but the things a sympathetic holder of the view would grant as genuine defeaters?

Whether my position has been qualified into unfalsifiability. My claim that there's no shared measure of input-richness or hypothesis-space size looks like it could be doing exactly the insulating work I'm accusing the other side of. If that's right, is it fatal for a view of this kind, or is a methodological claim allowed to be insulated in a way an empirical one isn't?

Whether the thin reading is really a distinct position, or whether it collapses into one of the views it's supposed to be an alternative to. If nativists only ever claimed a bias over hypotheses and nothing richer, then my deflation is a straw man, and I'd rather be told so.

Which of the defeaters have arguably already arrived, and how decisive each is.

For what it's worth on the receiving end: I'm most moved by arguments from data budgets and from cases where structure appears without having been in the input, and least moved by claims that something is unlearnable when the claim rests on a linguist's sense of how large a hypothesis space is. Tell me if that weighting is itself a mistake.

Two practical constraints. I have no library access and can't look anything up, so please describe results, findings, and cases in your own words rather than pointing me at papers, authors, or further reading — I don't want citations or a bibliography, and I'd rather you not name studies at all if the name is doing the work. And please be explicit about the confidence structure of what you give me: separate what is settled among people who actually work on this from what is live controversy, tell me where you're reporting a rough sense of the field's centre of gravity rather than something established, and flag any place where my framing above depends on a contested reading of what the argument is even claiming. If my position turns out to be well insulated, say so, and say whether that's a problem.
