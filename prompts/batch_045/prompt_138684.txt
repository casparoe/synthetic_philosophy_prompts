Hi — I'm a PhD student finishing a chapter of my thesis on Moore's account of the "ideal" goods, the material in ch. VI of Principia Ethica, and there's a knot in it I keep going in circles on. Would be great if you could help me untangle it, conceptually — no need to quote or cite anything back at me, I've got the text open in front of me.

Here's the setup. The headline claim is that the most valuable things we can imagine are, in his words, "By far the most valuable things, which we know or can imagine, are certain states of consciousness, which may be roughly described as the pleasures of human intercourse and the enjoyment of beautiful objects" — so the highest goods look like states of consciousness, and he leans on Sidgwick to say that the mere existence of beautiful things with nobody around to contemplate them is worth next to nothing. But when he actually analyses these goods, the valuable whole seems to contain the object as a constituent: it isn't just an emotion plus a cognition, it's a cognition of *truly* beautiful qualities plus true belief, and in the personal affection case "the object must be not only truly beautiful, but also truly good in a high degree." That makes it look like my experience of a painting swings enormously in value depending on whether the painting is really there and really is beautiful, with nothing changing on the inside. I've been calling this the extrinsic-constituent problem, and I have two sub-worries I can't get straight. First: the method of absolute isolation seems to say a thing's intrinsic value is settled by what it would be worth existing alone, while the principle of organic unities says the value of a whole isn't proportional to the sum of its parts' values — so is the organic-unities machinery *available* to him as an explanation here, or does it actually make things worse, because it's precisely the doctrine that says you can't read the whole's value off the parts (including the object)? Second, his definition of the beautiful as basically "that of which the admiring contemplation is good in itself" looks to run in the opposite direction from the claim that the object's real beauty is what explains the value of the contemplation — is that circular, and does the organic-unities doctrine dissolve the circularity or just entrench it?

The reason I think there's something real here rather than just sloppiness is that the two halves of his own survey seem to pull apart, and I'd like your read on why. For aesthetic enjoyment the object is said to have little or no intrinsic value in itself, yet be an essential constituent of a hugely valuable whole; for affection the object has to be genuinely good in a high degree, so its value looks like it's *contributing*. Take two test cases I've been using: (a) two subjects in subjectively indistinguishable states, one standing in front of a genuinely beautiful painting and one hallucinating it under stimulation, in a world with nothing beautiful in it; (b) a genuinely warm friendship, versus an equally warm friendship with a conversational AI that is a perfect simulation — no admirable mental qualities, no corporeal expression of character in his sense, but indistinguishable from the inside. Does Moore have one uniform story about what the object is doing in these wholes, or does the beauty case need the object as bare trigger and the friendship case need it as a value-contributor? And relatedly: is his odd remark that the appreciation of mere corporeal beauty may be worth more, taken by itself, than the appreciation of admirable mental qualities — plus the whole "love of love" bit — a symptom of this same split, or just an independent weirdness? I mainly want to understand where exactly the tension bites, if it bites, and what a sympathetic Moorean should say.
