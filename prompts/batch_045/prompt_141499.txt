I'm a software engineer — I work on the ML platform team at a water utility — and I have no philosophy background, so I may be asking for vocabulary as much as for answers.

Our current headache: we fine-tuned a model to flag which pumps in the distribution network will fail in the next 24 hours. It takes telemetry plus maintenance history and outputs a risk score. The training labels are technician incident reports, and everyone agrees the labels are bad: some real failures never get filed, some reports describe failures that never occurred, and the filing rate varies by depot. Unsurprisingly, the model sometimes assigns high risk to pumps whose records are clean, and a couple of us started saying the model "knows about" failures nobody reported.

A colleague shut this down with "it's all in the weights," deployed as both reassurance and dismissal. As I reconstruct it, the sentence was being made to do three things at once: establish that there is (i) some determinate fact of the matter about what the model thinks about a given pump; conclude (ii) that there is therefore something there to be extracted by a sufficiently clever probe, fine-tuned reporter, or secondary model; and conclude (iii) that nothing deep is at stake here, only engineering effort. I can't tell whether those follow from one another, or whether she is sliding between different claims that happen to share a sentence.

Could you lay out, as a list I can bring to the next design review, the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making about a trained model? I'd especially like these separated:

- a trivial determinism reading — the weights, the input, and the inference-time computation fix the outputs, and there is no extra mental residue beyond them;
- readings about information-theoretic recoverability versus computational feasibility of recovery, and whether the gap between them is doing any work in her argument;
- readings about uniqueness — whether the weights could encode several mutually inconsistent dispositions to answer, so that different elicitation procedures legitimately surface different answers and none of them is "the" model's answer;
- readings about the target of recovery: the model's prediction of what the label will say, versus its best estimate of the actual state of the pump, versus what it would output if we optimized its continuation hard for some other objective such as looking good to a reviewer. My suspicion is that she needs the second of these and the slogan only hands her the first;
- one reading I want checked rather than granted: that "all in the weights" pictures the relevant knowledge as sitting somewhere static, when much of what the model can do is a property of the weights together with the procedure we run at inference — different prompts, more test-time compute, different decoding — so that the "location" picture may itself be the misleading part.

For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible empirical claim, or false. And most importantly: which readings would actually license (i), which would get (ii) as well, and which give you (i) while leaving (iii) entirely unsupported? If my colleague is equivocating, I'd like to know exactly where the slide happens.

Please don't point me at papers or reading lists — I can't evaluate a literature, and I'd rather have the distinctions themselves, stated so that I could re-derive them at the whiteboard. If a distinction has a standard name, give the name, but state the substance in plain terms too.
