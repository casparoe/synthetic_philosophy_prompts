So there's a thing happening on Saturday morning that I've been dodging thinking about, and now two of my friends have made it worse by getting into an argument about it, and I said I'd referee and I genuinely can't.

Background, because it matters. In the spring, at a family dinner, I announced I'd ride the 100 km charity loop in September. My uncle, who is a weird guy about this sort of thing, wired ten grand to the charity the next morning and told me the money is not conditional on me actually riding — no tracker, no photos, nobody checks, and if I sit in the house all Saturday nothing happens to the donation. He said he just wanted me to mean it when I said it. Which I think I did? It's now three weeks out, the weather looks grim, and I definately don't want to do it, and I said something like this out loud and Marcus and Dee went at it for about forty minutes. Roughly, with the bad parts cut:

1. Marcus: you never intended it. Intending is just your practical judgement settling on a course of action, and your judgement on the night of the dinner already contained the fact that on Saturday every reason to ride will be gone. You can't settle on doing what you already know you'll have no reason to do. So the announcement was theater, and you should stop telling yourself it was a real intention that somehow evaporated.

2. Dee: that's way too strong. Intending something isn't a guarantee you'll do it — that's just what weakness of will is. I intend to floss basically every night of my life and I don't. So of course you can intend at the dinner and not ride in September; nothing about this case makes that impossible.

3. Marcus: those aren't the same shape. When you skip flossing you still think, right then, that flossing would be best — you just fail to do what you judge best. Here, on Saturday, you won't think riding is best at all, and you know that now. So it isn't weakness of will. It's that the intention would have to be formed against your own all-things-considered judgement at the very moment of forming it.

4. Dee: fine, then just make it the case that you have a reason on Saturday. Book the hotel, tell everyone, put the bike in the car, pay the entry fee. And more basically: once you've said it out loud, doing it matters for being the sort of person whose word means something. That's a reason on Saturday that you already have in the spring. So intending is easy here — it's just intending to do something for a reason you've got.

5. Marcus: those are two different tricks and neither one answers the question. Booking the hotel changes the situation — you've manufactured new reasons, hassle and refunds and people being disappointed, so it's not the same case anymore, it's cheating by re-description. And if the integrity reason is a real reason you'll have on Saturday then it's just the ordinary case again: you have a reason, so no puzzle. The question is whether the wanting of the money, by itself, can generate the intention. And it can't, because the money is already sent and doesn't care what you do next.

6. Dee: then the setup is rigged. You're banning every mechanism by which an actual person intends to do something hard — can you intend to X, where intending X gives you no reason and I've switched off all the other reasons. Maybe not, sure, but that's a fact about a case you built, not about intention. And here's the thing: what if you weren't certain you'd hate it? Some people love that stuff. Then you could intend it as a bet on the chance you'd enjoy it, and nothing paradoxical happens. So the whole difficulty depends on how sure you are now about what your reasons will be later, which makes this a puzzle about self-prediction, not about willpower.

7. Marcus: that's the best thing you've said and I think it half proves my point. Intention is answerable to belief — you can't intend what you believe you won't do — and belief is fixed by evidence, not by decision. But it only sorts cases into soluble and insoluble; it doesn't rescue yours.

8. Dee: then run it ten years in a row. Same announcement every spring, same ride every September. Obviously I can have a policy of following through, and in year seven I can intend to ride on the strength of that policy. If you say I can't intend it in the one-shot version but I can in the series, your theory of intention is hostage to how the case is packaged, which is a sign it's the wrong theory.

9. Marcus: here, let's cash it out instead of gesturing. Suppose your uncle had paid a thousand dollars for every week you were willing to hand over fifty dollars of your own money to be locked into the ride — non-refundable entry, bike in a shop across town, whatever. Then whether you intend to ride just is whether you'd pay to make backing out expensive. You'd pay, everyone would say you intended it, and no paradox shows up. So intending is a disposition to buy commitment, not some private act of will you perform in your head.

10. Dee: but that just moves the puzzle one step over. Would you pay the fifty? Only if you expected to actually ride once you had. And you don't. So the meter reads zero, and we're back where we started, except now with a number attached.
