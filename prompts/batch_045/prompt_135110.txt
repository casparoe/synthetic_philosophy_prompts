The starting thought is the one most people hit first: a property is just the set of the things that have it. Redness is the set of red things. That version died for me quickly, because the properties of having a heart and having a kidney have the same members in the actual world, so on this account they would be the same property, and they plainly are not.

So here is my repair. Do not identify a property with a set of actual objects. Identify it with a function that takes a possible world as input and gives back the set of the objects that have the property in that world. Call this the property's profile. Two properties are identical just in case their profiles are identical, i.e. just in case they pick out the same objects in every possible world.

I think this buys me three things.

First, it handles contingent coextension. Having a heart and having a kidney agree on the actual world but disagree on worlds where, say, there are creatures with a heart and no kidneys, so their profiles differ, so the properties differ. That was the objection that killed the naive set version, and I believe it no longer touches me.

Second, it handles properties that nothing has. The profile that assigns the empty set to every world is a perfectly good function, so there is such a property even though nothing ever instantiates it. I do not have to say that uninstantiated properties are a special embarrassment, and I do not have to say that there are no such properties.

Third, I take myself to have avoided spooky ontology. I am not positing a strange new category of entity that is somehow wholly present in many places at once. A mathematician already believes in sets and functions, and those are all I need. I have been thinking of this as a kind of realism with better bookkeeping: properties really exist, they are just not a peculiar extra ingredient in the world.

Two objections have been raised against me and I have set them aside, possibly too fast. I would like to know whether I was right to.

The first: the set of all red things is not itself red, whereas redness, if it existed, would be red. I think this is a quibble. A set is not the sort of thing that can be coloured, so of course it is not red. It seems to me that playing the role of a property does not require having that property, in the same way that a list of French people is not itself French and there is nothing wrong with that.

The second: someone said that my view hands me far too many properties, because any weird disjunction gives a profile too. Surely it just means that there are very many properties, most of them uninteresting. Why should the abundance of properties be an objection rather than a discovery?

There is also a regress argument I half-remember being used against views that explain resemblance by saying that resembling things belong to a common class.
