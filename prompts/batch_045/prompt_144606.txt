I sit on the board of a small contemporary art foundation, and I have no academic background in philosophy: my training was in accounting and logistics, and anything I know about metaphysics I have picked up from reading on my own. The board recently accepted a donation that has forced us to think about the old puzzle of the statue and the clay, and I would like your help setting an agenda rather than a syllabus.

The donated work is called Second Lump. In 2019 an artist 3D-scanned an ordinary lump of clay and used the digital file to cast a bronze sculpture in the exact shape of the lump. The donor agreement says three things. First, the foundation must melt down and recast the bronze every twenty-five years from the same digital file, using newly sourced bronze, so that the shape is preserved but the metal is never the same. Second, the original clay, now dried and cracked, must be kept in storage and may never be displayed. Third, the digital file is held in trust and may be subtly updated for structural repairs, but the visible shape must remain perceptually indistinguishable from the 2019 scan. We are now drafting a policy for insurance, loans, and donor restrictions, and we keep arguing about what the work actually is: the shape, the current bronze, the original clay, the file, or some combination.

We have funding for one part-time philosophical adviser for twelve months, about one day a week, plus a modest budget for a single public symposium at the end of the year. We are not asking for a literature review, a bibliography, or citations; we need a defended agenda that we could hand to the adviser.

So my question is this: given the Second Lump case, which open questions about material constitution, and which implications of the statue-and-clay debate for legal and museum practice, should the adviser prioritize over the next year? Please rank them. I am less interested in the list itself than in the reasoning behind the ordering. For each priority, tell me why it is upstream of the other questions we might ask—that is, which further policy conclusions depend on its answer—how tractable it is for a philosopher to make progress on in a year while also explaining it to non-specialists, whether it is genuinely open or already settled enough that we would waste the adviser's time on it, and what kind of argument or development in the coming year would move it up or down the list. Please also be blunt about which alleged implications of the statue-and-clay puzzle for our situation are overrated or do not actually follow, and say what makes them tempting but mistaken.

To give the ranking some structure, imagine the adviser's time is the scarce resource: one day a week for a year, with the symposium as the only major output. What should they work on first, what should they explicitly set aside, and why? Where does the Second Lump case reveal a gap in the usual way the statue and the clay example is discussed, and how should that change the ordering compared with the questions a metaphysician would prioritize in the abstract?

Please write this as a prioritization memo I could give to the board. Do not survey every position or name-drop authors; I need the ordering, the criteria you are using (importance, tractability, neglectedness, dependency, or others), the trade-offs between them, and your reasons for the ranking.
