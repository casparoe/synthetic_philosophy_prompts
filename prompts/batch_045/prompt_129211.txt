I'm opening Thursday's session of our philosophy of physics reading group with twenty minutes on whether Wigner's puzzle survives once you take surplus structure seriously, and I have managed to build an argument whose conclusion I do not believe but whose steps I cannot fault. I wanted to defend something modest — that the success of mathematics which was invented for internal mathematical reasons and later turns out to organize a physical domain is genuine evidence about the structure of the world, and not merely a fact about us. Every version I write either inflates into a realism I think is indefensible or deflates into the view that Wigner was never pointing at anything. The version I am least unhappy with runs as follows.

(1) Call a mathematical formalism *clean* if its central definitions and theorems were arrived at by mathematicians working on problems internal to mathematics, with no eye to any physical application, and *fertile* if, once applied to a physical system, it yields quantitative predictions that were not among the data it was fitted to, confirmed to a precision far greater than those data could have underwritten.

(2) There are clean and fertile formalisms. I will use a hypothetical, because I do not want the discussion to turn into an argument about historical priority: suppose mathematicians studying the statistical spacing of numbers in a certain integer sequence construct a formalism whose central object is a discrete spectrum with a distinctive repulsion property among its levels; forty years later, physicists measuring excitation energies in a newly isolated quantum system find that its spectrum matches that object to eleven significant figures across thousands of levels, with no parameter adjusted after the fact.

(3) If the world did not instantiate, at least approximately, the structure the formalism describes, then its fertility would be a coincidence — there would be nothing in the world for the mathematics to be answerable to.

(4) Coincidences of that scale and reliability are not to be taken seriously. Call this the tracking principle: success at anticipating nature to eleven significant figures is evidence that representation and world share structure.

(5) So in the case described in (2), the world instantiates that structure: the spectrum is real structure of the system, not a notational convenience of ours.

(6) But the formalism in (2) carries more than its spectrum. It admits an enormous group of transformations that leave every prediction fixed, so that infinitely many formally distinct states in the formalism correspond to a single physical situation, and nothing in steps (3) through (5) lets me confirm the spectrum without confirming all of that redundancy with it.

(7) And suppose there is a second, empirically equivalent treatment of the same system — one in which the state is a wave on ordinary three-dimensional space and the levels are eigenvalues of a differential operator on that space — which agrees with the first on every possible measurement while disagreeing with it about how many degrees of freedom the system has and about what persists between measurements.

(8) Applying (3) through (5) to each treatment, the world instantiates both structures. It cannot, since they are incompatible about the number of degrees of freedom.

(9) What makes me think the defect is structural rather than local is that an astronomer working a few centuries ago could have run steps (3) through (5) on the epicycle-and-equant apparatus — clean, in that it was geometry of circles rather than theory of planets, and fertile, in that it predicted positions within observational error for century after century — and concluded that the heavens instantiate its structure. We now think that apparatus worked because sums of circular motions are an extremely flexible representational medium, not because the sky is built out of them. If the tracking principle could not be trusted there, I do not see what entitles me to trust it in (2).

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I would rather keep (2) and (4) than anything else, and I would rather give up (5) than (3), but I would like to know the price of each. In particular, is there a reading of "instantiates the structure" on which (5) comes out true of the load-bearing part of a formalism and false of its redundancy — something like sharing structure only up to isomorphism once you have quotiented out the transformations in (6) — and if so does that reading still leave a Wignerian puzzle worth stating on Thursday, or has it quietly dissolved the puzzle into an ordinary claim about which representations are empirically adequate? And does the echo in (9) show that the tracking principle was never evidence-conferring at all, or only that fertility is evidence of adequacy rather than of structure?
