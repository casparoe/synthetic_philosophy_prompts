THE QUESTION (out of 20)

Background. Standard Sleeping Beauty. On Sunday a fair coin is flipped. If it lands heads, Beauty is woken on Monday only; if tails, she is woken on Monday and again on Tuesday. Each night she is given a drug that erases her memory of the previous awakening, and she knows all of this. Question: on waking, what should her credence be that the coin landed heads?

(a) [4 marks] Give the halfer answer and the thirder answer, each with the one-sentence argument that supports it. State explicitly whether either of them is committed to denying that the coin is fair.

(b) [4 marks] The halfer distributes credence over centred possibilities like this: P(heads & Monday) = 1/2, P(tails & Monday) = 1/4, P(tails & Tuesday) = 1/4. The thirder uses 1/3 for each of the three. For each view, compute (i) Beauty's credence that it is Monday and (ii) her credence in heads conditional on being told that it is Monday.

(c) [6 marks] Two-Room Beauty. Same as the standard case, except that on each awakening Beauty is put in one of two rooms, red and blue, which are otherwise identical, and she is told the rule in advance. If the coin lands heads, there is one awakening, and a separate fair coin flip decides whether it is in the red or the blue room. If it lands tails, there are two awakenings, one in the red room and one in the blue room, and again a separate fair coin flip decides which day is which. She wakes up in the red room. Working inside the thirder framework, what should her credence in heads be? Does the colour of the room make any difference compared with the standard case? Compare the situation with the standard case in which she is simply told that it is Monday.

(d) [6 marks] On each awakening she is offered a ticket costing 6 dollars that pays 15 dollars if the coin landed heads, with everything settled after the run ends when she is told the result. Because of the amnesia her situation at each awakening is subjectively identical and she knows this, so whatever she decides on one awakening she decides on all of them; evaluate the policy of buying at every awakening. Should she buy? At what ticket price would she break even? Does this case settle the halfer/thirder dispute, and if not, what exactly does it show?

THE STUDENT'S ANSWER

(a) A halfer says 1/2. Beauty knew on Sunday that she would be awake at least once, so waking up gives her no new relevant evidence, and the chance of heads is 1/2. A thirder says 1/3: for any given awakening there are three relevant centred possibilities, heads-and-Monday, tails-and-Monday and tails-and-Tuesday, and by symmetry these get equal weight, so two out of three of them are tails awakenings. Neither view has to deny that the coin is fair. Both sides agree that the objective chance of heads is 1/2 and that over many runs of the experiment half the coins land heads. The disagreement is only about how much Beauty's total evidence on waking, including the self-locating part, tells her.

(b) Halfer: P(Monday) = 1/2 + 1/4 = 3/4. Conditional on Monday, though, the day doesn't tell you anything about how the coin landed, so P(heads | Monday) = P(heads) = 1/2. Thirder: P(Monday) = 1/3 + 1/3 = 2/3, and conditional on Monday the two Monday possibilities are equally weighted and one of them is a heads one, so P(heads | Monday) = 1/2 as well. So the two views actually agree here: if Beauty is told it's Monday, both of them put 1/2 on heads. The interesting disagreement is only about the unconditional credence.

(c) The awakening structure is unchanged: one awakening per heads run and two per tails run. So before she opens her eyes, the unconditional credence on waking is the same as in the standard case, 1/3 for a thirder. Seeing the red room is then informative in the same way that being told it's Monday is in the standard case. In the tails branch there are two awakenings and only one of them is red, and in the heads branch the single awakening is red with probability 1/2, so the red awakenings are split one-to-one between heads and tails. Conditional on red, then, her credence in heads should rise from 1/3 to 1/2. The contrast with the Monday case is that here she gets the information for free from the room, without anyone having to tell her anything.

(d) A halfer values the ticket at 1/2 times 15, which is 7.50, so she should buy at 6. A thirder values it at 1/3 times 15, which is 5, so she should refuse. Since the halfer's expected gain is positive by her own lights and the thirder's is negative by hers, the betting case can't settle anything: rational betting behaviour just mirrors whatever credence you started with, so it can't be used as an argument for either view, and in particular it gives the thirder no advantage. What the case really shows is that credences about self-locating matters and decisions about bets can come apart once the number of bets you get to make depends on how the world turns out.
