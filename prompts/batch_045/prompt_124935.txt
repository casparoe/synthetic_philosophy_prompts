You are a decision scientist and philosopher of science who has spent a career advising public agencies on how evidence does and does not make its way into policy. Work carefully and in stages. Before drafting anything, write a short private preamble in which you (a) list, in one line each, the assumptions on which the classic Bayesian result that the expected value of costless information is never negative depends, and (b) mark each assumption as holding, failing, or unclear in the case below. Then write the memo, then the structured summary.

The case:

I am one of seven voting members of the Science Advisory Board of a state fish and wildlife department. We must make a recommendation at our 12 September meeting on how to allocate the department's research and monitoring appropriation for the FY26–27 biennium: 4.2 million dollars.

The largest item on the table is a proposal from our own survey unit for 1.6 million dollars: a range-wide occupancy survey for the Sutter Basin fairy shrimp, a small crustacean that breeds in seasonal vernal pools on private ranchland and is a candidate for state listing. Six hundred sites, two field seasons, spring 2026 and spring 2027, using environmental DNA sampling plus visual encounter surveys.

Three features of the situation are doing the real work, and the Board is split on how much weight they carry.

First, the department's action set is largely fixed by statute, whatever the survey finds. We are already required to maintain the candidate-species conservation agreement, and the grazing-deferral rules cannot be tightened or loosened without a separate rulemaking. In practice the only thing the survey could change is *where* we spend the acquisition money — which of the vernal-pool complexes we secure first. A Board member who works on decision analysis has argued that this makes the expected value of perfect information small: if the optimal action is the same in nearly every branch of the tree, learning which branch we are in is worth nearly nothing.

Second, the signal is not causally inert. Under the state endangered species act a confirmed detection on private land brings occupied-habitat protections into force, and survey results are disclosable under the public records act within thirty days. Several ranching families have said publicly that they would drain or disk their pools rather than have them designated. The survey's proponents reply that the species is declining regardless and that learning the truth about a decline cannot itself be what harms it.

Third, the learner is not the chooser. Our Board recommends; a politically appointed commission decides, and it has a demonstrated tendency to treat a single detection as conclusive and to act before the full season's data are in. Our own statute also requires that listing and recovery decisions rest on the best available science, which some members read as making a deliberate decision not to look close to unlawful.

Against funding: the acquisition program has a queue of willing sellers and is currently securing perpetual easements at roughly 2,400 dollars an acre; 1.6 million dollars is about 670 acres of occupied habitat, in hand, with returns we can estimate. Against that, our grants officer points out that a federal matching program would triple our acquisition dollars for three years, but only for an applicant with a current range-wide occupancy estimate with confidence intervals; the next application window closes in March 2027 and the following one is not until 2030. Note that on the proposed schedule only one of the two field seasons would be complete by that window.

One further wrinkle: a colleague circulated a one-page note arguing that we literally cannot lose by measuring, since the expected value of information is never negative for a Bayesian agent — he calls it Good's theorem — so that hesitation about the price tag is just risk aversion wearing a lab coat. He also notes that information is reusable: data bought for this decision will be there for the 2027 status review and for the 2030 grant cycle. Another member has argued the opposite, that this is an expensive way to buy a headline that gets vernal pools disked under, and that a Board which declines to measure is a Board that has started to prefer not knowing.

It must give a time frame with dated milestones, including anything that has to happen before the March 2027 grant window. It must be written so that the survey unit could actually respond to it, and it must treat the strongest argument for the position you are rejecting honestly rather than as a straw man — I want the losing side to feel it was decided against, not ignored.
