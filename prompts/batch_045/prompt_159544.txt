I'm a software engineer with no philosophy background, so sorry if I'm re-inventing something that already has a proper name. I'm building the component layer for a small game engine — entities are just bags of components like Position, Velocity, Sprite — and me and a coworker (Sam) got into an argument about whether two entities that both have a Position literally share something, or whether "has a Position" is just a label we slap on both of them.

The claim I want a number on, call it "types are structures": for two things to share a property is nothing more than for them to be structurally interchangeable — same shape, same role, swappable everywhere without anything else changing. On this view nominal typing (Java: two interfaces with byte-identical method signatures are still different types because the names differ) is a useful engineering fiction, and structural typing (TypeScript, or Python's Protocol classes) is closer to how properties actually work.

I'll count the claim as false if any of these three hold, and true only if all three fail:
(a) A type with no instances can still be real. I definately can write an abstract class Unicorn that nothing ever instantiates and never will, yet it still constrains what could legitimately be spawned in the world. If that's a genuine real thing, properties aren't just structures of what happens to exist.
(b) Two structurally identical types can still express different properties. My IMessenger and ILogger have identical members, but the code means something different by each one.
(c) Some properties are "natural" and others are gerrymandered, and that split isn't up to us: Position is one of the real ones, "is within three meters of the coffee machine" isn't, and that's not merely about what our physics engine happens to care about.

One wrinkle I keep coming back to, which I haven't seen anyone discuss: Java erases generics at runtime, so List<String> and List<Integer> are the same runtime type — "being a list of strings" is a property that exists at compile time and is gone at runtime. If something can be a property for one phase of a system's life and not another, I don't know what to say about whether it was ever real. Sam says that just shows it was never a property at all, only a constraint on what I'm allowed to write.

I'm at about 0.65 that "types are structures" is true; Sam is at about 0.2. Give me your own number, and decompose it. Since I'm treating (a), (b) and (c) as the ways the claim fails, your headline should be roughly the probability that all three fail — but don't just multiply three numbers together if you think they're correlated, and tell me if you think there's a fourth way it fails that I've missed.

Answer as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, no intro and no closing line:
1. Your headline credence, and one line on what exactly you're taking the claim to say.
2. Your probability that (a) holds — uninstantiated types can be real — with one line of justification.
3. Your probability that (b) holds — structurally identical types can express different properties — with one line, including what your view says about my erased-generics case.
4. Your probability that (c) holds — a mind-independent natural/gerrymandered split — plus how the three combine into your headline number.
5. Which of the three the 0.65-versus-0.2 gap between me and Sam most likely lives in, which term you're least confident about, and the single argument or finding that would move your headline number the most.
