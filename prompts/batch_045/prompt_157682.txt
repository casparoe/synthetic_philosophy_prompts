ok so this has been bugging me forever and I can never tell if I'm being taken in by a good story. The "hunter-gatherers were actually the affluent ones, farming was a trap" thing is one of those half-remembered anthro-101 vibes: Sahlins's affluent-forager picture, Diamond's famous Discover piece calling agriculture the worst mistake in the history of the human race, the skeletal evidence that early farmers were shorter, more anemic, more infected, teeth full of cavities. Some of that is definately real, some of it is a mood. What I can't get straight is what the claim is even supposed to be.

So here's the version I want a number on. Restrict to the people who actually lived through the transition in the Levant, call it roughly 9500 to 7000 BCE (dates approximate, don't @ me on the details). Proposition: the average person born into a farming household in that stretch had a worse life — worse in terms of what the life was actually like for them day to day, not just worse numbers on a skeleton — than the person they would have been had their community stayed foraging. Give me your credence in that as a single number, but I want the decomposition, with a number and one line of justification per term:

how much rides on whether the health proxies (stature, enamel defects, anemia and infection markers, joint wear) track experienced welfare at all versus just morbidity,
how much on who's in the denominator — kids, women, old people, and the people who exist only because farming raised fertility versus the ones who'd have existed anyway,
how much on whether you discount for hedonic adaptation, i.e. whether a farmer whose expectations were set entirely by village life has actually lost much by never having been a forager,
how much on the counterfactual being live at all — whether "their community kept foraging" was ever on the table once you've got grain stores, permanent houses, fields and extra mouths.

And here's the term I want you to add yourself, because I think it's carrying more weight than people admit. I've been calling it the reversion window: the stretch of time in which a given early farming village could plausibly have packed up and gone back to hunting and gathering at a cost the community could actually bear, before population density, fixed investment in fields and houses, and the slow loss of foraging know-how closed it off. My hunch is that the whole "mistake" framing quietly presupposes a wide-ish window, and that if it was narrow the honest description isn't "they made a mistake" but something more like: every household's move was locally the sensible one, the aggregate result was worse for everyone, and nobody was ever in a position to reverse it. Tell me how much of your number rides on how wide that window was, and say honestly what evidence could pin it down — or tell me the framing doesn't depend on it and why.

Then: name the one sub-question your final number is most sensitive to and say what would move it most. For what its worth I'm at about 0.5 and my partner is at about 0.15, she thinks the whole thing is nostalgia for a life that was nasty, brutish and short; where do you think the disagreement between us actually lives?

Last bit, because I want this to be answerable instead of just a vibe. Suppose in ten years archaeologists pull off a stress ledger: a method that reads something like a year-by-year physiological workload-and-stress curve out of dental cementum and joint degeneration, so instead of "health declined" you get a profile per individual, and suppose we get it for a few hundred early-farming skeletons and a few hundred roughly contemporaneous forager burials. What pattern in that data would settle this for you, and what pattern would leave you exactly where you are now? I suspect most people would say the data settles it and then quietly keep their prior, so say which way you'd actually go.
