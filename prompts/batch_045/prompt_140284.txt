I work in platform engineering and have no philosophy training, and I keep tying myself in knots about when it is right to say that someone knows something. Here are my verdicts on a set of cases I actually run into, and I would like to know whether one principle is behind them.

1. I read the config repo and see the flag set to true. A teammate asks whether I know the feature flag is on in production. Nothing rides on my answer beyond her curiosity. Verdict: I know.

2. Exactly the same evidence, but if the flag is actually off, a migration I have queued will drop the payments table. Verdict: I do not know; I should inspect the deployed config rather than the repo.

3. Same as 2, except nobody told me about the migration and I have no reason to think anything hangs on this. Verdict: I know. It seems wrong to say my knowledge was destroyed by a consequence I could not have known about.

4. Same stakes as 2, but the only live way I could be wrong is that someone renamed the cluster — in which case the migration fails harmlessly and never touches payments. The stakes are enormous; the error possibility has nothing to do with them. Verdict: I know.

5. Same stakes as 2, except that what is at risk belongs to a stranger at another company who will never hear my answer and whose actions my belief cannot affect. Verdict: I know.

6. Evidence upgraded: I checked the repo, the deployed config, and the running process. Stakes as in 2. Verdict: I know.

7. I am in the evidence situation of 2. My on-call colleague, with the same evidence, is the one who owns the migration, and he asks me, "Do you know the flag is on?" I cannot tell whether the stakes that matter are his or mine, or whether the question is even about my knowledge rather than his.

8. Same evidence and stakes as 1. Asked "do you know the flag is on?", I say yes. Asked "do you know whether it is on or merely stale-cached from yesterday's snapshot?", I say no. Nothing practical changed between the two questions.

The principle I keep reaching for is something like: a subject knows that p just in case their evidence puts the probability of error below a threshold set by the practical situation, where the threshold rises with the cost of being wrong and falls with the cost of checking further — so knowledge is one relation whose standard is fixed pragmatically, not a fixed standard plus a separate decision about whether to act.

Please test that principle against the eight cases and repair it where it breaks. In particular: does it survive 3 and 5 without quietly collapsing into "stakes the subject takes to be at stake"; does it handle 4, where the stakes are huge but orthogonal to the error possibility; and does it have anything to say at all about 8, where the verdict moves with no change in stakes? Then tell me what it predicts about two cases I have not listed: high stakes attached to a one-in-ten-thousand catastrophic failure versus near-certain trivial annoyance (does expected cost or worst case raise the bar?), and my asserting "the flag is on" in a low-stakes thread that someone downstream relies on for the migration. Finally, be explicit about which of my verdicts the best candidate principle forces me to give up, and whether the version that saves all eight is worth the extra machinery.
