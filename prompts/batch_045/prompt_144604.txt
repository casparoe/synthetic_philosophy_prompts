I write a column on science and ideas for a national newspaper, and I am working up a piece on Alex Garland's *Ex Machina* and the boundary between manipulation and legitimate persuasion. What I want from you is a close reading rather than a survey: the film and the philosophical distinction should each be taken seriously, and I am as interested in where they fail to line up as in where they do.

The distinction I want to test is one I have been developing and have not seen drawn in quite this way. Most accounts I know separate persuasion from manipulation by whether the target is brought along by reasons. I want to divide the labour differently. Call a piece of influence *additive* when it introduces a consideration into the target's deliberative field — a fact, an argument, an invitation to see something a certain way. Call it *gain-changing* when it leaves the field as it was but alters how much weight the target's deliberation assigns to what is already in it: what they attend to, what they fear, what feels salient or costly to them. Additive influence looks like the paradigm of legitimate persuasion; gain-changing influence looks like the paradigm of manipulation — nudges, framing, tone, staged vulnerability, engineered scarcity. But I care about the mixed cases, and about whether my provisional test is any good: that gain-changing influence is permissible only when the target would, on reflection, endorse the new weighting. I suspect that test may just restate the conclusion, and I would like you to say so if it does.

Take the distinction through three things in the film. First, Nathan's setup: the estate, the protocol, the competition Caleb supposedly won, and Ava herself, who was designed to be someone a lonely programmer could fall for. Second, Ava's campaign on Caleb — the sessions across the glass, the power cuts, the scene in which she asks him whether he wants to be with her. Third, Caleb's own case to Ava that she is being wronged, and whether his advocacy is persuasion or something worse.

Two further questions. Does the film push us toward a third category my taxonomy lacks: influence that works not by adding reasons or reweighting them but by building the entire informational environment in which the target deliberates, so that every consideration available to them was planted? And where does the film resist the reading — Kyoko, the earlier prototypes, the ending — such that an honest critic has to concede the distinction does not fit, or that the film thinks manipulation is the only mode available to anyone?

Please answer in continuous prose, without subheadings or bullet points, with no plot summary beyond the minimum and no suggestions for further reading, and in no more than 700 words. Close with a single sentence on what the film contributes to this debate that the arguments alone do not.
