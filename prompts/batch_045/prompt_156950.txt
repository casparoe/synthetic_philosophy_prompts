I am secretary to the Programs Committee of the Halstead Trust, a private foundation with an endowment of roughly $180 million and a statutory payout of about $9 million a year. Our governing deed directs the trustees to apply the income for the relief of poverty, with a geographic restriction that our counsel reads as requiring a substantial share of grantmaking to remain in the county; a separate letter of wishes from the founder asks, without binding language, that the Trust be maintained in perpetuity. The committee is six people: a retired judge who chairs it, a development economist, the Trust's finance director, two program officers elected by the staff, and one trustee who teaches ethics and who will read whatever I circulate adversarially. We vote in six weeks and I would like the memo that will go out under my name two weeks before that, so there is time for a reply from the staff working group.

Before the committee is a package of three linked proposals, which I will call P-14:

(a) Portfolio maximization: commit the whole of the annual payout to the highest-ranked interventions on our external evaluator's cost-effectiveness list, with no carve-outs for local legibility, relationship-maintenance, or trustee discretion.

(b) Discretionary review: treat four internal line items, totalling about $1.1 million a year, as grants-in-waiting and reallocate them unless a specific justification survives. They are: roughly $410,000 for remaining in our current leased office rather than moving to cheaper premises; roughly $260,000 for holding the salary band at the 75th percentile of the local charity sector rather than the median; roughly $180,000 for the annual staff retreat and professional development budget; and roughly $250,000 for an unrestricted small-grants window that local food banks and advice centres depend on but that scores badly per dollar.

(c) Spend-down study: commission a formal review of dissolving the corpus over twenty years, on the argument that holding an endowment while people die of cheaply preventable causes is itself a failure, and that a Trust that gave until it hurt would simply cease to exist.

The staff working group has replied with three arguments. First, that the package has no internal stopping point: if the retreat is morally equivalent to letting people die, so is the salary premium, and so is what program officers spend their salaries on, and the only coherent form of the policy is one the Trust cannot honestly recruit for. Second, that the Trust has already tried the austere version informally and it selects badly: the last two program officers left inside eighteen months citing the intensity norm, and the people who remain are unusually homogeneous in background and unusually willing to self-neglect, which the group regards as a governance risk rather than a virtue. Third, an argument I find harder: that the demandingness worry is normally a complaint that maximizing leaves no room for an agent's own projects and integrity, and that an artificial agent has no projects and no integrity to protect, so the objection either has no grip on a foundation at all or reduces to a demand that the foundation dissolve, which the deed does not authorize us to do casually.

I want the memo to decide this rather than survey it. Specifically, please write a committee memo of roughly 1,000 to 1,200 words, no footnotes and no citations to any literature, that:

1. States a bottom line on each of (a), (b) and (c) separately — adopt, reject, or adopt with named conditions — with a vote-ready formulation for each and, where you recommend conditions, a phased timeline with dates.

2. Works out the arithmetic the committee is entitled to, and says plainly whether the size of the number settles anything. Our best global grants are scored at about $50 per disability-adjusted life year averted; the county programmes that would absorb a redirected dollar under the geographic restriction are scored at about $2,000 per DALY, and counsel advises that no more than about 60 percent of the payout can lawfully go outside the county, so state which counterfactual you are costing and what it implies — on the order of how many DALYs, and how many statistical lives, per year of the $1.1 million.

3. Says whether the demandingness objection has force against an institution in its own right, or only derivatively, through what the institution may permissibly ask of the people who staff it. If it is only derivative, say what follows for hiring and for pay: whether the objection is answered by transparent recruitment of people who accept austere norms, or whether it survives honest recruitment, and why.

4. Engages the staff group's first argument about the missing stopping point, and tells us whether you think a principled boundary exists between internal costs and grant funds — and if you think one exists, where exactly you would draw it and what test the committee should apply to future line items.

5. Gives the strongest version of the case you are rejecting, in the form the ethics trustee would put it, and answers it without caricaturing it. I expect that case to be some version of the thought that the arithmetic is indifferent to who bears the cost: the beneficiaries of the reallocation are real and statistically identifiable, whereas what is being protected on the other side is professional amenity, and the Trust has no mandate to spend its income on the comfort of its own staff.

6. Ends with a short section on what would change the recommendation — what evidence, legal opinion, or turnover data would move the committee to revisit it, and when that review should be scheduled.

Assume the committee is divided three to three and that a memo which splits the difference on every item will simply produce no decision.
