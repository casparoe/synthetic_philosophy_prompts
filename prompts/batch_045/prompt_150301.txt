hey — I'm in a reading group on consequentialism this term and writing a short paper on cluelessness, and one of the other grad students has this argument that I think is either really good or quietly broken, and I cant tell which. Her claim is that cluelessness is self-defeating: if you really are clueless about the long run, then the unknown effects can't rationally move you at all, so you should just act on the effects you can actually forecast — which is what everyone was doing before anyone brought up cluelessness anyway. She writes it out like this:

(1) For any act available to me, my evidence underdetermines the probability distribution over its total long-run consequences so badly that I have no defensible expected value for it. The dominant contributors are ripple effects through other people's decisions, demographic and technological trajectories, and so on, and my credence over even the *sign* of the net effect is unstable.
(2) That isn't special to one option. The same background of unknowns is sitting there whichever act I pick.
(3) If two options are alike in every respect I have evidence about, and differ only in respects I'm completely clueless about, then I have no reason to prefer either one.
(4) So the long-run unknowns are evidentially inert — like adding the same unknown constant to every column of a payoff table. They cancel across options.
(5) So I should rank my options by their near-term, forecastable effects and ignore the rest.
(6) But then cluelessness was never a problem for consequentialism in the first place. It only looked like one if you assumed the theory required expected-value calculations over total consequences.
(7) And (6) has to be wrong. If (5) were right, I should be indifferent between two careers with identical near-term cost-effectiveness even if one of them plausibly propagates through durable institutions and the other doesn't, and I should give zero weight to the possibility that some small thing I do becomes one of those historical hinge points everyone talks about. That feels like throwing away the only evidence we actually have about how consequences work, which is that sometimes small acts have enormous and totally unforecastable effects.

The thing I keep circling on is step (4). The "same unknown constant in every column" picture seems to assume that the long-run noise is the same random variable whichever act I do, but if the ripple effects are the chaotic, path-dependent kind, then the long-run noise is *downstream of the very difference between the acts* — the butterfly flaps differently — so it can't be a common constant, and I don't see how it can cancel. But she says that just makes it more clueless and hence more symmetric, not less. I also have a suspicion that (4) is sneaking in the machinery of expectations — equal means, or identically distributed error terms — at the exact point where (1) said we don't have any expectations to work with, and that "I can't estimate the mean" is being treated as if it entailed "the means are equal."

So: which step do you reject, and why did it look fine? I'd rather keep (1) and (2) than (6) — giving up cluelessness feels like wishful thinking about how much we know, but I really don't want to end up having to say that no available act is ever better than any other. And I want to know what I'm paying either way. In particular: does the answer change if I stop pretending I have precise credences and adopt some decision rule for imprecise probabilities instead — something like interval dominance, or maximin over the long-run — or does that just relocate the problem? And is there anything to a thing a postdoc in my department calls "channel sensitivity," the idea that cluelessness isn't uniform because some acts route their long-run effects through channels we have real evidence about (institutional persistence, capacity that outlasts the people who built it) while others don't — i.e., that (2) is the premise to give up, which would turn cluelessness from a global theoretical problem into a bunch of local empirical ones?
