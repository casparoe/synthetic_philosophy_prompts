Hey — for the next hour please act as my in-house philosopher of history (and, honestly, as someone who's sat on too many committees): think carefully, and do some free-form brainstorming first about what rule set could even generate our track record before you commit to an answer. I sit on our city's Memory and Records Board, and we've made a mess for ourselves by never writing down our principles. Cases we've ruled on, roughly in order: (1) a plaque saying soldiers set the 1921 workers' hall fire, when the fire brigade's own report blames the wiring — we had the plaque recut to the archival version; (2) the town's founding-day festival, which commemorates a battle that the muster rolls place twelve kilometres away and a fortnight later — we left the festival entirely alone but paid for an interpretive panel at the actual site; (3) a deported families' association's oral history of expulsion from a village whose land registry burned in 1944 — with the record silent we accepted the memory as the best available evidence and put up a marker worded "according to the memory of the descendants"; (4) the state museum's display claiming a 1948 strike was organised by outside agitators, which turned out to have been drafted by the then-ministry with nothing behind it — we forced removal, saying explicitly that an institution's memory of itself gets no deference; (5) a bronze statue to a local woman said to have hidden nineteen children, where the archives name a different rescuer, but where the misattributed story is what actually inspired a rescue network that saved people in 1942 — we kept the statue and bolted on a second plaque naming the real person and explaining the error; (6) a neighbourhood association wanting a sign for "the green, common land since time immemorial", where the deeds show private ownership — we refused the sign.

Our new case is the one everyone's dreading. For about sixty years the town has held a dawn walk to a meadow where, by local memory, forty-one people deported in 1943 were shot; two independent archival series (transport rosters and burial registers, neither of them produced by any party to the dispute) put the shootings at a gravel pit three kilometres away and show the meadow was a storage yard with no burials. Three complications. First, the specific claim only appears in print from 1985 in a heritage society booklet, though residents interviewed a few years earlier remember the walk "always" going to the meadow without saying why — so the practice looks older than the claim, but we can't tell by how much. Second, since the nineties the walk has become the town's biggest civic event, run by an association that now includes descendants of people deported from other districts entirely, and a court ruling from that era — relying on the belief — blocked a road across the meadow; the meadow is up for sale and the buyer says the belief has no documentary basis. Third, an organised group of the actual descendants of the forty-one, a small community that's been marginalised in local life, want the walk rerouted to the gravel pit. I've also noticed something I can't get out of my head: our archive now holds a few hundred items — news stories, the court judgment, school materials, our own minutes — that all attest to the shooting at the meadow and that all exist *because* people believed it. So the record isn't clean of the memory; it's partly a sediment of it.

What I want from you: two or three candidate rules that fit all six precedents about equally well (something like record-primacy-with-an-interested-party-exception, versus a split rule where the record governs factual assertion and memory governs commemorative practice, versus something about what a false memory went on to *cause*), what each predicts we should do here, and your best guess with the reasons for preferring it — which precedent is really the closest analog, and what principle would we be adopting either way (that a practice built on a false claim inherits the defect, say, or that the archive is not a court of appeal for a ritual). Then tell me which single further case would best separate the surviving rules — I keep going back and forth between a case where the false memory is the descendants' *own* and they want to keep it, and a case where the record's only support is documents the memory generated. And please say something about that sedimentation point: how should a record-first rule treat documents whose entire provenance is the memory they're supposed to be checking? I care much less about which way we go than about whether we can say what we're doing.
