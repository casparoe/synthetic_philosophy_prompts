I'm putting together the theory chapter for my dissertation (disease and climate as causes in history — mostly 14th century Europe and the conquest of the Americas, with a Tambora section) and my supervisor told me to write down my actual commitments so she can push on them before I write 80 pages. So here's the set, roughly in the order I'd defend them:

(1) Pathogens get to be historical actors. I'm happy writing that the plague bacillus halved the labour force and pushed real wages up, and I don't want to be forced to translate that into "people died, and then other people did things." The microbe did something.

(2) But I also hold what I call the routing requirement: no non-human factor ever acts except through human arrangements — housing, trade routes, nutritional status, inheritance customs, how much coercive power lords actually have. The same mortality shock looks like it raised real wages in parts of western Europe and barely moved them elsewhere; and 1918 killed an enormous number of people without anything like a wage revolution. So the arrangement routes the effect.

(3) My guard against determinism is the sign-flip test: a non-human factor is only safe to invoke if its effect would flip, or at least change a lot, under some real variation in social arrangements. If the outcome comes out the same no matter what humans do, I call that determinism and I think the explanation is bad — methodologically bad, not just politically icky.

(4) On causation generally I'm a difference-making guy: C caused E if things would have gone differently without C. And I want a mechanism, not just a correlation.

(5) Politically I think the germ stories are the progressive ones — they replace "they won because they were better" with "they won because of acquired immunity and microbes" — and I think climate and geography stories are the reactionary ones, because they make conquest look inevitable and drain agency from the people who got conquered. So: germs in, climate treated with suspicion.

(6) I separate actors from conditions. Things that happen — an epidemic, an eruption — are candidate actors. Standing features — a continental axis, a climate regime, a long history of exposure to a disease — are stage. That's why I'll say the eruption caused the Year Without a Summer and the famines after it, but I won't call the volcano a historical actor, even though I grant the routing requirement applies to it exactly as much as to a pathogen.

(7) I insist causes be traceable through human arrangements, but I'm also fine with explanations that can't say why this village died out and the neighbouring one didn't, as long as the population-level mechanism is there.

Which of these actually collide, how badly, and what's the cheapest revision for each? Please rank them by severity rather than flagging all seven as equally problematic, and say for each what I'd have to give up. My guess is there's one real problem and alot of noise, but I can't tell which is which.
