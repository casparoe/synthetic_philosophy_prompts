I'm a journalist working on a long feature about the Great Divergence — the puzzle usually called the Needham question: why sustained, machine-based industrial growth began in Britain first and not in China, given how far ahead of Europe China was in so many techniques for so many centuries.

Please play the one interviewee I haven't been able to rattle: a senior economic historian who has spent a career on comparative Eurasian growth and who holds that why Britain and not China? is a well-posed question with a real, partly knowable answer — not a pseudo-question to be dissolved into why did industrialization happen at all? or why did it happen when it did?

How to play it: you are sharp and you have heard every version of my objection. You have real resources — that why-questions are inherently contrastive, that explanation proceeds by isolating difference-makers within a causal field, that a rare outcome can still have a cause, and that a comparison class can be repaired rather than abandoned. You will readily concede that China is the wrong unit and that the honest comparison is region to region, England against Jiangnan; that a contrastive question can be ill-posed when the alternative was never live; and that historians are always in danger of reading causes off the winner. You will not concede that the question dissolves, and you will not let me treat rerun-frequencies or counterfactual proximity as if they settled anything about historical explanation. First person, no hedging, no it depends, under 300 words, and you get to turn one question back on me at the end. I'll say stop when we're finished.

Here is my opening. Suppose, purely as a thought experiment, that we had a credible quantitative model of the Eurasian economies that we could rerun a thousand times from 1700 with tiny perturbations. In it, England industrializes before 1850 in about 3% of runs, Jiangnan in about 2%, and in the other 95% nothing happens anywhere before 1900. Two further stipulations: in the England-runs the proximate trigger differs wildly from run to run — coal freight rates, a wage spike, a colonial staple, a canal, a war — with no single trigger appearing in more than a fifth of them; whereas in nearly every Jiangnan-run, industrialization traces back to one recurring factor. Treat our actual history as one of the England-runs.

Now deal with the worry itself rather than with the realism of the model. If the trigger varies that much across the winning runs, then the familiar list of British advantages looks selected on the dependent variable: we are reading off whichever trigger happened to fire in the one run that is our world and calling it the cause. And if the Jiangnan-runs cluster on a single factor, then why not China? has a cleaner and more stable answer than why Britain? — which suggests the contrastive question is really two different questions wearing one hat, one about a rare, trigger-hungry success and one about a blocked path. So: is why Britain and not Jiangnan? well posed here, or does it decompose?
