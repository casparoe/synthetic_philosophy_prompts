I came to evolutionary anthropology from philosophy, and in the signalling reading group I've been sitting in on this term the same argument has recurred for months without anybody's position shifting by a millimetre, which makes me suspect it isn't the empirical disagreement it presents as. One camp reads the handicap principle in the spirit of Zahavi's original statement: honest signals are honest because they are genuinely burdensome, because the signaller is burning time, energy, or safety that could otherwise have gone into survival, and the whole point is that the ornament or display is a real drain — so the research programme is to go out and measure the drain (the metabolic and predation bill of a train, the wasted energy in a begging call, the money burnt on a conspicuous display) and to treat a signal that turns out to be cheap as a problem for the theory. The other camp reads it through the later game-theoretic formalisation usually credited to Grafen: what the equilibrium condition actually requires is that the marginal cost of producing a given signal level be higher for a low-quality signaller than for a high-quality one, which is a condition on cost curves and not a claim about waste at all, so that once you net out the benefit of the information transferred a signaller at equilibrium may be doing perfectly well, and the hunt for "waste" is a hunt for something the mathematics never predicted. Both camps then read the same literature — ornamented birds, stotting gazelles, begging behaviour, costly religious and charitable displays in humans — and each reports confirmation, with no agreed case that would count against either.

What makes me think the impasse is structural is a third kind of case I keep raising and that nobody quite knows what to do with: displays that are cheap to produce but expensive to get away with, because the cost is imposed afterwards by the audience or by rival signallers — a badge that is metabolically trivial but that gets you chased off the display ground if you wear it without the underlying quality, or a human display whose price is entirely a matter of who is watching and how they are disposed to react. Call these policed ornaments. My own hypothesis is that the real fight is about where the boundary of the signalling system gets drawn: whether the costs generated by receivers' responses are counted inside the signal's cost, as part of the mechanism, or outside it, as background enforcement that makes the case a different mechanism altogether rather than a variety of handicap. But I can also imagine it being a verbal dispute about "cost" and "handicap", a case of the two sides answering different questions under one name, or a genuinely empirical question about how costs are distributed in real populations that nobody has designed the right manipulation to settle.

So I'd like a diagnosis rather than a verdict: using what each side actually says, tell me whether this is verbal, substantive-but-hidden, a difference over which cases count as data, or a sign that the question "must honest signals be wasteful?" is ill-posed as it stands — and then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress. Would it take a formal result about whether signalling equilibria entail net fitness costs, a convention for measuring cost when signallers and receivers have co-adapted, an experiment that holds receiver response fixed while varying production cost, or simply a change of question? And if your diagnosis implies that no progress should be expected here, say so and say why.
