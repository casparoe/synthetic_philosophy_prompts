This has got personal for me in a way I didn't expect, so here's the backstory before the question.

About eight months ago I was put on a dopamine agonist for restless legs, and it has compulsive gambling listed as a possible side effect. Over the summer I lost a genuinely stupid amount of money on online slots. I've come off the drug since, and the pull basically vanished inside two weeks. My neurologist says, in effect: that was the medication, not you. My brother says: no, you wanted the money, the drug just turned the wanting up to eleven. I can't tell which of them is describing the world better, and it matters to me for non-philosophical reasons (whether I tell my partner, whether I trust myself with money again, whether "I wanted to" was ever true).

I've been poking around and found out this has a name and a whole literature, but I've also noticed I have a bunch of pretty firm verdicts on cases that don't obviously fit together. Here they are.

1. Morning coffee. I want coffee, I believe there is coffee in the kitchen, I walk to the kitchen. Suppose the neural firing pattern at that moment was all by itself enough, given the laws, to produce that exact walk. Verdict: the wanting and the believing still caused the walk. Anyone who says "sufficient physical cause, therefore no mental cause" has made a mistake, not discovered something.

2. Paper cut. I get a paper cut and snatch my hand back. Verdict: I don't want to say the *sting* did it. That feels like just what the nerve activity is like from the inside; the nerve activity moved the muscles. But I cannot see the difference between this and case 1, and it bugs me.

3. Alarm clock. It woke me because it was loud, not because it was 78.3 decibels, and not because of the exact air-pressure profile at my eardrum. At some point a description gets too fine-grained to be the thing that mattered.

4. Leaving the party. I left at eleven. I was bored — genuinely bored. Verdict: I did not leave because I was bored. I'd have left at eleven whatever I was feeling, because of the dog. So the mental story is a bad explanation here even though the mental state was really present, which is the opposite of what I said in case 1.

5. My colleague and the promotion. He applied, and we all say he did it because he wanted it. Verdict: that's not a causal claim, that's a redescription of the applying. Doesn't count. Case 1 does count, because there I could have wanted coffee and still not got up (had I remembered the kitchen was being repainted) — so the wanting isn't just read off the walking.

6. Chains. Say the wanting in case 1 was itself produced by low blood sugar and a pile of earlier neural goings-on. Verdict: so what. Being caused doesn't stop you from causing. I'm confident about this one — and I notice it's in direct tension with how I'm tempted to think about my own summer.

7. The protocol. A nurse enters the wrong dose. Verdict: the wrong default value baked into the protocol caused the error, and we're right to write it down as a cause in the incident report, even though nothing about the default physically did anything at that moment — the finger on the keypad did that. So "cause" has to include things that shape what happens without producing it.

8. Room for chance. Now suppose the neural state didn't guarantee the action, only made it very likely. Verdict: I feel better here, not worse — the wanting looks more like a real cause when the physics leaves a gap. But I also feel I can't have both the wanting and the neural state each pushing the probability up, since that would be counting the same push twice.

9. An absence. The junior tech didn't escalate the abnormal reading because he believed the senior had already looked at it. Verdict: that belief caused the non-escalation and we cite it. Never mind that a non-escalation isn't much of an event and the belief didn't produce anything.

10. My own case and I keep flip-flopping: some days the drug story feels like it *replaces* the wanting story, other days it feels like the drug caused a wanting which then caused the betting.

Here's my stab at the rule behind these. Something is a cause of what happened if: (a) had it been different, changing as little else as possible, the outcome — or how likely the outcome was — would have been different; (b) it's described at roughly the right level of detail, not so specific that it's really the cause of a different outcome and not so loose that it covers outcomes that didn't occur; and (c) it isn't something we only know about because the outcome happened.

I can already see where (a) breaks: in case 1, if the wanting isn't anything over and above the neural state, then there's no way to remove the wanting while leaving the neural state alone, and if I hold the neural state fixed then of course the wanting makes no difference. So my test either tells me there's no mental cause (which I reject) or it cheats by letting me change the physical state too.

So: run my three-part test against all ten cases, tell me which verdicts it can't save, and give me two or three better candidate principles instead — set out clause by clause, with the case that earns each clause, so I can see what's doing the work. I want to know which of my verdicts each candidate forces me to drop, especially whether 1 and 2 can both survive (and if not, which one the stronger view gives up and why), and what each one says about case 10 and about some case I haven't thought to list, so I can tell a real principle from a summary of my own list. Please just argue it out — I can't look anything up from where I am.
