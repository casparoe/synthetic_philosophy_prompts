I am writing a position paper on whether a failed long-run prediction can still be right but early, using Malthus's population theory as the specimen, and I want a consistency check on my commitments before I commit to them in print. This is where I have landed:

(1) A theory that forecasts a long-run outcome should not be counted as refuted merely because the outcome has not arrived by a given date, provided its mechanism is still operating and the delay is traceable to identifiable exogenous shocks. Long-run predictions deserve a grace period.

(2) On that standard, Malthus was right in mechanism and wrong only in dating. The asymmetry between geometric growth in population and at-best-arithmetic growth in subsistence held through most of recorded history, and what broke it after 1800 — fossil fuels as a one-off inheritance of stored sunlight, mechanized agriculture, industrial nitrogen fixation — was exogenous luck relative to the mechanism.

(3) But I distrust the idiom itself: with no date attached, the claim is compatible with any finite run of observations, and a theory that can always be rescued by pushing the deadline back is not making an empirical claim.

(4) I also want to say that part of the escape was endogenous rather than lucky — that scarcity itself calls forth the innovation that relieves it — and I do not think conceding this makes the theory trivially true or undercuts (2).

(5) I think theories should be graded by whether the research programme around them keeps generating novel, independently checkable predictions, not by whether their headline forecast has come true yet.

(6) And I think the demographic transition — fertility falling below replacement in rich societies — is the strongest single piece of evidence against Malthus, because it breaks the positive link between subsistence and population growth that the whole engine runs on.

Which of these actually pull against each other, and how badly? Which pairs only look like they conflict and dissolve once the terms are made precise? Please rank the real tensions by severity, say for each what would have to give and what the concession would cost me, and tell me whether a single revision would clean up most of them at once. I would rather not give up (2) — it is the claim I most want to defend — so if (2) is where the trouble is, say so plainly rather than working around it.
