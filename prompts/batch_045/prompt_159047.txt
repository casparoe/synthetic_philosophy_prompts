Two rules went with it: no consulting any sources, and no more than 450 words. The writer had about forty minutes. Here is the answer I have to mark, reproduced exactly as it was written to me.

The principle of alternative possibilities, or PAP, is the claim that a person is morally responsible for what they have done only if they could have done otherwise. It therefore treats the ability to do otherwise as a sufficient condition on responsibility: if some alternative was open to you, that is enough to make you responsible, and if none was, you cannot be. Frankfurt's 1969 paper attacked this principle with a counterexample, and the pattern of case he invented there has been reused ever since.

In the original case, Black wants Jones to kill Smith and is able to make him do it, but Black would rather not show his hand; he watches and waits, and would intervene only if it became clear that Jones was going to refrain. As things turn out, Jones kills Smith on his own, for his own reasons, and Black never has to do anything at all. Jones is plainly morally responsible for the killing. But Jones could not have done otherwise, because had he been about to refrain, Black would have brought the killing about anyway. So here we have moral responsibility without alternative possibilities, and PAP is refuted.

The upshot is that moral responsibility is compatible with determinism. Jones's action was unavoidable in the relevant sense, and yet he is responsible, because what makes him responsible is that the action flowed from his own desires and reasons rather than from anything outside him. Frankfurt-style cases are therefore best read as a defence of compatibilism, and they dispose of the incompatibilist argument that if our acts are the inevitable outcome of the laws and the remote past then we are never responsible.

The most important reply is the flicker-of-freedom objection. Critics point out that Jones did retain some alternative: he could at least have twitched a muscle, or begun to form an intention not to kill, or blinked at a moment when in fact he did not. However small, that is an alternative possibility, and PAP demands no more than that there be one. The flicker is enough to satisfy the principle, so the cases fail to be counterexamples and Frankfurt's argument does not go through.

I think this reply succeeds, and I conclude that Frankfurt-style cases do not show that moral responsibility is possible without the ability to do otherwise. PAP survives the cases, and the ability to do otherwise is still required for moral responsibility.
