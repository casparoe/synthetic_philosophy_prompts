My roommate and I got into a stupid three-hour argument last night that ended with him saying "Malthus wasn't wrong, he was just early" and me saying that's just a get-out-of-jail-free card, and we never resolved anything because we were both exhausted. The frustrating part is that he then used the exact same move on two more cases: those 1970s-ish claims that we were about to run out of key resources, and the more recent forecasts that antibiotic resistance will be killing something like millions of people a year by around 2050. Every time the shape was identical — the thing hasn't happened inside the window anyone actually cared about, so the reply is "right mechanism, wrong date."

So I want to know what that sentence is really claiming. Can you lay out the distinct precise theses "Malthus was right, he was just early" could be expressing, as a menu running from the trivial to the wild, with a verdict on each one (platitude / substantive and defensible / false / empty or basically unfalsifiable) — and, the part I actually care about, tell me which of them would do the work he wants them to do, i.e. which readings would genuinely license keeping a missed long-run prediction alive instead of just conceding it was wrong.

Here are the half-baked distinctions I tried out on him, which you should feel free to sharpen, merge, or throw out. (a) Mechanism vs. date: that the underlying mechanism was always real and only the timing was off, versus the stronger claim that the mechanism will dominate eventually, versus the mushy claim that it's just one force among many. (b) Whether "early" requires having said in advance how early: a claim that never named a window, or named one and blew straight through it, versus a claim that said something like "roughly by 1900" and was off by a century. (c) My favourite, which he called a cheap trick: nobody ever says a theory was "right but early" before the deadline passes, and I've never once heard anyone say a theory was "right but late" — it predicted disaster later than the disaster actually showed up — so is the phrase doing any work beyond marking a prediction as not-yet-falsified? (d) Whether the delay itself counts as evidence against the mechanism: if population growth slowed because people got richer and chose to have fewer kids, is that Malthus's mechanism arriving on a delayed schedule, or a different mechanism that he'd have to be credited with by hindsight? He also kept insisting that the real Malthusian claim was about the ratio between population growth and food production rather than about mass starvation, and that things like fertiliser and the green revolution just moved the boundary conditions — I coudnt tell whether that helps him or quietly concedes my point.

If it matters for which readings to stress: he's a physics grad student and at one point started talking about theories being correct "in the limit" and about boundary conditions, which I think is smuggling in a reading where the prediction was never really about any particular date at all. Where does that one sit on the menu, and is it salvageable or is it the death of the argument?
