My flatmate Rae and her friend Jonas got into a long argument after a talk last week and they've asked me to settle it, which I can't really do — i was only half following and I dont have the background. The topic was that acausal trade idea: two civilizations that can never causally affect each other (outside each other's light cones, no contact ever, in either direction) can still effectively trade, because their decisions are correlated — each runs roughly similar reasoning, so if you build, they probably build too. Concretely: each side can spend 1 unit of resources to build a beacon worth 10 units of value to the other side, and both sides face exactly the same choice.

They went at it roughly like this.

(1) Jonas: in the clean version, where the two decisions are perfectly correlated, paying is a no-brainer. Either you both build or neither does, so building gets you 10 units for 1.

(2) Rae: fine, but that's the toy case. Realistically the correlation is partial, and the number that matters isn't how likely they are to build, it's how much your choice moves their chance. Say Andromeda has some standing religious rule about building beacons, so they build with about 85% probability if you don't, and about 90% if you do. Your building shifts them by 5 points, so it buys you half a unit of expected benefit for 1 unit of cost. So don't build.

(3) Jonas: that can't be right. If I build, I collect 10 units of value with a 90% chance — 9 in expectation, minus the 1 it costs me, so +8. You want me to walk away from 8 units of expected value over a technicality about a 5-point correlation.

(4) Rae: you're comparing building against zero instead of against not building. If you don't build you still collect 8.5 in expectation, because most of that 90% is money you'd get either way. Your +8 is worse than +8.5.

(5) Jonas: ok, the arithmetic is yours in that particular case. But the case-by-case calculation is the wrong unit of assessment. What you really pick is a policy, not an act, and the policy of building when there's a correlated partner out there is the one that does well across the whole range of cases. A policy that haggles in each case over whether the shift is 5 points or 50 points is the same policy that will talk itself out of building when the shift is 99 points, by exactly the same style of reasoning. So I still build.

(6) Rae: that doesn't answer it, it relocates it. At the policy level you need the distribution of correlation strengths across the partners you might have, and you have no evidence about that — the partners you can never observe are precisely the ones you'd need the statistics for. Worse, the correlation isn't even well defined until you fix a reference class: all civilizations, all civilizations running your decision procedure, all decisions made by anything anywhere. Different choices, different numbers, so there may be no fact of the matter about how much your choice moves theirs.

(7) Jonas: that reference-class worry is completely generic. Every bit of probabilistic reasoning has it, and we handle it the usual way, by picking the most specific class that still has predictive grip. Nothing special breaks here.

Can you adjudicate this move by move? I don't just want an overall winner — I want to know which points are actually decisive, which are right but beside the point, where each of them makes a genuine mistake, and what both of them are missing. I genuinely have no idea who to side with.
