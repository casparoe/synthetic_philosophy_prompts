So I've been going through everything this philosopher (call her H.) has published on moral responsibility, because she's visiting our department for a workshop in March and I've somehow ended up in charge of the Q&A. I want to build one case she hasn't ruled on, spring it on her, and see if I can back her into saying what she actually thinks about determinism, which she has never stated outright — in one footnote she says something like: whether determinism as such undermines responsibility is a separate question her own view never had to answer.

Her track record, as near as I can put it together from about a dozen papers:

- The original Frankfurt setup: Black can read from Jones's face what he is about to decide and would force him to decide to shoot Smith if he showed any sign of deciding otherwise; Jones shows no such sign and shoots Smith on his own. Verdict: responsible, and fully.
- The flicker variant: the intervention is triggered by an involuntary blink, so the only thing left open to Jones is the blink itself. Verdict: responsible; she says a blink isn't the kind of thing that could ground anything.
- The chancy variant: the mechanism misfires. Had Jones shown signs of deciding otherwise, there was only about a 70% chance it would have redirected him. Verdict: responsible.
- The self-installed chip: six months earlier Jones himself arranged, sober and fully informed, to be wired so that any sign of deciding to drink would be redirected; he does not drink; the mechanism never fires. Verdict: responsible, full credit, no mitigation whatsoever.
- The omission variant: Black would have paralyzed Jones's legs the moment he tried to open the door; Jones never tries; the kid drowns. Verdict: responsible for the omission.
- The phobic driver: no intervener, no chip; a man with a panic response to spiders freezes at the wheel and hits a cyclist, and nothing about the mechanism that issues in the freezing is sensitive to reasons. Verdict: not responsible, exempted.
- The covert manipulation case: neuroscientists remotely bring it about that his reasoning runs as it does; he isn't coerced, he endorses what he does, he'd have responded to some reasons, but the mechanism was installed without his knowledge or any say-so on his part. Verdict: not responsible.

Two things she says in passing pull in opposite directions. In one paper: an intervener who never fires isn't part of the actual causal history, so it can't do any work. In another: the burden is on the other side to produce a case in which the agent genuinely could not have done otherwise, and no Frankfurt case in the literature delivers one, because either the case presupposes determinism or else the mechanism can't be made reliable in an indeterministic world.

The case I want to spring: everything Jones does is fixed by the laws plus the past — determinism is stipulated in the story, not smuggled in. Black's mechanism doesn't wait for a decision or a sign: it monitors subpersonally and pre-empts any neural precursor that would have issued in a different decision before it ever reaches Jones's deliberation, so there is no flicker, no blink, and no chance of a misfire. It never fires. Jones deliberates, weighs the reasons, decides for the reasons he takes to be decisive, is not alienated from what he does, and cheats his business partner out of about forty thousand dollars to pay for a boat.

What I want from you:

1. Will H. say he's blameworthy? Give me a probability, definately not just a yes or no.
2. The rule she seems to be running, and the other rules that fit those seven verdicts just as well — the reasons-responsiveness / actual-sequence line, the line that Frankfurt cases never really remove the alternatives, an identification or answerability view, whatever else you can make fit. Say what each predicts about my boat case.
3. What it would mean if she won't give a verdict at all and just says the case is question-begging.
4. The one follow-up case I should have queued up that will seperate whichever two rules survive her answer.

I care much less about which theory is true than about whether her own record really pins her down, or whether I'm about to discover she has more room to wiggle than she admits.
