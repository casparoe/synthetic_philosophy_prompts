I've been reading about just war stuff on my own — no philosophy background, just things online and a podcast — and I keep running into the same move. Someone will say that soldiers on both sides of a war are in the same moral position, and the picture they reach for is a boxing match. Two fighters agree to get in the ring, both fight by the same rules, neither is a criminal for trying to win, the referee doesn't care whose cause is better, and afterwards nobody says the loser should be charged with assault. So: a German conscript in 1944 and a British conscript in 1944 are morally on a par, both are legitimate targets, and neither is a murderer for killing the other. People call this the moral equality of combatants, and they use it to license all sorts of things — that refusing to fight on the unjust side is optional rather than required, that we shouldn't blame veterans, that targeting munitions workers is fine if targeting the people shooting at you is fine.

I dont trust it, but I cant say exactly where it goes wrong, because every time I pull a thread it turns out to depend on something the boxing picture quietly puts in. So could you audit it properly. I want the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the referee, the rules, the gloves, the consent, the bell, the crowd, the title — and then the places the mapping breaks. A few things I keep tripping over that you should probably deal with:

- boxers literally consent to be hit and can walk away; a conscript can't, and even a volunteer usually signs up to a country rather than to a particular war. Does the analogy smuggle in a voluntariness most soldiers dont have, and does that matter for the conclusion or not?
- a boxing match doesn't settle anything about who was in the right, and nobody thinks the better boxer deserves to win. But wars do settle things, and we do think one side might deserve to win. Is the analogy making war out to be a pure contest when it isn't one?
- in the ring you're fighting for yourself. In a war people say they're fighting for their country, their family, the person next to them. Does that third-party bit change the structure at all, or is it noise?
- I made up a case I think breaks it but I can't tell if I'm cheating. Take a keen volunteer who believes in the war and voted for it, and a conscript on the other side who thinks the whole thing is a disaster and is only there because he'd be shot otherwise. Both are doing the same thing — trying to kill enemy soldiers — so the boxing picture seems to say they're in the same position. That feels wrong in a way I can't articulate. Is it wrong, and if so does that kill the analogy or only part of it?

And then the bit I really want: what exactly is the conclusion supposed to be? I think people run together about four different claims — that both sides have the same permission to fight, that both are equally liable to be killed, that neither is blameworthy, and that both deserve the same respect or honour. Can the boxing picture carry all four, only some, or none? And my mate says the whole thing is really an epistemic point in disguise: since a soldier can basically never know whether his side is the just one, we treat them as equal because we can't expect them to work it out. If that's right then the boxing match is doing no work at all and we'd do better saying the uncertainty thing directly. Is he on to something, and does it change what follows — for an officer who does have access to the intelligence, say, or for someone who could just look at what his army is actually doing?

Finally: if the boxing match is the wrong picture, is there a better one? The obvious rival seems to be policing — the just side is like the police, the unjust side like criminals — but that has its own problems I can feel without being able to name (the police don't bomb neighbourhoods). Give me whatever you think is closest, and be explicit about what each picture quietly imports that the target doesn't have.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble and don't restate the question back at me. British English please, and I'd rather you argued it out than told me which famous books say what.
