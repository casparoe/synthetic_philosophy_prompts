I've got a third-year pragmatics seminar next term and I want to spend one session on whether the Gricean story about speaker meaning can survive unofficial communication — the sort where the whole point is that you can truthfully deny you ever said anything. I want to stress-test my objection before I inflict it on twenty undergraduates, so I'd like you to take the other side.

Please play a committed, well-read Gricean about utterer's meaning: dry, patient, happy to make distinctions all afternoon, willing to concede that "say", "tell", "address" and "mean" come apart, and that the calculability and cancellability tests are rougher than the textbooks let on. Do not concede that the reflexive-intention analysis should be swapped for anything weaker, like an intended-effect or reliable-signalling account. Stay in character until I say "stop". Replies around 150 words, no bullet points, no headings, and no reading lists or paper titles — I've read the literature and you haven't got a library, so just make the argument. You can ask me one question per turn, but answer first.

My opening case. Dev is in the common room, half-reading, not part of the conversation. Margo knows he's there. She wants him to know the department meeting has moved to Friday, and she also wants to be able to say to the Head of Department afterwards, truthfully, "I never told him anything — he wasn't even in that conversation." So she says to Priya, at exactly the right volume, "Right, I'll see you and the others on Friday then." Dev hears it, works out that Margo knew he was there, knows that she knew he'd work out that she knew, and so on up; he also works out that she wants to be able to deny it to the Head, and that he's supposed to act on the information without ever letting on where he got it. He does.

Two things seem to go wrong for you. First, the vehicle: part of what Margo gets across depends on her *not* addressing him, so the route Dev takes is one she can't acknowledge without wrecking it. An intention designed to be unattributable to anyone outside the pair looks less like "intend him to recognise my intention" and more like "intend him to end up believing", which your own analysis sets aside as mere showing. Second, the nesting: she means him to recognise that she means to be able to deny it, and to co-operate in keeping it hidden — so the intention is overt to one audience and, by design, invisible to another. That doesn't look like the kind of thing a maxim-governed, calculable inference is supposed to recover.

If your answer is that she didn't mean anything and merely got him to believe it, tell me honestly what that costs you, because my students are going to say that's exactly the case the theory was built to capture — and if your answer is that Dev really does recognise her intention so meaning is present after all, tell me what still distinguishes meaning from showing on your account.
