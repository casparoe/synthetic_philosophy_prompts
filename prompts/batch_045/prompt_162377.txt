I want a probability, and I want to see the machinery that produced it.

The question is conditional, and I want the conditional kept clean. Take as stipulated that (i) some version of Everettian quantum mechanics is true — unitary dynamics, no collapse, branching — and (ii) the branching structure is finite: a finite-dimensional Hilbert space, finitely many branches at each measurement-like event, finitely many branches across the whole history. I am stipulating (ii) not because I believe it but because it removes a familiar objection — that "how many branches?" has no well-defined answer when amplitudes are arbitrary reals — and I want to know how much of the Born-rule problem survives that concession. I also want it stipulated that the agent whose credences are in question knows the universal wavefunction and the dynamics with certainty, so that the only uncertainty left is self-locating.

The proposition, call it P: there exists a derivation of the Born rule from the bare Everettian formalism — unitary dynamics plus branching, no hidden variables, no collapse postulate, no separate probability axiom, and no additional structure whose specification already presupposes amplitude-squared weights — such that a competent critic would concede the derivation is not question-begging. Read P as about a derivation that is possible in principle; if you want, add a separate figure for whether one will actually be written down and widely conceded within thirty years.

Please give me your credence in P, decomposed. At minimum, I want numbers on:

1. Whether an agent certain of the wavefunction and dynamics can have non-degenerate self-locating credences at all — whether "probability" in this setting can be anything other than a stipulation dressed up.
2. Whether the decision-theoretic route — representation theorems purporting to show that a rational agent in an Everettian world must weight branches by modulus-squared amplitude — can avoid building those weights into its axioms, and, if it can, whether the axioms it does need are independently plausible.
3. Whether the enumerative route survives stipulation (ii): whether decomposing branches into equal-amplitude sub-branches and counting them delivers the Born weights, or whether the number of sub-branches is fixed only by already helping yourself to the weights.
4. Whether the arguments meant to show that branch-counting is incoherent — the style of case where an agent can survive in only one of the branches — establish anything stronger than the failure of naive counting.
5. Whether the Born rule is the right target at all: whether it is exact or an idealization that holds only for measurement-like interactions, in which case a successful "derivation" might be a derivation of the wrong thing.
6. Whether P has a determinate answer we would recognize as such — whether, if a derivation were produced, there is any realistic convergence on whether it begs the question.

For each, a number and one line of justification. Then tell me how you are combining them — conjunctively, as disjunctive routes, with what dependencies — and give the total. Then: which single term is your final number most sensitive to, which term are you least confident about, and what argument (not publication, not experiment) would move that term most.

Two more constraints. First, I do not want "it depends what you count as a derivation" as an answer; choose a reading of derivation and of question-begging, state each in one sentence, give the number under that reading, and then give a second number under a stricter reading if you think the choice matters. Second, I am at 0.25 on P conditional on (i) and (ii); a friend of mine, who has thought about this far longer than I have, is at 0.7. Given your decomposition, tell me which term that disagreement most likely lives in, and what each of us would have to concede to close it.
