Hey — I'm putting together a handout for my second-year normative theory seminar, the week we do supererogation, and I've decided I'd rather give them a fake internal document than another paper. Could you write it?

Format: an internal people-ops document from a mid-sized company — make one up — that has just added a fifth rating tier, "Beyond the Call," to its performance rubric, and made that tier a precondition for promotion. Numbered clauses, maybe 700–900 words, then a short FAQ, and a one-paragraph "calibration note" from the compensation committee at the end. Deadpan corporate voice throughout: no winking, no punchlines that sound like a comedian wrote them, nothing that breaks character to tell the reader this is a joke.

The humour has to come from the actual conceptual trouble, though. Things I'd like it to land, and feel free to add your own:

- The second a tier is required for promotion, the acts in it aren't beyond the call any more. The document should half-notice this and patch it with something worse.
- The company's own ethics training says to always do the most good you can. Take that seriously: on that standard nothing is optional, so there shouldn't be a supererogation tier at all. Let the document notice this in a footnote and carry on regardless.
- There should be a clause denying credit for acts done with an eye to the rating — and it should be obvious that the clause is both unavoidable and impossible to audit.
- Something on cost to the agent. If what makes an act supererogatory is partly that it costs the person something, then employees can farm the rating by making their acts expensive, and the company ends up grading how much something hurt instead of what was done. I'd love and "evidence of personal sacrifice" attachment.
- A case I made up last year and still can't resolve: a team does something no member was required to do, but no individual's share was more than anyone could reasonably have been asked for. Does the team get the tier? Whatever the document decides, I want the answer to sound completely sane in corporate and completely mad in the seminar room.

It's fine — maybe better — if the last clause quietly gives up and admits the tier exists because they needed a top bucket and "Exceeds Expectations" wasn't doing the work any more. I want my students to laugh, and then be unable to say whether the company has abolished supererogation or just demonstrated that it was never there.
