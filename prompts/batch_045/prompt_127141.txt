For the past eight months I have been the newest trustee of the Cascade County Community Foundation. I took the seat somewhat reluctantly after my mother's death, when I found myself the executor of a bequest she had left the foundation, and I have been asked to draft the allocation memo for that money ahead of our June meeting. The bequest is $24 million, restricted by the donor's letter to use for "the lasting benefit of the residents of Cascade County."

Three uses are on the table. Option A renews our Housing Stability Fund — direct cash and rental assistance — at roughly $4 million a year for six years. Its effects are well measured and, as far as anyone can tell, largely fade within a few years of the last payment. Option B commits about $20 million over fifteen years to a new Cascade Civic Trust, which would fund ballot-measure campaigns, redistricting-reform work, and local journalism; its measured effects are close to zero, and everything its advocates claim runs through political and institutional cascades that no one on the board can model with any confidence. Option C is to hold and invest the principal and revisit the question in 2029.

The difficulty, which the board has been circling for two meetings without naming, is this: for A the long-run effects are probably small but positive; for B the long-run effects might be very large, and we have no way of telling whether they are large and good or large and bad. Our uncertainty about the distant effects is not a thin haze around a solid estimate — it swamps the estimate. Call that cluelessness if you like. Our program staff have proposed a procedural fix, the "sign-stability rule": commission three scenario models per option (baseline, cascade, backlash), and if the sign of an option's estimated net effect flips across them, declare the long-run term evidentially inert, drop it, and rank the options by effects measurable within ten years. Trustee Halvorsen has proposed an amendment: when the long-run term is inert in that sense, we should prefer the option with the widest spread of possible long-run outcomes, on the ground that a certain small gain is worth less in expectation than a small chance of a very large one, and cluelessness is a reason to seek variance, not to hide from it.

Please draft the memo I will circulate to the other five trustees. It should: (1) open with a single sentence stating the recommendation; (2) commit to a concrete allocation with dollar figures and a timetable, including any checkpoints or conditions; (3) say whether we should adopt the sign-stability rule, Halvorsen's amendment, both, or neither as standing policy for future restricted bequests; (4) tell us how much weight reversibility deserves, given that any of the three can be halted at a three-year checkpoint but B's grantees would need eighteen months' notice; and (5) give the strongest version of the argument you are rejecting, stated well enough that its proponent would recognise it. Take a definite position rather than laying out the menu — where you think the underlying question is genuinely open, say so in a single closing sentence flagging which judgment your recommendation rests on.

Please keep the whole memo under 700 words.
