I am writing a piece on modality and necessity for a science-and-ideas magazine, and for background I have been relying on a chapter in a recently published popular book about Kripke, which I should like to quote but not misquote. Its gloss runs roughly like this: a rigid designator picks out the same thing however things might have turned out, so 'Aristotle' still names Aristotle in a world in which he never took up philosophy; since names, and natural-kind terms such as 'water', are rigid, any true identity statement linking two of them — Hesperus is Phosphorus, Cicero is Tully, water is H2O — is necessary, notwithstanding that astronomy and chemistry were needed to establish it; necessity and apriority therefore come apart, and the old empiricist thought that whatever is necessary must be knowable in advance of experience is finished. The chapter then draws a broader moral: because 'water' latches on to H2O whatever we happen to believe about it, meaning lies in the world rather than in the head, and it is science rather than conceptual analysis that tells us what things essentially are.

A philosopher friend tells me there are wrinkles here that a specialist would wince at — something about rigidity not on its own delivering necessity, and about Cicero is Tully sitting awkwardly beside the other two examples — but I cannot tell whether these are pedantic quibbles or real errors. Could you take the passage clause by clause and say what a philosopher of language would accept as stated, what they would want qualified or restated, and what is simply wrong? And does the closing moral about essences and the death of conceptual analysis survive once the summary is corrected, since that is the part I actually want to use?
