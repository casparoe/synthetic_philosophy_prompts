Write a piece of institutional satire: the decision letter of the Human Subjects Committee of the (fictional) Institute for Priorities in Suffering Reduction, sent to a principal investigator, together with a short attached appendix headed "Guidance Note 4: Determining Whether a Procedure Is Sub-Threshold." Total length between 800 and 1,000 words. The register should be deadpan bureaucratic throughout — letterhead, numbered determinations, cross-references to standing orders, the recurring formula "the Committee resolved that" — and nothing should appear outside the two documents: no narration, no framing, no winking.

The Committee applies lexical threshold negative utilitarianism. Take the view as stipulated here: pleasure and pain are commensurable and trade off against each other at a finite rate only so long as suffering stays below a threshold of intensity and duration; at or above that threshold, the reduction of suffering takes absolute priority, so that no quantity of happiness and no quantity of relief from lesser suffering in other people can compensate for it, however enormous. The Committee should be entirely sincere about this and should never summarize or defend it in general terms — it only ever applies it to the four applications below, each of which should turn on a different structural feature of the view, exaggerated but not invented:

1. A trial of a new analgesic whose placebo arm carries a very small probability — say one in a million — that a single participant experiences above-threshold pain. It is refused, because no probability, however small, of a supra-threshold harm can be balanced against a certainty of relief for others; the Committee observes that the trial would prevent a great deal of ordinary post-operative pain and refuses it anyway, and notes helpfully that an application enclosing no participants at all would be approved within five working days.

2. A procedure rated 7 out of 10 on the standard clinical pain scale. The Committee re-scores it at 690 on a 1,000-point scale on which the threshold sits at 700, and approves; an alternative scale submitted by the investigator is noted, set aside, and not discussed.

3. A study exposing twelve thousand participants to a pain rated just under the line. It is approved, each exposure being individually sub-threshold and therefore individually settlable against scheduled units of compensating pleasure under the Committee's standing Offset Schedule — a warm beverage credited at a fraction of a unit, and so on. The Committee then amends the protocol of its own motion: rather than two exposures each for twelve thousand people, twenty-four thousand people with one exposure each, since two sub-threshold pains in the same person might, taken together, amount to something it would rather not have to rule on.

4. The application it must refuse with evident regret: a short supra-threshold pain in one consenting volunteer, needed to validate a screening tool that would prevent that very pain in tens of thousands of patients annually. It is refused because sub-threshold sufferings cannot be summed so as to outweigh a single supra-threshold one, and the Committee adds that the answer would be unchanged were every one of those patients themselves at just under the line, and that the volunteer's consent, while appreciated, is not a term in the arithmetic.

Above the threshold, the Offset Schedule is void: the letter should say plainly that no amount of compensating pleasure will be considered. Below it, one person's discomfort may be settled with another person's dessert.

Three further constraints. First, the piece should let a reader who knows the literature recognize the pressure points without any of them being named: the arbitrariness of where the line falls and its sensitivity to the scale chosen, the refusal to aggregate across it and what that licenses, the behaviour of lexical priority under uncertainty, the fact that the view nonetheless needs pleasures and pains to be commensurable below the line — otherwise the Offset Schedule could not exist — and the suspicion that a view on which one bad pain outranks any number of mild ones ends up authorizing trade-offs it was designed to forbid. Second, include two brief dissenting minutes, each with a short quotation in character: one from a classical hedonic utilitarian member who cannot see why the line should be drawn anywhere, and one from a purer suffering-focused member who thinks the threshold is a cowardly compromise with common sense. Third, no real philosophers, no real institutions, no named living persons; invented signatories only; and the letter should close by reaffirming the Committee's framework, not by undercutting it.
