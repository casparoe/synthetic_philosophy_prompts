I've been going back and forth with a friend from a meditation group — this started after a retreat, of all places, over whether the point of the practice is that quiet contentment is *better* or merely that it is *safer* — and I want to know where the real disagreement is rather than where we argue loudest. She calls the view tranquilism; here is the thesis as I want it pinned down, and please hold me to this formulation rather than a looser one. As a claim about prudential value — about how well a life goes for the subject, not about moral rightness — the only states of a subject that contribute non-instrumentally to her welfare are the absence of suffering and the absence of craving; positive experience matters at all only when, and to the extent that, it is the stilling of a craving or the ceasing of a suffering, so that a craving's being satisfied adds nothing beyond the relief, and a life of flat contentment with no cravings is not thereby missing out on anything good. By "suffering" I mean any experience with unpleasant phenomenal character; by "craving" I mean an occurrent urge, want, or thirst directed at something not presently possessed. My credence that this is true is about 0.3; hers is about 0.75.

Give me your own number, but with the decomposition that produces it. The terms I keep circling among, which I'd like you to assign rough probabilities to (add any you think I'm missing, and say how you are combining them — I know they are not independent, so tell me how you are handling that rather than just multiplying): (i) the "pleasure is only soothing" thesis, that every positive experience is at bottom the relief or quiescence of something aversive, so that the difference between eating when hungry and eating when sated lies entirely in the relief and not in some extra positive quality; (ii) the case I cannot get her to answer, which I'll call scaffolded appetite — a mild, short-lived craving is induced by a harmless and fully reversible intervention in someone with no prior interest in the activity, the activity follows and is enjoyed, and let us stipulate both that the enjoyment is more intense while it lasts and that the person's considered verdict afterwards is that the craving made the whole episode better than the same activity would have been without it, not merely that it made the eventual relief sweeter. Does the view have to count that cycle as no better than the craving-free version, and how much does your credence fall if you think that verdict is wrong?; (iii) whether "craving" is a phenomenal notion at all, i.e. whether an urge that never surfaces in felt experience — something merely functional or dispositional, an unfelt goal-frustration — can be bad for the subject in the way the view needs it to be; (iv) whether there are positive states that are neither relief nor a mere craving-free zero — absorption, flow, quiet alertness, the felt energy of interest itself — and whether the absence-formulation can accommodate them without being stretched to the point of vacuity; (v) the collapse worry: whether the two clauses do independent work, or whether the view quietly becomes negative hedonism with "craving" just meaning unpleasant experience and "suffering" quietly expanded to swallow any counterexample; (vi) the methodology term — how much weight careful first-person report, retrospective judgement, and imagined idealized endorsement each deserve here, and whether anything of that kind can settle a question like this at all.

Then tell me which of those terms your final number is most sensitive to, in the sense that shifting it by, say, 0.2 would move the bottom line most; what single piece of evidence or argument would move you most and in which direction — a finding about the neural correlates of satisfaction as against relief, disciplined introspective reports from trained practitioners, a conceptual argument about the direction of fit of desire or about whether relief is itself a positive sensation; and, given my 0.3 and her 0.75, which one or two of these sub-questions is most likely where our disagreement actually lives. I don't need a map of the literature or anything that would require looking things up, and I'd rather you not gesture at positions by name — I want a number and the argument that makes it, with its weak points marked.
