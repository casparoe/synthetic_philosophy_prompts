THE QUESTION AS SET

Q3. 100 tickets, numbered 1–100, exactly one drawn at random. Payoffs:

Pair I
A: $1m whatever ticket is drawn
B: $5m on tickets 1–10; $1m on 11–99; $0 on 100

Pair II
C: $1m on tickets 1–11; $0 on 12–100
D: $5m on tickets 1–10; $0 on 11–100

(a) [10] Most people choose A over B and D over C. State the independence axiom carefully, and show, using these numbers, that this pair of preferences violates it. Be explicit about what the common consequence is in each pair and about what is left once you strip it off.

Explain precisely why this reply fails. Do the algebra with a general utility function u; you may not assume u($0) = 0. Then say briefly what kind of departure from EU would actually accommodate the pattern.

(c) [10] Someone concludes: so independence is simply false as a description, and the people choosing A and D are not being irrational. Assess this. Give the strongest argument you can that independence is a requirement of rationality rather than a convenient bit of formalism; then the strongest thing on the other side; and say whether the resulting stand-off is a merely verbal dispute or a substantive one.

ONE STUDENT'S ANSWER (unedited)

(a) The independence axiom (also called the sure-thing principle, Savage's axiom P2 — same thing, stated two ways): for any lotteries P, Q, R and any α with 0 < α < 1, P ≽ Q iff αP + (1−α)R ≽ αQ + (1−α)R. Mixing two lotteries with the same third one in the same proportion cannot reverse your ranking of them. Equivalently: if two lotteries agree on a common consequence — same outcome, same probability — you can ignore that consequence.

Pair I: A and B both pay $1m on tickets 11–99, so they share a common consequence of $1m with probability 0.89. Write A = 0.11A′ + 0.89[$1m] and B = 0.11B′ + 0.89[$1m], where A′ pays $1m on tickets 1–11 (i.e. $1m for sure) and B′ pays $5m on 1–10 and $0 on 11 (i.e. 10/11 chance of $5m, 1/11 of $0). By independence, A ≻ B iff A′ ≻ B′.

Pair II: C and D both pay $0 on tickets 12–100, again probability 0.89. So C = 0.11C′ + 0.89[$0] and D = 0.11D′ + 0.89[$0], where C′ is $1m on tickets 1–11 and D′ is $5m on 1–10, $0 on 11. So C′ = A′ and D′ = B′, and independence gives C ≻ D iff A′ ≻ B′ iff A ≻ B. Independence therefore requires A ≻ B iff C ≻ D. Someone who picks A and also picks D is committed to A′ ≻ B′ and to B′ ≻ A′, which cannot both hold for a complete and transitive preference relation. So the modal pair of choices violates independence. Note that nothing here depends on the particular amounts of money: any outcome the two options share, with the same probability, drops out, and the comparison that remains is the same one both times.

(b) The classmate is wrong, because risk aversion is not an extra ingredient you bolt onto EU — within EU it is already present, as concavity of u. Compute:
EU(A) = u(1m)
EU(B) = 0.10u(5m) + 0.89u(1m) + 0.01u(0)
So A ≻ B iff u(1m) > 0.10u(5m) + 0.89u(1m) + 0.01u(0), i.e. (subtracting 0.89u(1m) from each side) 0.11u(1m) > 0.10u(5m) + 0.01u(0).
EU(C) = 0.11u(1m) + 0.89u(0)
EU(D) = 0.10u(5m) + 0.90u(0)
So D ≻ C iff 0.10u(5m) + 0.90u(0) > 0.11u(1m) + 0.89u(0), i.e. (subtracting 0.89u(0)) 0.10u(5m) + 0.01u(0) > 0.11u(1m).
These two conditions are exact contradictories, and I never assumed u(0) = 0. So no utility function over money — concave, convex or anything else — delivers both preferences together. If u(0) = 0 you can see it more directly: A ≻ B needs u(5m)/u(1m) < 1.1 while D ≻ C needs u(5m)/u(1m) > 1.1. Concavity can give you A ≻ B on its own (that is just risk aversion, and EU is perfectly happy with it); what it cannot give you is A ≻ B and D ≻ C at once, since concavity is a fact about three money amounts and both comparisons involve those same three amounts in the same way.

What does explain the pattern is loss aversion. Measured against the sure million you can practically bank, the 1% chance of walking away with nothing in B looms far larger than 1% warrants — losses hurt roughly twice as much as equal gains please — so the bad tail of B gets weighted more heavily than its probability and A wins. In Pair II there is no sure thing on the table to act as a reference point, so nothing gets overweighted and the more attractive $5m wins. Prospect theory models this by a value function kinked at the reference point; EU is the special case where the reference point is final wealth and the kink is absent, so prospect theory generalizes EU rather than refuting it.

(c) The conclusion is half right. Independence is not merely convenient formalism. The best argument for taking it normatively is the cancellation argument: on tickets 11–99, A and B pay exactly the same, so those tickets cannot be a reason to prefer either one — whichever you pick you get $1m on them. The choice must therefore be settled by tickets 1–11, and restricted to those tickets A is C and B is D. So anyone who prefers A to B ought to prefer C to D. That is not a formal trick; it is a consistency requirement on what can count as a reason. A second argument runs through sequential choice: if you choose A over B in advance, then when it is announced that the ticket drawn is in 1–11 nothing relevant to the comparison has changed, so you should stay with A; but conditional on 1–11 A is C and B is D, so a plan to take A is a plan to take C, contradicting your preference for D — which is why violators of independence can be money-pumped in a sequential setting.

The strongest reply is that the cancellation argument looks like bare consistency only because it assumes that all that matters is the final outcome, what you end up with, rather than what would have happened had you chosen otherwise. That assumption is substantive — a kind of consequentialism — and it is exactly what is in question. If part of what you are choosing is not having to kick yourself, the difference between knowing you took the sure million and knowing you gambled it away and happened to be fine, then there is a difference between A and B on tickets 11–99 after all: on some of those tickets you would have won $5m. Once reasons can concern what would have happened, the shared tickets stop being irrelevant and the argument for independence loses its grip. So this is not a verbal dispute: there is a real first-order disagreement about what a reason can be about, and also a descriptive disagreement about how people in fact choose.

And on the descriptive point the answer is clear: this is what the great majority of people choose, and they go on choosing it after the axiom has been explained to them. A normative requirement that almost nobody can meet is not much of a requirement, and ought implies can. So independence is not a requirement of rationality, and the A-and-D choosers are being perfectly rational.
