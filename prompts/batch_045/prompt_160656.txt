You are a philosopher of language who specialises in speech acts and who has spent real time embedded in an institution that has to classify utterances under time pressure. You know the Austinian and Searlean apparatus in detail, and you also know how it degrades when non-specialists try to use it. Before writing anything, do a few paragraphs of private scratch work: list which distinctions from that apparatus could realistically survive translation into a checklist that a three-person committee can run in half an hour, and which cannot survive, and why. Then write the deliverable. Do not open with a summary of the theory, do not give me a literature review, and do not cite anything under any description — I know the literature and I will insert the references myself. What I need is the operational version.

My situation: I chair the research-integrity committee of a mid-sized scholarly publisher (twelve journals). We handle around two hundred matters a year, and almost every one of them turns, at bottom, on a question of the form *what did this person do in saying this?* — was that message a retraction or a request for one; was it an admission of misconduct or an expression of regret; did it retract consent to authorship, or merely grumble; did a funder's press release withdraw an endorsement or purport to invalidate a paper; was a statement a promise to correct the record or a statement of intention. We currently improvise, and we reliably conflate at least three things I want separated: whether an act was *performed at all* versus whether it was *performed but subsequently defeasible* versus whether it was *performed and effective but mis-classified by us in the record*. I also suspect we are conflating uptake by whoever read the message with uptake by whoever keeps the record, and we conflate an author's standing to speak for themselves with the authority to alter an institutional status.

Please write a decision procedure my committee could actually run. Concretely, I want:

1. A one-sentence statement of the principle the procedure implements, phrased so a non-philosopher committee member could repeat it back correctly.
2. A five-minute triage gate that resolves the easy majority and logs them.
3. The main procedure: ordered gates, in the order they must be asked, each with an explicit statement of what counts as a sufficient answer, where evidence is unavailable or ambiguous, and what to do then. Distinguish clearly between an utterance that is *void* (no act of the relevant kind was performed), one that is *voidable* (performed, but subject to being overridden or withdrawn), and one that is *effective but mis-recorded*. Treat separately the question whether the speaker had standing and the question whether the speaker had authority over the institutional status at issue. Distinguish hearer-uptake from record-keeper-uptake, and say which is required for which kinds of act.
4. A gate on prospective versus retrospective effect: does the act create a new institutional status going forward, or does it record a defect that already obtained? State what follows, under each answer, for citations of the paper made before the act — this is the question authors' institutions actually ask us.
5. A gate on the vehicle of the act: some force attaches to a format (a linked retraction notice, a banner on the article page, an entry in an index) rather than to the words used. Say when the format itself supplies the force and when it does not.
6. A gate separating whether an act was performed from whether it was adequate: an apology or a correction can be fully performed and still be a bad one. Mark the point at which the procedure stops classifying and hands the decision to a person, because the remaining question is one of values, proportionality, or sanction rather than of what was said.
7. A guard against over-attribution: do not attribute a force whose best paraphrase the speaker could reasonably reject. Say how the committee should handle jokes, irony, quoted or reported speech, non-native phrasing, and text the author did not themselves compose.
8. A written-record template: what we file for each case, in under a paragraph, such that it survives an author's appeal and a reporter's request.
9. A one-page field-card version to paste into the handbook.

Then run the procedure on three cases and show your working, not just the verdicts:

(a) A co-author writes in a shared thread: I can't vouch for the numbers in figure 3, take my name off it. The corresponding author replies that he'll handle it, and nothing further happens for eight months. Classify what was performed, and say what the committee should now do.

(b) An author posts on a personal blog: I'm sorry that people were upset by our paper. Tell me what act this is, whether it admits wrongdoing, whether it requires a recipient or an acceptance to be performed, and how the performance/adequacy split bears on what we record.

(c) A funder issues a press release saying it has withdrawn its support for a study and no longer regards its findings as reliable. The authors refuse to retract and the journal's own rules say that only the authors or the editor-in-chief may retract. My committee would very much like to log this as a retraction. Give me the answer your procedure actually yields, including anything it tells us we must not do — this is the case where I expect to dislike the result.

Finish by saying, without softening, what the procedure cannot do: the classes of case on which it will misfire, the steps that are judgment calls wearing the costume of a rule, and the kinds of utterance for which my committee should simply refuse to issue a classification. Aim for roughly 1400 to 1800 words.
