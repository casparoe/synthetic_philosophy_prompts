Hi. I've been reading, slowly and as a hobby (I'm a hospital pharmacist by trade, so this is all very much outside my day job), the literature that tries to get norms for what you should believe out of one idea: that a set of opinions is better the closer it sits to the truth, that you can put a number on how close, and that from there you can argue that certain ways of forming opinions are worse than others no matter how things actually turn out. I keep bumping into something I don't have a word for, and I want to know whether it has one.

Here's the toy I keep playing with. Four ways things could go, call them w1 to w4. Annie spreads her confidence evenly, 25% on each. Bella goes 40/40/10/10. Score them by adding up the squared gap between confidence and truth over the four possibilities, lower is better. If w1 or w2 is the actual one, Bella does better (0.54 against 0.75). If w3 or w4 is, Annie does better (0.75 against 1.14). So neither of them is better come what may. Now score them again over just two possibilities, "w1 or w2" and "w3 or w4". Annie is 50/50, Bella is 80/20 — nothing about either of their opinions has changed, they're the same opinions described more coarsely — and suddenly Annie wins outright: 0.5 against 0.68 whichever way it goes. The verdict "one of these people is doing worse however things turn out" versus "neither is" seems to depend entirely on how finely you slice.

Second thing, same flavour. Instead of asking who wins in each possibility, average each person's score using someone's confidence as the weights. If you use Annie's numbers as the weights, Annie comes out ahead (0.75 against 0.84 for Bella). If you use Bella's, Bella comes out ahead (0.66 against 0.75). Everybody's favourite is themselves. And it doesn't help to switch to the "must win in every possibility" version of the argument, because that's the version that flipped in the first case.

So: the verdict depends on decisions that the arguments don't seem to argue for — how fine the possibilities are, whose numbers do the averaging, and (a third variant) whether you score someone only on the claims she actually voiced or on all the claims her opinions commit her to like "A or B" and "not A". And my suspicion, which I can't quite make precise, is that the flipping isn't random: it tends to favour whoever got to set the scorecard up.

Does this have a name, or several names for what might be two different things? Please don't just hand me a label — I want to know what exactly the phenomenon is, whether my two cases are the same phenomenon or not, what it's NOT (it feels close to "any comparison can be wrecked by redescribing the options", but that can't be right either, since some redescriptions here don't change anything), whether there's a constraint on the numerical measures that makes it go away and what that constraint costs, and whether it means these arguments quietly assume part of what they're trying to show. If there's no established concept, coin one and define it, with a couple of cases that fall just outside it.
