I'm a high school senior writing a short position paper on whether neural networks really have representations, for an independent study I'm doing this term. I've read a good deal of interpretability material online and poked at some small models on my own laptop, but I have no formal training in philosophy, and before I hand the paper in I want to know whether the things I've ended up believing actually fit together. Here is the set, as I would defend it.

A. Modest realism. There is a fact of the matter about what a trained network's internal states are about, and that fact does not depend on whether any interpretability tool happens to surface it: interpretability discovers representations rather than manufacturing them. I want this to hold even in the hardest case. Call a "silent feature" a direction in activation space that (i) recurs across models trained independently on the same data, (ii) is not picked out by any linear probe trained on any labeled concept anyone has tried, and (iii) whose ablation changes nothing anybody has been able to measure on any distribution anybody has tested. My claim is that even a silent feature is determinately about something or about nothing, whether or not we ever find out which.

B. Directions, not neurons. Because a model can pack more features than it has dimensions, the representational units are directions in activation space rather than individual neurons; most neurons are mixtures, and asking what a single neuron "means" is usually asking a badly formed question.

C. Some neurons really are dedicated. In early layers, and in a handful of striking cases elsewhere, single units are cleanly and monosemantically about one thing, and I am happy to say that those neurons represent that thing.

D. Interventionism about content. A state represents X only in virtue of what would happen to the model's computation if you intervened on that state. Its causal role inside the model, not its correlation with anything outside the model, is what fixes what it is about.

E. Skepticism about dictionary features. The features a sparse autoencoder hands back are heavily shaped by the autoencoder's own setup: the dictionary size and sparsity pressure, the distribution it was trained on, the details of its optimization. So I do not think we should assume that the dictionary is the set of features the model itself uses.

F. A case verdict I want to keep. Suppose someone trains a linear classifier on activations in a model's residual stream, before the model answers, and it predicts from those activations whether the model is about to give a deceptive answer, well above chance on held-out cases. I want to say that this is evidence that the model represents its own intention to deceive.

Please audit the set rather than survey the field. I genuinely do not know where the problem is, if there is one, and I have no favorite among A through F, so tell me which commitment has to move rather than assuming I would quietly sacrifice whichever is least convenient. I would also rather you not recommend anything for me to read or cite; I just want the argument.
