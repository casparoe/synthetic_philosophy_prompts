I am co-editing a volume of short essays in which each contributor asks how a single historical thinker would respond to a policy question that could not have arisen in their lifetime, and answers by reconstructing that thinker's own commitments rather than by ventriloquising a general temperament. My assignment is Einstein and the political economy of machine learning. I have a rough answer in mind, and I want a reconstruction better than it.

The anchor is his 1949 essay on socialism, published in the first issue of *Monthly Review*. The commitments I take to be load-bearing are these: that the economic anarchy of capitalist society is the real source of the evil; that private capital tends to concentrate in few hands, partly through competition among capitalists and partly because technological development and an increasing division of labour favour larger units of production, yielding an oligarchy of private capital that a democratically organised polity cannot effectively check; that private capitalists inevitably control, directly or indirectly, the main sources of information, with the result that the individual citizen cannot come to objective conclusions or make intelligent use of political rights; that an education system oriented to careers cripples individuals and erodes creativity, and should instead develop each person's abilities together with a sense of responsibility for others; that the remedy is a socialist economy in which the means of production are owned by society and used in a planned way, sharing out the work to be done among all who are able to work and guaranteeing a livelihood to everyone; and that a planned economy is not yet socialism, because it may bring an all-powerful bureaucracy and the complete enslavement of the individual, so that the decisive question is what democratic counterweight to bureaucracy can be secured. Relevant too are his public warnings of the 1950s about armaments and about the political responsibilities of scientists. Work from these; do not supply or rely on citations.

Now the situation. Three proposals are before a large, rich democracy, and I want to know where he would land on each.

The first is a levy on the computing used to train large models, assessed on the electricity and specialised hardware consumed, with the proceeds paid out as an equal annual per-person dividend. Its advocates call it giving everyone a share of the machines.

The second is public model infrastructure: a state-financed, publicly owned capacity to build foundation models, including the weights, offered at cost, with private firms left free to build applications on top of it. Its advocates describe it as social ownership of the commanding heights of a new means of production without nationalising consumer technology.

The third is a statutory training-data levy, collected per model and distributed through collecting societies to those identifiable creators whose work entered the training corpus, defended as protecting the individual against extraction by concentrated capital.

What I want from you:

Say plainly which of these, if any, he would regard as touching the source of the evil and which as palliatives that leave the anarchy of production untouched, and whether any combination of them hangs together better than it first appears. Rank them rather than balancing them.

Then take the puzzle that interests me most. His argument about information was that capital controls the sources of information, so that citizens cannot form objective conclusions. In the present case what is privately controlled is not chiefly what gets published but what gets synthesised, weighted, and served: a mediation layer that sits between a population and the material. Is this the same evil in a new costume, or a different one that his argument as stated does not reach? Notice the difficulty: his remedy is ownership of the means of production, and it is not obvious that ownership of servers and weights engages a problem whose locus is inference rather than production. Tell me whether he would have to extend his framework here, and how.

Next, his concentration mechanism. He expected technical development and the division of labour to push toward larger units of production. Machine learning seems to do this with unusual violence, since frontier training involves enormous fixed costs and replication is nearly free. But open weights mean that a capable model now runs on a personal laptop. So the means of production in this domain are at once hyper-concentrated and radically dispersed. Would he read that as confirmation of his mechanism, as a refutation, or as a distinction his vocabulary cannot yet draw?

Then a scenario I want you to take seriously rather than wave away. Suppose the models do not produce mass unemployment. Suppose instead they progressively take over the parts of professional work that people found worth doing — the diagnosis, the argument, the proof idea, the lesson plan — while everyone remains nominally employed and, if anything, busier. Call it the hollowing-out case. In his picture, work is a burden to be shared out and reduced, and the good society guarantees a livelihood regardless. Does his framework have the resources to object to the hollowing-out case, or does it have to treat it as liberation? If he needs a concept he did not have, say what it is and how he would have had to acquire it.

Then the bureaucracy worry, which I think is where a careless reconstruction will go wrong. A publicly owned model capability whose training objectives are set by a state body is a more intimate instrument of intellectual uniformity than a board setting output quotas for steel. Given that he insisted on a democratic counterweight to bureaucracy, what safeguards would he plausibly demand here, and are those safeguards compatible with what the second proposal is trying to achieve? If you think they are not, say so and say which side he would sacrifice.

Finally, two self-applications. He thought education should serve individual development rather than careers: would automated tutors look to him like the recovery of that aim, or like its final defeat? And his own vocation is among the ones being automated: given his warnings about armaments and about scientists' responsibilities, would the greater danger in his eyes be frontier capability concentrated in a handful of firms, or absorbed into a state's military-scientific apparatus?

Two constraints on how you write. Ground every attribution in one of the commitments above or in something else he publicly stood for, and mark explicitly the points where your reconstruction turns on an interpretive choice you cannot settle from what he wrote. And do not write hagiography: where his framework is simply silent, identify the silence rather than inventing a view for him. I would also like, in closing, one paragraph on the claim most commonly made in his name that he would be least likely to endorse today, and why.

Around 1,200 to 1,500 words.
