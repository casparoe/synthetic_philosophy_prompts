I write novels — the last three have each had a mathematician somewhere in the background — and I am revising a chapter set in a café in Trieste in which a futures trader offers a client a bet and then refuses to name a price for it. The bet is one I have become slightly obsessed with: a fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads, say on toss n; if n is odd the client is paid 2^n/n, and if n is even she pays 2^n/n to the house. Every possible outcome is finite and the game ends with probability one, and yet the expected value — the sum of 1, minus a half, plus a third, minus a fourth, and so on — converges only because the terms are taken in the order in which the tosses would occur; arranged differently the same terms can be made to approach any number you please, or to diverge. I have also invented a variant I call the Trieste ledger, in which nothing about the game changes except that the croupier writes down the possible outcome-histories in an order of his own choosing rather than in chronological order, and I use it in the chapter to have my two characters disagree about what the bet is worth. In March I have agreed to give a twenty-five-minute talk on this to a room of about sixty adults at a literature festival: readers rather than students, most of whom will announce at the start that they were bad at maths at school, with no equipment beyond a flipchart.

So do not explain the paradox to me and do not write the talk; I think I follow it. Give me a plan for explaining it. I want the order in which the components have to arrive and the dependency that forces each ordering — what has to be in place before the idea of a probability-weighted average over infinitely many outcomes is even intelligible, and where the step from finite addition to infinite summation has to be made visible as a step rather than slid over. I want the one case to carry the whole twenty-five minutes, whether that should be the plain game, my Trieste ledger, or something better, together with what that case must contain and, just as importantly, what it must not contain: my instinct is that it must keep every payoff finite and the game terminating, and must give the audience no opening to start talking about the size of the casino's bankroll. I also want the misconceptions in the order they will actually form, with the exact beat at which each should be headed off rather than corrected afterwards. I can guess at several: that this is only the old lottery puzzle about infinite expectation dressed up, that the value is simply the number the natural ordering gives and the matter is closed, that the rearrangement trick means the bet has no value at all and expectation is a sham, that the real difficulty is that nobody could stage such a game, and that the moral is that calculation fails and one should trust instinct. Tell me which of these I have misjudged, which I have placed wrongly, and which I have missed.

Two further things. First, my own preferred way out of the puzzle is to say that the chronological order of the tosses is privileged, because any actual settlement of the bet has to happen in time; I want the plan to say where that suggestion belongs in the sequence, and what the audience would have to have already seen for them to recognise it as a proposal rather than as the answer. Second, since this is a room of literary readers and my chapter rather depends on the mood, I am worried about the talk collapsing into a comfortable mystification of infinity, which would flatter the novel and cheat the room; so tell me where in the arc I should name that risk out loud. Finish with one question I can put to the audience in the last two minutes whose answers would let me tell whether the idea had landed rather than merely unsettled them, and say what a right answer and a plausible wrong answer would each sound like. I intend to state openly, and then decline, three things: the proof that a conditionally convergent series can be rearranged to any limit, the formal definition of convergence, and any verdict on which theory of rational choice is correct. Tell me what else belongs on that list of refusals and at which beat to declare each one, and set the whole plan out as numbered beats with rough minute marks, in under 600 words.
