I am a retired systems physiologist. For most of my career I built models of homeostatic regulation: receptor–effector loops, controlled variables and their setpoints, disturbance rejection, feedforward compensation, slow–fast decompositions of coupled systems, and the various ways one decides where one system ends and another begins. I am fluent, not merely conversant, in that vocabulary — linear and nonlinear control theory, state-space descriptions, observers and internal models, entrainment and phase-locking, latency and gain, and the practice of treating two interacting subsystems as a single system when their interaction is fast relative to the dynamics one cares about. I have lately begun reading philosophy of mind more than casually, and I keep having the same unsatisfying reaction: the disputes look to me either empty or undecidable, and I cannot tell which. I would like to find out by translating rather than by arguing.

Please restate the extended mind thesis, as Clark and Chalmers set it out in the original 1998 paper, together with its principal supporting and opposing moves, in the vocabulary I have just described. Do it claim by claim. The claims I want translated are these:

1. The parity move: the contention that if a process outside the agent does the same job as a process inside the agent, it ought to be classified the same way, and that the boundary at skull and skin is not a principled place to stop.
2. The thought experiment of the two museum-goers, one of whom retrieves the address from biological memory and the other from a notebook he carries constantly, together with the conditions the authors attach — that the resource be reliably available, easy to consult, automatically endorsed when consulted, and endorsed in the past.
3. The objection that being causally coupled to something is not the same as being partly made of it, and that no one thinks the thermostat is part of the furnace.
4. What the thesis is a thesis about, in the end: the vehicles of cognition, the contents of mental states, the boundaries of the knowing subject, or something else again.

For each, tell me what carries over into my vocabulary without loss, what carries over only after you have quietly altered it, and what has no expression in my vocabulary at all.

I should also declare my own bias, because I suspect it is doing work I have not noticed. My instinct is to replace talk of functional sameness with a structural criterion: that an external resource counts as part of an agent's cognitive machinery just in case the loop running through it closes on a timescale comparable to the agent's own action cycle, the coupling across that loop is reciprocal rather than one-way, and the state of the resource is not itself being regulated on a slower timescale by some other controller. I would like you to say plainly whether this is a faithful translation of anything the disputants actually hold, or a new and substantive claim dressed as a translation; and if it is the latter, which assumption I have smuggled in — presumably something about the fast manifold being where the mind lives, which is my prejudice rather than their argument.

Two cases of my own, not drawn from the literature, which I would like the translated criterion to sort:

A. A sailor keeps no tide information in her head. Before every departure she reads the official table posted at the harbour mouth; she never checks it against anything, she would stake her life on it, and if the harbourmaster altered it she would be wrecked without noticing. The loop is fast, the resource is trusted and automatically endorsed — but the coupling is one-way, and its contents are set by someone else.

B. A patient wears a closed-loop insulin delivery system with continuous glucose sensing. The sensing, the computation, and the delivery are subcutaneous, reciprocal, fast relative to anything the patient does, and the device performs a regulatory function that her own pancreas no longer performs. Every structural condition in my criterion appears satisfied, and yet calling the device part of her mind strikes me as absurd.

Tell me where the translated criterion places each case, whether it places them where the philosophical literature would place them, and what follows about the translation either way. In particular: does the absurdity in case B show that my vocabulary lacks the resources to say what cognition is, or does it show that the thesis was never a claim about structure in the first place?

Finally, the residue report proper. I expect some things will not go across at all — the first-personal character of a subject's access to her own states, the difference between a state that merely appears in a model of a system and a belief someone can be credited with, held to, or held responsible for. For each item of residue, give me your judgment on whether it marks a genuine blind spot in control-theoretic description, or whether it marks a point at which the original claim was less determinate than it looked. Where you could complete the translation only by adding an assumption of your own, say what the assumption was and flag it as an addition. I would rather have an honest account of what my vocabulary cannot say than a tidy restatement that has quietly replaced the philosophers' question with one I already knew how to answer.
