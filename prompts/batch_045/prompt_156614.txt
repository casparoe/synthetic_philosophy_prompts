Me and my friend Priya got into a proper shouting match at the department social last night and now I have to write the final for my upper-level seminar on social norms (about 15 people, mostly philosophy majors with a few econ and polisci stragglers), so I've decided to just turn the argument into the exam. Here's the thing we were fighting about. Call a regularity a *lock-in convention*: everyone in population P does R in situation S; each of them, conditional on the others doing R, prefers to do R (deviating costs you — you get sanctioned, or quietly frozen out); but nearly everyone would prefer that the whole population did some alternative R* instead, with nobody sanctioning anybody; and — this is what set us off — almost nobody knows that the others would prefer R*, because each one believes the others genuinely like R. Concrete version: a research group where everyone pulls seventy-hour weeks, everyone privately wishes the whole group worked forty, and everyone thinks the others are true believers in the grind. Priya says this is a perfectly good Lewisian convention and that's fine, Lewis never promised conventions are good for anyone, and the fact that the account doesn't flinch at lock-in cases is a *virtue* — it shows the analysis is descriptive rather than celebratory. I said if lock-in counts then "it's a convention" can't be doing the explanatory work people want when they explain why a norm persists and how it ever changes, because the account gives you no grip on why this equilibrium rather than that one. Then we got onto escape: Priya thinks a small stubborn minority — she said 5% — who visibly switch to R* no matter what anyone else does will tip the whole population, because conditional expectations flip. I said Lewis's machinery by itself doesn't give you that: whether 5% is enough depends on how everyone else updates their *second-order* expectations, on whether the switch is genuinely public or just privately witnessed, and on whether the minority's behaviour even registers as a precedent rather than as noise. And I'd of thought the thing that actually dissolves a lock-in is revealing what people prefer (a survey, one credible announcement), not changing what anyone does — which is a move Lewis's behaviour-based mechanisms don't really have room for. We also rowed about normativity: I said Lewis can explain why you have a reason to conform but not why anyone is entitled to demand conformity from others or sanction them, and Priya said that gap is fillable with stuff Lewis basically already has.
