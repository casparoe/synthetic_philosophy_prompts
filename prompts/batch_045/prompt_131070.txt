I'm assembling a set of evaluation cases on Molyneux's problem: a man blind from birth, given sight as an adult, with a cube and a sphere on the table in front of him, can he tell which is which by sight alone, before touching them? Everything I can find just restates the two standard camps, so I want something sharper: one designed case that actually separates them.

Call the first camp the learned-correspondence account: nothing connects a felt feature to a seen feature except associations built up through experience, so at first sight the subject fails, and afterwards he can do exactly what that experience covered and nothing more. Call the second the amodal-geometry account: shape is one property available to both touch and sight, and the mapping from the retinal input to three-dimensional shape is fixed by the geometry of projection together with constraints the visual system already brings to bear, so a subject who knows the shapes by touch can recover the shape and match it at first sight.

What I want is a single case with two probes, same subject, same session, both before he touches anything, in which the accounts double-dissociate:

1. Probe 1: learned-correspondence predicts failure, amodal-geometry predicts success.
2. Probe 2: the reverse. Learned-correspondence predicts success, and amodal-geometry predicts failure even granting it the full retinal input and unlimited computation.
3. Probe 1 must not be rescuable by whatever pre-operative history you invent to make probe 2 work, and probe 2 must not be rescuable by anything the amodal-geometry account can extract from the stimulus.

Constraints: the scenario itself in one paragraph, realistic and ordinary (a hospital, cataract surgery, everyday objects), no science fiction and no technology beyond what real people actually use. You may give the subject any pre-operative history a real person could have had; if you give him a channel that carries visual information before the operation, whether that is residual light perception or a camera-to-tactile substitution aid he has used for years, say precisely what it carries and what it does not. No verdict may be handed to him: nobody may tell him in words which object is which, and nothing may turn on luck, on a fifty-fifty guess, or on simply stipulating that he gets it right.

After the scenario: derive each verdict from the resources of the account that gives it rather than asserting it; name the one feature of the case doing the work; show what happens to each verdict when that feature is removed, since I expect one probe to collapse and the other not; show that if the learned pairing in probe 2 were reversed, the learned-correspondence verdict would flip while the amodal-geometry verdict would not; and give the best objection available to the amodal-geometry side, which I expect to be that probe 2 changes the question rather than answering it, together with the reply you would make on the other side.

Finally, tell me honestly whether the case settles anything about the original cube-and-sphere question or only relocates it, and where exactly it now sits.
