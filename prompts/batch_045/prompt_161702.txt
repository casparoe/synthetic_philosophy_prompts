I teach a second-year undergraduate module on moral responsibility, and the first session of next term is on moral luck. I open each lecture with a short joke, and I want this one to do real work rather than merely serve as a warm-up: it should be funny on first hearing to students who have not yet met the material, and it should become more obviously structural once, later in the same hour, they have been through the standard cases — the two drivers, only one of whom kills a child; the assassin whose shot goes wide; the official who is never placed in the situation that would have revealed what he would have done.

The distinction I want the joke to turn on is one I introduce under the label counterfactual salience luck. Resultant moral luck, as standardly understood, concerns what actually happened; circumstantial luck concerns the situations into which one is placed. Counterfactual salience luck, in my usage, concerns whether the outcome one narrowly avoided is epistemically available to the people doing the blaming: how close the near miss was, whether anyone saw it, whether there is footage of it, how easy the alternative is to picture. A driver who misses a child by a metre and is never observed is, through nothing within his control, in a very different position from one whose near miss is caught on a doorbell camera and replayed on the evening news. The blame in the two cases differs, and what drives the difference is not the outcome itself but the vividness with which the outcome that did not occur is present to whoever is judging.

Please write me the joke, subject to the following constraints.

It should be a single joke of no more than about 120 words, composed in British English, and written to be read aloud at the top of a lecture: a set-up and a punchline rather than a sequence of one-liners, and nothing that requires props, voices, or elaborate delivery.

The punchline must turn on the distinction set out above. Someone who has grasped the difference between resultant luck and counterfactual salience luck should be laughing at the mechanism, not merely at the topic; swapping in a different philosophical distinction should break the joke, and a colleague who works on responsibility should find it genuinely funny rather than merely neat.

It may not rest on a pun, nor on an ambiguity in the words luck or blame, and it may not be a generic joke about philosophers, academics, or students.

It must not presuppose knowledge of, or name, any author, text, or publication: it has to land for students a week before they have read anything at all, and answering it should not require looking anything up.

The register should be dry and understated, and nothing in it should embarrass me in front of a large lecture theatre.

After the joke, add two or three sentences, in the same register and also in British English, identifying which feature of the distinction the punchline exploits and where the joke is most likely to fall flat — in particular, what a student who has misunderstood the distinction would take it to be about.
