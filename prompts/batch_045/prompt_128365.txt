My co-founder has decided — after one essay, which he will not stop talking about at every standup — that nobody is ever morally responsible for anything, and he wants it in the employee handbook: no praise, no blame, everyone gets managed and treated instead. I'm the one who has to actually write the policy, so I want to see exactly how it breaks.

Write me the handbook section, 500-600 words, as a deadpan HR memo: something like "Attitude-Neutral Workplace Policy (ANW-2)," in numbered clauses, with a To: line, a Re: line, and a cc: to the whole company. The voice is a tired HR director who genuinely intends to implement the request in good faith and keeps failing to.

The jokes should come from the actual structure of the view, not from a silly version of it:

- Nobody in the memo should mention determinism or free will. The point is that the policy doesn't rest on any such thesis — it just proposes to stop doing something, whatever the metaphysics turns out to be.
- The "objective" stance is the one we already take toward people whose agency is compromised: small children, the severely disordered, someone in the grip of something. So write the accomodation request using the vocabulary HR already reserves for those cases, and let the requester be insulted by it in a way he can't quite articulate.
- He can't have it both ways. If the manager stops blaming him, the manager also stops thanking him, trusting him, feeling let down by him, or treating his promises as anything more than predictions. Pick at least one concrete thing he actually wants — being trusted with the office keys, being credited for his work, being forgiven for the payroll thing — and show it as unavailable under his own policy.
- The stance isn't switched on at will; it's something you fall into or get talked into, and it slips. So let him opt back in without noticing: two clauses after demanding it, he is furious that nobody has thanked him for writing it.

Close on the one real cost, stated flatly with no punchline: what he's asking for isn't only blame-free, it's friendship-free.

Start with the memo itself — no preamble, no "here's a draft," nothing before it. A couple of lines after, on what you were exaggerating, is fine.
