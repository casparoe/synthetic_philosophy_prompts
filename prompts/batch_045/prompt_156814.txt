Who I am: I build and run large ensemble simulations of chaotic systems for a living. I think natively in trajectories, perturbation growth, measures on attractors, ensemble spread, and the distinction between a difference in initial state and a difference in the system's parameters or structure. I use chaos as a technical term, and I get irritated when it is used loosely.

The material to be translated. These are composites of things historians say, not quotations from anyone. Treat them as claims with content, not as slogans:

(1) The overdetermination-flavoured claim. Whatever this particular decision-maker had done, the broad outcome was coming anyway; too many independent pressures pointed the same way for one person's choice to have been the difference.

(2) The knife-edge claim. Everything turned on one small, local, easily-otherwise event; remove it and the whole subsequent sequence comes out differently.

(3) The path claim. Even where the endpoint is robust, the route to it is not. The historian's job is to explain the route, and the route is where contingency actually lives.

Part A — the translation. Restate each of the three claims in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, using terms that denote something in an actual model: initial condition versus parameter or structural change; sensitive dependence and its finite horizon; Lyapunov growth versus asymptotic measures; attractors and their basins; the measure of the set of initial conditions that land in a given coarse-grained outcome; transient versus asymptotic behaviour; shadowing; structural stability; ensembles and ergodicity; coarse-graining and the choice of observables. For each claim say which of these it turns out to be, and flag where a historian's claim is equivocal between two quite different dynamical statements. My suspicion is that claim (2) is the equivocal one: it could be a sensitivity claim about a perturbation inside a fixed regime, or a claim about a regime change, and those behave very differently. Check me.

Part B — two distinctions I want used and policed, because I think the historiography collapses them and my vocabulary keeps them apart. If either fails to do the work I think it does, say so and give me a better one.

Fragility versus leverage. Fragility is how small a perturbation at t0 has to be to change the outcome at T; leverage is how much of the variance of the coarse-grained outcome across the ensemble is attributable to that variable. These can come apart in both directions: high fragility with low leverage, where almost any tiny difference moves you to a different microstate but leaves the distribution of macro-outcomes untouched; and low fragility with high leverage, where only a rare and large intervention moves the macro-outcome at all. Tell me which of the three claims is really about which, and whether historians' talk of small causes is systematically conflating the two.

Perturbation versus regime change. An event that moves the state around inside a fixed dynamical structure, versus an event that alters the structure itself: the parameters, the effective vector field, the space of available states. Argue for which of the three claims, if any, requires the second. My hunch is that claim (1)'s many pressures pointing the same way is really a basin-of-attraction claim, while claim (2) often presents a regime change in the vocabulary of a perturbation. Check both.

One more thing to settle in this section: the old proverb about the lost nail, the lost shoe, the lame horse, the fallen rider, the lost battle and the lost kingdom is not a picture of sensitive dependence at all. It is a chain of counterfactual dependence under fixed background structure — a cascade of necessary conditions, not an amplification of a small difference. Say whether you agree, and say whether the difference matters for what historians mean by contingency.

Part C — the residue report, which is the part I actually want. What does not survive the translation? Candidates I have in mind; confirm, deny or replace them.

(i) My vocabulary is about ensembles, distributions and measures. History is n=1. There is no measure over how things could have gone that is not the modeller's own stipulation, so claims about the probability or typicality of an outcome may be untranslatable rather than merely hard.

(ii) My framework is largely time-symmetric in a way historical explanation is not: retrodiction and prediction, could have and will, are not interchangeable for the historian, and I am not sure my formalism can register that asymmetry.

(iii) Historians' contingency claims do normative and interpretive work. They underwrite blame, responsibility, and the sense that the alternatives were live for the agents who faced them. That work has no counterpart in a state-space description. Is that because the claim is really two claims bolted together, or because my framework is blind to something?

(iv) Coarse-graining. I have no canonical choice of observables, and in history the decision about what would count as the same outcome again is itself the interpretive act. So claims (1) and (3) may not be rival empirical claims about one sequence of events at all, but rival choices of description.

Part D — the verdict. Is any of the three claims simply false once translated, or only imprecise? Which is a genuine empirical claim a model could in principle adjudicate, and which is a claim about what we ought to attend to? And the bit I care about most: if my vocabulary cannot express what the historians want from contingency, is that a defect in my framework, or evidence that the word is doing several jobs at once, only some of which are about how the world evolves?
