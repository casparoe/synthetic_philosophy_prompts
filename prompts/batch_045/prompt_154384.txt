My nana has dementia and has been in a care home for about a year, and most evenings she asks whichever carer is on duty whether her husband Raymond is coming to visit. He died six years ago. The home's policy, which my mum signed off on, is that staff don't correct her — they say things like "not tonight love, maybe tomorrow." Two of my cousins got into a proper argumment about it at Sunday lunch, and somehow it turned into an argument about Augustine, because one of them did a theology module and the other one wouldn't let it go. I've forgotten most of what was said, and I don't know much Augustine beyond the basics, so I'd love you to go through it move by move. Roughly:

(1) Wes: by Augustine's definition this is straightforwardly a lie and therefore a sin. There are no permitted lies for him, not even to save a life; kindness only moves it down whatever scale he uses for how forgivable a lie is. What the carer should do is stay quiet or change the subject, which Augustine explicitly allows — you can withhold the truth, you just can't say what's false.

(2) Dana: that fails Augustine's own test. A lie is a false statement made with the will to deceive, and there's no deception here. Half the time Nana seems to know perfectly well that Raymond is dead and is asking to be told the nice version — she'll literally say "I know, just tell me he's coming" — like someone who asks you not to spoil a film. And Augustine's own view is that jokes aren't lies, because nobody is taken in and everyone can tell what's going on. Same shape here.

(3) Wes: jokes are different because in a joke nobody walks away with a false belief. Most evenings Nana does come away thinking Raymond might turn up, and the carer wants her to. And Augustine's test is about the speaker's will, not about whether the deception lands — you can lie to someone who doesn't believe you.

(4) Dana: then the test is tracking the wrong thing. Take a new carer who hasn't been told Raymond is dead and says "he'll be here in the morning," believing it. By Augustine's lights that's not a lie at all, it's just an error. But from Nana's end it's the identical event. If the criterion can't tell those two apart in the way that actually matters, the criterion is what's wrong, not the example.

(5) Wes: that's not a bug, it's the point. For Augustine the sin is in the will, not in the outcome, so an innocent mistake isn't a sin even when it does harm — that's just what a will-based ethics looks like. And on the consent bit: Augustine has no "she asked for it" clause. The truth is owed to God, not to whoever happens to be in the room, so what Nana wants doesn't enter into it.

(6) Dana: fine, last one then. What about the carer who won't say "he called," but says something true that she means Nana to hear as a yes — "the phone's been ringing off the hook all day" — and Nana does hear it that way. Not a lie on Augustine's account, presumably. But if that's allowed while "he called" isn't, then the absolute ban on lying is just a rule about sentence shapes, and the real question — whether it's ok to get someone to believe something false — is one his definition walks straight past.

(7) Wes: I think he does have resources for that. The whole point of speech for Augustine is to hand over what's in your mind, and using a true sentence as a vehicle for a false belief is a corrupt use of a sign, so he'd condemn it too, even if he wouldn't technically file it under lying. Dana: you're inventing a distinction he doesn't make.

So: who's right about which specific move, where is each of them misreading Augustine (if either is), and what are they both missing? I care less about who wins overall than about which individual claims survive contact with the actual texts.
