I'm a staff engineer at a company with about forty backend services spread across nine teams, and I've been asked to run a fifty-minute internal engineering-craft session on how technical conventions form and hold together. The reason I'm running it is a design review next week: we are about to rewrite our internal messaging layer, and the argument in the room keeps collapsing into the same shape — the retry policy is arbitrary, so let's just pick the better option and write it into the migration document. I've read a couple of summaries of David Lewis's account of convention and it looks like the right tool for what is going wrong in that room. I don't want to lecture at people about philosophy, and I don't want to put his numbered formal definition on the board, because I lose the thread in it myself and so will they. What I want from you is a plan for making the structure land with twenty-five engineers of mixed seniority, no philosophy background, one whiteboard and no slides, half of whom will arrive convinced that this is a management problem rather than a technical one.

The case I was going to build everything on: when a consumer fails to process a message, it either retries with backoff until the message goes through, or it retries a fixed number of times and then parks the message in a dead-letter queue that a human drains by hand. There is no written policy. Every team I asked does the second thing, and every team I asked assumed every other team does the second thing. It appears to have started with one incident about three years ago — a consumer stuck retrying held a malformed message against a checkpoint for nine hours — and the fix that got posted in the incident channel somehow became the way everybody does it. If everyone did the other thing instead, throughput would arguably be better and nobody would be draining anything by hand, so the current practice looks arbitrary, and that word is doing real damage in the design review.

There is a wrinkle. This is not a pure case of everyone just wanting to match: the platform team would rather each consumer team own its own dead-letter draining, and the consumer teams would rather the platform offered it as a feature. So there is a small dispute about who does the work sitting on top of the coordination problem. Tell me whether that wrinkle ruins the example, whether I should keep it and deal with it explicitly, or whether I should swap in something cleaner — say, two services that must agree on which of two functionally identical header names carries the correlation ID, which is cleanly arbitrary but invites someone to say we should just run a find-and-replace across the monorepo, and I don't know whether that objection is a problem with the example or the beginning of the lesson.

Concretely, please plan:

1. The order the pieces have to come in and what depends on what. In particular: does the definitional core — the stable regularity, the expectations each person has about everyone else, the fact that the expectations are themselves mutually visible, and the fact that some alternative regularity would have served just as well if everyone had converged on it — come before or after the case, and why? And at which point does the word arbitrary have to be repaired, given that they will hear it as costless?
2. Whether my case or the cleaner one should carry the whole session, and what the carrying example must have and must lack. My guess is that it must lack a linter, a mandate and a single owner who could flip everyone at once, and must have a real alternative that would have done as well — tell me if that's wrong or incomplete.
3. The misconceptions, and exactly where in the sequence to head each off. The ones I already expect: arbitrary means it doesn't matter, so pick the technically best option; a convention is just a habit, or just a rule nobody happened to write down; we'll announce it in the migration document and it will change; and — a fourth I only half-expect — that people will think the incident three years ago is why the policy is still there. Name the ones I'm missing.
4. One thing I can have them do or answer in the last ten minutes that would show me they've got the structure rather than the vocabulary, and how I'd tell the difference.
5. What I should openly refuse to explain in fifty minutes to this audience, and where the temptation to over-explain will be strongest for me.

Assume they are smart, allergic to theory, and will try to turn every point back into an argument about who should own what. Please don't give me a reading list or citations, and don't quote Lewis: paraphrase the conditions in whatever terms make them usable on a whiteboard.
