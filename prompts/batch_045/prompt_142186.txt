Doing my final paper for this seminar on philosophy and money (it's an early modern module, mostly Locke, Hume and Berkeley) and I've ended up on Berkeley's monetary stuff — the *Querist*, his mid-1730s pamphlet that's literally just hundreds of numbered questions, where he pushes Ireland to set up a national bank issuing paper money. I keep lossing the thread on one point and I think I need the conceptual thing explained rather than more reading.

Setup as I've got it, tell me if I've got him wrong: (1) Immaterialism — no mind-independent material substance, ordinary objects are collections of ideas, and they keep existing when no human is looking because God perceives them, nature being a kind of divine language. (2) On money he's anti-hard-money: gold and silver aren't valuable in themselves, they're just counters or tickets for transferring claims, and what matters is whether money actually sets people to work, since real wealth is industry and its fruits rather than the metal. Real versus nominal wealth.

Here's the tangle. The usual move here wants the goods and the labour to be real in a way the counter isn't. For a materialist that's free: the stuff is just there, mind-independently. For Berkeley there is no there in that sense. So what's the anchor that stops these tokens are mere counters from collapsing into everything is a counter? I can see two candidates and can't tell which one he needs:

A) opinion and coordination — money is sound to the degree that it is generally accepted and actually moves people to useful work, so wealth is a fact about what people will do.
B) the divine ledger — the goods are real because God perceives an orderly nature, so real wealth is whatever God's order underwrites, not whatever human opinion happens to converge on.

B worries me more: if the reality of the goods comes from divine perception, then hypotheticaly an island where nobody works but God perceives plenty of corn is really wealthy, which seems to make real wealth useless for the economic argument, since the whole point was whether people get fed. A worries me because it makes his attack on gold toothless: gold is coordinated opinion too, so on A the advantage of paper over specie is just that paper would be better coordinated, a policy claim dressed up as a metaphysical one.

Test case I've been using — tell me if it's rigged. Island A: everyone works, real wool and corn, tokens nobody outside accepts. Island B: heaps of gold, nobody works. Island C: a South Sea style bubble in claims on goods that will never arrive, but expectations are perfectly coordinated and trade is brisk. Berkeley wants C to be worse than A in a way that isn't just idleness is a vice. What in his metaphysics, not his morals, lets him say that — and if nothing does, is that a cost to him or just the price of immaterialism?

Walk me through how the real/nominal distinction is supposed to get its grip given (1), which of A or B (or some third thing) is doing the actual work, and where island C is cheating. Keep it under about 600 words, no citations needed, and finish with \answer{...} containing exactly one label: opinion, divine-ledger, both, or collapse.
