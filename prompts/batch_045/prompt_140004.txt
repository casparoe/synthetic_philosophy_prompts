Hey — I want a comparison of two things in the vicinity of Strawson's reactive attitudes, and I don't want the usual compatibilism-versus-incompatibilism setup, so either skip it or keep it to one sentence.

Setup first, in case you only half remember the paper. In Freedom and Resentment (1962), P. F. Strawson's idea is roughly that holding someone responsible just is being disposed to have participant reactive attitudes toward them — resentment when they wrong you, gratitude when they help, and their self-directed cousins like guilt and remorse — and that these attitudes don't get vindicated by some further fact about free will or desert. They're partly constitutive of being in a personal relationship with someone, and the search for an external rational justification of blame is a mistake. The contrast case is the objective attitude: what you take toward someone who isn't a candidate for the reactive attitudes (a small child, someone in the grip of a delusion), where you manage, treat, train or accomodate them instead of holding them to a demand. And Strawson's further claim is that you can't opt out of the participant stance wholesale for principled reasons: a world where nobody ever resented anyone wouldn't be a world where we'd discovered something about free will, it would be a world with no personal relationships in it.

The two things I want compared:

(1) Plain first-order reactive attitudes: I resent you for what you did, I'm grateful to you, I feel guilty about what I did to her.

(2) What I'm going to call meta-reactive attitudes, meaning reactive attitudes whose object is somebody's reactive attitude. Being angry with you because you feel no guilt about something shitty you did. Being ashamed of my own resentment after I find out the thing I resented you for was an accident. Resenting a friend for being grateful to someone I think has been manipulating her. Indignation on behalf of a third party at how little indignation her own family showed. Feeling guilty about feeling guilty, because the guilt is self-indulgent. Resenting someone for how quickly they forgave you.

What I actually want from you:

- Do the meta-reactive attitudes do any work that the first-order ones can't, or do they collapse into them? Strawson's own machinery says the reactive attitudes track quality of will: I resent you because what you did, or what you couldn't be bothered to do, showed something about how you regard me. If that's right, isn't an attitude just one more thing you can have that shows ill or good will — in which case type (2) is just type (1) with a slightly unusual object, and all the fuss is verbal? Argue that deflationary line, or argue against it, but pick a side.

- If we really do hold each other responsible for our reactive attitudes, then the reactive attitudes are themselves the sort of thing we make demands about. Does that smuggle normativity back in — correctness conditions of the very kind Strawson said we didn't need an external justification for? Compare two readings of what Strawson is up to: a descriptive, quasi-naturalistic story about a practice we happen to be in, versus a view that's implicitly committed to reactive attitudes being apt or inapt. Which reading handles the meta cases better, and what does the answer cost?

- The objective attitude. Strawson presents it as an exit from the participant stance, but can you be blamed for taking it — for being cold, clinical, above it, or for forgiving too fast? If yes, the exit isn't really an exit. Compare two ways of cashing it out: the objective attitude as suspension of the demand, versus the objective attitude as a different demand (be manageable, be predictable, get better). Say which fits the text better and which fits the phenomenon better.

- Iteration and regress. Guilt about guilt, resentment about resentment about resentment. If holding responsible is a matter of having an attitude, and there are attitudes about attitudes, is there a regress? If it bottoms out, where and why — is there a fixed point, some attitude that isn't itself the apt object of a meta-attitude? If it doesn't bottom out, is that a problem for the view or a feature of it?

- Finish by making the comparison concrete on one hard case of your choosing: small children, dementia, the folk category of psychopathy, someone raised inside a genuinely different moral order, or a system that is very good at sounding contrite without having any attitudes at all. Which of the two — first-order or meta — is the better diagnostic there, and why?

Please don't give me a litterature review or any citations; I want your own reasoning, including a straight answer about where you think the two are only verbally different rather than substantively different.
