Writing an essay for a reading group and I want something sharper than the usual rehash of the same two moves. Topic: do Frankfurt-style cases actually refute the principle of alternative possibilities? Take a side and defend it.

The bit I want you to actually work on is a variant I came up with that I havent seen anyone run, call it the dud device. Standard case: Jones deliberates and, for his own reasons, decides to shoot Smith; Black is monitoring him and would have forced the decision if Jones had shown any sign of going the other way, so Jones cant do otherwise and yet seems blameworthy. Dud case: identical except that, unbeknownst to everyone including Black, the intervention device has a dead battery, so if Jones had been about to decide otherwise the intervention would have silently failed and Jones would have refrained. Nothing fires in either case. Black's readiness, Jones's reasons, his deliberation, his phenomenology, what he ends up doing: all the same. The only difference is a battery.

So the pair gives you a case where the agent has alternatives and a case where he doesnt, differing in nothing but an extrinsic fact nobody knows about. If the blame judgement is the same in both, responsibility looks insensitive to alternatives even when alternatives are the only variable, which the friend of PAP will say just assumes the answer. If it comes apart, something outside Jones's actual deliberations is doing the work and you owe a story about why a dead battery partly excuses him.

So: weigh the case for and against PAP, treating this pair as the decisive test rather than a footnote, handle the flicker-of-freedom and dilemma-defence replies at whatever length they actually deserve, and say whether the view you end up with is stuck with a moral-luck consequence about blame (blame tracking a fact outside the agent's control and ken) and whether that's a real cost or no cost at all. Dont close on "the debate is finely balanced."
