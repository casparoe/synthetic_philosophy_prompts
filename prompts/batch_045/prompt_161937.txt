ok so I need help designing something, and I'm still mildly annoyed about it, which is probably why I'm writing this at 1am.

I had a long argument with a friend last night about strong longtermism — the view, roughly, that what we most ought to be doing is fixed by the very long-run effects of our actions, because the future might hold astronomically more value than everything happening right now. He thinks its bunk. His line was that its just the old wager move all over again: you multiply a probability you made up by a payoff you made up, and of course you can justify whatever you like. I kept saying no, the point isn't the invented number, it's the size of what's at stake plus the fact that we seem to be at one of those moments where things can still be steered. We went round in circles for about two hours and I think we were arguing about different things — he was attacking the epistemics, I kept defending the axiology, and neither of us noticed.

Anyway. I run a fortnightly applied ethics discussion group, about 16 undergrads, mostly philosophy but a quarter of them are econ and physics types who are comfortable with numbers. 75 minutes, a room with a whiteboard and no projector, no budget. I want one session where they run into this themselves. No lecture up front, and I'd rather they don't hear the words "longtermism", "expected value" or "existential risk" until the debrief — I want them to build the argument with their hands before anybody names it.

What I want them to feel, in order:

First, the scale thing. That a really tiny chance at an unimaginably big payoff beats a near-certain modest payoff, and not by a little. They should do that arithmetic themselves off cards or a sheet, not be told it.

Second, and this is the part I care most about, because it's where my friend actually had a point: the tiny probability isn't handed to you, you assemble it. I want them to have to construct their own number by chaining guesses — is the risk real at all; does this intervention actually move it; does the effect last; is the future that follows any good; and so on. And I want them to discover on their own that the number they end up with depends much less on how optimistic or pessimistic their individual guesses are than on how many links they decide to chain together and where they start the chain. Two tables with equally sensible guesses, one using three links and one using six, land orders of magnitude apart and rank the options differently — and everybody thinks they made a factual guess when really they made a structural choice about how to model the problem. I've been calling it the telescoping thing; whatever you call it, name it in the debrief, not before.

If you can get a third wrinkle in without bloating it: several tables are choosing between the same small menu of options, and I'd like them to hit the gap between the value of the cause and the value of their own contribution — overlap, somebody else would have done it anyway, that sort of thing. Drop it if it makes the session unwieldy.

What I need from you: rules, materials (cheap, printable, no real money — dice or cards are fine, calculators allowed), the payoff scheme, a timeline with rough minute marks, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the room onto specific bits of the argument. Also: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it — I can already see one group deciding the whole thing is rigged because another group got a bigger number — and a frank note on what a game like this cannot reproduce and that I'll have to say out loud myself. I'm guessing the big one is that we only get one history, so you can't check an expected value by running the lottery a hundred times.

Last thing: give me one sentence I can use if somebody asks "isn't this just the wager argument again?", because that's exactly what my friend asked and I fumbled it.

Keep the whole thing under about 1200 words.
