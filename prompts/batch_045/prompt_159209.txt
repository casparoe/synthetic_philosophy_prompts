I'm in a joint philosophy/economic history reading group — half of us are philosophers of science, half are people who actually work with eighteenth-century wage series and coal shipments — and we've spent about eight weeks on the Great Divergence, or rather on the Needham question in its 'why Britain and not China?' form, and we have completely stalled. Not for lack of facts: everyone at the table grants the same catalogue (coal close to where industry was, high wages relative to energy costs, Atlantic trade and colonies, guilds and property institutions, state capacity, the whole lot). What we can't agree on is what we're arguing about. One group says it's a perfectly ordinary contrastive why-question: there's an event, there's a foil, you cite the causal difference-makers and you're done, and the remaining disputes are just empirical, about which difference-maker has the bigger coefficient. Another group says the question is ill-posed, because China isn't the right kind of unit — an empire of wildly different regions at a different scale from Britain — so the contrast class is gerrymandered, and the implied counterfactual (Britain doesn't industrialise, so who does?) has no determinate answer to be right about. A third position, which is the one I keep drifting toward, is that both camps are right about different questions and the phrase is hiding a switch: sometimes it means 'why did sustained mechanised growth happen at all, and why then?', which is a question about rates and thresholds, and sometimes it means 'why here rather than there?', which is a question about a foil. I've started calling these rate-questions and foil-questions, and my diagnostic test is something I call swappability: swap the foil (why Britain and not the Netherlands? why not Japan? why not the Yangzi Delta around 1100?) and see whether a decent answer changes shape. If it does, the contrast is doing real explanatory work; if the same story about coal and wages comes back out unchanged for every foil you try, the question was never really contrastive and the debate has stalled because people keep answering a rate-question while being asked a foil-question. I floated this to my supervisor, and she said something I didn't quite follow about how a why-question's contrast class fixes which causal factors are even eligible to be cited, so that swappability might only be measuring how sensitive your favourite explanation is to a change of subject — which sounded like it might sink the whole test, but I couldn't get her to say more.

So: is my diagnosis anywhere near right, and what kind of disagreement is this actually? I want you to work from what each camp says rather than from the substantive history. Is it verbal — two groups using the same sentence to mean different things? Is it substantive but disguised, e.g. a genuine empirical question about whether particular regions within China were or weren't on a comparable trajectory, which nobody has settled and which the philosophers at the table are treating as a conceptual matter? Is it a disagreement about standards: about what counts as a good explanation, or about whether a counterfactual with an indeterminate antecedent can be explained at all? Or is it bedrock — intuitions about whether big historical questions have to be contrastive to be answerable, which neither side can argue for? And given whichever diagnosis you settle on, tell me what would count as progress here: a distinction, a change of question, a body of data, some formal result about contrastive explanation — or make the honest case that no progress should be expected and say why. Please skip a survey of the Great Divergence literature; I've read the summaries and their what stalled us. And please write in British English, and do tell me if the swappability test is junk.
