I am writing a paper on whether Augustine's absolutism about lying can be detached from his definition of a lie, and I have built an argument that seems to show it cannot. The trouble is that the argument now seems to run well past what I wanted, and I cannot identify the step that is doing the work. I would like a diagnosis.

Take as given the three claims I read him as holding:

A. A lie is an utterance in which the speaker says one thing with the voice while holding another in the heart, willing that the hearer take the utterance to express the speaker's mind. The falsity of the proposition uttered is not the essence of the matter: someone who says what is false while believing it true is not lying, only mistaken.

B. Every lie is a sin. No good consequence can turn a lie into a permissible act, and one must not lie even where an innocent life is at stake.

C. Misleading someone without asserting anything is not lying and is sometimes permitted: silence, refusal to answer, concealment, and the arrangement of things so that a pursuer forms a false belief.

The argument:

(1) By A, what makes an utterance a lie is the speaker's will to deceive; the truth-value of the proposition she utters is inessential to it.

(2) A will is individuated by what it is a will that. Two acts whose wills have the same object are, in respect of the will, the same act — and A says the will is where the wrong lies.

(3) First case. A pursuer is at the door; the fugitive is in the cellar. The pursuer has complete but inverted confidence in the speaker: he believes everything she says to be false. The only way to send him to the attic is for her to say truly that the fugitive is in the cellar, and she says it precisely in order that he form the false belief that the fugitive is not in the cellar. The object of her will: that he believe the fugitive is not in the cellar.

(4) Second case. Same door, same fugitive, a speaker with ordinary credibility, who says falsely that the fugitive is in the attic in order that the pursuer form the false belief that the fugitive is not in the cellar. The object of her will: the same proposition.

(5) By (2), the wills in (3) and (4) are the same in the only respect that, by A, is morally relevant. So (3) is a lie if (4) is.

(6) By B, then, the true statement in (3) is impermissible, and she must instead say nothing — which, since the pursuer knows she is hiding someone, costs the fugitive his life. Here the absolutism forbids the only act that saves him and permits the one that loses him, in a case where no false assertion was ever available as a means.

(7) The argument does not stop there. Third case: with the pursuer's back turned, she lights the lantern over the attic stair and leaves the cellar dark, intending that he infer the fugitive is in the attic. Her will has the same object as in (3) and (4), and she asserts nothing. By (1) and (2) the decoy is a lie; by B it is impermissible.

(8) Fourth case: she refuses to answer, foreseeing — and, stipulate, acting because — the pursuer will infer from her silence that he has the wrong house. Same object, no assertion. By the same route, her silence is a lie and impermissible.

(9) But C says the decoy and the silence are permitted. And C is not decorative: it is what makes the absolutism more than a counsel to die wherever silence fails. If the argument is sound, C is false, and the only advice left to the person at the door is to be silent while not aiming at the pursuer's error — a distinction between what is intended and what is merely foreseen.

(10) Conclusion: if the wrong of lying is a disorder of the will, and a will is specified by its object, then B and C cannot both stand. They can both stand only if the wrong is located in the assertion rather than the will — in the abuse of a faculty whose natural end is the expression of thought — which is to give up A, and with it the claim that lying is essentially a vice of the heart.

I would rather keep B and C than the will-based reading of A, but I want to know what I am paying. So: which step do you reject, and why did it look sound? If the argument is valid, which of A, B, C is least costly to give up and what else does giving it up commit me to — in particular, can a prohibition located in the assertion rather than the will still generate an absolute ban, given that the very same aim of misleading is conceded to be fine when pursued by other means? Is the intended-versus-foreseen distinction in (9) one Augustine can help himself to, or is it an anachronism? I have a vague suspicion that something shifts between (2) and (5), but I cannot say what, and I would rather you located it than confirmed my guess.

Two further checks on any repair you propose. First, the case of someone who says what she believes to be false but which is in fact true, intending to mislead: if your account holds that this is a lie, say why the failure of the deception makes no difference; if it holds that it is not, say what became of A. Second, as I understand it, Augustine handles the Scriptural deceivers — the midwives, Rahab — by saying that what was rewarded was the mercy and not the deceit, so that the act can be partly good while the lie in it remains a sin. Tell me whether that move survives the diagnosis you give, or whether it is the first thing that has to go.
