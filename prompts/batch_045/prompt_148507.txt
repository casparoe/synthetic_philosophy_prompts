my friend and i have a dumb running bet — loser buys dinner at a place of the winner's choosing, so the stakes are genuinely real — about whether P = NP would actually matter. He says yes obviously, industrial-revolution-scale within a couple decades. I'm at about 0.25, he's at about 0.85, and I want to know where that disagreement actually lives rather than just who wins.

To make it precise, here's the hypothetical, and I want you to grant it rather than argue with the setup: in 2027 someone posts a correct proof that P = NP, and it's constructive — an explicit deterministic algorithm for k-SAT running in something like 10^6 · n^4 on n-variable instances. No funny business about galactic constants, no non-constructive existence proof, no oracle weirdness. Grant too that it gets verified, and that working implementations are widely available within two years.

The question: what's your credence that by 2047 this has produced changes in the world that a historian would describe as comparable in scale to the industrial revolution — or to transistors, or whatever yardstick you prefer; pick one and say which.

But don't just hand me a number, decompose it. Here's roughly how I think about it; use my decomposition or replace it with a better one:

- How much of real scientific and economic value is actually bottlenecked by combinatorial search over a space where we already have a cheap, reliable, well-specified objective for scoring candidates? My worry is that in most of the places people point to — drug design, materials, compiler optimization, chip layout, logistics — we either don't have a good objective at all (our scoring functions are proxies, and hand a perfect optimizer a proxy and you don't get what you wanted, you get the fastest possible Goodharting) or the binding constraint is wet-lab iteration, fabrication, regulation, trials, data collection, or just physical time.
- Granted a good objective, how often are the instances we care about ones that existing solvers and heuristics already handle fine? My sense is that modern SAT solvers already crush a large fraction of real-world instances, and the genuinely hard ones are things like random instances near the clause-to-variable threshold or crypto-shaped ones — so is the marginal gain mostly "we can now do adversarially constructed instances," which is a big deal (public-key crypto more or less dies) but maybe not an industrial revolution on its own?
- Whether mathematical and scientific discovery is bottlenecked by search at all. I think this is my friend's strongest argument and it isn't really about optimization: if finding a proof is search and search becomes cheap, the thing mathematicians do gets mechanized, and that does feel different in kind. How much weight does that carry, and does it depend on whether the relevant search space for the theorems people actually care about is polynomial-sized in any useful sense?
- Knock-on dynamics: does better optimization compound — better optimizers designing better hardware designing better optimizers — and how fast does that loop close relative to a twenty-year window?

Give me a number for each sub-question, one line of justification each, say how they combine into your headline number, and tell me which sub-question the final number is most sensitive to. Then tell me which of the two of us is more likely wrong about which sub-question.

Two more things. First, if you think the whole setup rests on a confusion — e.g. that worst-case asymptotic complexity was never the philosophically interesting notion and the interesting question was always about typical-case or resource-bounded tractability in the world we actually live in — say that plainly, but I still want a number for the version I asked. Second: does your answer change much if what we get instead is only a proof that the polynomial hierarchy collapses to the second level, or "merely" a much better exact algorithm like 1.3^n? I ask because I'm not sure how much of my 0.25 is really about P = NP specifically rather than about the general claim that faster search isn't the binding constraint, and I'd like to know whether those two come apart for you.
