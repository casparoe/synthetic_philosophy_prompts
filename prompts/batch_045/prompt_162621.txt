I am writing a paper for a literature and moral philosophy seminar and want to work out whether Kazuo Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go can be read as an extended dramatization of the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism, or whether that framing flatters both the novel and the objection. I would like a reading, not a summary, and I would like it to be willing to say where the fit is merely apparent.

Here is the objection as I want to work with it, both because I need the version pinned down and because it is often run together with neighbouring complaints it should be kept apart from. The core claim is that a moral theory goes wrong when it treats the goods and ills of distinct people as if they belonged to a single subject, so that losses to one can be offset by gains to another and no one in particular needs to be the person for whom the outcome is better. Standardly this is what is meant by the charge that utilitarianism fails to take the distinction between persons seriously, and it is usually coupled with the further thought that the relevant measure is not aggregate welfare but something like each person's share, or each person's inviolability, or the agent-relative standing of each person's own point of view. It should be distinguished from three things it is easily confused with: an objection from rights or consent (that using someone without their permission is wrong whatever the sum); an objection from integrity or demandingness (that the theory asks too much of agents); and an objection from fairness in distribution (that the benefits and burdens are spread unequally). I want to know which of these the novel actually needs, because I suspect the case is usually made to look like a pure anti-aggregation case when in fact the text supplies plenty of material for the others.

The novel looks almost too convenient for the reading, and that convenience is itself what I want interrogated. A state-sanctioned programme raises clones in schools like Hailsham for the sole purpose of harvesting their organs, in a short series of donations ending in what the book calls completion; its defenders, we are told, treat the resulting cures as worth the cost; and each clone's body is literally dismembered and its parts installed in someone else. That last feature is the one I am most suspicious of. The literal transfer of organs gives the objection a vividness argument does not have, but it may also trade on a confusion: an aggregative morality need not move anyone's organs, and the person who objects to aggregation on principle can in principle accept consensual donation. So part of what I want is an account of what the novel's literalization achieves and what it smuggles in.

I would like the reading to work through a few scenes in particular: the rumour of deferrals for couples who can prove they are in love, and the discovery that it was never true; Miss Emily and Madame's later explanation of Hailsham to Kathy and Tommy, in which the school and the gallery of the children's artwork are presented as a kind of amends; Madame's revulsion when the students crowd around her; Kathy's work as a carer, moving from donor to donor; and the closing stretch in which Ruth tries to make reparations by giving Kathy and Tommy the address that might buy them time, and Kathy and Tommy are left on the marsh after it fails. I do not need quotation, and I do not need the reading to be exhaustive; I want these scenes used as evidence for what the novel thinks the wrong is.

Two theoretical complications should be brought to bear, since I think they decide whether the reading is worth making. First, the ex ante and ex post versions of the objection diverge, and I want to know which one the novel indicts. Utilitarians have replied to the Rawlsian line by arguing that when the choice is made under uncertainty about whose life one will have, maximizing expected utility is itself the way of giving each person's prospects equal weight, so that the anti-aggregation intuition may look like an artifact of evaluating outcomes after the identities are settled; the reply has in turn been resisted on the ground that fairness to persons is not the same as efficiency for their prospects. The novel is unusually well placed to test this, because Hailsham is explicitly presented as better than the alternatives for the children who are raised there, and so could be defended as the arrangement that is best for each of them prospectively. If the programme's wrongness is only ex post, then the novel is doing something the philosophical argument is sometimes accused of failing to do. If it depends on an ex ante unfairness, I want to know what that unfairness is supposed to be. Second, I want the objection decomposed. The claim that distinct persons' goods cannot be summed is not the same as the claim that no person is substitutable or replaceable, and it is not the same as the claim that compensation cannot repair a use, even when the compensation is large and is given to the very person who was used. Those are separable, and my suspicion is that the novel's real energy goes into the second and third — into the fungibility of the clones, and into Hailsham's kindness as something that looks like hush money — rather than into aggregation proper.

There is a further angle I would like considered, and it is the one I am least sure about. The separateness of persons is a claim about how value may be aggregated across subjects; but the donation schedule parcels a single life into stages, and the horror attaches, among other things, to a life being cut into measured yields rather than lived as a whole. There is a structural analogue here, about whether the good of one person over time is just the sum of its temporal parts. I do not want this overclaimed, but I would like to know whether the novel can be read as staging a worry about the unit over which value is aggregated, rather than about the number of persons involved, and whether that is a real philosophical point or a metaphor the text invites.

Finally, the honest part. I want the reading to tell me where it strains: where the novel is about mortality and love and the ordinary human condition of ending, rather than about justice; where the clones' quiet acceptance is a failure of the book's moral imagination rather than an insight into it; and whether the first-person narration is itself the novel's counter-argument — a voice insisting on the value of what happened to this one person in particular, in a world arranged so that no one occupies the standpoint from which that insistence could be made. And then, if the reading survives all of that, what it adds: what the novel shows about the objection that the arguments do not, whether that is a new case, a phenomenology of being fungible, a claim about the fragility of the standpoint the objection requires, or something else.
