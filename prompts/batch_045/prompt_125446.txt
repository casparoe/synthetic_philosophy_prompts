I'm drafting the moral-status section of a cause-prioritization report for a funder whose portfolio spans invertebrate welfare and work on artificial systems, and I've been asked to write down, in one place, the commitments the portfolio actually rests on. I had assumed they were mutually reinforcing parts of a single more-or-less picture. But every time I try to turn them into an actual formula I get numbers I don't believe, and I can't tell which commitment is responsible. I'd be grateful for an audit rather than a lecture.

Here is the set.

1. What matters is valenced experience. Nociception, learning, flexible behaviour, and internal representation of the environment are not enough on their own; a system that registers damage and adjusts without anything feeling like anything to it has nothing at stake. And sentience, so understood, is a matter of degree: there is no binary property that a creature either has or lacks, and the apparent sharpness of the line in our own case is an artifact of having one word for a cluster of capacities that vary continuously across animals.

2. Moral status is scalar. Nothing morally important turns on crossing a threshold; the difference between a human and a mouse is one of more and less, not of kind.

3. A creature's status scales with what I'll call its welfare range: the intensity and qualitative spread of the valenced states it can occupy. It does not scale with cognitive sophistication, species membership, self-recognition, ecological role, or how much we like it.

4. Thermostats have exactly zero moral status. Not an amount too small to be worth acting on, but none at all.

5. Insects are probably sentient, and because they are so numerous, expected insect suffering is plausibly the largest single source of suffering on Earth. Invertebrate welfare therefore deserves a large share of the portfolio.

6. Uncertainty about sentience should be handled by expected value: a creature's effective moral weight is the probability that it is sentient, multiplied by its welfare range, multiplied by how much experience it has.

7. That said, I don't want to be a fanatic. I reject Pascalian reasoning, and I don't think a sufficiently small probability of a sufficiently large good should be allowed to dominate a decision.

8. There is an asymmetry: a given amount of suffering matters more than the same amount of enjoyment, so preventing suffering is more urgent than producing enjoyment.

9. The number of subjects is not itself a matter of degree. What varies continuously is how richly or intensely a subject experiences; whether there is a subject there at all is determinate.

10. Diachronic unity is not required for mattering. A creature with no conception of itself as persisting and no memory linking its moments can still be a subject whose experience matters.

11. A life's welfare is the additive total of the valenced moments in it, and death is bad primarily because of what it deprives the subject of.

Two constraints on any revision. First, I don't want to build the zero point of the scale out of anything essentially biological, since the framework is supposed to have something to say about artificial systems. Second, I'd rather not give up the gradations in (1) and (2): that the picture is continuous rather than binary is the whole point of the exercise.

What I want is this. Tell me which pairs actually pull against each other and which only look like they do, ordered by how much damage each real tension does to the report's conclusions. For each real tension, say what would have to give and what giving it would cost me elsewhere in the list. If there are places where two commitments are formally consistent but their usual motivations point in opposite directions, I want to know that too. And if the list fails to determine a quantity I need in order to do the arithmetic at all — the number of subjects, the dimension along which sentience is supposed to come in degrees, how much experience fits into a second, what it would even mean for a creature to be 0.4 sentient — say so plainly rather than smoothing it over. If a single revision would resolve more of the trouble than the others, tell me which one it is.
