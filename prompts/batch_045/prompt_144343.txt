On Saturday I'm giving the speech at the last-ever opening day of the branch library on Marlowe Road — it's closing after sixty years, everything's being rehoused in the new place a mile away, which is honestly handier for most people, so I can't pretend this is about convenience. About sixty in the room: neighbours, the two librarians (being redeployed, not thrilled), the council leader, who has been going around saying 'a library is a service, not a building', and my old MPhil supervisor, who works on the extended mind literature and will wince if I overclaim.

The thing I want the speech to get at is this. You'll know the classic notebook case: someone whose memory is failing relies on a notebook that's always there, easy to get at, and trusted without checking, and the claim is that the notebook isn't a tool he uses but literally part of his mind. But those conditions were obviously written for one person's pocket notebook. A branch library is shared, fixed in place, owned by nobody, and you can't automatically endorse a building. So I don't want a speech that just announces that the library was part of our minds — that's too easy, and I'd definately get an email about it.

And I can't use the council leader's line either, because the whole point is that the service isn't some disembodied thing that can be picked up and moved intact. What's lost is at the level of what the place let people do together, not a matter of anybody's beliefs getting deleted. A shared, durable scaffold matters partly because it outlasts any one person's trust in it — the handwritten index in the shoebox by the counter was kept by a volunteer called Maureen, who died in 2019, and its being pulped, not digitised.

So write me a five-minute speech: warm, no lecturing, that says what's actually lost without claiming our minds are being dismantled and without letting 'it's a service, not a building' stand. My supervisor should find it defensible, the librarians shouldn't feel patronised, nobody should feel preached at.
