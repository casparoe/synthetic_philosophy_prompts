Hey — quick one. I'm prepping next week's session for a first-year seminar I teach (it's called something like "The Past and Its Uses," mix of lit and philosophy majors) and we're doing Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day. I want to read the whole book through Nietzsche's early essay on history — the one with the three modes, monumental, antiquarian and critical — and I've been arguing with a friend who teaches European history about whether that's a genuinely good lens or whether I'm just stapling a nineteenth-century German polemic onto an English butler novel and hoping something sparks.

Here's what I'd like, roughly. Give me a reading of Stevens in terms of the three modes, but in the weeds rather than at the level of "antiquarian = reverence for the past." A few things I want you to actually work with: what Nietzsche says about antiquarian history degenerating when it can no longer tell the great from the merely old and ends up preserving everything with the same damp piety, which I think is exactly the register of the silver, the pantry inventory, the fussing over placement at table; where the monumental mode operates in the book, since the thing Stevens is loyal to isn't a series of dead great men but a standard of "dignity" in his profession that he can only have met in a couple of glimpsed exemplars, and Nietzsche's own worry about monumental history is that it works by an analogy that is partly a fiction and mostly an effect; and then the hard one — whether Stevens ever gets anywhere near critical history, the mode Nietzsche associates with someone who suffers and needs to put the past on trial in order to be able to live at all. My sense is that the book's whole motion is toward that and then away from it again in the last few pages, and I don't know whether to call that cowardice, mercy, or the most a person so constituted could manage. Also, if it's useful, Nietzsche has that notion of a culture's or a person's capacity to take up what is past and foreign, transform it, and heal its wounds — I think the German is something like "plastic power" — and the paired idea that you sometimes have to be able to forget, to be unjust to the past, in order to act at all. That seems to me to be the place where the novel is doing its own thinking rather than illustrating Nietzsche: it's a study of someone whose capacity to do exactly that has atrophied, and the question is whether the book treats that as a moral failure or as a cost of the kind of conscientiousness it also admires. My friend also floated a fourth mode she calls "curatorial history" — the past kept in impeccable order with neither reverence nor use, an archive nobody consults — which she thinks is what Stevens's letters and his professional self-improvement actually are. Argue with her if you like, or propose a better fourth category; there's also whatever it is he's doing when he uses the past neither to preserve it nor to judge it but to keep a particular story about himself intact, which maybe deserves its own name.

Concretely: about 700–900 words, plus a short list of the three or four moments you'd actually put on the board (the dismissal of the two housemaids, Miss Kenton weeping behind a door and Stevens declining to ask why, his conversation on the pier at the end, whatever else you'd pick), and a line or two on whether it's worth showing the Merchant Ivory film clip for any of them — I think the movie flattens the ending but the students will have seen it. Please mark where you're confident about what Nietzsche is up to and where you're extrapolating, because the essay is notoriously loose: is the threefold schema even meant to be exhaustive, is there a "suprahistorical" standpoint in there that is a fourth thing or just him being skeptical about the whole enterprise, and is critical history really a mode of history or just what happens when life turns on the past?
