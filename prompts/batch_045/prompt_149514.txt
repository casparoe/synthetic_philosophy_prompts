I run a small Sunday-evening reading group — five of us, none with any philosophy training — that picks a topic and works through it on our own. This term it has been whether what we believe can reach into perception. I write the questions and mark the answers, which is the part I feel least able to do well, so I would like a careful second opinion on one answer before I hand it back.

The question was as follows (one of two, twenty marks, twenty minutes):

Question 2. We have been trying to keep two claims apart:

(A) Cognitive penetration: what a person believes, expects or wants changes the content of the experience itself — the person literally sees something different.

(B) Mere top-down influence: belief and expectation change where someone looks, what they attend to, or what they say and how they describe things, while the experience stays the same.

We also looked at two reasons the difference is supposed to matter:

(i) the argument from the science of vision — if experience shifts with whatever a subject happens to know, background knowledge can never be held fixed, and there is no stable object for a psychology of perception to study;

(ii) the argument from the tribunal — perception is where we go to settle a dispute between two beliefs, and it can do that only if it is not itself shaped by the beliefs in dispute.

Case 1. A very experienced birdwatcher is told, if the warbler is in this picture at all, it is in the left third. She finds it in well under a second. Told nothing, she takes several seconds and often misses it.

Case 2. Mara has played a competitive game for six years in which hostile targets are outlined in a particular orange. Now orange regions in ordinary scenes look to her as though they stand forward and are slightly nearer than equally distant regions of other colours. It happens in photographs too, where she knows there are no targets, and a component of her visual response appears about ninety milliseconds after the picture is shown.

(a) Say, for each case, which side of the A/B line it belongs on, and what further evidence would decide it.

(b) If penetration does occur, does it follow that the experience is bad evidence, or that perception can no longer settle disputes between beliefs? Answer using (i) and (ii).

This is the answer I have in front of me:

Case 1 is straightforward cognitive penetration. The birdwatcher's new belief — that the bird is on the left — reaches into vision and changes what she sees: where before there was only leaf, there is now a warbler. This is what the old New Look work on perception was getting at, and it is the clearer of our two cases, because we know exactly what changed her (the instruction), and we can see the result in her performance. Nothing further is needed.

Case 2 is the harder case, and I do not think it can be settled from what we are told. Mara's experience differs from ours and from what hers used to be, so this is not merely a matter of what she is willing to say: she reports the effect even in photographs, where she knows there are no targets, which rules out an explanation at the level of judgement or report. Against that, ninety milliseconds is early but only evidence, not proof; and the persistence in photographs cuts both ways, since a fixed recalibration of early vision built up over six years of play would also survive her knowing better, and that would be a change in how her visual system is tuned rather than a belief getting into perception on the spot. To decide it we would want to know whether the effect tracks what the orange outline meant to her or only its low-level statistics, and whether it transfers to new shades.

(b) This is where (i) and (ii) come apart, and I think the answer is no. (i) is a worry for psychologists, about whether there is a stable object to study; (ii) is the philosophical worry, and it is the encapsulation theorist's worry in the end: if a belief helps produce the experience, the experience cannot be used to check the belief, because the check would be circular. But that does mean such an experience stops being evidence, or at least becomes unreliable — if your expectation helped make the experience, the experience cannot tell you whether the expectation was right. So penetrated perception would be poor evidence, which is why (ii) is the stronger of the two arguments.

Please mark it against the twenty marks on offer: give a mark out of 20, and then comments keyed to particular sentences or claims, saying what the answer gets right, where a step fails or two things get run together, and what a stronger answer would have said instead. I have deliberately not formed a view on it myself, so please do not read any opinion of mine into the question or the answer.
