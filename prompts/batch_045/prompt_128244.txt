The question is whether 'better than' is transitive, and it seems to me to have been argued about in the same shape for decades without anyone visibly moving. The engine of the debate is the spectrum argument: one constructs a long sequence of outcomes O1 … On such that each successor looks better than its predecessor — typically because it is reached by a change that improves a few lives a great deal while worsening many lives by a barely perceptible amount, or by adding people whose lives are barely worth adding — while nevertheless On looks worse than O1. One camp treats transitivity as non-negotiable: it is either part of the logic of comparatives, or it is forced by the cycle argument, since a betterness relation that permits cycles makes agents exploitable — you can be walked around the sequence, paying at each step for an improvement, ending up worse off than you began. So, they conclude, the sequence shows that at least one of the pairwise verdicts was never really a verdict about all-things-considered betterness, and the right response is to go back and revise. The other camp treats the verdicts as data: each adjacent comparison is independently compelling, the endpoint comparison is independently compelling, and what follows is a discovery — all-things-considered betterness is not transitive, because value is essentially comparative or because aggregation across persons is not an ordering. If that leaves us with no guaranteed non-exploitable choice rule, that is a fact about our predicament rather than a reductio: exploitability is a complaint about choice rules, not about axiology.

My own hypothesis is that the dispute is really about the functional role of 'better than': whether it is answerable to a choice-guiding role, in which case cycle arguments and representation theorems supply the right constraints, or whether it is a purely axiological relation whose only job is to rank outcomes, in which case they do not. But two alternatives I cannot rule out are, first, that 'better than' is covertly ternary — better relative to a standard or comparison class, roughly as 'tall' is — so that each side is describing a different relation and neither is wrong; and second, that the disagreement is really in the epistemology of value, about whether the existence of a cycle gives one any higher-order reason to go back and revise a first-order pairwise verdict, which would be a question about the structure of axiological reasons rather than about betterness.

Which of these is it, or is it something else? Support your diagnosis with what each side actually says.
