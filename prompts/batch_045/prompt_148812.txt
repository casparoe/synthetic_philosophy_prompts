I am writing a paper on how norms get started and how they persist, and I keep running into a sentence that is doing far too much work in discussion: "conventions are arbitrary." I met it most recently in a reading group on social normativity, where it was used twice inside ten minutes, and on both occasions to close a question off rather than to answer it.

The first use: we were discussing a regularity that solves a recurrent coordination problem but which, judged on its own terms, distributes its burdens unfairly across subgroups. Someone said that because conventions are arbitrary, there is nothing here to criticise — by the nature of the case some other regularity would have done the job just as well, so the complaint has no object.

The second use: someone else argued that if a regularity is arbitrary, then the only thing holding it in place is each person's expectation that the others will keep to it; so a sufficiently public shift in expectations ought to dissolve it, and if it fails to dissolve, that shows it was never really a convention at all but something with genuine content behind it.

The background both speakers were gesturing at is the familiar treatment of conventions as regularities in behaviour that are equilibria of recurring coordination problems, where part of what makes a regularity a convention is supposed to be that an alternative regularity could have served instead. What I want to know is what the word "arbitrary" is contributing.

Please give me a menu of the distinct precise theses that sentence could be expressing — a disambiguation, not a single favoured reading with commentary. I suggest separating at least three dimensions:

1. What is claimed to be arbitrary: the content of the regularity; the fact that this regularity rather than an alternative got selected; each agent's reason for complying; the regularity's causal history; or its susceptibility to deliberate replacement.
2. The sense of "arbitrary": substitutable by an alternative that would do as well; unsupported by any independent reason; capricious or unchosen; morally inert; epistemically ungrounded; or simply "up to us."
3. The intended scope: all conventions whatsoever, or only pure coordination cases as opposed to ones where the options also differ in independent payoff; and conventions that accreted gradually versus ones laid down by someone in a position to decree.

For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive claim that survives scrutiny, or straightforwardly false — and, which is the part I actually need, whether it would support either of the two moves described above. My suspicion is that one reading may support the first move only by making it uninteresting, and that no reading really supports the second, but I cannot get the argument into focus.

I would also like two cases used as probes. (a) In a shared laboratory everyone uses the left-hand microscope because the lamp in the right-hand one has been broken for two years; people still call this "the lab convention," although no alternative regularity would currently serve. (b) In a corridor everyone keeps to the left, although the right-hand side is the side with the fire exit and is for that reason mildly safer. Does each candidate reading count these as conventions, and where it does not, is that a consideration against the reading or merely an oddity of the example?

Finally, I have been toying with a three-way partition of my own — content-arbitrariness, selection-arbitrariness, and revisability — and I would like a verdict on whether it cuts at the joints. In particular I worry that it may conflate what a convention could have been with what it could now become, given that its history has made some of the alternatives unreachable in practice.

Please write in British English. A table of the readings with a verdict and a note on which move each would support, followed by short separate discussions of the two moves, the two probes, and my proposed partition, would be the most useful shape for the answer.
