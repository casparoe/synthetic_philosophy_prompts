I'm finishing a dissertation in political theory on early modern arguments about authority, and I also sit on the committee at my university that triages our response to legally binding directives we take to be wrong — state demands for syllabi, subpoenas for student organization records, federal reporting mandates with clauses we object to. We meet weekly, we have days rather than months, the other members are lawyers, librarians and faculty with no philosophy training, and whatever we produce has to be signable by the general counsel in under two pages. I want to stop improvising.

The material I want the procedure built out of is Pascal's treatment of justice and force in the Pensées: justice without force is powerless and force without justice tyrannical, so what is strong must be made just or what is just made strong; laws are obeyed because they are established rather than because they are just; and it is dangerous to tell people that the laws are unjust, since belief in their justice is the mechanism of obedience. Two things I think follow from this and have not seen spelled out anywhere. First, the decision has to be split into a first-order question and a second-order one, the second-order question being what our response does to the standing of a legal order we have independent reason to value — and I suspect that question should dominate. Second, because authority works by being taken to be legitimate, the publicity of a refusal should matter more than the magnitude of the injustice, so that loud defiance and quiet non-compliance have to be treated as different kinds of act rather than as more and less of the same thing.

Turn this into a procedure my committee can actually run: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in comply or in refuse, where it hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than fact, and where it should say plainly that the Pascalian framing gives no guidance at all. Then run it on three cases: a subpoena for a student group's membership list; a state requirement that syllabi and reading lists be posted for public inspection; and a federal reporting mandate containing a definitional clause the committee regards as straightforwardly unjust — the last is the one I most want to defy and expect the procedure to tell me not to. Finally, say how confident you are in each stage of what you propose and single out the step you are least sure of.
