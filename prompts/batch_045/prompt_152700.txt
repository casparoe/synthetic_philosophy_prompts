So me and my friend got into a two-hour argument about federalism last night and I'm still annoyed. The bit we kept going in circles on: the federal government isn't allowed to just order a state legislature to enact something, or order state police officers to carry out a federal program, because that's supposedly an insult to state sovereignty. But it is allowed to hand states a big pile of money on the condition that they do exactly those things, and that's totally fine. So the rule seems to be: you can't command a state, but you can buy one. What's the actual difference supposed to be? My friend says it's about coercion vs. offers, but that just pushes the question back: what is it about a state that turns an offer it can't realistically refuse into something like a command? And here's the bit that broke us: imagine the feds have been sending a state this money for forty years, the state has built its whole budget around it, and now a new condition shows up attached to it. Or the money is for roads while the condition is about the state's criminal procedure, which has nothing to do with roads. What picture of a state does the whole setup presuppose — an agent with a will that can be overborne, a protected sphere of jurisdiction, or just a convenient administrative unit? And if the answer is sovereignty, why is buying allowed when ordering isn't?
