I'm on the grants committee of a small family foundation and I need some help before Thursday, because I've been going in circles on this for two weeks and I still don't know what I think. We have to pick one of six programs. They all cost the same and they all run for about sixty years. Our staff built a table: for each program they estimate (a) how many people will be alive in the region over that period who owe their existence, or at least their level of wellbeing, to the program, (b) the average quality of life of those people on a 0–100 index their team put together, where 0 is "a life nobody would choose to start" and 50 is "clearly worth living but nothing special," and (c) the sum of the two, i.e. the total. Program 1: 1,000 people at an average of 90. Program 2: 1,500 at 80. Program 3: 2,200 at 70. Program 4: 3,200 at 60. Program 5: 5,000 at 50. Program 6: 9,000 at 35. The totals rise at every step and the averages fall at every step, and mechanically each program works by taking the previous one, adding a lot of people through maternal-health and housing work, and then spreading the money a bit more thinly — so the people who were already there end up somewhat worse off than they would have been under the program before it.

One member, Marco, says the choice is obvious and we should go straight to 6: every step adds real happiness that wouldn't otherwise exist and subtracts less than it adds, so each program is an improvement on the one before it, and if I disagree I have to name the exact step where it goes wrong and say what's different about that step compared to its neighbours. I can't do that, because any line I draw between two adjacent programs looks totally arbitrary to me. Then Priya makes a slightly different point about the extra people: the ones who exist at step 6 wouldn't have existed at all under program 1, so nobody is being traded away for anybody else, and once they're here they'll be glad to be alive and would vote to keep the program going. But I still can't get myself to say that program 6 is a better outcome than program 1, which is a thousand people living genuinely excellent lives. And if I go by the averages instead, program 1 wins — except someone pointed out that on that logic the best possible outcome would be a single person living a perfect life, which is crazy too. There's one more wrinkle: the 35 in program 6 is a mean with a wide spread, so a real number of those lives would land below zero, meaning not worth living at all. Maybe that's my reason to stop earlier? Except I don't think it is, because if the staff came back and said the spread was tiny I'd feel exactly the same way, and I don't want to hide behind a statistics complaint.

So: is there a principled way to think about this that isn't just "add everything up" or "take the average"? If the principled thing is to refuse one of the steps, which one, and what makes that step different from the ones around it? And practically — what do I actually say at the meeting, because Marco is definately going to ask me which step I'm rejecting and I need something better than "the end result feels bad." I should say the numbers are soft and i'm not wedded to them, so if the right way to think about it doesn't turn on the exact figures, that's even better.
