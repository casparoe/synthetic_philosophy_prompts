I'm writing a short essay for a collection on chance and fate in contemporary American film, and I want to give the coin scenes in the Coen brothers' *No Country for Old Men* (adapted from Cormac McCarthy's novel) real philosophical weight, rather than let them stand as decorative fatalism. I'd like a close reading of those scenes, and of the film's ending, through the philosophy of objective chance — specifically through Lewis's Principal Principle and the problem of admissibility.

Here is the doctrine as I understand it, so that we are working from the same formulation. Let X say that the objective chance, at the moment of the toss, of heads is one half; and let E be whatever else the agent knows, provided it is admissible — roughly, facts about how things stand up to and including the moment of the toss, the chances themselves among them, but nothing about how the toss actually comes out and nothing about later times. Then reasonable credence in heads, conditional on X and E, is one half. Credence defers to chance; it does not take evidence about outcomes into account twice.

The material I want read:

(i) At the desert gas station, Chigurh makes the proprietor call a coin. The man has no evidence about the toss itself. What he has is the knowledge that calling wrong means death. He calls, he lives, and Chigurh leaves him the coin, treating it as lucky and telling him not to spend it.

(ii) Near the end, Chigurh offers Carla Jean the same coin and she refuses to call. As I remember it, her reason is that the coin has no say in it, that it was always Chigurh. He kills her regardless, walks out, and is then struck by a car at an intersection and left injured, wandering off out of the story.

Questions I want taken seriously rather than gestured at:

1. What is Chigurh's own epistemology of chance? He presents himself as deferring to the coin, as though the coin and not he were the decider and as though its verdict bound him — he treats the coin as having a history and a destination. Can that posture be reconstructed as a coherent one on the standard view, and if so, what would he be deferring to: the toss, the procedure, his own code? Or is his talk of chance pure mystification of a decision already made? Which reading does the film's own evidence favor?

2. Carla Jean's refusal. On one reconstruction she is simply denying that there are any chances here to defer to: the outcome is fixed by a will, the chances are one and zero, and the Principal Principle has nothing to say. On another she is doing something subtler — treating the coin's history of outcomes as bearing on the chance-making basis itself, the way a Humean, best-system account of chance permits sufficiently rich information about the pattern of outcomes to shift the chances rather than merely be weighted by them. That is the shape of the familiar undermining worry about the Principal Principle. Is that the right frame for her refusal, or does it over-intellectualize a woman who simply sees that Chigurh is going to kill her? Say which, and say what hangs on the difference.

3. The proprietor, and the gap between credence and action. His evidence that a wrong call means death is admissible — it says nothing about the toss — so the Principal Principle still returns one half. The doctrine that governs what he should believe is therefore silent about the thing the scene is about. Is that a defect in the scene's philosophical interest, or is the scene exactly right, and the point is that deference to chance is a norm on belief and nothing more, with the drama belonging to decision theory and to what his life is worth to him? I want a clear statement of which part of the machinery does which work.

4. Admissibility itself. In the standard formulation, the clause restricting the evidence is load-bearing and, by common consent, the hardest part to state without circularity. Does the film give any purchase on what should count as admissible? The drug money Llewelyn Moss stumbles on, the bodies, the encounters that drive the plot, Chigurh's own final accident: it is a world in which agents act on information whose bearing on outcomes nobody can gauge. Does that world vindicate the thought that there are no clean chances to defer to, or does it merely dramatize agents with poor evidence?

How I'd like the reading to land. Don't flatten the film into an illustration of a formula, and don't bend the formula to make a scene come out elegant; say plainly where the fit is loose — if Chigurh's coin turns out to do no real philosophical work and the interesting claim lies elsewhere in the film, I'd rather be told that. And I want the ending's irony assessed rather than admired: the man who made others defer to chance is destroyed by a piece of bad luck he never got to call. Is that the film's own verdict — that chance is real, indifferent, and not to be identified with anyone's will — or is it symmetry mistaken for argument? What would the film have to say back to Carla Jean for the ending to be more than a reversal?

Finally, on confidence: distinguish what is settled about chance, admissibility, and the best-system account from what is genuinely contested among people who work on these questions, and mark your own reconstruction as reconstruction. Don't present a contested position as the consensus; if you take the undermining problem to be damaging to the doctrine, say how widely that is thought. And flag any detail of the film you are unsure of from memory rather than asserting it — I can check the scenes, but I can't check a reconstruction that has been stated more confidently than it deserves.
