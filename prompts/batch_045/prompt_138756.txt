I keep returning to an argument a friend uses against the strange-loop account of the self, and I can't locate the mistaken step even though I'm sure the argument overreaches. It is meant to show that nothing material ever literally represents itself.

(1) A representation has determinate content only against a background that it does not itself depict: the content of a sentence is fixed by a language and an interpretation, the content of a picture by a surface and a viewer, the content of a machine state by the rules of the machine. Call that background the inviolate level: it makes content definite, but it is not itself among the things represented.

(2) Escher's Drawing Hands looks like a violation of this. A drawn hand draws the hand that is drawing it; the level that represents and the level that is represented switch places, and neither is prior.

(3) But the appearance is misleading. The picture is intelligible only because the sheet, the ink, and the lithographer's real hand lie outside the depicted scene. The loop is closed from outside: the tangle is a spiral, and seeing it as a loop at all depends on a level that isn't in it.

(4) Generalize: whenever a system appears to contain or refer to itself, its determinacy is on loan from a level outside it. Apparent self-containment is always containment relative to something not contained.

(5) So nothing is self-interpreting. A language cannot carry its own semantics, a machine cannot be about its own operation except derivatively, and a brain's self-model cannot literally be about that brain.

(6) But a self, if it is a physical system's representation of itself, has no outside: no homunculus, no inner theater, nothing but the system.

(7) Hence the self-symbol a brain deploys is not about that brain at all; it is about some surrogate, and the impression that there is a subject who has these thoughts is contributed by the rest of us, standing at the inviolate level. No material system ever literally represents itself; self-representation is always somebody else's view of a system.

I won't accept (7), and I think the argument proves too much: the same reasoning appears to rule out a program that prints its own source text (a quine), a video feedback loop's stable image, and fixed-point semantics in which a language carries a truth predicate for its own sentences by admitting a third truth value — cases where the self-directedness looks as determinate and as internal as anything could be.

Which step do you reject? Name the move, explain why it looked compulsory, and price the alternatives: what must be given up, or made more precise, to block (7) while keeping as much of (1) and (6) as possible? If forced to choose I would sooner keep (1) and (6) than (7), but I want to know what that costs me.
