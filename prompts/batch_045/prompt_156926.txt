I've been reading a popular book about how modern states see their citizens. Its opening chapter sets up three "regimes of visibility" and uses them as a frame for everything after: German data protection, American openness, and Chinese social credit. Here is the paragraph where it summarizes the German view and contrasts it with the other two:

"The German answer is informational self-determination. After the Federal Constitutional Court's 1983 census ruling, Germans concluded that the state's knowing things about you is itself the injury: people who can be watched behave differently, and the Court said that this chilling effect is the real harm. So German law treats personal data as withholdable by default — the individual, not the state, decides what gets collected, and data protection just is the right to control your own file. Which makes it a right to hide; and since the law-abiding have little to hide, in practice it is mostly people doing something wrong who reach for it. America is the mirror image. There, openness is the disinfectant, on the principle that if the state can see you then you can see the state, which is why Americans tolerate commercial data mining and open public records, and why their privacy fights are almost always about crime. China is where the German worry was really pointing. Its social credit system gives every citizen a score that rises and falls with conduct, and it shows that what makes state knowledge dangerous is the power to punish."
