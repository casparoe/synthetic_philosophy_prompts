Our political economy reading group — four of us, we meet in the back of a pub, and one guy is definately too into the Austrian stuff — has spent most of this term stuck on the socialist calculation debate, and I mean properly stuck: every session we rerun the same moves and nobody's numbers move even a little. The gist as I understand it: Mises says that without private ownership of the means of production there are no money prices for factors of production, so a planner has no common unit in which to compare heterogeneous inputs and literally cannot tell whether his plan is wasting steel or not; Lange replies that the planning board can just announce a list of parametric prices, tell managers to produce at minimum average cost and set output where marginal cost equals the announced price, then nudge prices up wherever there's excess demand and down wherever there's unsold surplus, iterating until things clear — and adds the cheeky point that general equilibrium theory is precisely the tool the planner needs, and that the Walrasian tatonnement process looks like a better description of a planning board than of any actual market. Then Hayek comes along and, as far as I can tell, changes the subject: the problem isn't computing an equilibrium from a given data set, it's that the relevant knowledge is dispersed, local, often tacit, and changing faster than any board could collect it, and that markets are best understood as procedures for discovering things rather than as devices for calculating an answer that was already determined. And in the background there's the fact that both sides claim the historical record — the Austrian guy points at the collapse of the Soviet-type economies, the market-socialist points at early Soviet industrialisation and at China's growth, and the third guy says neither of those is a test of anything because of confounds.

My working theory is that this isn't one debate but three or four questions wearing one name, and that's why it can't close: (i) given a fixed, already-collected description of technology and preferences, can a coherent allocation be computed at all — where I think Lange is basically right and I suspect even Mises would have to concede it; (ii) can the relevant knowledge actually be collected by anybody, given that much of it is local, inarticulate and about transient circumstances — where Hayek is doing the work; (iii) even if it could be collected, would the people who have it have any reason to report it accurately rather than game the reporting rule, which is the incentive-compatibility worry, and my hazy memory is that mechanism design has actual results saying you can't have informational efficiency and incentive compatibility both in full generality — correct me if I'm garbling that; and (iv) whether the benchmark is allocating a known stock of known goods or a process that has to handle goods and uses that don't exist yet, since a planning board can't announce a price for something nobody has thought of. I also want to know how much of the impasse is just verbal — about what counts as socialism, what feasible means, whether rational allocation is a property of an end state or of a procedure — because we waste a lot of time there.

So: diagnose this for me, engaging the actual arguments rather than surveying them. Which of these questions (or what else) is really carrying the disagreement, which ones have been answered and by whom, and where exactly do the two sides differ about facts versus standards versus starting intuitions? Two extras. First, a thought experiment we tried and couldn't settle: hand the planning board an arbitrarily good predictive model of the economy as it has been — every recorded transaction, perfect forecasts of demand at announced prices — but no access to counterfactual local circumstances or goods that don't exist yet. Does Hayek's objection survive that gift, or does it dissolve into something narrower, and if narrower, what? Second, given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a formal result, a computational experiment, a historical natural experiment, a conceptual distinction, or a change of question? If you think nothing would, say so and explain why. Please write the answer in American English, and keep it as a diagnosis, not a reading list.
