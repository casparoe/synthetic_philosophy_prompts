my flatmate is trying to convince me that no actually infinite collection of concrete objects can exist, and he's built this argument that I cant find the hole in — but it also looks to me like it proves way too much, so i want a second opinion.

Setup: Hilbert's Hotel, rooms numbered 1, 2, 3, ..., one guest per room, every room occupied.

1. The manager can do the following in one operation: evict everyone in an odd-numbered room, and move whoever is in room 2k into room k. Afterwards every room is occupied again, and the guests before and after can be paired off one-to-one. So he has removed infinitely many guests and there are exactly as many guests as before. Nothing has got smaller.

2. Now he does that same operation over and over: at 11:00, at 11:30, at 11:45, ..., intervals halving, so that infinitely many rounds are finished by noon.

3. Every guest is gone before noon. Someone who starts in room k survives the first n rounds only if k is divisible by 2 to the n; no positive integer is divisible by every power of 2. So each guest is evicted at some finite stage, and nobody is ever let back in.

4. So at noon: zero guests. The hotel is empty.

5. But after every one of those rounds the hotel was completely full, and step 1 says the operation never reduces the number of guests. There is no stage at which the number drops. So there's no point at which it could have dropped — at noon there are still infinitely many guests.

6. 4 and 5 contradict each other, so the hotel can't exist. And since nothing in the argument used anything about hotels, no actually infinite collection of concrete objects can exist: no spatially infinite universe with matter everywhere, no infinite past, no supertasks. His extra flourish: a hotel can't go from completely full to completely empty with nothing happening at the moment it changes, so even if you want to say "it's just empty at noon," that's the absurdity, not a way out.

The bit I can't get past: I think I can run the exact same argument on a hotel with one room and one guest. At 11:00 he's evicted and immediately readmitted; same at 11:30; same at 11:45; and so on, all done by noon. At every stage there is exactly one guest in the hotel and the number never changes. And he is evicted at every finite stage, so by the reasoning in steps 3 and 4 there are zero guests at noon. So 1 = 0. But there's nothing infinite anywhere in that story — one room, one guy. So it looks to me like the argument is really an argument against supertasks, not against actual infinities, and that it only looks like it's about infinity because the infinite case gives the word "number" something exotic to do.

He says the one-room case is disanalogous: there the story simply doesn't say what's true at noon, so there's no contradiction, just an incomplete description, whereas in the infinite case both "empty" and "still infinitely many" are forced and that's a real contradiction. He also says walking across a room isn't a supertask, because crossing the halfway point isn't something you do seperately from crossing the room — you just walk — so I can't use the continuity of space and time to save supertasks. And he's perfectly happy biting the bullet that the universe is finite. But then I don't see why he needs the hotel at all, and I definately don't see why I should give up infinity rather than whatever step is doing the work.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Of everything in play I'd most like to keep these three: that a spatially infinite universe is at least possible, that space and time are continuous, and that Cantor's bijection criterion is the right account of "same number" for infinite totalities. Anything else I'll drop without complaining.
