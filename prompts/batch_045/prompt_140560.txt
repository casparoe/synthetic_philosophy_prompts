Hi — I need a Q&A prep sheet. In two weeks I'm presenting to the early modern moral psychology reading group (joint philosophy/economics thing, about nine people, mostly grad students plus three faculty) and I want to walk in having already gotten yelled at.

The thesis I'm defending: Smith's impartial spectator is doing two jobs that he runs together without noticing. Call them the calibrating spectator and the appraising spectator. The calibrating one tells me how much of my resentment, gratitude, shame, whatever, is in proportion — roughly, what a real, well-informed, disinterested bystander would actually feel in my situation. The appraising one is supposed to step in when the real bystanders feel the wrong thing: it tells me the verdict of actual spectators is itself distorted and that I should disregard it. Smith's own material on how admiring the rich and looking down on the poor warps our moral sentiments is the clearest case, and there's the later part on custom and fashion too. My claim is that the mechanism of sympathy — imaginative projection of what I'd feel in your situation — can only generate calibration, because it's built out of actual spectators' responses and inherits whatever distortion they have. Smith needs the appraising job, since he plainly wants to criticize some very widely shared sentiments, but his machinery can't deliver it, and if he smuggles in a standard to do the appraising he stops being a sentimentalist.

The constructive bit I'm offering: reconstruct the appraising spectator as a kind of fixed point. Not just what would a well-informed spectator feel, but what a spectator would feel if they were well informed about everyone affected *and* about how there own sympathetic habits got shaped — then re-run it, and say the spectator's real verdicts are the ones stable under that iteration. Distorted convergence doesn't survive it, genuine verdicts do. That's definately the softest part and I know it.

Audience: two people who read Smith as a second-personal theorist — they'll say the spectator is a stand-in for being answerable to someone, not a detached viewpoint, and that my framing of calibration already gets that wrong. One Humean who thinks the general point of view does all of this already and will ask what's new. Two behavioral/labor economists who've read Theory of Moral Sentiments once and mostly know Wealth of Nations; they'll want to know whether the spectator does any real work or is just a name for a focal point, and whether the iteration has a fixed point at all. One hard-nosed moral psychologist who thinks no theory that grounds verdicts in what spectators happen feel can get normativity out at the end. And an early modernist who'll ask whether I'm reading a post-Enlightenment individual/community distinction back into a text that couldn't of had it.

What I want: eight or so objections, ordered by how likely they are times how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that particular person would actually say it (the economists should sound like economists, not like ethicists), each with a reply I can give in about ninety seconds and an honest note on where the reply is thin. Tell me what to concede in the first two minutes before anyone asks anything, and which single reply has to land for the talk to survive. Include the one question I'm hoping nobody asks.
