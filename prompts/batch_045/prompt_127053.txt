I'm writing a dissertation chapter on the Cartesian theater, and the fight over Dennett's multiple drafts model has stalled on me in a way I want diagnosed rather than adjudicated.

Here is where I take the two sides actually to stand. Dennett's target is the picture of a finish line: distributed processes churn away and then converge somewhere, at some moment, on a content that thereby becomes the conscious one, with the rest of the organism as audience. Against it he argues that the neural career of any content is smeared in space and time; that different probes of the same event — a key press, a verbal report, a confidence rating, a wager, a later memory — return different answers about when and whether the subject was conscious of it; and that there is then no non-question-begging way to single out one draft as the one that was conscious. The Orwellian/Stalinesque contrast is his lever: if no experiment could distinguish a content's having been conscious and then rewritten from its never having been conscious at all, then, he concludes, there is no further fact to be had. Methodologically, subjects' reports are data for a heterophenomenological description, not authoritative verdicts on the theory.

The phenomenal-realist critic bites from the other side. Grant all that, she says: you have catalogued the functions — report, discrimination, self-monitoring, voluntary control — and then declared the remaining fact, that there is something it is like, either unreal or unaskable. And when Dennett's positive story appeals to what is available to the subject or globally accessible, she hears the theater creeping back in with the audience deleted.

Now the piece I want you to take as stipulated, because I can't find it discussed anywhere and I think it makes the structure of the dispute visible. Call it complete probe-dependence. Suppose that for a simple, well-controlled visual display, every way we have of asking when the subject became conscious of a feature turns out to be subserved by partially non-overlapping neural populations, with no single region and no single time at which they converge; that which probe puts the moment of consciousness earliest varies with which probe you use; and that each probe, taken on its own, delivers a sharp, confident, apparently determinate answer. So there is no privileged draft, exactly as the model predicts — and yet the divergence is not error, uncertainty, or confabulation on the subject's part, which is where I take the model's own rhetoric to want such divergence to go. My hypothesis is that even complete probe-dependence would settle nothing, because the disagreement was never about the facts of access but about the explanandum: about what a theory of consciousness is answerable to, and about whether a first-personal datum may be dissolved as the artifact of a bad picture or must be taken as the thing to be explained. But I can see two competitors: that this is a hidden empirical question in disguise — whether phenomenal character does causal or explanatory work over and above the access facts — and that it is simply two questions under one name, a theory of cognitive access and a theory of experience, contested over the same examples.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a survey of the positions and not a verdict on who is right about consciousness. Using what each side actually says, tell me which of these the dispute is — or what else it is — and where precisely the two arguments cross without touching. Then, given your diagnosis, tell me honestly what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, an experiment, an abandonment of the question. And if the honest answer is that nothing would, say whether that is because the question is ill-posed or because the sides are playing different games under one heading — and what either answer implies for someone trying to write a chapter about it.
