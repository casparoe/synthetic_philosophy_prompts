QUESTION 3 (30 marks)

A university research-data committee has five voting members. On any proposal it must record a view on four linked propositions, and hence on their negations:

P: the consent requirement is satisfied
Q: the anonymisation requirement is satisfied
R: the data may be shared
S: the proposal is eligible for expedited review

The committee's standing rules fix two connections: R holds if and only if both P and Q hold, and S holds if and only if P or Q holds. Each member votes Yes or No on each of P, Q, R and S. Throughout, assume that at each meeting every member's own set of answers is consistent with the standing rules and complete, and that a properly formed collective judgment should likewise be consistent and complete.

The votes at two successive meetings (same agenda; one change of membership in between) were as follows.

Meeting 1
M1: P yes, Q yes, R yes, S yes
M2: P yes, Q no, R no, S yes
M3: P yes, Q no, R no, S yes
M4: P no, Q yes, R no, S yes
M5: P no, Q yes, R no, S yes

Meeting 2
M1: P yes, Q no, R no, S yes
M2: P yes, Q no, R no, S yes
M3: P no, Q yes, R no, S yes
M4: P no, Q yes, R no, S yes
M5: P no, Q no, R no, S no

(i) [8 marks] Determine the outcome of propositionwise (issue-by-issue) majority voting at Meeting 1. Explain precisely what has gone wrong, and why it is not merely the effect of a tied vote, of a badly drafted agenda, or of a member who voted inconsistently.

(ii) [10 marks] Two remedies were proposed in the seminar. The premise-based procedure: the members vote only on a designated set of premises, and the collective judgment on the remaining propositions is then derived by logic. The conclusion-based procedure: the members vote only on the designated conclusion, R. Give the outcome of each procedure at Meeting 1, on the assumption that the premises are P and Q, and say what each procedure costs the committee. Then locate the premise-based procedure with respect to the impossibility result we covered, which states that for agendas of this kind no aggregation rule can satisfy all four of the following: universal domain (every profile of individually consistent and complete judgment sets is admissible as input), collective rationality (the collective judgment set is always consistent and complete), anonymity (no member is singled out for special weight), and independence (the collective judgment on each proposition depends only on the individual judgments on that proposition). Which of these four does the premise-based procedure give up, and why?

(iii) [6 marks] At Meeting 2 a member proposes that the committee designate P, Q and S as the premises and R as the sole conclusion: take majority verdicts on P, Q and S, and derive R. Evaluate this proposal.

(iv) [6 marks] A second member argues as follows. If each member is more likely than not to be right about each proposition, and if errors are independent, then by the Condorcet jury theorem the majority verdict on each premise is more reliable than any individual member's verdict on that premise. Since the premises entail the conclusion, the verdict on R that the premise-based procedure derives inherits that reliability, and so the premise-based procedure must yield a verdict on R that is at least as likely to be correct as the verdict obtained by voting on R directly. Assess this argument.

Mark scheme: (i) 8 marks, (ii) 10 marks, (iii) 6 marks, (iv) 6 marks. First-class work gets the computations right, diagnoses the phenomenon rather than redescribing it, and treats the claims in (iii) and (iv) as claims that stand in need of argument rather than as premises. STUDENT ANSWER

(i) Taking each proposition on its own, the committee accepts P by 3 votes to 2, accepts Q by 3 votes to 2, accepts S unanimously, and rejects R by 4 votes to 1. The collective judgment set is therefore {P, Q, not-R, S}. That set is inconsistent: the standing rules make R equivalent to the conjunction of P and Q, so a committee that accepts both P and Q is committed to accepting R. Propositionwise majority voting has thus taken five members, each of whom voted consistently, and produced a majority view that no consistent committee could hold. Nothing here turns on a tie, since no vote is tied; nothing turns on a poorly drafted agenda, since the inconsistency arises from P, Q and R alone, which are exactly the propositions the committee exists to apply; and nothing turns on any member having voted inconsistently. This is the discursive dilemma.

(ii) On the premise-based procedure with P and Q as the premises, only the votes on P and Q are counted. Both are accepted, and R and S are then derived. The output is {P, Q, R, S}: consistent, complete, and an answer on every proposition on the agenda. The cost is that it reverses the 4-to-1 majority against sharing the data, and the question the committee was actually asked was the question about R. The conclusion-based procedure takes only the majority verdict on R, and so rejects R; the other three propositions are left undetermined, because not-R is compatible with several different combinations of P and Q and the procedure itself supplies no way of choosing among them, although any consistent way of filling them in yields a consistent set. So the conclusion-based procedure is safe but largely uninformative, while the premise-based procedure is informative but overrides an explicit majority. As for the impossibility result: the premise-based procedure satisfies universal domain, collective rationality and anonymity, and it escapes the theorem by giving up independence, since the collective judgment on R is settled by the pattern of individual judgments on P and Q rather than by the individual judgments on R itself.

(iii) At Meeting 2 the majority rejects P by 3 votes to 2, rejects Q by 3 votes to 2, and accepts S by 4 votes to 1. Taking P, Q and S as the premises, the premise-based procedure records not-P, not-Q and S, and R is then rejected, since R requires both P and Q. The output is {not-P, not-Q, not-R, S}: a determinate verdict on every proposition, so the difficulty has been avoided. And the point is general. Provided we vote on the premises by majority and derive everything else, we obtain a consistent and complete collective judgment set whichever propositions we designate as premises, and that is the procedure the committee should adopt.

(iv) The epistemic argument is also worth taking seriously. If each member is more likely than not to be right about P and about Q, and if errors are independent, then by the Condorcet jury theorem the majority verdict on each premise is more reliable than any individual member's verdict on it. The collective verdict on R is derived from those two majority verdicts by logic alone, and so inherits their reliability: the premise-based procedure must therefore produce a verdict on R that is at least as likely to be true as the verdict we would get from voting on R directly. That is a reason to prefer it to the conclusion-based procedure and to propositionwise majority voting, notwithstanding the cost noted in (ii). The argument does rest on the independence assumption, which is strong, but it is no stronger than the assumptions behind any other defence of majority rule.
