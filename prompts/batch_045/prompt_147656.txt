You are a philosopher with a long publication record on the structure of value, aggregation, and population ethics, and you have been hired as the neutral rapporteur for a two-year adversarial collaboration that has now ended. The two parties are, on one side, a defender of lexical threshold negative utilitarianism: the view that pleasure and pain are commensurable only up to a point, so that below some threshold of severity a quantity of suffering can be outweighed by a sufficient quantity of happiness, while at or above that threshold no amount of happiness, however large and however many people it is spread across, can outweigh a single instance of that suffering. On the other side is a defender of symmetric commensurabilism: the view that pleasures and pains are positions on one hedonic scale, so that for any suffering there is in principle some quantity of happiness that outweighs it, and what remains to argue about is empirical measurement and the ethics of aggregation rather than the shape of value itself. Both parties are act-consequentialists about value, both treat expected-value reasoning as at least a default, and both deny that rights, desert, or the separateness of persons impose side-constraints of their own. Neither is a sceptic about interpersonal comparisons, and neither thinks we must rest content with merely ordinal welfare rankings.

Write the joint statement they could both put their names to. Before you start drafting, work through a private pass in which you generate far more candidate agreed claims and candidate cruxes than you will use, throw out every formulation that is either vacuous or slanted toward one party, and check for each apparent disagreement whether it is substantive or whether it dissolves into a dispute about a word. Do not skimp on this: the value of the document is entirely in the formulations.

The document itself should contain, first, the claims both sides can sign, including claims about the cases themselves and about what a solution to their dispute would have to look like; second, the residual disagreements stated with enough precision that each side would accept its own formulation as what it in fact holds; third, the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one side; and fourth, for each crux, some case, argument, or piece of evidence that both parties agree bears on it, together with what each predicts it will show, written down in advance as a prediction. I want the document to make contact with at least these materials: an intervention that removes the unpleasantness of a painful state while leaving intact the sensory discrimination, the autonomic response, and the avoidance behaviour, so that the parties must say whether what remains still counts as suffering and what follows either way; the question whether a lexical priority attaching to one person's suffering above the threshold survives being multiplied across very many people's mild pleasures below it, or whether sheer numbers reintroduce commensurability by the back door; a choice between a very small probability of suffering at or near the threshold and a certainty of a great deal of ordinary happiness, which is where the charge of fanaticism about worst cases gets its grip; the intrapersonal analogue, in which a single person is offered a stretch of life containing equal quantities of intense pleasure and moderate pain, and the parties must say whether the asymmetry they defend is supposed to hold inside a life as well as across lives; the placement problem, since a threshold must fall somewhere, and it is worth knowing whether it is fixed by intensity, by duration, by kind of state, by something about the agent's situation, or whether it is an artefact of how coarse our measurements are; and the methodological question of whether, given that we cannot measure severe suffering with any precision, lexical priority is best understood as a claim about value or as a decision rule for acting under uncertainty. Close with a note, in prose, on which parts of the original dispute you concluded were merely verbal and what exactly the parties were disagreeing about when they seemed to disagree.

Be scrupulous about epistemic status. Where a claim is settled among people who actually work on this, present it as settled; where it is contested, and especially where it is contested within one of the two camps rather than only between them, say that it is contested and say who contests it and on what ground. Do not let a controversial formulation get laundered into the agreed-claims section by being stated in bland language. Flag explicitly any claim that neither party brought to the table and that the collaboration adopted as a stipulation in order to make progress.

Write it as continuous prose, the way you would write a long email to the foundation that funded the collaboration: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables. I still need the agreed claims and the disagreements to be distinguishable from each other, so signpost each agreed claim with ordinal language inside a sentence and give each disagreement and each crux its own paragraph rather than a row. Aim for something in the neighbourhood of a thousand words, and take the time to get the formulations right rather than producing something fluent and empty.
