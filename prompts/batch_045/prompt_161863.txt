I organize an advanced seminar on practical rationality — a LessWrong-adjacent reading group that has already worked through the sequences: expected utility, Bayes, hyperbolic discounting, costly signaling, Ulysses contracts — and I'm writing the final problem set. I need one question on the sunk cost fallacy that survives both the standard answer (the money is gone; only forward-looking consequences are decision-relevant) and the standard escape hatches (the expenditure is evidence about your earlier self's private reasons; continuing is costly signaling to rivals or predators; persistence is a commitment device against your own future akrasia). So stipulate those out in the scenario: the agent knows with certainty that the continuation value is negative, the spent resources carry no information about anything, no one else is observing, and no later copy of the agent will be moved by the precedent set. The question should then force students to say what kind of claim the sunk cost principle even is — a theorem that falls out of separability of utility across times and worlds (and so only as secure as that assumption, and silent about agents with history-dependent or narrative preferences), a substantive empirical claim about agents like us, or something else entirely — and to state what would have to be true of an agent or a decision procedure for honoring the sunk cost to be permissible anyway. Give me the question as it would appear on the problem set, a model answer of roughly 700–900 words, and a marking scheme worth 20 marks built around five must-hit moves, each with a one-line note on how students typically get it wrong (silently reintroducing an information or signaling channel I've stipulated away, treating "irrational" as a synonym for "I wouldn't do it", and so on).
