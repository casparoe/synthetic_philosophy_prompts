I'm a science journalist — mostly neuroscience and machine learning, with the odd digression into psychology — and I've rather rashly agreed to run a forty-minute "explainers" slot at a science-press conference in the autumn. The room will be about twenty-five other reporters: people who cover brain imaging, AI, mental health, that sort of thing. Barely any philosophy between them, but every one of them has at some point filed a piece with "consciousness" in the first paragraph and felt slightly grubby about it afterwards, and several are currently writing about whether systems that describe their own internal processing are, in some sense, conscious. I want to spend the slot on higher-order theories: the thought that what makes a mental state conscious is that there's a further state which is about it, rather than anything intrinsic to the state itself. Don't write the explainer for me — I need the plan for building it in the room, and I'd like the whole thing in British English, please.

Here's roughly what I'm after. First, the order the pieces have to go in and what depends on what: my guess is I can't say anything useful about "a state about a state" until I've seperated three things this crowd will arrive with all fused together — being conscious, attending to something, and being able to report it — but tell me if that's the wrong opening cut. Second, the one case that carries the idea. My candidate is a clinical-flavoured one: someone whose interoceptive monitoring is systematically mislabelled, so they have a churning, unpleasant gut state and the higher-order state that targets it files it as anxiety about something in their life, when in fact they ate something dodgy at lunch. It seems to me to have everything I need and to lack the thing that would make it useless — it doesn't settle the question by stipulation, and it forces a choice about whether the conscious character is fixed by the first-order state, by the higher-order one, or by the relation between them. But tell me what properties the carrying case has to have, and what it has to avoid, and give me a fallback, because someone in the front row will definately say "that's just interoception being weird" and I need somewhere to go.

Third, and this is the bit I care most about: the misconceptions, in the order they'll actually form in a room like this, and the exact point in the sequence where I head each one off rather than listing them at the end. I can predict several — the rock worry, so if I have a thought about a rock's state does the rock become conscious; the regress worry, isn't the higher-order state itself unconscious so hasn't the theory just moved the bump in the carpet; the collapse of the view into self-awareness or into metacognition as psychologists use that word; and the one that will genuinely eat my forty minutes if I let it, the leap to "so a model that monitors and reports on its own activations is conscious, then?". I want each defused at the point where it becomes load-bearing for whatever comes next, not parked in a Q&A. I also want to be told what I should openly refuse to get into at this level and say out loud that I'm refusing, because with this audience the temptation is to end up arguing about qualia for the last twenty minutes. Finally, how do I know in the room that it's landed rather than that I've just made people nod: give me one question I can put to the whole group near the end where the wrong answers are informative, so I can tell from how they get it wrong which part of the structure didn't take. Two small details that might matter: it's the slot right before lunch, there's a flipchart and no projector, and my producer will be there knowing even less philosophy than the reporters — so if there's a one-line version of the through-line I can hand her in advance, that'd help. I was chewing some of this over with a couple of friends after dinner on Sunday and we got stuck on exactly the mislabelling case, which is partly why I'm drawn to it, but I couldn't tell whether it was doing real work or just being atmospheric.
