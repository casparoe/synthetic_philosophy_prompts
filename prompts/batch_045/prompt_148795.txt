Two people in my flatshare got into it after dinner and I got nominated to referee, wich I cant really do, so I'm passing it to you. Here's the case they were arguing about, then roughly how it went.

The case: a hospital system rebuilt the decision aids it gives patients with early-stage prostate cancer. Patients get all the same numbers either way, but the booklet frames them as 5-year survival ("94 out of 100 men are alive in five years") rather than 5-year mortality, it books the follow-up consult before the patient has actually decided anything, and it includes two patient testimonials with photos. The oncology board thinks surgery is underused relative to active surveillance and that, on average, men in this group do somewhat better with it. Nobody is lied to and all the numbers are in the booklet.

Rosa: that's manipulation. The hospital is exploiting a documented irrationality — if patients processed the statistics properly, switching survival for mortality would do nothing at all, so the entire effect consists of a glitch in human cognition being used to steer people. It doesn't engage their capacity to weigh reasons, it goes around it.

Dev: there is no framing-free presentation. The numbers have to be given as survival or mortality, absolute or relative, five-year or ten-year. If the hospital picked the "neutral" option it would just be picking a different framing with a different push. So "they're using a framing" can't be the line between persuasion and manipulation.

Rosa: fine, the line has to be which framing, and here's a test — the reverse test: would you keep this presentation if it pushed people toward the option you think is worse? If you'd drop it the moment it pointed the other way, you're not informing people, you're steering them. The honest version is to put both formats side by side, or to use whichever format patients themselves find clearest once they've seen both.

Dev: that test convicts way too much. A doctor who leads with the scary number because she wants her patient to quit smoking fails it. So does a charity that shows one named kid with a photo instead of a table of famine statistics. And look at what your test actually demands of the hospital: deliberately choose the framing that moves patients away from what the evidence says is better for them. That isn't respect for patients, that's paternalism running in reverse.

Rosa: you keep sliding between "the hospital thinks surgery is better" and "the patient would agree." Their evidence is population-level averages; individual patients are trading off continence, recovery time, time off work. And the pre-booked appointment bothers me too — booking the follow-up before the decision isn't an argument for anything, it just makes declining the path of least resistance take effort.

Dev: on the appointment, every choice architecture has that feature. The question is whether the person can still opt out, and they can; nobody is trapped. And on the first point, let me offer a better test than yours, the calibration test: an influence is legitimate when its effectiveness tracks the strength of the case — when it moves people more the more reason there is to be moved. Framing effects don't do that; they're a fixed-size shove in whatever direction you point them. So my own test convicts the hospital, and I'll grant that. But the hospital has an answer: the framing is cancelling out a different fixed-size distortion pointing the other way, and two distortions that cancel leave the patient closer to their own considered judgement than either one alone.

Rosa: you don't get to do that. Neutralising one bug with another bug means the patient's decision now depends on the hospital's guess about how big each bug is, and they have plenty of incentive to guess in the direction that keeps operating theatres busy. Also, "we corrected your bias by adding a bias" isn't something you can say to the patient's face, and I think that matters: persuasion is influence you could narrate to the person afterwards without them feeling played.

Dev: maybe, but your narration test gives weird results too. Telling a patient "we used the vivid testimonial because vividness moves people" doesn't undo anything, and most patients would shrug and say fine, just tell me what you'd recommend. Which suggests the real disagreement was never about the technique, it's about whether it's okay for institutions to aim at outcomes they take to be better for you.
