You are a professional philosopher who has spent a career at the intersection of the metaphysics of persons, normative ethics and the philosophy of action, and who has also sat on grant panels that had to decide where scarce money actually goes. Please think carefully and at some length about the request below. Before you commit to an answer, spend a few paragraphs brainstorming how the space of candidate questions might be carved up — the different axes you could cut it along, the levels of granularity available, the questions you are tempted to include and then drop — and only afterwards present and defend a final ordering.

I sit on the programme committee of a small foundation — call it the Hallowell Fund — with roughly £1.2m to disburse over three years on conceptual, non-empirical work on the self: what persons are, what it is to have a first-personal point of view extended over time, and what we owe to ourselves or to others in the way of shaping our own self-conception. The trustees have constrained the end: every funded project must plausibly feed into one of two downstream uses. The first is clinical and legal practice around disturbance or loss of selfhood — advanced dementia, severe dissociation, gender transition, acquired brain injury — where practitioners and judges lean on talk of a person's story, identity or true self in ways that strike philosophers as load-bearing and yet slippery. The second is adjudicating claims about artificial systems: whether a system with long-term memory, stable goals, a persistent public-facing persona and the capacity to give an account of its own history could come to have something like a self, and whether that would make it the kind of thing towards which duties of narrative integrity could be owed. My own starting image, and the one several trustees find attractive, is Dennett's: the self is not an inner item but more like a centre of gravity, an abstract focal point around which a creature's memories, self-directed stories, projects and excuses organise, doing real explanatory work without being an extra thing in the world.

Here is roughly where my thinking has got to, so that you can push against it rather than start from nowhere. I keep returning to four or so clusters and I cannot tell which of them are worth years of somebody's career. First, diaristic versus narrative selfhood: much of the discussion assumes that what constitutes a self over time is story-shaped recollection, yet a great deal of ordinary self-tracking is dated, unshaped, recorded rather than recounted — a lifelog, a calendar, a medical file. Is a self constituted by records rather than by narrative still a self in the relevant sense, and does the centre-of-gravity image survive if the gravity is exerted by an archive rather than by a telling? Second, externally located centres: if the records, reputations and narratives that fix who someone increasingly live outside that person — in other people's memories, in institutional files, in data used to fit models — then a person's centre of gravity may itself partly lie outside them, and I want to know whether that is a coherent extension of the image or a quiet reductio of it, and whether an institution or a firm can literally have a centre of narrative gravity at all. Third, narrative inertia: the resistance of an existing self-story to revision, which looks like a vice when it entrenches self-deception and like a constitutive condition of agency when, without it, nothing a person does hangs together; is there a principled line between healthy and pathological inertia, and is that the line clinicians actually need? Fourth, plural or contested centres: fragmentation, ambivalence, the experience of having several incommensurable stories about oneself — does the metaphor tolerate several centres, or does it tacitly presuppose uniqueness, and if so is that a discovery or a defect? I also suspect two further questions are more upstream, or more tractable, than they look: whether narrative self-constitution should be read as a descriptive claim about what we in fact do or a normative claim about what we ought to do, and whether the two readings have been conflated in ways that make the applied conclusions look better supported than they are.

What I want from you is a defended agenda, not a survey and not a reading list: four to six questions you would actually put money behind, in the order you would bet on them, with the reasoning exposed. For each, tell me how much other work depends on its answer — which questions are upstream of which, and where a wrong answer would poison everything downstream — how tractable it is for philosophers with about three years and no laboratory, how neglected it is given that selves and narratives have been discussed intensively for decades, and what it buys for one or both of the trustees' downstream uses. Be explicit about the trade-offs between these criteria: I would rather see you argue that a question is mediocre on tractability but worth funding because nothing else can proceed until it is settled than see you average the criteria into a bland ranking. Then tell me what would move things: what kind of result, argument, or change in the world over the next three years would push a question up or down your list, and which one or two candidates you would deliberately defund, either because the answer would not change practice or because the interesting answers are already available. If you think one of my clusters is a dead end, or that the most important question in this area is one I have not gestured at, say so and say why; and if you think the trustees' two downstream uses pull in incompatible directions, tell me which one you would weight more heavily and on what grounds.
