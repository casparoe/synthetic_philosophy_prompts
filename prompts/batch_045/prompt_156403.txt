I have been asked to run a ninety-minute evening session on Hofstadter's 'Person Paper' satire for a mixed adult-education group at a lifelong-learning institute: about twenty people, mostly retired, no linguistics or philosophy between them, and politically mixed — several will arrive already convinced that the point about sexist language is settled and faintly boring, several will arrive convinced that it is fussiness about words while the real issues lie elsewhere, and one member will say, early and with some feeling, that she has chaired committees for thirty years and has never meant anything by 'chairman'. We have a whiteboard, one double-sided photocopy of the piece, nothing to project on, and I cannot cite any empirical psychology even where it exists, so the session has to work through example and argument alone. The last time a group like this discussed the piece it split into two camps and stayed there: one read the satire as though it were a proof that generic uses of 'man' are simply false, the other read it as a joke that establishes nothing and could therefore be shrugged off. I do not want an explanation of the satire; I want a plan for explaining it.

The part I am least sure about is the dependency structure, so please be explicit about what has to be in place before what, and about what must be deliberately withheld until later. My own instinct is that nothing works until the group has first felt, rather than been told about, the asymmetry between the two members of a pair — that one term (the unmarked one) can stand both for the whole category and for one half of it, while the other is the marked term, formed by addition or derivation and usable only for its own half — and that the satire cannot land until they have also seen why applying the substitution uniformly produces comedy precisely where the word containing 'man' has stopped being parsed as a compound at all. I have a candidate for the case that carries the whole session: the way 'right' does double duty as a direction and as 'correct' in English and related languages, with 'left' carrying the marked, deviant, faintly sinister sense. I suspect this is the wrong choice, because in that pair the double duty is largely historical and evaluative in a way the gender case is not, so the group may draw the analogy along the wrong feature; tell me whether to keep it as a warm-up, replace it, or restructure around something else, and say what the carrying example must have and must lack. I have also been separating the diagnoses as follows — (i) the unmarked term is ambiguous between the generic and the specific reading, so predicating it of a woman is either false or oddly marked; (ii) even where the generic reading is unambiguous, the unmarked term sets who is pictured by default; (iii) the specific term for women is morphologically derived from the male one, which encodes the male case as the original and the female as the variation; (iv) the satire's comedy feeds on the residue of compounds and idioms where the male element has gone opaque. Tell me which of these the satire actually runs on, in what order to introduce them, and whether I have the dependency backwards.

Then the misconception map, with a position in the sequence for each: that the whole issue is politeness or etiquette; that a neutral term such as 'chairperson' solves it, so that neutrality is a property of words rather than of defaults in use; the paired errors of treating the satire as a proof and treating it as mere jest; the etymology objection, that 'man' originally meant human being and so nothing can be amiss; the symmetry objection, that swapping the marked term round would be equally funny and so nothing is shown (I do not think it is symmetric, but I want to know why, and where in the hour to deal with it); and the claim that a speaker who means everyone has thereby done nothing questionable. Please also give me one closing check I can run in the last ten minutes that a participant who has absorbed only the etiquette lesson — replace 'chairman' with something neutral — would fail, and that would show me the mechanism has transferred rather than the vocabulary; and tell me what I should openly refuse to settle at this level, since I would rather name those limits than bluff. Give a rough division of the ninety minutes, and write the plan in British English.
