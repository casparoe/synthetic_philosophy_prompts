hey — I'm in a seminar paper on minimalism about truth (the view that truth is exhausted by the equivalence schema: the proposition that p is true iff p) and I've def worked myself into a hole. Here's my reasoning, and I'd like to know where it breaks before I say any of this out loud in supervision.

Step one: if the minimalist is right that "it's true that p" says nothing over and above p, then "true" isn't doing any semantic work. So it should be eliminable in principle — a paraphrase is always available. People object that you can't actually delete it because of things like "everything the Pope has ever said is true", since we'd need an infinite conjunction. Fine, but that looks like a merely practical limitation: our heads and our lifespans are finite. It's not a semantic point about the word. So I've started calling my view "finite-domain deflationism": whenever the domain of generalization is finite and enumerable in principle, truth-talk is dispensable, because you can swap every generalization for a real, finite conjunction or disjunction. I tried it on a few toy cases and it seems to hold up.

Step two, where I think I've shown something stronger: if that's right, the minimalist owes us a story about why truth only becomes genuinely indispensable at infinity. And any such story is going to make truth's function depend on our cognitive finiteness — which is a psychological or sociological claim about creatures like us, not a claim about truth. That smells psychologistic to me, so I'm inclined to say minimalism is false, or at best it's an empirical thesis about language users dressed up as metaphysics.

One thing I anticipate being thrown at me and want to pre-empt: someone will say "at least one thing the detective told me yesterday is false", and I obviously can't write out the disjunction because I don't know which claims she made. But that's just ignorance on my part, not a defect in the paraphrase — the disjunction exists whether or not I can produce it. Where am I going wrong?
