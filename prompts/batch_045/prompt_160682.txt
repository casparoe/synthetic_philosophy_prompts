I sit on my health system's Nosology Review Board, which decides what counts as a disease — the classification then drives coverage, screening policy, and research funding. Our written precedents read roughly as follows. Classic phenylketonuria: a disease. Asymptomatic stage-1 essential hypertension, averaging about 145/92 with no end-organ damage: a disease, and treatment recommended. Asymptomatic heterozygous carriage of a recessive lethal allele, with entirely normal function: not a disease. Age-typical androgenetic alopecia in a seventy-year-old: not a disease. Congenital bilateral deafness in a fluent signer who is thriving and wants no intervention: a disease, with an explicit note that classification carries no implication that treatment is wanted. Uncomplicated grief six weeks after a bereavement, with disturbed sleep and appetite: not a disease. A chronic-fatigue-type condition with disabling post-exertional malaise, normal standard investigations and no identified mechanism: a disease, the Board having accepted suffering and functional impairment as sufficient given a presumed mechanism.

Next month we must rule on a variant described only in the last few years: a heritable down-regulation of the inflammatory cascade. Carriers have chronically low C-reactive protein, blunted or absent fever, slightly slower wound closure, markedly lower rates of atherosclerosis and late-onset dementia in cohort follow-up, lower all-cause mortality after seventy, and a modestly raised risk of death from certain intracellular bacterial infections before the age of five. No carrier reports feeling ill; most report the opposite. Population frequency is a few percent. I should disclose that a consumer sequencing panel told me last week that I carry it.

What should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Please set out the two or three classificatory rules that fit our track record taken as a whole — a value-free biostatistical rule, a dysfunction-plus-harm rule, a normativist or medical-appropriateness rule — say what each predicts here, identify which precedent is the closest analogue and why it fails to settle the case, and name the further case that would genuinely discriminate between them. I would also like to know whether the ruling should differ for the screening question in children, and whether our willingness to count an asymptomatic risk elevation in the hypertension precedent commits us to anything here.
