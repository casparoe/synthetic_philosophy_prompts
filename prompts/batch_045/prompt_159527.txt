For my upper-level seminar on the Fermi paradox this term we have to write a paper that reads one work of science fiction against one proposed solution to the paradox, and I have chosen to read Denis Villeneuve's Arrival (2016) against the dark forest hypothesis — the idea, from Liu Cixin's fiction, that spacefaring civilizations stay silent and destroy anything that reveals itself, on the grounds that survival is a civilization's primary need, that expansion continues while the matter available is fixed, and that the chain of suspicion and the possibility of a technological explosion make a preemptive strike rational. I would like a reading of the film in terms of that hypothesis, and I want it to be an argument rather than a survey, so please do not spend space summarizing the plot.

The distinction I want to organize the paper around is one I have not seen drawn in our readings, and I would like you to test it as much as use it. Call the first version the informational version: strike-first and stay-silent are optimal because of what you cannot know — whether the other party is hostile, whether they think you are hostile, whether their capacity will leap ahead of yours before you could respond. On that version, the equilibrium is a product of ignorance, and better information would dissolve it. Call the second the positional version: silence and preemptive strike are optimal even under full common knowledge of everyone's intentions, because growth is superlinear, destructive capacity is cheap and scalable, a small lead compounds, and revealing is irreversible while hiding is nearly costless — so silence is weakly dominant no matter how benign everyone is. On that version, no amount of communication helps.

So the central question I want argued: which version does Arrival actually engage, and does it refute that version or merely avoid it? Work through the scenes that matter here. The twelve craft arrive loudly at once, and the heptapods show presence while disclosing nothing about themselves. The full message turns out to be split into twelfths, so that no state can read it alone. The crisis turns on whether a logogram means weapon or tool, and the film attributes the hostile reading to the fact that the Chinese team established contact through a competitive game. General Shang's ultimatum, which other states then join, is the human-level version of the strike logic, and Louise defuses it with information she could not have had. The heptapods say they have come to help humanity because in three thousand years they will need our help; they rescue Louise and Ian at cost to themselves; and they then leave, permanently, without saying where they came from or what will threaten them.

Two things I want you to press rather than gloss. First, the film's fatalism: acquiring the heptapod language gives Louise knowledge of a future she does not alter. If knowledge of the future leaves action untouched, then the film has undercut the informational story about how conflict gets dissolved — and if the informational version is the version Arrival refutes, the film has refuted something its own metaphysics cannot endorse. Second, the possibility that the real hazard the film depicts is not hostility but untranslatability — parties who share no frame for what kind of interaction they are in — which is a state the three-action game of destroy, broadcast, or stay silent does not represent at all.

Where does the film fail as a test? It never puts us in the position of calling out, so the practical question the hypothesis is usually invoked to settle never arises on screen; I want your view on whether that disqualifies the film or is itself the datum. And I want, at the end, a claim about what Arrival adds to the debate that the game-theoretic framing of the hypothesis cannot: something the film shows about first contact that arguments alone do not. Where the reading strains the film or the film strains the idea, say so plainly; I would rather defend a narrower thesis than a tidy one.
