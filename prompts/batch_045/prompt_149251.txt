I'm in a small philosophy of maths reading group at my uni — three undergrads plus one PhD student who works on scientific realism and who, annoyingly, steers every single discussion back to realism versus antirealism — and we just had a session on the continuum hypothesis that left me with a pile of analogies everyone is leaning on and no idea which of them is carrying the weight. I'd like them audited rather than just explained to me.

The big one, which was in the pop-maths book we read to prep and which the PhD student keeps repeating, is that CH is "the parallel postulate of set theory": it's independent of the other standard axioms, so just as you can take the parallel postulate or its negation and get Euclidean or non-Euclidean geometry, you can take CH or its negation and get different set theories, and — this is the use he makes of it — therefore there is no fact of the matter about whether there's an intermediate size of infinity between the naturals and the reals; it's a choice of framework, like a choice of geometry or of units. One of the other undergrads says the analogy is being run backwards: on her telling, the parallel postulate story is a story of axioms failing to pin down an intended subject matter, and the way we settled it was by going and looking at space (and ending up with non-Euclidean geometry being the physically true one), so the moral is that independence should push us to say more about what the universe of sets is like, not to declare the question empty. A third person brings in the axiom of choice as a second data point: also independent of the rest, and nobody took that to mean there's no fact of the matter about whether every set can be well-ordered — people mostly ended up accepting it, on extrinsic grounds, because the mathematics goes better with it — so independence as such can't be doing the anti-realist work. And I floated one of my own that nobody either liked or disliked: the first-order theory of real closed fields doesn't pin down the real line either (the real algebraic numbers form a real closed field that isn't Dedekind complete, and there are non-Archimedean ordered fields satisfying all the same first-order sentences), yet nobody concludes from that there's no fact of the matter about whether bounded sets have least upper bounds; we say the axioms were too weak to describe the structure we had in mind and we add a stronger completeness principle. If that's a fair parallel, why isn't CH in exactly that boat — ZFC as a too-weak description of the cumulative hierarchy, to be fixed by a stronger axiom, which is more or less what the people looking for axioms with "verification from below" (large cardinals, forcing axioms; someone mentioned one that would pin the continuum at ℵ2, though I may well be mangling that) think they're doing?

So: for each of these three — the parallel postulate, the axiom of choice, and my real-closed-fields one — can you set out what maps onto what (a small correspondences table is fine: what plays the role of the other axioms, of the intended subject matter, of going out and looking at space, of the rival theories), where each mapping breaks, and which features of the source domain each analogy quietly imports into set theory that set theory may not have (I suspect an intended interpretation supplied from outside the theory, and an empirical arbiter, are the two big ones). Then tell me which of the disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion the PhD student wants — no fact of the matter — and which are just noise. Two things I'd especially like addressed: whether the internal shape of the set-theoretic case is even like the geometric one, given that the CH-false and CH-true models come from forcing extensions and inner models of one another rather than sitting side by side as unrelated structures (this seems to be what the "multiverse" talk is about, but I can't tell whether it helps him or her); and whether the Gödel-sentence case is a helpful comparison or a misleading one, since a Gödel sentence is independent but generally taken to be true in virtue of what it says about itself, and nothing remotely self-guaranteeing seems to come attached to CH's independence. Finally, is there a better analogy for this situation, or is the honest answer that it's sui generis and the analogies are all just ways of restating a prior realist or antirealist commitment? Please don't give me a reading list or citations — I want the argumentative structure, not references.
