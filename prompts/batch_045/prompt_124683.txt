I am applying for a one-term research grant to turn a chapter of my dissertation into a standalone article, and I need the written proposal: two pages, single-spaced, in the form a selection committee would read. The chapter is on the demandingness objection to consequentialism, but I want the proposal to argue for a reframing of the debate rather than assume it.

Here is my half-formed idea. The literature standardly measures how demanding a moral theory is by how much it requires the agent to give up: income, time, projects, relationships, integrity. It then argues about whether that quantity is unacceptably large. But my sense is that much of what makes consequentialism feel demanding is not the size of the required sacrifice but the shape of the requirement: that it is recomputed continuously, that new information can raise it at any point, and that consequently no personal commitment, plan, promise, or career can ever be treated as settled. Call this the openness of the requirement, though I am not attached to the label. Two moral requirements could impose the same expected lifetime sacrifice and differ enormously in how much of an agent's practical life they occupy, and if that is right then objections and replies in this debate may be answering different complaints. A weaker version of my worry is that a maximizing theory cannot simply close off the requirement, by fixing a rule or a threshold once and for all, without giving up what motivated it in the first place.

What I want from the proposal: state the question so that it has a determinate answer rather than being an invitation to look at the topic differently; separate the quantity of required sacrifice from the structural features of the requirement and say which of them the demandingness objection really rests on; give a method for settling that, together with a reason for the method over the obvious alternatives, such as surveying intuitions about demandingness directly; set out the possible conclusions and which considerations would decide between them, including the possibility that the standard replies answer only one of the two complaints and the possibility that the structural feature I am pointing at is, in the end, just another cost that a maximizing theory already weighs; and give an honest statement of scope and of what would count as the project failing. My own candidate failure condition is that the distinction collapses because expected sacrifice is itself a function of how revisable the requirement is.

Do not give me a literature review or any references; I will add citations myself. Engage with positions and arguments, not with named authors and their texts. If you think the framing is mistaken, say so inside the proposal and give the stronger version of it: I would rather submit a project that survives its own framing being revised.

Give me the proposal as exactly five numbered items, each no more than about ninety words, with no preamble and no closing note.
