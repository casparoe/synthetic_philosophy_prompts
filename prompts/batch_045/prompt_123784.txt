Last month our school's philosophy club hosted a talk on the modern Stoicism revival, and two of the seniors stayed afterward and argued for close to an hour without either of them budging. I was mostly a bystander, and I've been turning it over since. I've tried to keep their actual points while compressing a long exchange, and I've numbered the moves so you can go through them one at a time.

(1) Maya: The heart of the revival — sort everything into what is up to you and what isn't, then spend your energy only on the first pile — is just the opening move of Epictetus's handbook, restated in plain language. The packaging is modern; the idea isn't.

(2) Dev: The problem isn't the translation, it's the concept. The ancient Stoics were determinists: everything that happens follows from fate. So "what is up to us" can't mean "what I can causally steer." It means roughly what is genuinely attributable to me — my judgments, my assents, my choices. Modern versions start from a two-circle diagram of things I can influence versus things I can't, which is a piece of practical management advice, closer to the famous serenity formula about changing what you can than to Epictetus. He called this the efficacy slide: the claim quietly turns from a metaphysical point about where agency lives into advice about where to spend effort.

(3) Maya: Even if that's the correct reading of the ancients, the upshot for someone trying to live is the same on both readings, so the slide doesn't do any harm. And determinism cuts her way too: if the Stoics had no contra-causal free will, then "up to us" can't mean contra-causal control either, so a causal-influence reading is arguably closer to something a modern person could actually accept.

(4) Dev: The upshot isn't the same, and there's a case that shows it. Suppose someone insults you. On the modern reading the advice is: you can't control the insult, so control your reaction — which treats distress as a cost to be minimized and your equilibrium as the goal. On the ancient reading the point is that the insult is an indifferent: it has no bearing on whether you're a good person or on your eudaimonia, flourishing, and the work is on your value judgment that being slighted is something bad. The first version leaves your goals untouched and manages the feelings; the second attacks the goals.

(5) Maya: But "virtue is the only good and everything else is indifferent" is unusable advice for anyone who isn't already a sage, and the Stoics themselves separated the full doctrine from the exercises they gave to people still making progress. Call it the training-wheels defense: a teaching simplification can be faithful in spirit without being accurate in its metaphysics, and Epictetus's opening division reads exactly like a training device rather than a piece of theory.

(6) Dev: That concedes the point. It's an adaptation, not a summary — and this particular adaptation has a characteristic failure mode, because the two-circle picture invites you to grow the inner circle: build influence, negotiate harder, optimize your leverage. The ancient practice runs the other direction. Don't widen your reach; narrow your desires down to what is actually yours. His claim was that the modern version risks converting a discipline of contracting the self into a productivity technique.

(7) Maya: Maybe so, but if the modern version measurably reduces anxiety in the people who use it, then holding the anachronism against it is a bit academic. Stoicism was always meant to be a practical school, not a seminar.

(8) Dev: "It reduces anxiety" is the wrong scorecard for this tradition anyway. Stoicism judges a practice by whether it makes you better, not by whether it makes you feel better.

I don't want just an overall verdict, and I genuinely can't tell who's winning. Then say what, if anything, both of them are leaving out — the thing a better version of this argument would have to get right about what the dichotomy is supposed to be for.
