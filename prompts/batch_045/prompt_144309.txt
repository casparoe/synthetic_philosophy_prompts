Ive been turning over a fairly strong antinatalist package and I think I have come to accept it. Start with Benatar's asymetry: the claim that pain is bad, pleasure is good, the absence of pain is good even if there is no one there to be benefited by it, and the absence of pleasure is not bad unless there is somebody who is deprived of it. Now add a principle I have started calling the Collective Absent-Pain Thesis: we are allowed to aggregate the goodness of absent pains across all the merely possible people who would have existed if we had not prevented their existence, so preventing a new sentient being from coming into existence is not merely something one may do, it produces a large collective good and grounds a serious pro tanto duty not to create new sentient beings. Call the whole package Collective Asymmetry Antinatalism.

I want you to tell me what would count against Collective Asymmetry Antinatalism. Do not just rehearse the usual objections to plain Benatar-style antinatalism -- I mean the compensation objection (that a life with enough pleasure can be worth its pains), the point that existence is not a property a merely possible person has so nothing can be worse for them, and the brute intuition that a contented life is worth starting. Assume I already know those. Tell me instead what defeaters, observations, arguments, formal results, or cases should move a defender of this specific package, sorted by how decisive they would be, from fatal to merely awkward. Which would a committed defender concede as counting against the view, and which would they dismiss as resting on a misunderstanding? I also want to know whether the Collective Absent-Pain Thesis has been insulated into unfalsifiability: if the aggregation runs over merely possible people and their absent pains, can any empirical evidence or policy intervention ever touch it, and if it cannot, is that a bug or a feature for a view of this kind? Include any results in population ethics or about the non-identity problem that the view stands or falls with.

I should say I am most moved by comparisons between a world with extra happy people and a world without them: I just do not feel the missing pleasures of the non-existent people as a bad-making feature of the smaller world, and if that intuition is unreliable or question-begging, that would probably move me most. Please be commited: give me your own ranking, say which defeaters you actually think succeed and which you think a defender should concede, and tell me how confident you are in that ranking. Do not just lay out the options and leave me to pick -- take a definite position on which defeaters are fatal, which are merely awkward, and which are dismissible, and own the reasons for that ranking.
