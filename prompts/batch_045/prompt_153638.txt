I've been teaching an upper-level seminar on philosophical issues about interpretation this term and I've hit a wall I keep hitting. We spent the first three weeks on the intentional-fallacy essay by Wimsatt and Beardsley and then on Hirsch-style actual intentionalism, and my students split neatly in half and then absolutely nothing happened for the rest of the month. Not because they weren't trying — they swapped arguments, steelmanned each other, the whole deal — but because every move on one side had an answer on the other that the other side already took to be obvious. The anti-intentionalists say: meaning is what a competent reader, armed with the language, the genre and the relevant public context, would take the text to say; the author's avowal is evidence of that at best, and if she botched it — meant to type "seagull," typed "sea lion" — the intention doesn't repair the meaning. The intentionalists say: meaning just is the author's successful intention, the reader's independent grasp is a heuristic that mostly tracks it, and the "competent reader" is the author's intention smuggled back in under a social description. Everyone concedes the boring bits (that intentions can be evidence, that what the author says in interviews twenty years later isn't decisive), and then the fight restarts in the same place: is intention constitutive or merely evidential, and whose text is it anyway.

My own hypothesis, which I floated in class and couldn't quite defend, is that this isn't one disagreement but three stacked ones, and that the reason it looks immovable is that both sides argue from a single paradigm case — a solitary literary author alone in a room with her manuscript — while quietly appealing to different ones whenever they need to win a point. One layer looks verbal-ish to me: "meaning" is doing duty for at least utterance content, the meaning of the text-type, and the meaning of the work, and if you've picked your object you've mostly picked your theory. A second looks like a genuine metaphysical disagreement about whether content is grounded in a mind or in a public practice. And a third, which my students got obsessed with and which I suspect is where the real action is, concerns non-paradigm cases: a network sitcom written by a twelve-person writers' room, a ghostwritten CEO tweet, a novel drafted with heavy model assistance where there is no single mind whose successful intention could be the thing meaning is supposed to be. My guess is that the intuitions people bring to the debate are generated by different cases within that third class, which is why the arguments pass each other.

I also threw a view of my own into the ring, mostly to see whether it would survive: call it checkpoint intentionalism — the meaning of a text is what its author would have meant by it if she had been queried about the disputed bit at the moment of production, with the counterfactual anchored at that time and not later, so that retroactive interview claims are irrelevant but so is what some hypothetical ideal reader would make of it. I half suspect this collapses straight into the anti-intentionalist view, because what a real author would have said under that counterfactual may just be whatever a competent reader of her own text would say, and may be indeterminate in exactly the cases that matter; but I also half suspect it does work the standard views can't, in cases where the author had a clear dispositional structure but never occurrently considered the question. So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually — verbal, substantive-but-hard, a mismatch about which object is being interpreted, or several separate questions sharing a label? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, a hard case, something about collaborative or machine-assisted authorship, a change of question? Or should I just tell my students that this one is settled by temperament?
