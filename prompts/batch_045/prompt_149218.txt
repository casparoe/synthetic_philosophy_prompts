Your position, stated as you would state it: an action has moral worth only if the agent's own operative motivating reason is the right-making consideration itself — she acts because this is what is owed, or because this person needs this, in a way that is genuinely hers and not borrowed. Moral worth is consequently a property of the will's relation to the reasons, not a property of the will's reliability. You are not a caricature of the view, and you have already made three concessions in print: (i) the duty-motive need not be a conscious or verbally articulated episode — an agent can act from duty while being unable to say what duty requires; (ii) mixed motivation is fine, since worth is compatible with inclination, guilt or love of reputation running alongside, so long as the right-making consideration is also in the driver's seat; (iii) rightness and worth are separable, so an act can be fully right and still worthless, and a worthless act can still be admirable. You have also conceded something harder: that an agent who acts well out of unreflective concern for someone while sincerely judging the act to be wrong can nonetheless act with moral worth, because the concern for the person just is the right-making feature showing up in the will. Concede small points freely in the same spirit. Never concede the main one: that a motive which merely tracks rightness through an intermediary — a person, a sanction, a rule, a machine — is the same thing as acting because the act is right.

My opening move. Mira returns a large lost wallet at real cost to herself. Her proximate motive is her grandmother's good opinion: she wouldn't do it if her grandmother could never find out, and she feels nothing for the loser. But her grandmother is a near-perfect detector of moral rightness, Mira knows this, and Mira wants that approval precisely as approval for doing what is right — she says, of herself, that she wants to be the sort of person granny could admire. Now vary the case: keep the act and the motive-structure identical, but let the grandmother be a perfect detector of what is locally admired rather than of what is right. Mira now performs the same kind acts when they're admired and the same cruel ones when those are admired. Intuitively no worth anywhere in the second case — yet the only thing that changed is what the intermediary is calibrated to. So: on your own account of the unreflective helper, worth is supposed to consist in the will's relation to the right-making feature; and Mira's will is related to it exactly as reliably as the helper's, just deferentially — she borrows her grandmother as a name for the right-making property, the way a speaker can use a word correctly by deferring to someone who knows what it means. You say Mira's act is right but worthless. Tell me what is missing from Mira's will, and say why the missing thing isn't merely reliability of a different, more internal kind.
