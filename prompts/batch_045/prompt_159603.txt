I was appointed six weeks ago as a lay member of the mental health review tribunal for our region, and I am putting together a two-page handout for my fellow members — a consultant psychiatrist, a solicitor, a social worker, and a service-user representative. None of them has studied philosophy, and I have only an undergraduate memory of it. Our days are spent deciding whether people remain liable to detention, whether they are discharged conditionally, and whether they are made subject to a community treatment order under which a clinician may recall them at her discretion. Nearly every disagreement among us turns out to be a disagreement about whether someone is free, and we keep talking past each other because we are using different conceptions without noticing. I want a decision tree that sorts the conceptions we are actually relying on, built so that a member arrives at a named view by answering questions about cases rather than by choosing a label.

What I need:

A tree with six to eight leaves, each reached by a path of three or four questions — call it ten to fifteen questions in total, some of them with three answers. Every question is a concrete case drawn from a caseload like ours, phrased so that someone who has never heard the terms "negative liberty," "positive liberty," or "non-domination" can answer it, and the answer options are verdicts — "she is not free here," "she is free," "this case doesn't settle it" — not theories. Build the branches from the logical space of the questions, the combinations of answers they allow, and only afterwards locate the established conceptions on the leaves; where a named view is silent on one of your questions and so occupies more than one leaf, or none, say that rather than forcing it. Every question must genuinely separate: if all the leaves would give the same verdict on it, cut it.

Three splits the tree has to make, because these are the ones we actually argue about.

First, whether unfreedom requires actual interference or can consist in a standing power to interfere that is never exercised. Please use at least these two cases: a patient who has been discharged, is living in the community, and has had no contact with services for months, but remains liable to recall at a clinician's discretion that has never been used against her; and a patient subject to a ward rule that is applied to her daily, but which is published, appealable, and has in fact been overturned on appeal in other patients' cases.

Second, what makes power arbitrary, if arbitrariness is what does the work. I can hear three candidates in our arguments and I want them pulled apart: power that does not track the interests of the person subject to it; power that the person subject to it has no way of contesting; and power the person has not consented to. Include cases where these come apart — I have in mind a rule that serves patients' interests well but was imposed without consultation and cannot be challenged, and a rule that is fully open to challenge but is applied in ways that pay no attention to what patients want or need.

Third, whose will matters when a person's own will is divided. Include at least the case of a patient who signed a written advance directive requesting treatment if she should later refuse during a relapse, and who is now refusing; and the case of a patient whose current refusal looks to us like an expression of the illness rather than of anything he would endorse.

The leaves should include, at minimum: freedom as the absence of interference; freedom as non-domination; freedom as self-mastery or acting on one's real will; and freedom as effective opportunity or capability. I also want a leaf for the view that only power held by a person or an office can make someone unfree, and one for the view that unpersoned arrangements can do it too — a funding formula, a waiting list, an eligibility algorithm — since this is the point on which the social worker and I keep colliding. Each leaf should carry the name of the conception, or "unnamed" if it is a position nobody has labelled, plus one sentence on the most awkward thing its holder has to accept: the implication I could put in front of my colleagues to show them the view has a cost.

Where a combination of answers turns out to be unoccupied, say briefly whether that is because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves your questions cannot separate instead of pretending the tree distinguishes them.

Start your reply with the tree itself — no preamble, no restatement of what I have asked for. Any notes that follow should be headed sections, each opening with its bottom line in the first sentence.
