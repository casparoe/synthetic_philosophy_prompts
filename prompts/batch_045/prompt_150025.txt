I work on the model-validation team at a hospital system that has spent the last two years deploying a deep model that flags likely deterioration in ward patients, and almost all of my practical arguments are with a colleague in clinical informatics who thinks our uncertainty handling is superstitious. We never ship a single calibrated probability. Our house rule — we call it the "no free precision" rule — is that whenever our ensembles disagree past a threshold, or a chart falls outside the training distribution on our out-of-distribution score, or our conformal sets come back too large to be useful, we report an interval rather than a point, and the clinicians downstream are told to read the width as a signal in its own right. My colleague wants to abolish the intervals. His proposal, which he half-jokingly calls "confidence laundering," is that whenever an interval is too wide to act on, we should replace it with its midpoint and log the width separately as a "caution flag" in the chart, so that the number people actually act on is always a plain probability. I have a strong sense that this destroys something the interval was doing, but when I try to say what, I end up saying things like "the midpoint pretends we know the shape inside the interval," which he correctly points out is just a restatement of the disagreement. Someone on our side mentioned that there is a whole formal literature on imprecise probabilities, I got as far as the Ellsberg paradox and Knightian uncertainty from my old econ reading, and now I'm doing the rest of this reading on my phone at three in the morning next to a six-month-old, so I would rather get one careful pass than a map of a literature I have no time to walk.

Here is what I think that literature is committed to, and what I want translated. As I understand it, the position is that an agent's doxastic state is properly represented not by a single probability function but by a set of them — a credal set — with lower and upper probabilities read off as the envelopes over the set, and with coherence requirements that are weaker than standard probabilism but not empty: you must not expose yourself to a sure loss, and your lower and upper bounds have to be related to each other in the right conjugacy way rather than being two numbers you made up. Updating is done by conditioning every member of the set on the evidence and taking the envelopes again, which apparently produces a genuinely strange phenomenon — I have seen it called dilation? — where conditioning can widen your interval instead of narrowing it, and can do so no matter which outcome of the experiment you condition on. And there are, as far as I can tell, at least three different decision rules you can run on top of a set: maximize the worst case over the set; treat an act as permissible if it maximizes expected utility under at least one member of the set; and treat an act as permissible if no other act beats it under every member of the set. Correct any of that if I have it muddled, but don't spend the answer on the corrections.

What I actually want is this translated into the vocabulary I use every day, as faithfully as you can manage: a nominal predictive distribution with an f‑divergence ball around it, distributionally robust optimization as worst-case minimization over that ball, an ensemble of networks as a stand-in for a posterior over parameters, temperature scaling and calibration on a held-out distribution, the split between epistemic and aleatoric uncertainty, out-of-distribution scores, and conformal prediction sets. For each of the commitments above, tell me what it becomes in those terms, what only survives after I distort it, and what has no expression in that vocabulary at all. Then give me the residue report. In particular I want to know three things, in prose: whether my colleague's midpoint move is secretly a second-order probability distribution wearing a disguise, or something worse than that; whether an out-of-distribution score or a conformal set is already an implementation of an imprecise credence or merely looks like one; and whether the residue you find is a real gap in how we engineers talk about uncertainty, or a sign that the imprecise-probability people are claiming more clarity than they have. Please write in continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables — the way you would write a long and careful email to a colleague you respect but disagree with.
