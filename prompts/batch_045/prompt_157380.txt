I am a novelist, and for some years a philosopher friend has been urging on me that my working method is the best available illustration of Molinism — that novelists have grasped middle knowledge all along without any of the metaphysics. I have begun to think she is right, and that the case is stronger than she realises. Before I say why, let me state the doctrine as I understand it, so that we are talking about the same thing: middle knowledge is God's knowledge, had prior to any decree to create, of what every merely possible creature would freely do in every circumstance in which it might be placed, and which He then consults in deciding which world to actualise — knowledge, that is, of counterfactuals of freedom that are true independently of His own will, and hence are there to be known rather than settled by Him.

My method, and the reason it seems to me to matter, is this. I do not plot. I settle who a character is, place her in a circumstance, and record what she does; my characters, as writers say, write themselves. Now distinguish two kinds of counterfactual about my character Iris. The first kind concerns scenes I do go on to write: had I put the offer of a bribe in Chapter Nine rather than Chapter Twelve, she would still have refused it and then concealed the fact that money was ever offered. These I concede are worthless to my argument, since I wrote the refusal. The interesting ones are of the second kind: counterfactuals about circumstances I never write at all. Iris is asked at a reading what she would have done had the bribe been ten times larger, or had it been offered to her brother. I have stipulated nothing about this; there is no passage in any draft, published or abandoned, that settles it. Yet such counterfactuals strike me as determinately true or false, and corrigibly so. A reader who has lived with Iris as long as I have can tell me that I have got her wrong, and I can come to see that she would have taken the money after all. Nothing I decided makes these truths true, and yet something does.

Here, then, is my question. As I understand it, the standing objection to middle knowledge is the grounding objection: there is nothing about a merely possible creature — no essence, no nature, no set of properties — that could make it true that she would freely do this rather than that in circumstances she never faces, and so there is allegedly nothing for middle knowledge to be knowledge of. But my characters seem to be precisely the case the objector declares impossible: thinly specified, non-actual, and yet such that counterfactuals about their free actions are determinate, discoverable, and answerable to something other than my own decision. If Iris can ground her own counterfactuals, why cannot a possible creature ground hers? Is knowledge of unwritten scenes not middle knowledge in all but name, and ought Molinists not to claim the author-and-creature analogy as their answer to the grounding objection rather than merely as a pretty image of providence?
