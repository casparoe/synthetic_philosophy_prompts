Write me a dialogue between two people arguing about whether thought experiments in science can really hand you knowledge about the world, or whether they're just arguments whose empirical premises got hidden in the scenery. Call them the argument view and the seeing-it-in-the-armchair view, or don't call them anything at all — I don't want citations or name-dropping, I want the arguement itself.

Setup: they're sharing a taxi back from a conference dinner, both tired, one a physicist who builds toy models for a living, the other a philosopher of science who doesn't trust intuition much. Both should be good enough that they can state the other side's position better than its fans usually do, and neither gets to win by being dimmer. Around 1000–1200 words, and they should actually answer each other rather than take turns giving speeches.

The case they keep returning to is one I made up and have never seen anywhere: a sealed tank of water ten metres tall, with a loop of cable running through it carrying identical hollow rigid spheres, evenly spaced, entering through a seal at the bottom and leaving through a seal at the top, rising through the water and coming back down through the air, the whole thing geared to a generator. The pitch is that the buoyancy on the way up is enormous and it costs nearly nothing to poke a sphere in at the bottom, so: free power. In fact it comes out at exactly zero. You can see it the slow way — shove a sphere of volume V in at depth h and you pay pressure times volume, ρghV, which is precisely what buoyancy pays you back over the height h. Or the slick way — once the cable has advanced by one sphere spacing, the water and the spheres are jointly in exactly the configuration they started in, so there is no energy to be extracted per cycle, no matter what the fluid is. Swap the water for mercury, or a compressible gas, or for some fluid whose behaviour you know nothing about; tilt the tank; put it on the moon; the verdict doesn't budge.

Things I want them to work through:

The physicist opens with the fact that this is knowledge, that she got it in her head at 1am, and that it generalises across every possible fluid — so she isn't deducing from some law of buoyancy, she's seeing a constraint that any such law has to satisfy.

The philosopher gets to point out that the slick argument is conservation of energy plus a pile of stipulations about the model (perfect seals, rigid spheres, steady state, even spacing) — none of which is a priori, and none of which is true of any machine you could actually build. Its insensitivity to the fluid's microphysics just means she's leaning on a very general framework principle, which is empirical however inevitable it feels.

Then the export problem: the verdict is about an impossible object. What licenses the move to no real machine of this type will ever work? The physicist should have something here — friction and leaky seals only make it worse, and that's a qualitative point, not a measurement. The philosopher should push back that this robustness claim is itself a continuity assumption about how real systems approximate ideal ones, and that she's quietly downgraded the claim from zero to less than zero, and you need experiment to say how much less.

At some point one of them should ask why, if it's all argument, anybody bothers with the story — why not just write the deduction down. The other one needs a real answer, not just rhetoric: the imagination seems to do work the formalism can't do yet, and sometimes you get the answer before you have the theory and the answer then tells you which theory to reach for (Einstein wondering what a light wave looks like if you run alongside it is the obvious example, keep it short, I've read all that). And the reply: the history also has armchair verdicts everyone was certain of that later got overturned, so the feeling of necessity is just the feeling of a deduction from premises you've stopped noticing.

If you can get them there, have them argue about whether what thought experiments produce is knowledge at all or something adjacent — making explicit what your existing commitments already entail. The physicist should object that this can't be right, because the results are frequently suprising and sometimes force you to abandon a commitment you liked; the philosopher gets to say that's exactly what explication feels like from the inside.

Don't wrap it up. End with each of them saying, concretely, what one thing would settle the dispute — a thought-experiment verdict that is robust and traceable to no premise at all, versus a convincing story about where the hidden empirical premise sits in every celebrated case — and let it be clear why each thinks their demand is the fair one.
