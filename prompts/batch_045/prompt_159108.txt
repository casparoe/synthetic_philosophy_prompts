I'm finishing a dissertation chapter on self-amending rules — rules that specify the procedure for their own amendment — and I keep meeting the same move in comments from my committee and in referee reports: constitutional precommitment is explained by analogy to Ulysses and the Sirens. The version I keep being handed runs roughly like this: the framers are Ulysses before he reaches the mast, the later electorate is Ulysses once the singing starts, the amendment rule is the rope, the courts are the crew instructed to ignore whatever he shouts afterward, and majority passion is the song. The conclusion drawn is that there is nothing paradoxical about a legal order that lays down the procedure for changing that very procedure: a self-amending constitution is just self-binding, self-binding is an ordinary rational activity, so the literature on paradoxes of legal self-reference — the regress worries raised about the basic norm, about constituent power, and about unamendable provisions — is addressing a problem that only looks like a problem, and unamendable provisions are legitimate for exactly the same reason.

I want the analogy audited rather than redeployed. Please work with three stipulated kinds of rule; I am defining these so that we do not end up arguing about labels:

1. Self-embracing: the procedure the rule specifies must also be used when amending that procedure itself.
2. Bootstrap: an amendment to the rule takes effect only if it was enacted in conformity with the procedure that the amendment itself lays down — including, in the limiting case, a rule that lets the entire order, itself included, be replaced by the procedure it specifies.
3. Path-dependent: the rule sets its amendment threshold by reference to the threshold used the last time the rule was amended, so that its content depends on its own amendment history.

What I would like:

- A correspondence table: what plays Ulysses at the time of binding, Ulysses under the song, the crew, the ropes, the mast, the wax in the crew's ears, the song, and the voyage.

- An account of where the mapping imports features the target lacks and where it quietly omits ones the target has. The candidates I have in mind, which you should press on rather than simply endorse: the crew's capacity to restrain Ulysses is causal and its authority is not derived from his orders, whereas a court's authority is derived from the very document it polices; the rope constrains without needing Ulysses's continuing cooperation, while an amendment rule binds only for as long as officials continue to treat it as binding; Ulysses knows the content of the song in advance, whereas framers do not know what later majorities will want; Ulysses is one agent across the episode, while the electorate is not.

- Which of those disanalogies are load-bearing for the conclusion and which are cosmetic, and whether the verdict differs across (1) through (3). My hunch is that the analogy is at its strongest for (1) and comes apart somewhere around (2) and (3); give me the argument either way rather than my hunch returned to me.

- What a legal order would have to be like for the analogy to hold exactly — an enforcer whose authority is independent of the order? a court whose jurisdiction is not granted by the document it reviews? — and whether that repair is actually available.

- Whether the no-paradox-here conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was doing the work; and, if it should be retired, what should replace it — a grammar that contains rules for changing the grammar, a bootstrapping compiler, a will naming an executor empowered to rewrite the will — with the same audit applied briefly to whatever you propose.
