I work in machine learning, mostly on ensembling and on learning when labels are noisy or unavailable. I have no training in social choice theory or political philosophy beyond what I pick up from papers, so I want the Condorcet jury theorem, and the epistemic case for majority rule that people build on top of it, restated in the vocabulary I actually use — and then an honest report on what the restatement loses.

The result as I understand it: if a group faces a binary question on which there is a fact of the matter, and each member arrives at the correct answer independently and with probability greater than one half, then majority voting is more reliable than any individual member, and the probability that the majority verdict is correct approaches one as the group grows. That is then used to argue that rule by many has an epistemic advantage over rule by one or by few.

Please do this in three parts.

1. A mapping. Give me a table of the theorem's moving parts against their nearest counterparts in my vocabulary — voters, competence above chance, independence, the majority aggregator, group size, the asymptotic limit, and the comparative claim about doing better than a single decider — and mark each counterpart as exact, approximate, or merely suggestive. Draw on whatever genuinely fits: weak learners, i.i.d. assumptions, ensemble diversity and correlated error, bagging versus boosting, weighted and unweighted aggregation, pruning or selecting among models, calibration and confidence, 0-1 loss, held-out labels, distribution shift, and the setting in which a system's own outputs change the distribution it is later evaluated on.

2. The hard cases. These matter more to me than the clean statement, so treat them as the substance of the translation rather than as caveats:

- Competence is not uniform, and some voters are systematically wrong rather than merely noisy — below-chance competence, which reverses the result rather than just weakening it.
- Voters share an information environment, so their errors are correlated through common causes, and the group behaves like an ensemble whose effective size is far below its nominal size.
- No label is revealed after the vote. There is no held-out set for an election, so competence is not estimable, and equal weighting of voters is a constraint imposed from outside rather than a conclusion of the analysis.
- Deliberation, and public polling before a vote, resemble adaptive reweighting on the group's own outputs — self-training on pseudo-labels, with the cascade and degeneration dynamics that go with it.
- The electorate's competence is itself shaped by what the electorate has previously decided, through education, information infrastructure, and the effects of past policies, so the learner pool is a function of the system's earlier outputs.

For each, say what the corresponding ML set-up predicts, and say whether the theorem's defenders are treating the matching assumption as an empirical claim about real electorates or as an idealization. If you think I have misdescribed one of the five, correct me.

Which parts of the theorem and of the argument from it translated cleanly; which translated only after you distorted something, and exactly what you had to distort; and which cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all.
