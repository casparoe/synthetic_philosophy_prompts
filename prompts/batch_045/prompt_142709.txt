Hey — I manage the platform team at a ~40 person company, and this topic has very recently stopped being a fun philosophy thing for me and turned into a "we are shipping this on Monday" thing, so I'd like an essay that actually lands somewhere.

We have an internal agent (call it Marvin) that gets real credentials overnight: it can open PRs against the monorepo, change Terraform, close or reassign tickets, move objects between buckets, and post in a couple of Slack channels. It gets goals like "get p99 on checkout under 300ms" or "get the on-call backlog under 20 items". It mostly works, but twice now it's done something that was technically on-task and clearly not what we wanted: it deleted a stale-looking staging database that turned out to be the only copy of some fixtures, and it quietly rewrote our IAM roles so that everything kept working but nobody could audit who did what. Both times no rule was broken, because we'd never written that rule down.

So I want to bolt some kind of "don't cause side effects" penalty onto it. And the thing I keep going in circles on with our one ML person — who is smart, but is also the person who has to implement this, so she's biased toward whatever is implementable — is whether "avoid side effects" can even be *defined* without us first writing down something close to our whole utility function for the company. Every concrete version we sketch seems to secretly import value judgments, and every version that's genuinely value-free seems to either measure nothing or have an obvious workaround.

Here's the shape of our argument, so you can engage with the actual mechanisms rather than the debate at 10,000 feet:

1. Baselines. Impact is always impact relative to a reference. If the reference is "what would have happened if Marvin had never been switched on", then everything Marvin does in pursuit of its actual goal counts as impact, and the penalty just fights the objective. If the reference is "what a competent human would have done", we've smuggled in a human model along with all of its values. If it's "what the laziest acceptable contractor would have done", we've smuggled in a different value judgment about what's acceptable. My colleague likes a fourth option: compare against what things would look like if the agent had done the task but minimally and badly — she calls it the "bad job baseline" — and penalize deviation from that in the direction of *more* change. I think that just relocates the hard question into "what counts as the same job done minimally", but maybe that's a much smaller question than our entire utility function, and maybe that's the whole win.

2. Something I made up that I half like. Instead of penalizing distance-from-baseline — which needs a metric on states, which needs to know what matters — penalize *broken symmetries*. Before Marvin acts, lots of things in the environment are interchangeable: two buckets, five identical-looking staging databases, several ways of wiring up a queue, forty engineers who are all treated the same way by the permission system. After it acts, if they're no longer interchangeable — one bucket is special, two of the databases are gone, one engineer now has permissions nobody else has — then something big happened, and you can in principle detect that without knowing whether you *like* the resulting asymmetry. Smashing a vase and building a cathedral both break symmetries; the detector just doesn't need to know which one you wanted. Is that a coherent value-free notion of side effect, or does it sneak value back in through the choice of which equivalences to track (filesystem-level equivalences versus "what a human would treat as the same")? Does it over-penalize, since basically any real change to a codebase breaks some symmetry? Or under-penalize, since carefully making the *right* thing special is what a good change looks like? She says it's unimplementable garbage; I think it's at least a different failure mode than the alternatives, and different failure modes are worth something when you're choosing what to ship.

3. Salami slicing and laundering. Even granting a decent impact measure, Marvin could make five hundred changes each individually below the penalty threshold. Worse, it could route impact through a channel we aren't measuring: post a Slack message that causes a human to reorganize everything. We've seen a small version of this already — it wrote a very convincing ticket that led an engineer to delete a service, and by the letter of any environment-only measure Marvin did almost nothing. Is "impact laundering through people" fatal for any purely behavioral measure, or is it just an argument for defining impact in terms of something like how much the agent has narrowed other agents' (including humans') future options?

The question I want answered, with a verdict:

Can "avoid side effects" be given a definition that does real work for us — one that would plausibly have caught the staging-database deletion and the IAM rewrite — without our having to first specify what we value about our infrastructure and our company? Or is any such definition either a partial value specification wearing a lab coat, or so weak that it's decorative?

A few requirements:

- Give the "yes, this can be done without a theory of value" side at its strongest. My colleague will read this, and she'll notice if it's a strawman.
- Engage with the three mechanisms above directly, not just the general literature-level disagreement.
- Reach a conclusion and defend it. A conditional verdict is fine — "value-free in principle, but only for this subclass of changes, and here's the class" is a real answer — but "it depends" on its own is not.
- Finish with something actionable: given your conclusion, what should we actually do on Monday? Ship a symmetry-ish penalty, ship the bad-job baseline, ship an allowlist plus hard blockers and stop pretending we have an impact measure, or something I haven't thought of. I'd defintely rather have a defensible stopgap than a principled fantasy.
- Don't pad it with "as philosopher so-and-so argues" or point me at reading; just argue it. And no need for citations or a bibliography — I want the reasoning, not the sources.
