I've been doing some grunt work for a neurophilosophy person — sorting her case notes, nothing glamorous — and she has a habit of giving a straight verdict on every case: one subject of experience, two, or "that's not a yes/no question." I want to see if I can predict her before she does it herself. Her verdicts so far:

- Ordinary complete callosotomy in an adult, tested the usual way (stuff in the right visual field gets named, stuff in the left gets pointed at with the left hand): two subjects, only one of which you can actually hold a conversation with.
- Adult who had a whole hemisphere removed for a tumour: one subject, and not a halved one — nothing like the sense of having lost half a mind.
- Someone born without a corpus callosum, language in both hemispheres, tested as an adult: one subject. The bilateral language doesn't move her.
- Only the back half of the callosum cut, front half intact: one subject, even though visual information no longer transfers.
- Alien hand after frontal damage, callosum fully intact: one subject, two agents. The hand has aims of its own but, as she puts it, no point of view behind them.
- The purely subcortical stuff that's left after a forebrain commisurotomy — shared arousal, sleep/wake cycles, nothing else: not enough. Still two.
- A made-up case somebody raised at a talk: two heads on one body, separate brains, a shared midline structure, overlapping touch and part of the visual field. Her answer: two subjects who happen to share a body and partly share what they feel. Not one.

She also said something offhand that I wrote down: in the slow cases, where a lesion disconnects the hemispheres over months instead of a surgeon doing it in an afternoon, there's no determinate moment at which one becomes two, and she'd refuse to give a verdict before the disconnection is complete.

Now the new case. Same complete surgical callosotomy, but the patient gets an implant: it reads a very coarse summary of each hemisphere's state — roughly valence plus arousal, refreshed a few times a second — and stimulates the other side so that it reproduces it. No content at all: no images, no words, no object identities, nothing he can name or point to. After six months: he reports a new background mood he can't source and says it feels like it's coming from another part of him; the old conflict cases mostly stop happening (the left hand no longer does things the speaking hemisphere denies knowing about); and he is still at chance when asked to guess what the right hemisphere is being shown. The hemispheres are surgically as disconnected as ever — the device is the only route between them.

What does she say: one subject, two, or "not a yes/no question"? I'd guess two, but I can't really say why, and I suspect I'm just anchoring on the surgery. What I actually want is the rule she looks like she's running, the other rules that fit her seven verdicts just as well, what each of them predicts about this case, which one you'd bet on and why, and which of her earlier verdicts would be doing the most work in that bet. And: if you could hand her exactly one more case to settle it, what would it be? I was going to propose cranking the implant up until it passes one word or one object category at a time, but I'm not sure that actually separates anything.
