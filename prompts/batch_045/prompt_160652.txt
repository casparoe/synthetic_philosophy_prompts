I'm revising the session on probabilistic theories of conditionals for a graduate seminar, and I want to hand students a defensible statement of Lewis's triviality argument rather than a slogan. The survey article I've been using as a refresher puts it like this:

"Lewis's 1976 argument shows that the central idea of the probabilistic approach is unworkable. The idea is the equation: the probability of 'if A then C' equals the conditional probability P(C|A). Assume the equation holds for every pair of propositions in the language. Conditionalize on C: given C the conditional has probability 1, given not-C it has probability 0, so its unconditional probability is just P(C). By the equation, P(C|A) = P(C) for arbitrary A and C: every proposition must be probabilistically independent of every other, which no genuine probability function can deliver. Hence (i) conditional probability, useful as it is, is not really a probability but a bookkeeping device; (ii) the Ramsey–Adams program, on which your confidence in a conditional is your conditional confidence, is refuted; (iii) the culprit is the assumption that a conditional expresses a proposition with a truth value, so the remedy is simply to move to a three-valued setting, de Finetti-style conditional events that are void when the antecedent fails, and this costs nothing, since the usual Boolean algebra of propositions is left intact; (iv) the argument needs a rich space, at least four atoms, so an agent with a coarse algebra can keep the equation."

I'm fairly confident about (iii): the three-valued escape is a real option, but I take it you pay for it in Boolean structure and in the treatment of embedding. Beyond that I don't trust my own judgment, and I can't tell whether the proof sketch quietly assumes more than the equation itself. So go through the passage claim by claim — what a specialist would accept, what they'd wince at, what they'd reject — and tell me which of (i) to (iv) actually follow from the argument as stated.

Three things I'd particularly like checked. First, my sense is that the proof only goes through if the equation is required to hold for the agent's conditionalized credence functions as well, not merely for her current one, and that this is the real hinge rather than anything about truth values. Second, a colleague insists the argument essentially uses conditionals inside conditionals, so a flat, unnested fragment — a fixed finite partition of the questions the agent might settle — is safe; my own attempt suggests the collapse already follows from three suitably overlapping propositions and needs no nesting, but I can't do the counting reliably. Third, I half-remember a separate impossibility result about belief revision and the Ramsey test, and I suspect the summary is running the two together; if so, what distinguishes them? I'd also value your view on what belongs on the handout as the fair, non-overstated version of the result.
