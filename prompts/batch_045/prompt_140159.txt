I work adjacent to this literature rather than in it, so I may be abusing some of its vocabulary; correct me where I do.

Here is the case I cannot get straight. In a study I have been reading, subjects viewed a brief array of twelve colored letters. On a random third of the trials a post-cue asked them to name the letter at one cued location; on the remaining trials no cue and no response were required. On those uncued trials the experimenters decoded, from MEG, a sustained content-specific pattern that distinguished which letter had stood at each location, using a classifier trained on the cued trials. Three further things held on uncued trials: the decoded content predicted the size of the pupil response to the array, it predicted which of two arbitrarily paired symbols the subject would pick minutes later in an unrelated preference task, and it was decodable from sensors over prefrontal cortex. Yet at the end of the block subjects said they had seen two or three letters clearly and the rest as a blurred smear, and in a control session in which they were asked after every trial whether they had seen anything at all, they denied seeing a letter on trials where the decoder still succeeded.

My reading is that this is a global-workspace result, and that it refutes the theory. As I understand it, the theory says that a representation is conscious just in case it is globally broadcast: sustained, content-specific activity in a frontoparietal workspace that makes the content available to the full set of consumer systems. Here we have content that is sustained, decodable from prefrontal sensors, and demonstrably available to downstream systems — it moves the pupil and it moves a later choice — while the subject reports no experience of it. That is global availability without phenomenal consciousness, which is the one combination the theory rules out. And because this is a no-report paradigm, the usual complaint does not apply: the decoder is reading experience off the brain itself rather than off what subjects are willing to say, so there is no report confound left to appeal to.

Assuming I have the result right, two questions. First: should defenders of the global-workspace framework simply concede that it has been empirically refuted as a theory of consciousness, and that a framework which starts from the phenomenology and derives a physical measure from it — integrated information theory, say — is the more promising successor? Second, if they should not concede, what exactly does a result like this show: evidence that phenomenal consciousness overflows access, evidence about the neural correlate of reporting, or something else?

I would rather have a verdict than a survey of positions. Tell me which reading you think is right, how confident you are, where your own commitments are doing the work, and what further result would change your mind. And if the way I have framed this is confused — about what the theory actually claims, about whether decodability from prefrontal sensors amounts to broadcast, about what counts as a downstream system in the relevant sense, or about what a no-report paradigm can establish — please say so plainly instead of answering the question as I have put it. I would also be grateful if you did not settle for the observation that the hard problem is hard.
