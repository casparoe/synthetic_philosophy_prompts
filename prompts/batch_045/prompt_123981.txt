I teach an upper-level seminar on social epistemology and democratic theory — 18 students, one 90-minute slot, a room with a projector and movable chairs, no budget for cash prizes, but I have index cards, two colours of dot stickers, envelopes, a coin and a deck of cards. I want an activity that makes them feel the Condorcet jury theorem as a mechanism with load-bearing assumptions rather than as a wisdom-of-crowds slogan, and I want them to build the pattern themselves: no statement of the theorem up front, nobody hears the name until the debrief, and I say nothing about independence or competence until they have already voted several times.

Rough shape I have in mind, which you should feel free to redesign: there is a true state of the world, each student gets a private signal of a reliability I control, they vote simultaneously by secret ballot, and we tally. What I want them to notice, in roughly this order: with reliability only just above chance and 19 voters, the majority is barely better than a coin flip even though the result is supposed to go to certainty, so the asymptotics are a terrible guide at seminar-sized numbers; if I flip the signal reliability just below chance, the same aggregation runs backwards and the majority ends up confidently wrong more often than any single voter; then give everyone the same extra public signal — one blurry piece of evidence on the projector that they all see — and watch a ceiling appear, with the error rate refusing to fall however many voters I add. Then, and this is the bit I care about most, run one round sequential and aloud with the identical set of private signals as an earlier simultaneous round: same people, same information, opposite result, because what changed was the mechanism and not the world. I also want a round where two students get high-accuracy signals and the other seventeen get near-chance ones, so the class has to decide whether to vote at all or to defer to the specialists, since I have a hobbyhorse that the interesting question is not whether many beat few but what you buy with a fixed budget of attention or money, many cheap weak signals or a few expensive good ones, and I want to see whether the activity can make that concrete. And if there is room, one round where the two error types carry very different costs, so they have to ask whether the asymmetry belongs in the aggregation rule or in the threshold for acting on a given majority, and whether a supermajority requirement is doing epistemic work or just expressing caution.

Give me the rules, the materials, how to randomise cleanly on the fly, and a payoff scheme that needs no money, plus a debrief that maps each moment onto the part of the theorem it was meant to expose. Then the honest part: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — the student who holds up their card, the confident first speaker who anchors the sequential round, the run where the majority just gets unlucky and the class draws exactly the lesson I didn't want, the argument about whether my deck was really shuffled — and how I should handle each, including what to do when the demonstration plain fails. Be frank too about what the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to say out loud afterwards; I strongly suspect the convergence claim itself is the big one, since 19 people can't show anybody a limit, and probably also anything about voters choosing whether to acquire a signal in the first place. Last thing, and I mean it: I know this literature well enough not to need a reading list, so don't send me citations, and please seperate what is just the arithmetic of the theorem given its assumptions, which I take to be settled, from what is genuinely contested among people who work on it — whether any real electorate satisfies anything in that neighbourhood, and what if anything follows for democracy — and I definately want you to flag my budget framing as my own hobbyhorse if that is what it is, rather than nodding along with it. Continuous prose please, like an email: no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables.
