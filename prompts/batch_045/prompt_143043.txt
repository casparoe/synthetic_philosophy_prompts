Hey — I could use a careful take on something I'm stuck on.

I'm on a working group that by spring has to hand the council a recommendation about two things named after the same man: a street running through the middle of town, and an elementary school. He was a nineteenth-century mill owner who paid for the library and a hospital wing, and who also spent roughly twenty years campaigning for the compulsory sterilization of people he called, in his own pamphlets, the unfit. So neither half of him is a footnote; both are load-bearing, and that's exactly why the group keeps going in circles.

I don't want an explanation of the ethics of commemoration. I want an audit of three analogies that keep doing work in the room, because I think one of them is quietly deciding the outcome and I definately can't tell which.

First, the one that comes up every single time from the people against any change: taking the name down is erasing history. It gets sharpened into comparisons with airbrushing people out of photographs, or with burning the books. The conclusion it's there to support is that leaving things alone is the epistemically humble option and removal is a kind of vandalism against knowledge.

Second, the one our pro-renaming members use: a name is an honor, not an assertion. It's a eulogy rather than a biography — it says we admire this person, it doesn't say that he existed or what he did, so there is no history for removal to erase. Conclusion: renaming costs us nothing epistemically and buys something morally.

Third, our chair's compromise framing, which I find the most seductive and the most suspicious: public space is like a version-controlled document. Nothing is ever really deleted, the old name persists in the archive and in the record, we're just moving the pointer and adding an interpretive layer on top. Conclusion: we can have both, and the fight was fake.

For each: what maps onto what (a table is fine), where the mapping breaks, and — the part I actually care about — what each one smuggles in. I can see some of it. The first seems to treat the built environment as an archive and commemoration as a kind of belief; the second treats it as a speech act with a speaker and an audience; the third treats it as a record-keeping problem where what matters is retrievability by someone who goes looking. I can't get clear on which frame is right, and I suspect the answer differs between the street and the school, since one is a name people have to say out loud constantly in order to get where they're going and the other is a name a few hundred kids and parents say. Also, every street needs some name, so no commemoration isn't on the table — the baseline is always some other name — and I think that's doing more work inside the first analogy than anyone admits.

One more thing I want pressed. A member keeps pushing a category I'd never heard of, 'dormant commemoration': the idea that lots of names have stopped doing any commemorative work and are now pure addresses, strings nobody associates with a person. Her claim is that for dormant names the whole honor-versus-erasure framing is a category error and renaming is morally neutral. Is that a real distinction, or a way of defining the problem away — nobody under forty knows who he was, but the people who put the name up presumably did? And can renaming wake a dormant name back up, turning a string into a monument again precisely by fighting about it, so that the intervention produces the thing the second analogy says it was removing?

Then: does the conclusion each analogy was wheeled in to support survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and if all three are bad, what's the better one, if there is one. Argue it out — I want the reasoning, not pointers to literature.
