I size bets the usual way: pick the fraction of my pile that maximises the expected log of what I'll have after the bet. Call the gap between my estimated chance and the price I'm being offered my "edge". Two things I keep running into.

First: when I plot how fast the pile compounds against the fraction I bet, I get a very lopsided hill. The peak sits at the fraction equal to my edge. If I bet twice that fraction, my growth rate comes out at essentially zero in the sims — I'm not shrinking much, but I'm not going anywhere either, no matter how many rounds I run. If I bet half of it, I keep something like three quarters of the best possible compounding. So guessing too big is near-fatal and guessing too small is a mild haircut. Consequence: whenever I'm unsure about my own numbers I shade down, hard.

Second: the same lopsidedness seems to show up when I'm not betting my own numbers but combining two people's. Say two friends each give me a probability for the same repeated event. I can merge their probabilities into one number and bet that, or I can split my money in half and let each of them bet their own number on their own half. What I get depends on how they're wrong. If their mistakes point in opposite directions and roughly cancel, merging beats splitting hands down. If one of them is plainly closer to the truth, splitting wins — but only after a few hundred rounds; for the first few dozen it just looks like a pointless compromise, and then the better friend's half slowly takes over everything.

Now the bit I can't pin down. The shading-down instinct in the first case doesn't feel like risk aversion to me: I'd happily take a wildly swingy bet if I was confident about the odds, and I don't care about smooth wealth for its own sake.
