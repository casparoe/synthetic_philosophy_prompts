Me and two mates from my old philosophy department have been running an informal reading group for about three years, and we've spent the last eight months or so completely stuck on dialetheism in a way that's starting to feel like those arguments where everyone knows everyone else's moves in advance. Here's the shape of it. One of us is a convinced dialetheist: thinks the liar and its strengthened cousins, plus naive comprehension and maybe a few odd cases in law or fiction, are best handled by allowing that some contradictions are true, that explosion is not a valid rule, and that the right logic is something like the Logic of Paradox with extra machinery bolted on. The other two resist, but for reasons that don't line up with each other. One thinks the paraconsistentist has simply changed the subject: once 'not' no longer excludes what it negates, you aren't denying a classical claim, you're saying something else in a shared vocabulary. The other is happy to grant the semantics as a formal object but thinks the real question is whether the dialetheist can get classical reasoning back where it matters — modus ponens, the classical theorems for the consistent fragment — and that this recapture burden is where the position either succeeds or quietly collapses into something that can't do ordinary reasoning, or that has smuggled consistency back in through the conditional. And every move has a counter-move: the gap theorist gets the strengthened liar thrown at them, the dialetheist gets told that paraconsistent techniques handle the strengthened liar just fine without costing you any true contradictions, the dialetheist produces a conditional, the classicist says that conditional just reimposes what was supposed to be at issue. We've been round this loop maybe four times now.

My own hypothesis — and I'd rather it got pressure-tested than confirmed — is that the whole thing is really a fight about what a logic is supposed to be, and that 'are there true contradictions?' isn't one question but two that people keep answering interchangeably: a descriptive question about whether the best formal model of the semantic and inferential practice we actually have contains gluts, and a normative question about which inferences we are entitled to make. I've also been toying with a position somebody suggested to me in the pub, which I genuinely cannot tell is deep or just a cop-out: grant gluts at the level of models — accept that the right semantics for truth, or for naïve comprehension, assigns a sentence and its negation both some positive status — but deny that this has any revisionary consequence for inference, because what you may infer is fixed by a separate, classical-looking normative layer. Call it 'inert glut realism' if you like; I made the name up. I can't work out whether that's a real third option, whether it's dialetheism with the interesting part deleted, or whether it's secretly what the recapture project is trying to buy in the first place, and whether it collapses as soon as you ask what makes the normative layer classical if the semantics isn't.

So: what kind of stand-off is this, actually? Is it verbal, a fight about 'not' or 'true' or 'valid' where each side is right about its own thing? Is it a disagreement about what counts as data — natural-language paradox intuitions versus mathematical practice versus the cost to everyday reasoning? Is there a hidden formal question doing the work, about recapture, or about how strong the conditional has to be, or about whether a logic without explosion is even the sort of thing that can be correct or incorrect? Or is it bedrock: two pictures of what logic is for, neither of which can be argued for? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of the positions and definitely not a reading list — we've read plenty on both sides, that's not what's missing. And whatever you think the diagnosis is, tell me concretely what would count as progress: a distinction that should be drawn, a formal result that would settle something, a reframing of the question, or — if you think no progress should be expected here — say why, and say what that would tell us about the question itself.
