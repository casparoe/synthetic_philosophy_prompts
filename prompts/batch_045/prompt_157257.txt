hi — I'm on the collections committee of the special collections library where I work and I've got to circulate a decision memo before Thursday's meeting, and I genuinely don't know what I think yet, so I need the memo more than I need to be argued at.

The case: about eight years ago we took in the working papers of the labour and welfare office of the Kestrel Brook Colliery Company, 1929–1984 — housing allocation ledgers with "housekeeping" grades, conduct cards rating roughly four thousand named men (and noting their families) on things like docility and regularity, clinic inspection books, the welfare officer's correspondence, that sort of thing. Almost nothing in the whole collection was written by the people it's about; the only exceptions are a few petition letters that survive purely because the company filed them as enclosures to its own memos, sometimes with the manager's scribble in the margin. So the workers' own words are in the archive only as a byproduct of a filing decision made by the people they were complaining about. The one place the collection is loudest is where the company cared: one woman has eleven medical inspection entries and not a single sentence she wrote herself. The 1953 strike shows up as maybe one line in a ledger plus a reference to a correspondence file that isn't in theboxes — and we can't tell whether it was destroyed or just weeded. Now the Kestrel Brook Descendants' Association and an oral historian have proposed we commission a hundred-ish life-history interviews in 2026–27, take them on deposit under some kind of co-ownership arrangement, describe them in the same finding aid as the company papers with item-level links both directions, and insert what they call "silence notes" at series level wherever the company papers are systematically mute (the strike, women's and kids' own arrangements, the informal subletting of allotments, the names of the men the company used as informants). Two catches: it costs about what three years of digitising the company papers would cost, and we'd already more or less told the research community that digitisation was next in line; and the association is currently putting together a compensation claim, so some of these interviews are going to be made partly in the shadow of that claim. Also the interviews will name foremen, a company doctor, and neighbours — sometimes alleging violence or collusion — and a lot of those people's grandchildren still live in the village, and our practice with sensitive identifiable data is pretty ad hoc (we shut files for a fixed period and that's about it), so we have no policy at all for born-digital interviews: can someone embargo until after they die, can someone withdraw after deposit, what do we do about the third parties who never consented and never will.

I had a three-hour fight with my friend about this last night which ended badly, and her version is: the co-description is a "parity illusion" — put a 2026 interview and a 1948 conduct card side by side with links between them and they start to look like two symmetrical sources on the same event, when one is a contemporaneous act of bureaucracy and the other is memory formed seventy years later and partly out of the company's own stories, and researchers will cite them as mutually corroborating when all the interview can really be is evidence about what the company did to what people later said. She thinks the silence notes are worse: they turn an absence into a catalogued item, so "nothing survives about the strike" becomes a thing in the catalogue that gets treated as a documented finding, and the gap stops feeling like a gap — her line was that we'd be filling the silence with a plaque saying this was a silence, which is how an archive quietly launders its own bias into findings. She also says the co-ownership part is a decoy, that we're buying consent with a title while keeping every real decision. I half believe her and half think she's using a good argument as cover for the comfortable option — just digitise the company papers, which is what our profession already knows how to do and which mostly benefits people who already have institutional access. Meanwhile our head of technical services says provenance is not negotiable: the interviews are a different creator, they get their own collection with a pointer, and putting archivist-authored interpretation in a finding aid makes it look like description. What I need from you is the memo itself: a decision document for the committee that lands on an actual bottom line we can vote on Thursday, with phasing and dates, that says yes / no / yes-with-conditions on taking the interviews at all, on co-describing them with the company papers versus separate collection with pointers, on the silence notes (in the finding aid, in some separate editorial document, or nowhere), on consent and embargo and withdrawal and what we owe to the third parties named in the interviews, and on how the three-year digitisation money gets spent. It has to give the losing side its best day in court rather than flattening it — she's going to read it, and she can smell condescension from fifty paces.
