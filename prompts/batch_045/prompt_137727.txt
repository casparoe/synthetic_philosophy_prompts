I'm taking an upper-level metaphysics seminar and we've just spent two weeks on the special composition question, reading van Inwagen on the argument from vagueness and Lewis on unrestricted composition. I also picked up a popular-level philosophy book from the library that compresses the whole debate into a single paragraph, and I'd like to know how much of it I can rely on before I build part of my essay around it. I've reconstructed the paragraph below in my own words (I no longer have the page in front of me, so this is a close paraphrase, not a transcription):

"Metaphysicians ask the special composition question: under what conditions do some things add up to one thing? In the end there are only two serious answers. Universalism says: always. Take any things whatsoever — your left shoe and the Eiffel Tower — and there is an object that they compose. Nihilism says: never. Composition never occurs, so there are no tables, chairs or people, only mereological simples, the smallest material bits, arranged tablewise or personwise; it is a job for physics to tell us what those bits are. The tempting middle answers, such as the view that some things compose something only when the result is a living organism, are eliminated by the argument from vagueness: every restricted rule generates borderline cases, as with a chair and a chair-plus-one-adjacent-atom, and since there cannot be vague objects or vague existence, no restricted rule can be correct. That leaves always or never, and universalism plainly wins. Composition obviously occurs at least sometimes — there are chairs — and nothing could single out one stopping point as the uniquely right one; any restriction would simply be arbitrary. The price, which universalists pay willingly, is an ontologically bloated world: corresponding to every plurality of things there is an object, so the things of ordinary life are not among the world's real furniture, and most ordinary counting claims, like the claim that there are exactly two things on the table, come out false."

Two things make me suspicious, though I can't say whether either is decisive. First, from the van Inwagen reading I took away that his own positive answer is a restricted one — composition occurs only when the result is a living organism — and that he arrives at it partly by using the argument from vagueness against other restricted answers. So the book's claim that the middle of the spectrum has been eliminated, and that the real fight is only between universalism and nihilism, sounds like it has van Inwagen backwards. Second, my professor mentioned in passing a position she labelled "innocent universalism": accept unrestricted composition, but hold that the quantifiers in ordinary speech range over a contextually restricted domain, so that "there are exactly two things on the table" can be perfectly true even though the shoe-and-Eiffel-Tower fusion exists. If that is coherent, the book's conclusion that universalism forces ordinary counting claims to come out false seems to collapse.

Could you go through the paragraph claim by claim and tell me what someone working in mereology would accept, what they would wince at as a simplification, and what they would reject outright — and whether the paragraph's final conclusion about ordinary counting claims follows from anything true in it? More specifically: is the either-always-or-never framing faithful to how the space of answers is actually set up, or does it misdescribe what the restricted views are claiming; is the argument from vagueness being stated in a form van Inwagen would recognise, and does it refute restricted answers or merely put a burden on them that many think can be met; is non-arbitrariness really universalism's central advantage, or is that a popularizer's version of a different argument; does the gloss on simples — that they are the smallest bits of matter which physics will eventually identify — misstate what a mereological simple is, or what the nihilist is committed to about there being any; and is the step from "composition is unrestricted" to "ordinary objects are not really part of the world's furniture" a conflation of two distinct claims, one about what exists and one about what exists fundamentally? Finally, does my professor's innocent universalism actually dissolve the counting worry, or does it relocate it somewhere equally uncomfortable — for instance into the account of context and domain restriction that it needs? I don't need references or further reading; I want the conceptual corrections, and I'd rather hear where my own two suspicions go wrong than have them confirmed.
