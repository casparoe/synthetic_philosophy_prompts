I have just come out of a long argument with a friend about a system my hospital network is procuring, and I would like something better than the stalemate we reached. The system recommends how a scarce, high-cost therapy should be allocated in a weekly round among eligible patients: it ranks the candidates, attaches a short written justification, and a clinician signs off. In practice the sign-off is a click-through, and the justification goes into the patient's file and to the appeals board. My friend's position, pressed to its end, was that the machine does no ethics at all: the ethics is in the policy our committee wrote and in the law, the machine is a calculator that applies them, and where the policy is silent that is a gap for us to close rather than a judgment for the machine to make. Mine was that the policy is silent in exactly the cases that matter, that falling back on the existing queue order is itself an allocation with winners and losers, and that a system which emits a justification is doing something we are designing either deliberately or by default. We eventually noticed that we were using the same words for quite different architectures, could not say what the alternatives actually were, and gave up.

So I want a classification instrument I can put in front of our oversight committee — two clinicians, two administrators, one ethicist, one engineer, one patient representative, none of them specialists in this literature. Specifically: a decision tree that takes someone from their initial reaction to this procurement through to a named position on what ethical reasoning, if any, should be built into the system. Eight branch nodes, nine to twelve leaves, each leaf carrying a short label and one sentence on the position's most awkward commitment.

The design constraints that matter to me:

Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: give me the separating questions and the combinations of answers they permit first, then locate the actual positions in that space, and tell me where the familiar textbook categories land — the top-down rule-based / bottom-up learning / hybrid trichotomy, and the ladder that runs from merely operational morality up to something like full moral agency. I want to know what the tree reveals that those categories hide.

Every node must be a question my committee could answer without knowing which leaf it leads to, and without any prior view about machine moral agency. No node may be phrased as an opinion about whether machines can be moral agents — that is what my friend and I were actually arguing about, and I want the tree to sort us by what we think should be built instead. Where a node turns on a factual question we do not yet have an answer to, say so explicitly and say what would settle it.

Five separators must appear, though you may rephrase them: (1) the underspecification remainder — when the written policy and the score do not settle a case, does the system escalate to a human panel, defer to the attending clinician, default to the existing queue order, extrapolate the spirit of the policy, or choose the allocation with the best expected outcome under an estimated objective; (2) the counterfactual-policy question — if our committee had written a different, or a corrupt, policy, should the system's recommendations change to match, which separates designs that track a principal from designs that track outcomes or reasons; (3) annotator disagreement — when the clinicians whose judgments the system learns from disagree, is that noise to be averaged over, evidence about which judgment is better supported, or a reason to shrink the system's discretion; (4) advising versus acting — whether it ranks and recommends or actually withholds and allocates, and whether ranking counts as acting once sign-off is a click-through; (5) reason-transparency — whether the system must produce a justification a clinician and an appeals board can check, even on the assumption that a less transparent design would allocate better by our own lights. Choose the remaining three yourself, and if you think one of mine is the wrong fork, drop it and say why.

Please also give me: at least three combinations of answers that no position occupies, each with a one-line diagnosis of why the region is empty — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; any pair of leaves your tree cannot in fact separate, flagged as such rather than forced apart; and a short diagnostic shortcut of the three questions that do most of the pruning, with a note on how much of the space each one eliminates.

Then place the two of us. My commitments: the policy's silences are unavoidable and designing for them is unavoidable too; I do not want the system's recommendations to track whatever we happened to write down, since I can imagine our committee writing something bad; I think clinician disagreement is mostly evidence about which judgments are better supported rather than noise; I want checkable justifications even at some cost in allocation quality; and I am uneasy about training on judgments made by clinicians who were looking at the system's own earlier recommendations. My friend's commitments: the ethics is upstream in the policy and the law; the system should track the policy we actually wrote, and if the policy is bad the remedy is to fix the policy; clinician disagreement should be aggregated rather than adjudicated; justifications matter mainly for audit and can be templated; and the feedback loop does not worry him, since the clinicians remain responsible. Tell me which leaves are consistent with each of us, which answers you had to infer because our argument never reached them, and if our commitments fail to pin down a single leaf, give me the extra question that would separate us.

Finally, commit. Recommend the one leaf you would defend for this procurement and argue for it against the two strongest rivals rather than presenting the options and leaving us to choose; end with the single objection to your own recommendation that you are least confident you can answer, and with your honest estimate of how likely a committee working through this tree in good faith is to land where you did. Please keep this usable as a one-page handout plus at most a few paragraphs of prose, and do not include citations, footnotes, or suggestions for further reading — my committee will not follow them, and I am not asking who has defended which view, only which view we should build.
