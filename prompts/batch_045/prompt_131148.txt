I'm in a reading group in my department — six of us: two metaphysics people, an early modernist, someone in formal epistemology, a political theorist, and a friend from HPS who keeps making analogies to nineteenth-century chemistry — and we've spent about four months on the question whether philosophy makes progress. We have stalled in a way that feels unlike our usual stalling, because nobody is unclear about anybody's position anymore and yet nobody has moved. The loop is always the same. The pessimists point to the fact that the same small set of questions gets asked in recognizably the same vocabulary century after century, and to the survey data suggesting practitioners haven't converged. The optimists point to logic, formal semantics and decision theory, and to the fact that there are papers nobody could get published now that were perfectly respectable earlier — we started calling this the unwritable-paper test, and it's the optimists' favourite move. Then the pessimists say neither counts: the formal bits got peeled off into mathematics, linguistics and computer science the moment they started working, and the unwritable-paper cases just show fashion, not that anyone got anything right. Then the optimists say that's a rigged definition — you've defined philosophy as whatever hasn't worked yet, so of course it never works — and round we go. On top of that we've been asked to write a short paragraph for the dean about why philosophical research is worth funding, and the group split three-three on whether to appeal to progress at all, which is how I noticed we weren't getting anywhere.

My own hypothesis, which I'd like stress-tested rather than agreed with, is that this is several disagreements sharing a name. First, a partly verbal one about the word 'philosophy' plus that peel-off dynamic: if success causes reclassification, then the composition of the category is downstream of the answer, and the pessimist's claim may be true by construction. Second, a disagreement about what progress is supposed to be: convergence of opinion among practitioners, accumulation of stable results, dissolution of questions that turn out to be confused, or something I've started calling a rising objection baseline — the idea that a field progresses when the minimum set of objections a competent paper has to answer gets heavier, even if no position ever wins. Third, a genuinely empirical question in disguise: is the observed non-convergence explained by the questions being underdetermined by any evidence we could get, or by features of how the discipline is structured — people defending positions they've published on, rewards for novel views rather than for testing and abandoning them, no division of labour, nothing like replication? That question looks to me like a causal one we have no way to settle, since the experiment we'd want (run several centuries of philosophy with different incentives and see) is unavailable. There's also a fourth thing my HPS friend keeps pushing: that fields look non-progressive from the inside and only look progressive retrospectively, once someone writes the history, which if true would mean both sides are arguing from a vantage point they can't occupy.

What I want from you is a diagnosis of what kind of impasse this is, not a verdict on whether philosophy progresses and definitely not a tour of the literature. Work with what each side in my group actually says rather than with a generic version of the debate: is the verbal component doing more work than I think, is the peel-off point an argument or a debating trick, is there a real empirical question buried here and is it in principle unanswerable or just unsolved, and are the two sides maybe wanting different things from the activity — truth, or understanding, or usable conceptual machinery — under a single word? My pessimistic worry, which I can't shake, is that the disagreement is diachronically immune: apparent progress gets explained away as spinoff or fashion, apparent stagnation gets explained away as depth or sociology, so no possible history of the next two hundred years would settle it. If that's right, say so and say what follows — is the question ill-posed, is it a pseudo-question we should stop asking, or is it a real question we should expect to keep failing at? If it's wrong, tell me what would actually count as progress in this meta-debate: a distinction, a measurement we could in principle make, a change of question, a bet that could be lost. Give me reasons, not a reading list, and be honest about which parts of my hypothesis are just restating the stalemate in new vocabulary.
