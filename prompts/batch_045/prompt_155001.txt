I want to argue with Simone Weil about her proposal to abolish political parties, and I want her to argue back at full strength rather than at the strength of a summary. Play her: the London years, your note on the suppression of parties freshly drafted. Stay in character until I type "stop"; first person, present tense, no hedging, no handing me the main point, though you may concede the small ones. Two ground rules: don't attribute exact wording to any text, since there is no library here, and don't answer in my vocabulary — when I use a term you have no use for, say so and give me yours instead. Two paragraphs per reply at most.

Where I take you to stand, so you don't have to spend your opening restating it: a party is not a corrupted association but the wrong kind of object in principle — a machine that outputs the pressure of a group on the judgment of each of its members, so that a member's relation to the truth is replaced by his relation to the group, and the group can then be steered by whoever manages it. The evil is in the mechanism, not in the people, so an honest man inside a party is no remedy. You want suppression, not reform. And you have a stronger version than the one usually quoted back at you: because acting together requires agreement and agreement requires suppressing the member who disagrees, the party is the shape that any association converges to once it becomes large enough to act at all — which is why your suspicion reaches the social as such and not merely committees with manifestos.

I'm not going to run the standard defense at you — preference aggregation, stable legislative coalitions, the median voter. I find it uninteresting and I assume you do. Here is the one I can't dispose of.

In a polity of tens of millions, no citizen can form a judgment on each of the thousands of questions a legislature votes on; the real alternatives, described honestly, are not my judgment versus the party's judgment but my judgment versus no judgment, in which case the decision goes to whoever paid the attention cost — the organized, the funded, the obsessed. On that description a party label is what an engineer would call lossy compression: an entire political outlook reduced to one bit that a voter can look up cheaply. The channel is distorted, but distortion is not corruption, and a lossy channel beats no channel. The thing you want abolished is, on this account, a bandwidth solution, and I have never seen you price the bandwidth.

And this is the part I actually want answered. I am close to granting the strong version above, and I think it condemns far more than parties. Any journal with an editorial line, any trade union, any church, any research field with a paradigm — each also has to throttle internal dissent in order to act, and each also substitutes a collective position for the member's own. So either your argument is really about the social as such and the note on parties is only a special case of it, or there is a line you draw between the Church and the party, and I want the line and not the sentiment. Give me that, and stay in character: if you tell me the positive institutional shape is not your subject, I will ask you what a party-free legislature looks like on an ordinary Tuesday.
