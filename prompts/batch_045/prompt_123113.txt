I'm in the second year of a philosophy MA and I share an office with a physics PhD student who builds large-scale simulators; we've fallen into the habit of arguing after the Thursday cognitive science seminar, and last night's argument — after a guest lecture on whether large language models can be said to believe anything — went on far too long over cold dumplings and ended unresolved, so I'd like an outside ruling on it.

Here is where we got stuck. My officemate's position was that, on the view associated with Dennett, belief attribution is just a matter of whether a certain predictive strategy pays off: if treating a system as an agent with beliefs and desires lets you predict its behaviour better than any available alternative, then the pattern is real and the system really has those beliefs; since that strategy plainly pays off for contemporary language models, we should say they have beliefs and stop making a mystery of it. I resisted this with a pair of cases I've been calling stance-inverting twins. Twin A: take a trained network and apply a transformation that leaves its input–output behaviour exactly as it was but scrambles its internal organisation so thoroughly that no function can be read off its parts any more — nothing in the machinery supports any designed-for-this-purpose story — even though treating it as a believer still predicts it beautifully. Twin B: a system built to a precise specification, whose designer's rationale is perfectly legible and which behaves exactly as specified, but which was deliberately engineered so that predicting it by attributing beliefs and desires — even optimally charitable ones — does measurably worse than predicting it from its design. Twin A seems to me to show that the intentional stance can be predictively indispensable while the design stance is empty; Twin B seems to show the reverse. My officemate replied that these are mere curiosities, that the real question is which stance describes what is really going on, and that the answer has to be the physical stance, because it is the only complete one: every event in these systems is physically determined, and in principle a sufficiently detailed simulation reproduces every output exactly, whereas the design and intentional descriptions are lossy approximations we resort to only because our simulations are too expensive. So, he concluded, the other two stances are at best pragmatically useful fictions whose usefulness is an artefact of our computational poverty, and any pattern they find is 'real' only relative to a predictor with a limited budget — which, he pressed, is just instrumentalism about the very thing this view wanted to be realist about. (I also tried a third move — that if a real pattern is just a regularity permitting compression, then any behavioural record can be compressed by some description or other, so the criterion is trivially satisfied — and he said that was obviously a bad objection without saying why, which I'd also like explained.)

My first question is whether his argument, if it works, really does collapse pattern-realism: once you grant that the physical stance is complete and the others are approximations to it, is there anything left for the realism to be a realism about? I keep feeling that the three stances are being treated here as three descriptions of one and the same sequence of physical states, ranked by how accurately they track it — and that if that is the right picture then he wins — but I also have a nagging sense that this picture is precisely what the notion of a real pattern was meant to undermine, and I can't say why. My second question is more specific: which stance is supposed to adjudicate my two twins, and is there any stance-independent fact of the matter about whether Twin A really has the beliefs we attribute to it while Twin B really lacks them? He says yes, the physical stance adjudicates, and the answer is that neither has beliefs; I want to say that the twins show the intentional stance's verdicts coming apart from the design stance's, and that this is somehow the whole point. I don't want a reading list or a list of papers — I have plenty of those — I want the mistake in the framing identified, and I'd like to know honestly whether my twins are doing any work or whether they rest on the same confusion as his argument.
