I'm putting together something for my grad reading group (like 8 people, mixed levels) on metaphysical grounding and fundamentality, and I want a "which grounding package do you actually hold?" quiz — not a poll of labels. Around 9 or 10 questions, each a concrete little case with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key that sorts answer patterns into views and says what to do with ties.

The cases I want used (most of them anyway): whether the whole can ground its parts (the universe's total state grounding facts about its parts) rather than only parts grounding wholes; whether an absence can be a ground (the tower standing partly because nothing is pushing it over); whether two facts can ground each other, e.g. the glass's fragility and the conditional about what would happen if you struck it; whether grounding has to be well-founded, i.e. no infinitely descending chains; whether a necessary fact can be grounded in a contingent one; whether something can be grounded but not ontologically dependent (explanatory dependence w/o ontological dependence); and whether a fundamental thing can depend on another fundamental one — so whether fundamentality is just "ungrounded," or "ungrounded and everything else is built from it," or a kind of independence, or something that comes in degrees.

Leaves I want covered: grounding as a primitive hyperintensional relation; grounding collapsing into necessitation with no separate relation; contingent/non-necessitating grounding; priority monism; the "no fundamental layer" infinitist-ish view; grounding deflationism (grounding talk as bookkeeping); and one leaf for views that drop well-foundedness. Build it from the logical space first — the questions and the answer combinations they allow — then drop the actual views in, and where a combination is empty say whether that's because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Flag any two leaves your quiz can't really separate — I'd rather know then have it fudged.

No item should just ask "is grounding just explanation?" or name a view and ask people to agree with it. Don't cite anyone by name or give a reading list; I only care about the structure. Answer in American English, under about 900 words.
