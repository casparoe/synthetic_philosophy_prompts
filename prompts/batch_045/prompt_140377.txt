I do freelance data work — a couple of hospitals, a university admissions office, a school district, a few startups — and I keep hitting the same annoying pattern: the total says one thing, every subgroup says the other. A statistician friend calls it Simpson's paradox, which as far as I can tell is a name and not an explanation. Here are the calls I've actually made, and I want to know whether one rule is behind them or whether I'm quietly asking two different questions.

1. Hospital compares two drugs for a skin infection. Severity is recorded at intake, before anyone chooses a drug. Severe: new drug 24/80 (30%), old drug 6/40 (15%). Mild: new 96/100 (96%), old 285/300 (95%). Overall: new 120/180 (67%), old 291/340 (86%). I told them the new drug is better, full stop — the total only looks bad for it because doctors save it for the worst cases.

2. Randomized trial of two physio programs, 500 patients each. A: 400 recovered (80%). B: 350 (70%). Then someone split by whether the patient was pain-free at week two, which the program itself affects. Pain-free: A 380/400 (95%), B 245/250 (98%). Not pain-free: A 20/100 (20%), B 105/250 (42%). B wins both rows. I still said A is better and to ignore the split.

3. University admissions. Men 58/200 admitted (29%), women 24/140 (17%). But inside each division women do better: Division 1, men 8/100 (8%), women 12/120 (10%); Division 2, men 50/100 (50%), women 12/20 (60%). Here I won't let the division numbers dissolve the headline gap — the gap is the thing I care about — but I also don't want to say any division is biased, and I genuinely can't tell whether those two stances fit together. This is the one I go back and forth on.

4. Reading programs. Published trial, population was 60% weak readers: P 56% pass overall, Q 62%. By level: weak, P 30/100 vs Q 45/100; strong, P 95/100 vs Q 88/100. My district's classes are 80% strong readers, so I threw out the published total, re-weighted the two rows to my own mix, and got P 82% vs Q 79%. Felt right, but I can't say why it differs from case 1, where I also threw out the total and used the rows.

5. Startup checkout-page A/B test, 20,000 users per arm: N converts 6.0%, M 5.0%. An analyst sliced it forty ways and found slices where M wins, including one with six people in it. I said none of that counts. But I dont have a principled test for when a subgroup reversal is real and when it's noise-hunting.

My working rule has been: trust the split, unless the thing you split on was caused by the treatment, in which case trust the total. Case 3 breaks that, case 4 needs something about whose population you're weighting by, and case 5 needs a size-or-robustness test I can't state.

So: is there one principle here, or am I collapsing two questions I should keep apart? Give me something precise enough that a client could apply it without me in the room, tell me honestly which of my verdicts it forces me to drop, and if there are two candidate rules that both fit the five cases above, give me the case that would tell them apart. Also tell me what your rule says about two cases I keep dodging: (a) a reversal where nobody left on the team knows whether the variable we'd split on was measured before or after the treatment went out, because the documenation is gone; (b) the same reversal, except the variable is a noisy proxy, like a severity score that's right maybe 70% of the time.

No citations or reading lists please, I can't get at them anyway — just the rule and why it holds.
