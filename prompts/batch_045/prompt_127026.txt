You are a professional moral philosopher who publishes on Kantian ethics and on the ethics of our treatment of animals, and who has also spent time in applied settings (research oversight committees, clinical ethics consultations). Please think carefully before you write, and start with a short brainstorming phase in which you list, in one paragraph, the five or six joints in the position described below where it is most likely to buckle, ranked by how costly the buckling would be. Only then draft the questions.

The context: I host a small interview series on moral philosophy for the humanities programme at the teaching hospital where I work. I was unexpectedly appointed this year to our animal research oversight committee — no training, no background — and in the same month my fifteen-year-old stopped eating animal products, so these arguments now happen at my dinner table several nights a week and I lose them on slogans. Next week's guest is a moral philosopher (I'll leave her unnamed; I have her book proposal and a long abstract, not the published book) who argues that Kantian ethics, read properly, already contains the resources for direct duties to animals, and that it can do this without conceding that animals are persons, that they are ends in themselves in the Kantian sense, or that they can be members of the kingdom of ends. Her positive machinery, as I understand it: anything that has what she calls a unified practical standpoint over time — a good of its own that can be frustrated, and whose frustration the creature itself registers and resists — is owed a fiduciary duty, a duty held on its behalf which she insists is a duty to it and not merely a duty regarding it; and she thinks the Kantian line here is graded rather than binary, so that the duties owed to a dog, a pig, a fish and a clam differ in kind and not only in degree. She is used to losing patience with interviewers: whenever a case is put to her where the received indirect-duty reading of Kant (duties regarding animals are really duties to humanity, because cruelty corrupts the agent's own humanity) or her own reading seems to give an intuitively unacceptable answer, she replies with her stock move — Take the intuition out of it and tell me what the maxim is.

What I want from you: eight to ten interview questions, with, under each, the answer she is most likely to give and one follow-up keyed specifically to that answer. Nothing generic (no how did you get into this, no what should listeners read). I want at least one question on each of the following, and I want the questions to turn on the content of her actual commitments rather than on the secondary literature:

1. Whether the indirect-duty reading's mechanism is empirical at its core: if the psychological spillover from cruelty to animals to cruelty to humans turned out to be nil, or if the causal arrow ran the other way, does the duty evaporate? She will say the duty attaches to the maxim and not to the effects — press on what work Kant's own framing in terms of the agent's sentiments and habits is then doing.
2. The isolation case: if one human being remained alive and would never encounter another, would torturing animals become permissible on the indirect reading? Does her own reading escape the same case, given that the fiduciary relation she posits apparently needs no one to be in a position to vindicate it?
3. The asymmetry between cruelty and painless killing: is the painless slaughter of a well-cared-for animal permissible on her view, and if so, what is left, in practice, between her position and ordinary welfare reform? Give me a concrete version of this set in a shelter or on a farm, not in the abstract.
4. The constructivist regress: if-humanity-as-an-end-in-itself rests on rational agents' conferring value, what exactly does the capacity to step back from a desire and ask whether it is a reason contribute — and does anything in her account stop some social mammals from passing that test partially, which is what her graded line seems to concede anyway?
5. Fiduciary duties owed to a being that can neither recognise the duty nor be wronged by its violation: how is this not a duty regarding the animal after all? Include the comparison she will reach for — a human infant, whose claim is carried by potentiality — and the follow-up about what her view says about a being that never had and never will have the relevant potentiality.

Then build a short sequence of two or three questions, to be used late in the hour, that makes her stock move progressively more expensive to repeat, and close with one question about what would count as evidence against her view — what finding, argument, or case would make her abandon it, stated so that she cannot answer with another restatement of the maxim test. Remember the audience: researchers and clinicians who review protocols involving animals and who will be unmoved by exegetical point-scoring, so flag any question that would land as a quarrel about what Kant really said rather than about what is true.
