I have kept the issue tracker for a medium-sized open-source project for about fifteen years, and I have become preoccupied by one word in it: "same". Whether two reports are "the same bug" decides what gets merged, what gets closed as a duplicate, which release notes say "fixed", and who gets the credit. Everyone on the team makes these calls dozens of times a week, and we nearly always make them without hesitation. What interests me is that we do not make them by a rule, and that the reason we give changes from case to case.

Three cases from the past year. (1) Two users report a crash with different messages and different stack traces; a week of digging shows both come from one unguarded null. Nobody argues: same bug. (2) Two users report byte-identical wrong output from unrelated causes, one a rounding error in the backend and one a caching fault in the client. We insist these are not the same bug, and we are genuinely puzzled by a newcomer who says they obviously are, since they look identical from the user's chair. (3) A single root cause produces two symptoms needing two different fixes, in two subsystems owned by two different people. Here the team splits and stays split: half of us say one bug with two faces, half say two bugs with one ancestor.

I have read a little philosophy, so let me head off the easy answers. This does not feel like vagueness: there are no heaps, no "sort of the same", no sense of a borderline. In each case every competent person has a confident view, and where we differ we differ confidently and at length. It does not feel like ambiguity either: nobody hears two senses of "same bug" and picks one. We resist the suggestion that we are picking at all, and say things like "no, really, those are the same bug". A friend who does philosophy rattled off some candidates — vagueness, polysemy, family resemblance, open texture, cluster concepts — and none seemed to me to be quite it, though I could not say why. The cases are not held together by overlapping features; they are held together by a story about causes and fixes, and the people who know the causes beat the people who know the symptoms.

Three further things make me think this is a phenomenon of its own.

First, the shifts are graded, and the grading is shared. Some re-identifications are accepted the moment they are proposed; others are argued over for months; and this ordering is stable across our team and, as far as I can tell, across projects. What marks a good triager is not refusing to shift but shifting by the right amount: the pedant who splits everything and the enthusiast who merges everything are both criticised, and criticised in the same words.

Second, the relation is not transitive, and our repairs for that are lopsided. It is common to have A the same as B, B the same as C, and A not the same as C. Repairing it by merging, we say "we were wrong about that"; repairing it by splitting, we say "it turned out to be two things after all". One direction is confessing an error, the other is reporting a discovery — as if the relation were at once stipulated and found. I have never seen that asymmetry discussed.

Third, every attempt to write the policy down fails in the same way. "Same root cause, same issue" and "same user-visible failure, same issue" each handle the routine cases perfectly, and each misfires precisely where the shifting was doing the work — and systematically, not accidentally: the cases the rule gets wrong are exactly the cases we would have argued about.

So: is there an established concept for this? I have taken to calling it "negotiated identity" in my own notes, which I am sure is wrong, if only because none of us experiences it as a negotiation. I am after the carving rather than a label: what exactly the phenomenon is; how it differs from each of its nearest neighbours; which of them, if any, it really is; and where its edges lie, including whether it shows that nothing here was ever strictly the same. If nothing established fits, please coin a term, define it tightly enough that I could decide whether a new case falls under it, and give me the boundary cases where it should not be applied. I am not after a reading list or citations — I want the distinctions. I shall be circulating the answer to my team, whose house style is American, so please write it in American English.
