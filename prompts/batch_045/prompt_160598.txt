I had a long argument with a friend last night about epistemic injustice that we could not resolve, and I want a thought experiment that either settles it or at least shows us what we were actually disagreeing about.

The disagreement. We agreed on the standard shape of the account: a speaker suffers testimonial injustice when a hearer, because of identity prejudice, affords them less credibility than they deserve, and the wrong consists in the speaker being undermined in their standing as a knower. We then got stuck. Suppose the prejudice-driven credibility assessment is, at the moment of the exchange, exactly as low as the speaker's own presentation merits, because an earlier history of being under-credited has made that speaker hesitant, hedging, and deferential in how they testify. My friend says that in that case the hearer has done nothing wrong: the credibility given is the credibility the speaker's presentation supported, and whatever injustice there is belongs upstream, to whoever produced the hesitancy. I say the hearer is still committing a wrong, because what the speaker deserves is not settled by the distorted presentation but by what that presentation would have been had the speaker not been shaped by a history of prejudice. Call these the local-calibration reading and the history-adjusted reading.

I want a case that makes the two readings come apart cleanly. I would use it in a paper section arguing that testimonial injustice can be diachronic: that a hearer can wrong a speaker even while their credibility judgment is perfectly in line with everything available to them in the moment.

Constraints on the scenario:

1. Ordinary and realistic: a hospital handover, a workplace incident report, a school, a benefit office, a police interview. No science fiction, nothing exotic.
2. One paragraph, roughly 150 words.
3. The hearer must be someone who takes themselves to be scrupulously evidence-driven and is not a bigot by their own lights; the deficit must be one a careful, well-meaning professional could actually exhibit. No cartoon villainy, no overt slurs.
4. The speaker must genuinely know what they say. The case must not work by having the speaker be unreliable, junior, or uninformed.
5. The identity cue (accent, race, age, class, disability, gender) must be doing the work: swap it for a cue associated with the dominant group, hold everything else fixed, and the deficit disappears.
6. The hearer must not be the person who produced the speaker's hesitancy. Otherwise the case collapses into an ordinary single-wrongdoer case and my friend will agree with me for the wrong reason.
7. The case must not stipulate the verdicts. It has to be built so that each reading generates its answer from its own content. If you find yourself writing that this is a testimonial injustice rather than showing it, the case has failed.

Please give me the following sections, in this order:

1. The scenario — one paragraph, about 150 words.
2. Why the identity cue is doing the work — about 120 words: run the swap test from constraint 5, and show that the non-prejudicial explanations (inexperience, surprising content, time pressure, genuine unreliability) are each screened off.
3. What the local-calibration reading says — about 150 words: what it takes the credibility owed to be, and why it returns no injustice here.
4. What the history-adjusted reading says — about 150 words: what it takes the credibility owed to be, how it fixes the counterfactual baseline, and why it returns an injustice.
5. Which feature of the case generates the split — about 120 words: name the single structural feature the divergence rides on, and say what other cases would share it.
6. The objection my friend would make — about 150 words: the strongest thing a defender of the local-calibration reading can say to stop the case from counting. My guess is that the counterfactual baseline is indeterminate, or that compensating for history is not something an individual hearer can owe, or that the upstream wrong already exhausts the injustice. Say whether that reply is actually available in this case.
7. What breaks if I alter it — about 120 words: what happens to the split if the hearer did cause the hesitancy, and what happens if the presentation is distorted by something with no social history behind it, such as a stammer or a bad night's sleep.
