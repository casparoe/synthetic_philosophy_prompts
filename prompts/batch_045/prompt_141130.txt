After a long argument with a friend last night I want a document, not a verdict. My friend holds that the St Petersburg paradox is a sound reductio of unbounded utility: within expected-utility theory, expected value stays finite for every game of that general shape only if utility is bounded above, so the repair belongs in the utility function, and a bounded agent pays only a modest fee for any such gamble. I hold that the paradox indicts the decision rule instead: maximizing an expectation that is infinite, or undefined because the payoff series fails to converge absolutely, was never a requirement of rationality, and a theory of rational choice should be answerable only to prospects an actual counterparty could offer — so the repair belongs in the rule's domain or in its treatment of vanishing probabilities, not in the shape of our values.

We did agree on one thing: the argument got sharper when I proposed this game. A fair coin is tossed until the first heads or until ten tails, whichever comes first. If the first heads falls on the n-th toss you receive 2^(2^n) dollars; if ten tails come first you receive nothing. It has eleven possible outcomes, lasts at most ten flips, and has finite support — yet its expected payoff is on the order of 2^1014 dollars, while the chance of winning more than a billion dollars is about one in sixteen.

Draft the joint statement we could both sign: a numbered list of agreed claims — about the formalism, about what any resolution has to achieve, about the compact game above, about the variant whose payoff series is only conditionally convergent, and about what repeated-play asymptotics do and do not show — followed by a disagreements table stating each side's position in wording that side would accept. I suspect the cruxes are: whether a normative decision theory owes verdicts on prospects it concedes are physically undeliverable; whether bounded utility is independently motivated or ad hoc, given that it makes an agent nearly indifferent between payoffs differing by astronomical factors; and whether the diagnosis should differ between infinite and merely undefined expectations. For each crux give one case or argument both of us would accept as bearing on it, with each of us predicting the outcome in advance — including how each expects willingness-to-pay to move as the exponent in the compact game is scaled up, and what each thinks happens when the payoffs are denominated in lives saved rather than dollars. If any part of the dispute is merely verbal, say which part and why.
