I'm in a joint philosophy/politics seminar on democratic theory and we did the boundary problem last week — who has to be in the demos for a decision to be legitimately democratic. The tutor basically said the problem is insoluble in principle because any rule for drawing the circle presupposes a circle already drawn, and that felt like a cop-out, or at least like something I want stated as a result rather than asserted as a shrug. So before I go back to the standard answers (the all-affected interests line, the all-subjected/coercion line, the "the people constitute themselves" constitution-making line) I want the spec first: what would a theory of the demos's boundaries actually have to deliver to count as adequate?

Fix the purpose so this isn't just armchair: I want the theory to be good enough to be written into a constitution's rules about the franchise for a binding referendum — including a secession referendum and a merger/joining referendum, not just ordinary elections. So it has to be usable by an actual electoral commission with a deadline, in a situation where people are arguing about who counts, and where nothing authoritative has yet settled whether a given group is inside or outside. Give me the desiderata list with the case or consideration behind each one, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are really preferences of one camp dressed up as requirements — I strongly suspect "it must yield a unique determinate answer for every person" is a prejudice rather than a requirement, but I don't trust myself on that. Cases I want covered: the long-term undocumented resident whose kids are in local schools; the cross-border commuter who pays tax in one jurisdiction and uses services in another; the non-resident citizen who owns property but hasn't lived there in twenty years; children; prisoners; future people who eat the costs of a hundred-year infrastructure or waste decision; and people abroad who are badly affected by this state's emissions or sanctions but are subject to none of its laws. Two requirements I've half-invented and want you to test rather than just accept: (1) the self-induced-affectedness filter — if a state unilaterally extends its reach over people (annexation, occupation, sanctions), it doesn't get to count them as members of its demos on the grounds that they're now affected by it; and (2) the who-counts-the-counters requirement — the criterion must not be such that whoever controls the residency register or the ID rules effectively picks the demos, since then it's not a criterion at all.

Then the part I most want: tell me which subset of the desiderata you think can't be jointly satisfied, and be explicit that this is a result about the list rather than a failure of imagination — I'd guess the real trap is between "the demos has to be able to authorise itself" and "no body gets to certify its own constituency," but argue it rather than agreeing with me. And say which single requirement does the most work in knocking out otherwise popular answers. A very short scorecard against the three standard answers at the end is fine, but keep the weight on the criteria. Please also flag how confident you are in each thing you assert: which of your desiderata you'd actually defend under pressure, what argument or case would make you drop or add one, and where your reasoning is most likely to have smuggled in a substantive theory of legitimacy.
