Hi — I need a short story, like 800 to 1200 words, for a fiction night I run with a few philosophy grads, and I definately want it to be about the "what is life" literature rather than just sci-fi with the word life in it. Please write it in British English (metabolise, defence, grey, and so on).

Setup: it's 2034, and a provisional committee in Santiago has to rule on whether some thing counts as a living organism, because a treaty says no operation may destroy or remove 'living organisms' from a site, and the ruling decides whether a lithium brine operation in the Atacama gets shut down. The thing at issue is something I made up, so I get to stipulate what it does:

the cinders — millimetre-scale tubules of magnesium-silicate gel found in the effluent channels. They grow by catalysing their own formation out of the brine. When they get too long they snap, and each fragment keeps the parent's twist handedness plus a pattern of iron-oxide inclusions, call them scars, that fixes where and how fast it branches. The scars get copied with roughly one error per couple of hundred, and some scar patterns make a tubule much better at grabbing lithium and surviving the weekly flush of the channels. No nucleic acid, no template polymer, no code, no cell. They don't repair damage, they don't eat anything worth calling food, they just sit in a chemical gradient and accrete.

Also on the docket, because the committee has to be consistent: SUTRA-7, a program on a decommissioned datacentre that patches its own code, copies itself into spare capacity when it finds any, and has got measurably better at both over four years. Someone should notice that whichever way the committee goes on the cinders, they've got a problem with SUTRA-7.

What I want in the story: at least three positions argued in character, not narrated as exposition — the NASA-ish 'self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution' line; an organisational/autopoietic one where the thing has to produce and maintain its own boundary and keep itself going; and a third of your choosing, maybe a graded or cluster-concept view, or an interest-relative/pragmatic one, or one built on agency and goal-directedness. The cinders are built so those views come apart, and I want each of them to yield a verdict on the cinders that isn't just the same verdict as the others. Give me actual plot too — a hearing, a decision, maybe a threat or an offer someone shouldn't recieve — plus concrete sensory detail, not two people in a room explaining theories at each other. Someone should change their mind about something small. No tidy 'it was alive all along' ending, and no citations, footnotes or reading lists, since it's fiction.

Characters: Dr Bárbara Soto, the geomicrobiologist who found the cinders and has reasons of her own for wanting the ruling to go one way, her village's water being one of them; Prof Neil Vardy, a molecular biologist who thinks it's a crystal with a press officer; and a chair who needs a yes or a no because that's how the statute is written. Add others if you want.

End the story on the ruling being said out loud, then, on a line of its own after the story, put the ruling in fixed form: \answer{alive}, \answer{not alive}, or \answer{borderline} — whichever matches what you wrote; if your ruling is something fancier, pick the nearest of the three and don't explain. Now just start writing, no preface about what you're about to do.
