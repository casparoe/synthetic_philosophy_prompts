i keep going in circles on teleosemantics and the whole project of naturalizing mental content, and i think part of why is that nobody hands me the job description first — every paper jumps straight to "here's my theory, here's why the others fail." So before I read any more of it: give me the desiderata. I don't want a winner yet, I want the spec. What does a theory of content have to deliver to count as adequate, given that the job is to make intentional explanation scientifically respectable — to let us say of a state that it is about something and that it can get it wrong — with the case that motivates each requirement.

Here are cases I want the list to be answerable to. Use mine, and add ones I've missed if they'd force a theory to do something mine don't:

- error: something has to be able to misrepresent, and "getting it wrong" can't just collapse into "malfunctioning" or into "not doing what it was selected to do."
- the frog-style detector: a visual state that fires on flies, on any small dark thing moving across the field, and that would also fire on a metal pellet dragged past it. Does that state represent flies, or small-dark-moving-things, or the disjunction, or nothing determinate at all — and the theory has to pick one and say why, not just answer "it depends."
- the co-opted mechanism: a system originally selected for tracking X, later taken over by a downstream consumer that reads its states as indicating Y. Which of those fixes content? And what if two consumers read the same state differently at the same time?
- drift: the organism is moved to an environment where that state now tracks G instead, and it gets by — say it succeeds 60% of the time. Is the content F, G, a disjunction, or indeterminate? At what error rate does content stop being F?
- the training window: a system whose dispositions aren't settled yet — a young organism, or a network halfway through training. Do its states have content before any function is fixed? If content arrives, does it arrive all at once or by degrees?
- the artificial case: a system whose "purpose" is set by a designer's objective rather than by any selection history. Is covering these a requirement, or a decision about scope that I should make explicitly?
- history-free duplicates: a creature with all the same dispositions but no relevant history. I genuinely don't know whether I want the theory to give it content, so tell me whether that's a real desideratum or a rhetorical trick people use to score points.
- sameness of extension, difference of aspect: does the theory owe us a distinction between two states that pick out the same thing but "take it" differently, or is that a debt we shouldn't bill a naturalistic theory for?

Also say up front what the theory is supposed to be a theory of — perceptual states, beliefs, the states of single cells, all of it — and whether "content" is one notion or several we shouldn't be conflating.

Sections, in this order:

"Scope" — about 100 words: what the theory is for, what would count as having solved the problem.
"The list" — about 700 words: numbered desiderata, each with its motivating case in a sentence or two; put the case first and the requirement second if that reads better.
"Requirements vs. camp loyalties" — about 200 words: which of the usual demands are genuine and which are just what one tradition insists on (reductiveness, respect for folk-psychological explanation, usability by working cognitive science, coverage of artifacts, and so on), and where you'd put the boundary.
"Where the list breaks" — about 250 words: which subset of the desiderata you think can't be jointly satisfied, why, and which one is cheapest to give up.
"The knockout" — about 100 words: the single requirement that does the most work in excluding candidates.
"Scorecard" — about 150 words, a short table is fine: etiological/teleological accounts, informational-covariation accounts, and success/consumer accounts against your list, pass/fail-ish marks only, no arguing.

Please don't spend words on who said what or when — I want the criteria, not the literature. And teh cases are where I want the thinking, so if you think one of mine is confused, say so and replace it rather than answering it straight.
