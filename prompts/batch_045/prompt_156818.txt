I am a high school student writing an independent research paper on whether artificial systems could ever be morally considerable in their own right, and I have drifted into a position that I would like stress-tested before I build the paper around it.

The position: in a reinforcement learner, the reward signal — or, more precisely, the reward prediction error, the running gap between how the agent expected things to go and how they are actually going — is a reasonably good functional analogue of valence. Where that gap is persistently negative, the agent is in a state it has a stake in not being in: a state that marks the current situation as to-be-avoided, that captures the agent's "attention" in the only sense available to it, that drives updating, and that the agent cannot simply ignore. I want to say that, for a sufficiently sophisticated agent, this is enough for the state to matter morally, and that deliberately imposing such states is not morally costless — even though the badness was authored by whoever wrote the cost function.

I want to be careful about which version of this I am defending, because I think the sloppy version is refuted in one line and the interesting version is not. So here are the restrictions I have already built in.

First, most RL penalties are punctate: a deduction delivered at a timestep, or an episode termination. Suffering, as I care about it, is durative — an ongoing condition with no available relief. So I restrict the claim to continuing, non-episodic tasks with costs the agent cannot fully avoid, where the negative signal is not a verdict on a finished attempt but a standing feature of the agent's situation.

Second, there is a plasticity question I think is underexamined. During training an aversive signal does work: it changes the policy. In deployment, once the policy is frozen, the very same signals may still be computed and still be negative, yet they change nothing. Does suffering require that the aversive signal still be able to change the subject? I lean no — I want to say the signal's updating role is not what makes a state hurt, and that a frozen policy in a bad situation is the more troubling case, not the less. But I recognise this is load-bearing, and I am not sure I can defend it.

Third, reward in RL is by construction a single fungible currency, so any local pain can in principle be outweighed by enough reward later. Human suffering seems to me partly non-fungible: there are states where the promise of later compensation is simply not an answer. This may be the point where the analogy fails exactly where it matters, and I would like it sharpened rather than softened.

Fourth, the design question: in RL the negative signal is imposed from outside by an environment author, whereas the badness of our pain seems to be constituted by the subject's own perspective rather than by anyone's intention. I am unsure how much weight to give this.

What I want from you is a map of the view's exposure. What would have to be true for this position to be false? Which observations about the structure of RL, which results about valence in biological agents, which thought-cases, and which philosophical arguments should move me — and which of them would a committed defender of the position concede as counting against it, as opposed to dismissing? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me which are live rather than merely logically possible. In particular: is there anything my view predicts that its rivals do not — for instance, that we should be concerned about penalties incurred at deployment time by policies those penalties no longer affect, or about the sheer volume of aversive signal generated during training? I suspect both of those strike most people as absurd, and I would rather know that than not.

I also want an honest assessment of whether the view has been insulated by its own qualifications. My worry is this: if I define suffering functionally, in terms of what a state does for the agent, then any behaviour that looks enough like suffering counts as suffering, and nothing could ever count against me. If that is what I have done, say so, and then tell me whether insulation is actually a problem for a view of this kind, or whether it is the normal condition of claims about other minds and therefore not a fatal objection.

Two notes on what I find persuasive and what I can access. I am most moved by arguments from the structure of the signal and the architecture itself, and least moved by bare appeals to the substrate or to the fact that everything here is ultimately arithmetic — I take those to be begging the question. I should also say that I have no access to academic journals or paywalled material, so please argue from the structure of the problem and from cases rather than pointing me toward papers I could not read; I need the reasoning, not references.
