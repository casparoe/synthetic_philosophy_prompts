You are a professional philosopher specialising in the philosophy of language, especially speech act theory (Austin, Searle and their critics) and its application to non-standard utterance situations: automated systems, mediated communication, institutional speech. Please think carefully before answering: start by sketching the two or three principles that the verdicts below are jointly consistent with, then test each of them against the new case, and only then commit to a prediction.

I work on a project that classifies commitment-bearing utterances in customer-service transcripts, and I keep being referred to the verdicts of a senior colleague, who is our house authority on when an utterance really undertakes something. Her rulings so far:

1. "I promise I'll pay you back on Friday," said sincerely to a friend in the pub — a promise.
2. "You'll have the money by Friday, I give you my word," said sincerely, with no performative verb — a promise.
3. "I promise to pay you back," said by an actor to another actor while filming a scene — not a promise.
4. "I promise I'll pay you back," said at the bedside of a friend in a coma, who never hears it — a promise.
5. "I swear I'll pay you back," said by someone with no intention whatever of paying, who then doesn't pay — still a promise, though an insincere and bad one.
6. A £20 bet on a match between two strangers in a pub, in a jurisdiction where wagering agreements are unenforceable — still an undertaking.
7. "I promise I'll pay you back," written in a letter that is never sent — not a promise.

The new case: a ferry operator's automated cancellation system sends a customer an email reading "We undertake to refund your fare within five working days." No human composed or authorised that particular message; it is emitted by a template triggered by the cancellation. But the operator's published conditions of carriage do provide for refunds within five days, its staff routinely honour such emails when customers quote them back, and this customer relied on it. The refund does not arrive.

Would my colleague count that email as an undertaking — a promise-like commitment — or not? Which principle is she most plausibly running: a Searlean one (an undertaking requires a speaker who purports to intend to bind themselves, and who addresses it to someone); an Austinian one (force comes from a recognised conventional procedure invoked by a properly positioned party, with the other precedents settling which abuses are fatal and which are not); or something closer to a scorekeeping or accountability view (force is conferred by a practice in which the utterer can be held to the utterance and owes an answer for default)? Give me your best guess with your reasons for preferring it, and say what the precedents cannot settle. Finally, name the further case that would most sharply separate the two principles you think are still in play — my own candidate is the same automated message emitted because a contractor misconfigured the template, so nothing inside the firm authorised it, while customers and the regulator still treat the wording as binding; tell me whether that is the right discriminating case or whether there is a better one. Please answer in British English.
