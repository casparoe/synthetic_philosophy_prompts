I teach a first-year seminar on self and skepticism, and next term I want to put Lewis Carroll's Alice's Adventures in Wonderland next to Zhuangzi's butterfly passage — but not as the usual maybe-we're-all-dreaming bit. I've been telling students that the butterfly thing isn't really an epistemolgy puzzle: nothing hangs on whether Zhuangzi can rule out that he's a butterfly dreaming he's a man; the point is rather that the boundary between the two doesn't mark anything that matters, that there's no fact of which of them he really is, and that this is a claim about the transformation of things, not a worry about our access to the world.

So: read Alice through that Zhuangzi and not through Descartes. Does the Caterpillar scene — Alice being asked who she is and failing to answer, because she has been six inches tall and nine feet tall since breakfast and every answer gets thrown back at her — do the real philosophical work, with her changes of size figuring as transformation rather than as unreliable perception? And does the frame Carroll puts round it all, Alice waking up and filing the whole adventure as her dream while her sister drifts into one of her own, quietly betray the reading by reassuring us there was a normal-sized waking Alice behind it the whole time? I also want you to say honestly where the book resists: if Carroll is only after a dream-versus-reality giggle, I dont want to dress him up as a metaphysician.
