I should say plainly that I have no academic background. I left school at eighteen, I run a small electrical contracting business, and whatever I know about housing I have picked up by sitting through planning committee meetings in our town for the past three years and arguing about them afterwards with my neighbours. The neighbourhood forum is holding a drop-in session in a few weeks, and I would like your help making a printed sheet for it.

The difficulty I keep running into is that the usual way of framing these arguments — those who welcome new building against those who resist it — does not sort anyone I actually know. I can think of neighbours who would happily accept forty flats at the end of their road and yet insist that the road itself, and not the council, should decide what gets built there; and others who oppose a particular block whilst taking the view that the person who owns the land owes the town a large part of the windfall once permission is granted. Under the usual framing both of those look contradictory, and I have come to think they are not. So I would like the sheet to sort people by the shape of what they believe rather than by which side they think they are on.

What I am asking for is a decision tree, roughly eight branch questions and ten to twelve leaves, that takes someone from a few plain reactions to a named position, together with a scoring key. Three conditions matter to me. First, every branch question must be a concrete case on an identifiable street, answerable in a sentence by someone who has never opened a book on the subject — nothing of the form do you believe in a right to the city, and nothing that requires any knowledge of planning law or of what economists have said about supply. Second, the questions should be derived from the logical space rather than from a list of opinions: build the tree from the distinctions themselves, work out which combinations of answers the distinctions permit, and only then place the actual positions inside that space. Third, where a branch turns on a guess about how the world works — whether new building lowers rents nearby, whether it pushes anyone out — flag it and set it aside, so that the sheet measures what people value rather than what they expect to happen.

The distinctions I would like to drive the branching are these: whether being present in a place, being consulted about it, and being able to stop what happens in it are three separate claims or one and the same; who any such claim is held against, since it seems to me very different to say that the council owes me something, that the person selling the land does, and that the people who would move in do; whether a claim grows with length of residence or whether long residence instead brings an obligation to make room for whoever comes next; whether claims attach to persons or to dwellings; and whether compensation of any kind — money, a better park, priority in the new flats — is part of the claim or merely a concession made to people who have none.

The cases I would like used, though you may replace any of them with better ones, are: a proposal of forty flats on a street where the primary school and the surgery are already full; a block whose eight flats have stood empty for years and are owned by a company registered abroad; a family wanting to turn a garage into an annexe for an elderly parent, so that an extra household appears without an extra building; a street where the objectors mostly rent rather than own; land owned by a distant pension fund compared with land owned by a family who have farmed it for four generations; the case where the street votes three to one against the scheme whilst the town as a whole is for it, and its mirror image where the street says yes and the town says no; a scheme reserved for nurses at the local hospital compared with the same scheme sold off-plan to buyers who will never live in it; the person who grew up on the street, was priced out fifteen years ago, and now wants one of the new flats; and the neighbour who says openly that she would move out tomorrow if she could afford to, but objects to the building regardless.

Amongst the leaves I would like, somewhere, a position on which the owner's decision is essentially their own; one on which permission is a public power that must be used for the widest benefit; one on which the gain created by permission belongs to everybody; one on which long residence earns a real say; one on which the first duty is to those already priced out or pushed out; one on which the first duty is to people not yet here; and one on which the street itself, as a body, holds a claim that none of the individuals in it holds separately. Give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Mark the combinations of answers that nobody in fact occupies, and say whether each is incoherent, simply unattractive, or merely unvisited. Flag any two leaves your tree cannot really separate rather than pretending it can, and tell me which leaves the ordinary for-against framing collapses together.

I would be grateful for something I could lay out on a single sheet of A3, with the questions in plain English, the labels at the leaves only at the very end, and a short note at the foot on the two or three questions that most reliably separate neighbours who believe they agree.
