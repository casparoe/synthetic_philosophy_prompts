I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy, writing a dissertation chapter on whether mental illness could be a natural kind. My supervisor has asked me to circulate a written position statement to be attacked at my proposal defence. Here is the set I've arrived at, including two devices of my own that I am trying to make do real work. I think there is a mistake somewhere in it, but I don't know where, and I'd rather you didn't assume I'm especially attached to any one item.

(a) A mechanism criterion for disease. Something is a disease only if there is an identifiable malfunction of a mechanism that has a biological function, where function is understood etiologically (selected-effects) rather than statistically. I reject the biostatistical version of naturalism, on which function is species-typical statistical contribution to survival and reproduction. I hold this criterion for all of medicine, not just psychiatry.

(b) A Szaszian historical claim. I accept the core historical claim of Szasz's 1961 book: that the extension of mental illness in his time was fixed substantially by social deviance, institutional convenience and professional interest rather than by pathological findings. I also think it is genuine, if weak, evidence for a version of that claim today that our categories are revised by committee deliberation and vote.

(c) Melancholia in, major depressive disorder out. I want to say that severe melancholic depression is a disease on my own criterion, while major depressive disorder as currently defined is not, because that category is heterogeneous, threshold-based, and its boundary moves with referral and screening practices.

(d) Neurodiversity, partially. Autism and ADHD should not be called diseases. The impairment is largely a mismatch between someone's traits and the demands of an environment we chose to build, and changing the environment can remove it.

(e) Coercion without disease vocabulary. I support compulsory treatment in a narrow range of cases, but I want to justify it without any disease talk. My proposal: the state's power should run through a capacity tribunal that asks only whether, in the domain at stake, the person's practical reasoning is impaired enough that they cannot act on reasons they themselves endorse. Detention and treatment should be authorised separately, by two different bodies, on two different showings — call it the two-keys rule.

(f) Veracity plus treatment. I think the chemical-imbalance story about depression is a misleading oversimplification at best. I also think it would be a kind of neglect to withhold medication from someone in a severe episode on the ground that we don't know the mechanism, and that a physician should offer it.

(g) Permissible diagnostic insincerity. I think it is often permissible, and sometimes required, for a clinician to record a diagnosis she believes does not pick out a disease, because access to care and reimbursement are gated on it.

(h) A meta-position that may be in two minds. I think conceptual analysis of disease is nearly inert for policy: questions about coercion and resource allocation should be settled by consequences — suffering reduced, liberty curtailed. But I also think a great deal turns socially on whether a condition is called a disease, because the label redistributes blame, obligation and standing.

(i) Two devices of my own. First, a distinction between mechanism-first and predicament-first clinical kinds. A predicament-first kind is defined by a situation a person is in — cannot sleep, cannot stop drinking, hears voices that comment — and is vindicated only if intervenable mechanisms cluster under it stably enough to guide treatment; it need not name a single mechanism. Second, a prevalence-invariance test: if a category's extension would expand or contract merely because the social environment changed, with no change in anyone's biology, treat it as a predicament rather than a disease.
