Draft: "Three ways a model can lie"

The F-twist — the more significant the theory, the more unrealistic the assumptions — is usually fought over in general: does predictive success license false assumptions, yes or no. Both sides argue as if "unrealistic assumption" named one thing. I think it names at least three, that they have different licensing conditions, and that in every case the license is local: it covers a quantity, over a range, for a kind of use. It is not a general permission slip for the model.

1) Subtractive unrealism. You set a real difference-maker to zero: no friction, no transaction costs, no entry barriers, no lags. There is a natural default license here, and it is a continuity license — as the factor goes to zero the model's predictions approach the predictions of the messier true system, and the factor is small in the target domain. Nothing deep; this is the case critics weirdly never mention.

2) Additive or fictional unrealism. You put in something that isn't the limit of anything real: a single agent with a complete and stable ranking over every contingency, a representative agent standing in for a population of heterogeneous people who mostly respond to each other, perfect foresight in a setting where nobody has it. Continuity can't help, because there is no nearby world where the fiction is true. The only license I can see is what I've been calling role-detachability: the model needs the fiction only for some formal property — say, that aggregate demand is a smooth decreasing function of price — and that property is one some much messier real mechanism could also deliver. The fiction is scaffolding, and the question is whether you can take it down without the building falling.

3) Singular or knife-edge unrealism. Sometimes subtracting a vanishingly small quantity changes not the numbers but the shape of the solution space: take away an epsilon of noise, or of myopia, or of the finiteness of the horizon, and you don't get a slightly different equilibrium, you get a different set of equilibria, or a different selection among them. Here the limit isn't just discontinuous in the output, it's discontinuous in what the model says is even possible. I think this is where the F-twist is genuinely dangerous rather than merely permissive, and I want to make this the emotional centre of the piece.

Then, separately, three ways a license that works gets used for something it doesn't cover:

(a) Level accuracy is not slope accuracy. A model can get the level of a quantity right within the tested range and still be wrong about its derivative with respect to a policy variable — wrong magnitude, or wrong sign. But policy advice is advice about slopes, about which way things move when you turn a knob. So "this model predicts well" and "this model tells you what to do" can come apart even when the predictions are excellent.

(b) Window-locking. The neglected factor is often small throughout your data: it was small in the sample period, or you calibrated the model on data generated under a regime where it was small. Then predictive success confirms robustness inside the window and says nothing outside it. Call it extrapolation smuggling when a model validated in the regime where X is negligible is then used to score a policy whose whole point is to make X large. There's a nasty feedback here, which is that the policies you most want a model to evaluate are exactly the ones that push you out of the window.

(c) The as-if license doesn't transfer across interventions. Even a model with a great input-output fit in the observed regime can be wrong under intervention, because intervening rewrites the mechanism, not just the inputs. Friedman's own as-if framing invites exactly this transfer — if the firms behave as if they maximize, why not treat the maximizing machinery as available for counterfactuals too? I want to say: because as-if is a claim about the fit of the output, not about the causal plumbing, and counterfactuals under policy are claims about the plumbing.

The objection I'm most worried about, and haven't answered: 1, 2 and 3 may not be a real taxonomy. Setting friction to zero can be described either as removing a force or as adding the law "no frictional force acts", so the subtractive/additive line looks like it depends on how you choose to write the model down, and any given case can be flipped by redescription. If that's right my three-way distinction is a distinction in notation, and the whole first half collapses.
