Hey — I run a small decision-theory reading group out of a pub back room, and we just finished the stretch on coherence arguments: Dutch books, money pumps, the representation theorems, the whole idea that being exploitable shows you're not an expected-utility maximizer. The group split in a way I found interesting and want to make fun of. Half of them came away thinking this is the practical part of the syllabus — here's a proof that your preferences have to be transitive and here's a quantity you should go maximize. The other half came away thinking it's close to empty, because utility is defined off your preferences and seemingly any agent who isn't outright cyclic can be represented as maximizing something, so the advice "maximize expected utility" might not say anything about what to do on Tuesday. I need one centerpiece for the zine I'm putting together for the last session, and I don't want it to be a lecture — I want it to be funny in a way that makes the second half's worry unavoidable.

So: write it as a fake one-page insurance policy, 700-800 words, deadpan insurance-legalese, with a letterhead, a schedule of covered perils, numbered clauses, an exclusions section, a claims procedure, and one short testimonial from a satisfied customer. It's from a company that insures you against being money-pumped or Dutch-booked. No real philosophers named anywhere, invented spokespeople only, and no winking: nobody in the document should announce that the joke is that the coverage is circular. All the humor has to come from getting the arguments right — I want people who've actually read them to laugh and then feel slightly bad about it.

Specific things I want in there, worked into the fine print rather than listed: the difference between a sure loss in money and a cycle in your own preferences, plus the fact that the company settles claims only in utils, at an exchange rate it sets itself after reviewing what you in fact chose; the customer who defeats the pump by simply declining the last trade in the sequence and is therefore denied coverage; the assumption that someone out there knows your preferences and will keep offering you the same series of trades until you're bled dry; the assumption that what a whole sequence of choices is worth is just the sum of what its steps are worth; and the way your preferences have to be cleaned up before coherence can even be assessed — no regret, no caring about how an outcome was produced, no caring whether uncertainty gets resolved early or late, no caring about what else happened to be on the menu, and nothing at all that you refuse to trade off against money — with the company charging a processing fee, in utils, for performing that cleaning on your behalf. The climax should be the eligibility clause: the policy only covers agents who were already representable as expected-utility maximizers. End with the testimonial from someone who's been made coherent and can now rank his own death against a modest sum of money, and I want the last line to leave a reader who knows the literature with the real question — whether these arguments establish a requirement of rationality, or just attach a price tag to inconsistency and quietly assume everything they were supposed to prove.
