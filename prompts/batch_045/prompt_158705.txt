Quick context: I write novels, not philosophy papers, but the nine-month fellowship I'm applying for makes everyone, fiction people included, hand in a one-page "research proposal," and I'd rather actually fill it in than fake it.

Here's my half-formed thing. I'm writing a novel from inside the head of a man in one of the reserve police battalions that shot civilians in occupied Poland, and I keep bumping into the Browning-versus-Goldhagen fight: ordinary men pushed by situation, obedience and peer pressure, versus willing executioners driven by a specifically German eliminationist ideology. What both sides seem to share is that they treat the job as causal — which factor produced the killing. What my novel actually tests is a different achievement: whether his reasons can be made followable, whether a reader can track his deliberations as reasons-for-him rather than as symptoms of a cause. Call it deliberative intelligibility. Second distinction, which I think is where they're really talking past each other: ideology might supply a repertoire — it makes an action articulable, available, citable as a reason — rather than force, which makes you do it. My hunch is the dispute looks unresolvable partly because both sides run repertoire and force together, and because neither asks what kind of intelligibility an explanation of a perpetrator is even supposed to deliver.

Turn that into a proposal: question stated precisely, why the existing answers leave it open, the method month by month (writing the novel is part of the method, not separate from it), what a finished result looks like, and honestly what would count as failure — my worry is "followable" quietly collapses back into "caused" or just into "sympathetic," and if it does the whole project is empty. No citations or reading list, I'll add those myself.

Format: exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no intro and no closing line.
