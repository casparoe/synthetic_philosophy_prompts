my grandmother died in March, in a country where you can file an advance request for assisted dying and have it honoured after you've lost capacity. She was diagnosed with Alzheimer's at 72 (young enough that everyone spent a year calling it "just getting older"), wrote the request at 74 with the GP she'd known since she was a district nurse, and it was carried out last spring when she was 79. By then she had maybe two good sentences an hour, didnt reliably recognise my mum, and spent most afternoons in the garden humming along to the radio and being — by every account, including the palliative nurse's — cheerful. The nurse is coming to the memorial. So is my uncle Paul, who has never actually said the word but who I'm fairly sure thinks a doctor killed her, and who has started every conversation with me since March by asking how the "research" is going in a voice that means something.

I've been asked to speak: ten minutes maximum, in her garden, about forty people, half of them neighbours and old colleagues who know nothing about any of this and at least two of whom I think assume she died of the dementia. I'm a PhD student working on agency over time and personal identity, so everyone expects me to have something ready, and I do — I just can't find a version of it that isn't either a betrayal of her or a slap in the face to Paul. What I want isn't a speech that argues a position; it's one where the philosophy quietly does the work of ruling things out, so that what's left can be said in plain words to a room of people holding paper plates. So: no "it's what she wanted," because I don't think "she" is singular here and I won't paper over that in front of Paul; no "she was suffering," because mostly she wasn't at the end and everyone who was there knows it; no "she's at peace" (stern lifelong atheist, would have found it mushy); no "she'd want us to celebrate," which is just the first thing in a party hat. And I don't want the line my own department would reach for — that the woman who signed at 74 was the real one and the woman humming in the garden was a remnant — partly because it isn't how I remember that afternoon, and partly because I don't think it survives pressure: the 74-year-old who signed was already on the same slope, so any continuity story that makes her authoritative has to pick a cut-off point on a continuum, and I've never found a non-arbitrary one. The framing I've been playing with in my notes is that a request like hers could be a command (which later evidence can't touch) or testimony — a claim about what would be good for her, which later evidence can defeat — and that she may not have known which one she was writing; if that's useful, use it, but don't say the words "command" and "testimony" out loud in the speech.

What I'd like from you is the speech itself, 800-900 words, plus a short note at the end on which sentences I should cut if I lose my nerve in the third minute. Two more constraints. First, she was a district nurse for thirty years, kept bees badly and loudly for twenty, and had a thing about not being a burden that everyone in the family is now over-interpreting; the speech should not make the manner of her death the most interesting thing about her, and it shouldn't turn her into a heroine of autonomy either — she'd have hated that more than the mushy stuff. Second, it has to land for the nurse and for Paul at once, which I think means finding something true you can say about both of the women she was, without deciding which one gets to speak for her. If the honest ending is that the two of them don't reconcile, say it that way; I'd rather leave it open than fake a resolution in front of forty people who watched the last three years happen.
