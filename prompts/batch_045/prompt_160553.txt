Been lurking on the rationalist blogs since roughly the SIAI days and there's one argument on there that never dies, so I'm trying to force a document out of it instead of another comment thread. Two people in my reading group — a Langean and a Hayekian — have been going at each other for about two years over the calculation debate, and I want the adversarial collaboration they should have produced by now.

The reason I think it's worth doing now is that my Langean's position isn't her grandfather's market socialism. Call it PlanBoard v2: a planner that keeps a differentiable simulator of the whole economy — household preferences as one fitted model, firm technologies as another — and updates prices by gradient-based tatonnement instead of the old trial-and-error rule. She says the speed objection is dead: you can re-converge in milliseconds and continuously, so the stale-prices complaint goes away, and you can evaluate capital projects by running counterfactuals inside the simulator, which she thinks answers the point about there being no market in capital goods. My Hayekian says this misses everything: the objection was never about how fast you can compute, the data aren't given to anyone in advance, and a simulator whose inputs were learnt from yesterday's behaviour is definately not going to surprise anyone with a new industry.

What I want from you: the claims both of them could actually sign — about the formalism, about what a price system has to do, about what the old arguments did and didn't establish, about what PlanBoard v2 would and wouldn't be doing. Then their residual disagreements as a table, each one phrased so that its own side would accept the wording (my Langean won't put her name to anything containing "central planning fails"; my Hayekian won't sign "computation solves it"). Then the cruxes — sub-questions where an answer would move at least one of them — and for each crux a concrete case, measurement or argument both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance, plus what each would count as evidence against their own view.

Cruxes I suspect, but use your own judgement and add: whether the allocative and the discovery functions of prices can be seperated; whether the dispersed knowledge is best thought of as missing inputs to a computation or as something that only comes into existence when people trade with their own money on the line; how much of the value of a price system shows up in handling goods and preferences that didn't exist at t=0, and whether a planner can fund experimentation without profit-and-loss selection; whether the benchmark should be Walrasian equilibrium or an ongoing disequilibrium process; and whether claims about local and tacit knowledge are testable at all.

Also tell me honestly which parts of this are verbal disputes about "knowledge", "rational calculation" or "planning", and which are real. Format: numbered agreed claims, then the disagreements table, then the cruxes.
