Hey — I'm in a metaphysics seminar and weve been doing free will, and last week we got onto the question of which varieties of free will are actually worth wanting. Two people in my study group, Ines and Marc, ended up arguing about it for like forty minutes after class and asked me to settle it. I really couldnt. Can you go through it move by move and say who's right about what?

Ines: The thing I want isn't the ability to have done otherwise in exactly the same circumstances. If my action comes out differently with everything relevant held fixed, that's just noise — randomness wouldn't give me control, it would take it away. What I want is that the action comes out of *me*: my reasons, my values, the way I actually deliberate. I'd have done otherwise if I'd had a reason to. Determinism is totally fine with that.

Marc: That's a rebranding, not an answer. On your account you'd still count as free in a world where a team of people arranged every input into your deliberation — they shaped your values over years so you'd pick X, you pick X, it flows smoothly from your character, and you're reasons-responsive the whole way down. If you're happy calling that free will, you've quietly dropped the thing anybody wanted in the first place: that you're the source, that it stops with you.

Ines: Being the ultimate source — causing your own causal powers, causa sui and all that — is just incoherent. You'd have to already exist, with the very powers you were supposed to be giving yourself. You can't want an impossible object, so the only version worth wanting is one that could actually be had. The coherent one wins by default.

Marc: Two things. First, you keep sliding from "I can't make it metaphysically tidy" to "so I must not really want it." The felt openness of deliberation — that it isn't settled yet — is part of what's wanted, and you don't dissolve it by pointing out that neurons are implementing it. Second, causa sui at a single moment is incoherent, fine, but that only kills the instantaneous version. You can be self-made over time instead: each real deliberation reshapes your character, so the character you act from now is partly your own earlier doing. Recursive authorship rather than an unmoved mover.

Ines: The recursion bottoms out. The first deliberation you ever made came out of a character you didn't choose — genes, upbringing, whatever. So you get more links in the chain, not authorship: at every link, the thing doing the authoring is something you didn't author. And the feeling of openness would be there just as strongly in a deterministic world, so it can't be evidence for anything; if the feeling is all you wanted, a pill could hand it to you.

Marc: I'll grant authorship is never total — wanting the 100% version is the incoherent part, I agree. But "worth wanting" was never all-or-nothing. A self that's spent decades noticing itself, arguing with itself and revising itself is substantially self-shaped, and that's a threshold you can actually reach, and it's the one that does the work for praise, blame and self-respect. You're using the impossibility of the maximal version to make the version we can actually have look like nothing.

So: who's right about which specific point, whose moves are actually weak, and what (if anything) are they both missing? I don't need a verdict on free will in general, just on this argument as they actually made it.
