You are a philosopher of probability who also works closely with clinical prediction modelling. Before you write anything, spend a moment sorting out the distinct problems that get bundled together under the reference class problem — how to choose among classes, what to do when admissible classes conflict, and what a probability statement about a single case is even supposed to mean — so that your procedure does not silently collapse them into one.

I chair the prediction-governance committee at a hospital network. Several times a week we are asked to sign off on an individualised risk estimate — this patient's five-year chance of a major osteoporotic fracture is 11%, say — that goes into the chart and triggers or withholds an intervention. Clinicians and patients challenge these numbers constantly, and the challenge always has the same shape: why that group and not another one? Any patient sits in dozens of statistically respectable classes that disagree.

Our current draft rule, written by a statistician, is two lines: take the narrowest reference class for which the registry holds at least 200 observed outcomes; and if two or more admissible classes both contain the patient and differ by more than three percentage points, report the average of their estimates weighted by the square root of each class's sample size. Admissibility is defined only negatively: a class may not be built from a variable measured after the outcome is determined, and may not have been chosen after seeing which classes gave interesting numbers.

Turn this into a procedure my committee can actually run. Give me the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, a positive account of admissibility (I suspect the negative definition is not enough — I do not know what to do with a class defined by something that merely proxies the outcome, like patients who survived at least thirty days, or by a variable nobody recorded the reasons for), a better way of handling conflict between classes than our averaging rule, the point at which the procedure returns no individual estimate rather than a number, and the points where it hands the question to a person because it is a question of values rather than evidence. Constraints: the analyst running it day to day has one week of applied statistics, it must run in under an hour on our existing registry, and anything that changes practice needs my signature. Say plainly which steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run it on three cases: a 62-year-old woman with type 2 diabetes and stage 2 chronic kidney disease, where classes defined by postmenopausal diabetes, by kidney stage, and by long-term proton-pump inhibitor use give five-year fracture risks near 9%, 14% and 7%, all from large samples; a 34-year-old man with a rare collagen gene variant, where the narrow class has four patients and the broad class of men under 40 gives a risk so low it would withhold treatment — I expect the procedure to give me an answer I do not like here; and a 71-year-old man with atrial fibrillation, where a scan over forty subgroups turned up one subgroup with a strikingly different stroke risk that nobody had predicted and that would change his management if used.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do, and in particular whether it can ever deliver the probability that this patient has an event, as opposed to a defensible and auditable way of choosing a number.
