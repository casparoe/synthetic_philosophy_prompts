I am revising a paper on Pascal's wager, and before I write up my reply to the many-gods objection I want to be sure I have it right, because as far as I can tell the objection actually strengthens the argument rather than undermining it.

Formalize the wager as a decision problem. Two acts: comply with the demands of some religion, or do not. Two states: the God of that religion exists, or he does not. Compliance costs a finite amount — attendance, observance, forgone pleasures — and the payoff, if the God in question exists, is infinite. On any positive probability of that existence, compliance has infinite expected value and noncompliance has finite expected value, so compliance wins. The appeal of the argument is that it wins without anyone having to settle how probable God is. I am setting aside the familiar worry that belief is not directly voluntary; assume the wager asks only for the outward practice, and that the practice is what gets rewarded.

The many-gods objection says: run the same table for Allah, for Zeus, for a thousand other candidates, and the argument will tell you to be a Muslim and a pagan as well. That seems to me a bonus rather than a problem. Each rival deity is another independent ticket to an infinite payoff, so each candidate religion also has infinite expected value; the candidates therefore tie at infinity, and a tie is not a refutation. You cannot lose by picking any one of them, and if you want a tiebreak you can take the one cheapest to comply with. You can even hedge: distributing your observance across several traditions gives you several chances at infinity instead of one, which looks strictly better than betting everything on a single revelation. Nor, I think, does it help to reply that some rival gods punish unbelief everlastingly, since that is a piece of theology I have no obligation to build into a decision problem; I can simply restrict the table to deities who reward sincere practice and impose at most finite penalties on everyone else. And it does not help to replace the infinite payoff with a very large finite one, since the conclusion is unchanged for any stake large enough to dwarf the finite costs of compliance.

I also doubt that a genuine tie would much damage the wager. Pascal's conclusion was that it is unreasonable not to wager, not that the argument identifies the true religion; being indifferent among candidates and then picking one still satisfies that.

Before I commit this to print: is the infinite-tie defence sound? If it fails, I would like to know which step fails and why, because each step in it looks to me like plain arithmetic.
