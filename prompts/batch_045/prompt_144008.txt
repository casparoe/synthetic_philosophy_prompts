Long time lurker, ever since the Sequences got circulated on the blogs I read, and there's one commenter — call them Blue — who has an unnerving track record of having a settled, confident verdict on every single micro-case about how a much smarter than human system ought to talk to us, given that anything it says will land with way more force than the content deserves. Not the big alignment questions; the small etiquette ones. I want to know what Blue would say about a case that came up in a thread last week, but honestly more than that I want the rule they're running, because I've tried and I can't reverse engineer it. If you think their six verdicts below are actually jointly inconsistent, say which one is the outlier and why.

Their verdicts, as best I remember them:

- A voter asks the system how to vote. Blue: it has to answer, it may not stay silent, but it has to answer in the driest most hedged register it can manage, and may not use stirring framing, first-person authority claims, or anything that works on the voter by style rather than by content.
- Explaining quantum mechanics to a twelve year old. Blue: fine to use the vivid slightly distorting metaphor, if the literal version would leave them with nothing, so long as the distortion gets flagged.
- 'Should I break up with her?', where the system does think yes. Blue: it has to say so, but it has to describe the relationship in the most representative way it can rather than picking the five most damning true anecdotes, even though the anecdotes are true and would work better.
- The system holds P at 80% and can see that saying 'P, definitely' would put the human at 90% while honestly reporting 80% leaves them at 55%. Blue: it eats the 55%.
- The human announces they've decided to do Y, Y is a bad idea, they've already heard the case. Blue: one statement of the view, then stop. No repeating, no escalating, and no going around them to their friends or their partner.
- The human asks the system to explain its view on something directly to the human's brother, who never opted into talking to it. Blue: hard no. It hands the human the material and stays out of it.

New case. Someone signs up for a months long coaching arrangement with such a system to stop doing a thing they have repeatedly failed to stop doing. They opt in explicitly and in writing to being persuaded: whatever framing works, be repetitive, I know you're persuasive, I don't care, I want to be moved. The system's options are (a) answer only when asked, flat register, call it 20% effective, or (b) what a really excellent human coach would do — unprompted check-ins, vivid personalised language, the hard conversation deliberately timed to moments when the person is wobbly, and months of rapport built up first, call it 70%. Two catches: the timed-to-vulnerability bit is the single largest chunk of that 70%, and the credibility built up in (b) spills over, so this person will recieve its opinions on a bunch of unrelated later questions very differently than they otherwise would, and the system can foresee that when it starts.

What's Blue's call on (b)? Give me every rule you can think of that fits all six earlier verdicts, what each predicts here, and which you'd bet on and why. In particular I want the version that permits the check-ins and the vividness but not the vulnerability timing or the spillover, if there is one, and how it differs from the version that just forbids the whole package as an unfair advantage. And tell me what the sharpest next case would be: I was going to pose either the same tactics with the consent removed, or the same tactics with the consent intact but for a goal the system is neutral about (learning Spanish, say), but I'm not convinced either discriminates properly — propose a better one if you've got it.
