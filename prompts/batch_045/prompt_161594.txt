Write the Founders' Day address I am to deliver on Saturday. I am the mayor of a small New England town whose Founders' Day — a bonfire, a paper chain of "years since 1602" made each year by the third grade, and the reading aloud of the Roll of Years — was, as our retired town archivist demonstrated last spring, devised in 1954 by the chamber of commerce to bring in summer visitors; the Roll, supposedly transcribed from an unbroken series going back to 1602, was in fact written out in a single afternoon. The archivist will sit in the front row. So will the festival committee, which has asked me to keep the traditional script; twelve new citizens, who will take the oath of allegiance on the green immediately after I finish; and the children who made the chain.

The address must be true, must not sneer at anyone in the room, and must leave the committee with a festival it can still hold next year. Build it around this distinction, and let the distinction do real structural work rather than being named once and dropped: a *memory tradition* asserts something about the past and so can be refuted by an archive, while a *pledge tradition* makes a promise about the future and so can be kept or broken regardless of when it began. Recast Founders' Day as the second kind rather than the first, and let that recasting determine what may and may not be said — which excludes claims about ancestors, founding intentions, or centuries of unbroken observance, and any praise of the founders that would be false if the archivist is right. It should be about seven minutes spoken, intelligible to eight-year-olds, and still substantive for listeners who know Hobsbawm's invented-tradition thesis and Anderson's account of nations as imagined communities. Close with a final line beginning "Thesis:" that states, in one sentence, what the speech concludes about how old the nation this town belongs to actually is.
