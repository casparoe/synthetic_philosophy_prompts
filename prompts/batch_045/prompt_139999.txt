I sit on the bylaws committee of a 60-unit housing co-op, and last month we discovered that our amendment procedure has quietly eaten itself. The structure is roughly this: a general provision says bylaws may be amended by a two-thirds vote; an entrenched provision says *that* provision may be amended only by a three-quarter vote at a meeting with 60% of members present; a third provision says that where two provisions conflict, the more recently adopted one wins, which everyone assumed was a harmless tie-breaker until someone pointed out that it makes the ordering of the code a function of adoption dates; and a fourth, adopted years ago, says that any provision marked with an E may be repealed only by three-quarter votes at two consecutive meetings. Then came the parking amendment. It passed with barely a quorum, it reallocated the spaces, it contained a clause saying no later provision would affect its allocations unless that later provision named it by section number, and — this is the part I keep turning over — it asserted that for the avoidance of doubt it should be treated as having been adopted four years earlier. Whether it is in force now seems to depend on questions our bylaws do not answer and cannot answer from within themselves: whether a provision may stipulate its own adoption date; whether a clause that shields a provision from later unmentioned provisions is itself an entrenchment that needed the E procedure; which text counts as the authoritative record when the minutes and the signed copy disagree; and who decides any of that, and by what authority. My committee-mates keep treating it as a parking dispute. I have become convinced it is a dispute about self-reference that happens to be about parking.

I also run a fortnightly reading group on law and logic for about sixteen people — two or three lawyers, several programmers, a handful of undergraduates, a few who will not have done the reading — and I have a 90-minute session coming up that I want to spend on exactly this: not on my co-op's bylaws as a case study, but on the structural pathologies that my co-op stumbled into. What I want is an activity in the spirit of that old parlor game where changing the rules is itself the move (Nomic, if I have the name right), but rigged rather than open-ended: I do not want to hand out an ordinary rulebook and hope something interesting happens, because in my experience half the room proposes nothing and the other half proposes joke rules about hats. Please design a game whose seed ruleset looks completely boring — the kind of procedural boilerplate a real association would actually adopt — but which is engineered so that the group very likely walks into three specific traps. First, self-amendment: a properly passed change that alters the test for what counts as properly passed, so that the group cannot say without circularity whether that very change is in force. Second, a priority deadlock: two rules that each say they win conflicts, with no third rule saying which of them wins, so the group must resolve it and then notice that nothing licensed the resolution. Third, self-defeating entrenchment: a protection around the amendment procedure that is itself removable by an amendment passed under the procedure it was meant to protect. I would also like an authoritative-record gap built in — something about whose written version of the rules is the official one — since that was the thing that actually paralyzed us. Two further constraints. The stakes should be real but harmless: I am happy for the resulting ruleset to govern the group's remaining four sessions (who presents, whether there are readings, how long people may talk), and I would like a scoring scheme in which points accrue for proposals that are still in force at the end rather than for proposals that merely won a vote, so that participants start caring about validity rather than about winning. And nobody should hear the words rule of recognition, entrenchment, or self-amendment until the debrief; the distinctions should be things the group has already collided with and then names. If it helps, you can give two or three participants private briefs nudging them toward a particular move, but the traps should be structural, in the seed rules, not dependent on hidden cards being played well.

What I would like back is the following, in this order and roughly these lengths. "Setup and materials" (about 120 words): what I need in the room, what gets handed out, and what must not be said up front. "The seed ruleset" (about 250 words): the full text I would put in front of them, with a note after each rule on what it is for. "Private briefs" (about 150 words): any role cards or nudges, and how many to hand out. "Timeline" (about 200 words): a minute-by-minute plan for 90 minutes, including how the session ends before the debrief. "How the traps are rigged" (about 200 words): for each of the three traps plus the record gap, the mechanism in the seed rules that makes it likely, and what I do if the group misses it entirely. "Debrief" (about 300 words): the mapping from moments in the session onto the distinctions I named above, in the order I should raise them, including how to get from their confusion to the point that a system's own tests for which rules are in force cannot be validated by that system. "What will go wrong" (about 150 words): failure modes — the room that repeals everything in the first ten minutes, the room that never touches a second-order rule, the lawyers who start importing real constitutional doctrine, the quiet members who end up holding a veto — and how to handle each. "What the game cannot teach" (about 120 words): be blunt about which part of the idea no 90-minute exercise can reproduce and which I will have to say in so many words myself.
