I've mostly come round to moral particularism — not the fluffy "context matters" version but the real one: no non-vacuous moral principles, and holism about valence, so the same feature (that it was a promise, that it caused pain, that someone was lied to) can push the other way, or not at all, depending on what else is going on, with no default. I want to know what would genuinely count against this.

Skip the standard "then how do you teach ethics / how do you deliberate" as a gotcha unless you can give the particularist's best answer and say why it fails. The three things I'm actually stuck on: (1) if moral perception is something we get trained into, there has to be some systematic pattern across the cases a novice is trained on that she's picking up, and it's hard to see how that isnt a generalizable feature, just one nobody has written down yet; (2) particularists often help themselves to something like "some reasons have a default valence even though it's defeasible," and that looks like generalism readmitted through the back door, or at least a collapse into it; (3) if it turned out holism about valence holds for epistemic or prudential reasons too, does that help particularism (same phenomenon everywhere) or gut it (because then it says nothing distinctively moral, and we still seem to want principles in epistemology)?

Rank whatever you give me: fatal, versus forces an awkward amendment, versus merely embarrassing. And separate what particularists would themselves concede counts against them from what they'd rightly dismiss as changing the subject. Be honest about whether the view has been qualified into something unfalsifiable — if every apparent counterexample gets reclassified as "you just failed to see the whole context," is there anything left that it risks? What would move me most is a concrete pair of cases where the view is committed to something plainly unacceptable.
