ok so I keep coming back to Parfit's hitchhiker and going round in circles, and I think what I actually need is a spec rather than another argument. The version I've been working with, beefed up a bit: I'm stranded in the desert, a driver will take me to town for £1000 but only if she's convinced I'll actually pay once we arrive, and she reads character and behaviour about as well as a person can; we get there, my life is saved, the money is sitting in an account, and paying now costs me £1000 and does nothing for me. Ex post, paying is plainly irrational. Ex ante, being the sort of person who pays plainly gets me saved. Everyone agrees on that much and then the arguing starts.

So write me the desiderata. What would a theory of rational irrationality have to get right to count as adequate — the cases it has to handle, the distinctions it can't collapse, the questions it has to give a usable verdict on — with the consideration behind each requirement, and say for each whether you think it's a genuine desideratum or just a prejudice of one camp. Purpose: good enough that I could hand the theory to someone in a real-life version of that situation and have them know what to do, and good enough to settle whether "rational irrationality" is one phenomenon or four things wearing one name.

Things I want on the list, or want explicitly ruled off it — add your own, these are just the ones that keep biting me:

- what the relata of "rational" even are here: acts, plans, dispositions, meta-choices about what to become. Is a theory allowed to get its result just by switching relatum?
- the vehicle: is the useful irrationality a false belief, a shifted desire, or a disposition to act against your own better judgement, and does the answer matter? My worry is that false beliefs leak — they mislead you in every other decision you make — whereas a shifted desire might not.
- the leakage/scope problem: whatever gets me to town shouldn't also make me pay every blackmailer, tip every waiter, and hand my savings to anyone who can stage something promise-shaped.
- the creepy-driver case: a driver who can tell not just whether you're sincere but whether you engineered your disposition. She can see you took the "make me a promise-keeper" pill, and she won't pick up anyone who did, because someone who deliberately acquired the disposition is still maximising one level up. Is "you must be able to acquire it on purpose" a real requirement, and if so does it tip into a regress?
- the unwind case: once she's driven me, I'm offered a pill that removes the disposition at no cost. Take it? And if I know the pill will be offered later, does that undo the whole move?
- the external-commitment case: if I can just escrow the £1000 in advance, the puzzle seems to dissolve. So is rational irrationality only needed where commitment devices are unavailable? Desideratum, or reductio of the whole enterprise?
- one-shot versus repeated: does the theory have to say anything different when reputations are in play, and is the iterated case secretly doing all the work in the intuitive answer?
- first-personal or third-personal: must it give me something I can act on from the inside, or is it allowed to say only "it would be better if she were disposed to pay", as a seperate third-personal verdict?

Flag any pair you suspect can't be jointly satisfied. My guesses: (a) the disposition has to be credible to a very good reader of character, which wants it to be reliable and not something I can quietly revise; (b) it has to be revisable and tightly scoped, so it doesn't leak and doesn't cost me once we're in town. I suspect those pull apart, and maybe "acquirable on purpose" against "not strategic enough to fool a perceptive driver" too. If some subset is jointly unsatisfiable, say which one to drop — that's the bit I most want. And tell me which single requirement does the most work in knocking candidates out.

Then, briefly — a few lines, not a chapter — how the three obvious contenders do against your list: plain maximising (don't pay, and that's that), the disposition or character view (be the kind of person who pays), and self-binding (pre-commit however you can). A table is fine if that's less writing. Dont bother with citations, I just want the reasoning. British English please, and lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — one sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble and no restating my question.
