hey — I write novels, mostly literary-ish mysteries (three out, fourth one is giving me hell), and over the winter I went through one of those phases of reading pop-science about the brain as a prediction machine. I half-understand it: something about the brain running a generative model that predicts its own sensory input, updating the model when prediction error arrives, action being a way of making the world fit the prediction instead of the other way around, hierarchy of levels, and some kind of weighting of how much a given signal should be trusted. I'm not going to pretend I can follow any of the math, and I don't want a mini-lecture — I want a translation.

Here's the deal: my actual working vocabulary is craft vocabulary. Show-don't-tell. The contract a first chapter makes with the reader about what kind of book this is. Foreshadowing, red herrings, the unreliable narrator, defamiliarization, pacing as a budget you spend. Plus two things my writers' group uses that I've never seen written down anywhere: the *debt rule* (any physical object you spend more than one sentence describing is a promissory note to the reader, and it has to either be paid off later or knowingly defaulted on, and you have to be able to say which you're doing) and a scene's *tolerance* (how much surprise a reader will absorb in a given scene before they stop trusting the narrator — which in my experience is high in chapter one and near zero in the last thirty pages). Could you restate predictive processing in *that* vocabulary — the generative model, priors, prediction error, the precision/weighting bit, active inference, the hierarchical stuff, and the free-energy thing itself — as carefully as you can, using my words instead of yours, and then give me the residue report: what carries over cleanly, what you had to bend to make it fit, and what simply cannot be said in craft talk at all. Two specific puzzles I want addressed head-on, not dodged. First: the theory seems to be about minimizing surprise, but people pay twenty-five dollars to be surprised, and my whole genre is a machine for delivering violations of expectation in a controlled way — so where does that go in the translation, and is the answer "the theory isn't about novels" or is it something more interesting? Second: is any of this actually falsifiable, or is it a redescription that would fit whatever a draft does? Because if it's the second, I'd rather know now than build a revision method on it.

Then run the translated vocabulary on three things from my draft, since that's the only test I care about. (1) Chapter four opens with half a page on a bowl of pears going soft on a kitchen table; the pears never come back. The group said cut it; my debt rule says it's an unpaid note. What does the translation say — and does it say anything my rule doesn't? (2) In chapter one I withhold that the narrator is eleven years old until the fourth paragraph. Every beta reader reported going back and re-reading those first three paragraphs differently once they found out. I want that re-reading described in the translated vocabulary, precisely, including what changes and what doesn't. (3) There's a scene I love where almost nothing happens — a woman drives forty minutes to a beach and sits in the car — and every reader so far has called it dead weight. Give me the honest verdict the translation produces here, even if it's the one I don't want. Constraints: I revise alone and nobody signs off until my agent sees the whole thing, so this has to be something I can run in about fifteen minutes per scene without a glossary next to me; mark clearly which steps are real tests and which are judgment calls wearing a lab coat; and don't give me a table of "X in the theory = Y in craft" one-liners — I want prose I can argue with. I'm eight months into this book and about to do a full pass, so I'd rather have a tool that misfires in known places than an elegant framing I can't use.
