Our reading group is working its way through a popular book on logic and paradox, and this week's chapter was on Lewis Carroll's short 1895 *Mind* dialogue "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" — the one in which the Tortoise grants A and B but refuses to grant Z, and Achilles keeps adding hypotheticals (C, then D, then E) without ever getting him to take the last step. The author uses the dialogue to launch a much broader claim about whether deduction can be justified at all, and while the tone was very confident I can't tell how much of the reasoning is actually carried by Carroll and how much is being read into him. I've reconstructed the central passage as best I remember it; I can't vouch for the exact wording, but I'm fairly confident about the content:

The Tortoise is perfectly cooperative: he grants A and B, and he grants every rule Achilles asks him to grant — first the hypothetical that if A and B are true then Z must be true, then the hypothetical that if A and B and *that* hypothetical are true then Z must be true, and so on indefinitely — and he still declines to accept Z. The moral is that no argument can rationally compel anyone to accept anything: you can pile up all the premises and all the rules of inference you like, but applying a rule to a premise is itself a step that requires a further rule, and then a further rule again, without end. This is really just the ancient regress of reasons in a new costume — the Pyrrhonian point that any justification must either stop at a bare assertion, go round in a circle, or run on forever — and Carroll's version makes vivid that deduction lands squarely on the third horn. The consequence is that the rules of logic cannot be justified: a deductive justification of modus ponens would be circular, an inductive one could at best show that it has worked well so far, and there is no third option. So accepting a rule of inference is never a matter of having a reason; it is a habit drilled into us by training, and in the end which inferences count as valid is just whatever the community that trained us will let us get away with. Notice also that the Tortoise is not being irrational or inconsistent — he never contradicts himself, he merely declines to infer — which shows that consistency alone does not oblige anyone to believe the logical consequences of what they already believe.

My own suspicion concerns that last claim: if the Tortoise genuinely accepts A and B *and* accepts that Z must be true if A and B are true, then it looks as though he is already committed to Z, so the portrait of him as a logically impeccable but uncooperative reasoner can't be right as stated. But perhaps the puzzle needs the hypotheticals to be read in some other way — as expressing a rule rather than a proposition, or as claims about what he is entitled to conclude — and I can't tell whether that rescues the chapter's argument or quietly destroys it. A friend of mine also proposed a fix for the regress that I can't evaluate: instead of infinitely many hypotheticals, add a *single* self-referential premise S, the proposition that if A and B and S itself are all true then Z must be true. Since S occurs in its own antecedent, he says, it licenses its own application and the regress stops after one step.

Could you audit the passage claim by claim — what a philosopher of logic would accept, what they would wince at, and what is straightforwardly wrong, including any place where two claims Carroll's puzzle keeps apart get run together — and then say what the dialogue on its own actually establishes, as opposed to what the chapter infers from it? Please also work through the consistency question I raised and through my friend's self-referential premise: does S halt the regress, or does it merely smuggle the conclusion back in (or fail to be a well-grounded proposition at all), and how does that compare with the more familiar move of relocating the rule into a metalanguage as a rule of inference rather than a premise? Finally, does the chapter's grand conclusion — that our acceptance of modus ponens is an unjustified habit and that validity is a matter of communal training — follow from anything true in the summary, or would a defender of that conclusion have to add premises Carroll's dialogue does not supply?
