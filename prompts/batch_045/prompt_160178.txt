Hey — I'm a novelist and I'm stuck on a scene, so I want to do something slightly unhinged: run the actual thing as a game with real people, watch them squirm, and steal what I see for the page.

The book: a developer goes bust and walks away from a half-finished resort town in the desert, and the forty-odd people still living there end up having to govern themselves — no owner, no county that wants them, no legal anything. My protagonist is on the committee that has to write down, on paper, who gets a vote. Half my draft is people being extremely reasonable about this in ways that are transparently self-serving, and I want to know what that actually sounds and feels like in a room instead of in my head.

So: I'm running a two-hour session at a writers retreat next month. 14 adults, mostly fiction writers, no philosophy background (one of them will have read a book — plan for that). Big room, index cards, name tags, a whiteboard, no tech, and I can put up maybe fifty bucks of real money as a stake if that helps. What I want from you is a game in which the group has to constitute a decision-making body for something they collectively control, and then discover on their own that the question of who votes cannot itself be settled by voting, because any vote is counted over some already-specified set of people — and the people who got to specify that first set were just whoever happened to be in the room, or spoke first, or was me. I want them to hit that circularity and get genuinely annoyed about it, and I especially want the moment where someone proposes that everyone affected should get a vote and it detonates: affected by what, how far out, and who here speaks for the ones who aren't here.

Some things I've been playing with that you should use or junk as you see fit: a round zero whose membership is set by an obviously arbitrary criterion (arrival order, alphabetical, whoever is holding a red card) plus a ratchet rule — the voting set may be expanded by majority vote of the current set but never contracted, so the initial accident is permanent and every later inclusion arrives looking like a gift from the insiders; proxy cards for parties not in the room (a kid, an unborn grandchild, a seasonal worker, an absentee landowner who has never visited, the river, an investor), where the holder must say out loud before voting whether they cast that card in the absent party's interest or their own; and a mid-game event — a new arrival, an outside buyer, a fire — that changes who is affected while a rule is already on the books, forcing the boundary to be re-litigated under the old rule.

Give me the setup and materials, the roles and cards, the rounds with timings, what the facilitator says and — more important — refuses to say, and how the incentives are arranged. Then tell me what I should be listening for and noting down as a novelist: the actual sentences people use to justify excluding someone, how the excluded behave, who goes quiet, who starts performing reasonableness. Then a debrief that maps specific moments onto the actual problem — the regress, the gap between everyone affected and everyone subject to the rules, what the ratchet is for, why the proxy cards are a trap — plus what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm guessing someone will try to dissolve the whole thing with obviously everyone votes, and that it will tip into a personality contest) and how I handle that without turning into a lecturer.

Last thing, because I'm going to put this in a book: separate for me what's basically settled among people who actually work on this from what's a live argument, and don't let a contested view come back to me dressed as the consensus. I don't want my character declaiming something as obvious fact that would make a political theorist roll their eyes. And be straight with me about what a two-hour game in a barn can't show me — the parts of the problem I'll have to supply in prose because the activity can't produce them.
