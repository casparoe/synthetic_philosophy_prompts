Something about the way rich countries actually run their borders has been bugging me, and I cant put my finger on the shape of the theory behind it. Here's the bundle of things I mean, all of them completely standard practice:

- They let in plenty of people, but a lot of them on temporary work visas tied to one employer. Quit, or get fired, and your right to be there evaporates. So whether you stay depends on continuing to be useful to one particular private party.
- Someone whose grandparent was a citizen, who has never set foot in the country and speaks none of the language, can show up tomorrow, vote, get a passport. Someone who was brought over at age two and has lived there thirty years, pays taxes, has citizen kids, is removable — and the removal is enforced, by people with guns, against someone with no vote and no say in the rule that's being enforced on them.
- Inside blocs like the EU, the borders between member states are basically open and everyone treats that as obviously fine; the outer edge of the same territory is fenced and patrolled and everyone treats that as obviously necessary.
- Nobody seriously claims a right to stop people leaving, and stripping an actual citizen and kicking them out is treated as grotesque. Refusing to let someone in is treated as the least controversial thing a state does.
- Except asylum, which is the one route where the state's own preferences arent supposed to matter, and which states visibly resent having to honour.

So: what does this package presuppose? I dont mean is it justified — I mean, what view of membership, of territory, of consent, of who owes reasons to whom, does the whole thing only make sense under? Is it one theory, or several bolted together — does the guest-worker bit come from a different place than the descent bit, or the outer fence bit? Is there a real inconsistency in there, or does one story cover all of it?

My own half-baked framing, take it or leave it: it looks to me like what's being claimed isn't really a right to exclude so much as a right to refuse without having to say why — a right to not be answerable. Asylum is then interesting precisely because it's the one place that privelage is withdrawn, and states experience the withdrawal as a loss of something. Does that reconstruct the practice better than the usual framings (a club with freedom of association, an owner of territory, a trustee of a people's self-determination, a manager of a labour market), or is it just relabelling?

Concretely: if you swapped in the view that membership tracks subjection and actual participation — you're a member of whatever really rules you and whoever you actually live among, not of wherever your grandparents came from — what would have to change? Which bits collapse right away, which survive (does the internal/external asymmetry survive?), and which bits are just underdetermined, where several theories would license the same practice and the practice tells us less than we think?
