hey — I'm on the six-person Collections and Commemoration Committee at Halloway College (small liberal arts place, ~1,400 students, founded 1871 as a normal school). We owe the Board of Visitors a written recommendation before their March meeting and we're split pretty much down the middle, so I got volunteered to draft. Here's the case.

Last year the Pell family offered us $6M — that's about four years of our financial-aid budget for a school our size — to build and endow the "Pell Heritage Room," a permanent exhibition of the college's own administrative records from 1871 to 1954. That means the admissions ledgers showing the college refused Black applicants right up to 1954, the minutes of the committee that ran compulsory chapel and fined students for skipping it, and the correspondence around the 1919 dismissal of a professor for pacifism. It also means the good stuff: the founding charter, the 1890s records of the college admitting women on the same terms as men, the faculty's depression-era relief work in town.

The catch is a clause in the gift agreement. The room has to display items "with the descriptions affixed at installation," and any future interpretive material — wall text, labels, audio guide, website copy — needs written approval from the Pell descendants, in perpetuity. Our general counsel says it's enforceable, that it runs with the gift, and that if we breach it the money comes back.

Our head archivist, Maren Osei, has put forward something she calls the "rolling seal." The most damaging documents — she has a list of roughly forty items — get exhibited for twenty years, then withdrawn from public display and sealed for twenty, on a cycle; sealed items stay preserved and available to researchers by application, they just aren't on the wall. Her argument is that a permanent unchanging exhibit of your own worst documents doesn't produce reckoning, it produces numbness or a permanent institutional identity built on self-condemnation, and that a cohort that meets the ledgers fresh is going to read them differently than one that walks past them every day on the way to the mailroom. Two of us think this is the most interesting thing anyone's put in front of us in years. Two think it's a euphemism for hiding things and that the student paper will say exactly that within a week.

Meanwhile student government has passed a resolution demanding the whole collection be digitized and put online with no restrictions at all. And, separately, our faculty member Dana Reinholt keeps insisting the entire question turns on Nietzsche's early essay on history and that the gift is "pure antiquarian piety with the critical mode contractually disabled." I don't entirely disagree, but shouting "antiquarian" at a donor agreement is not a decision, and I need something the Board can actually vote on.

So: draft me the memo. What I need in it:

- A bottom line for the Board — accept as offered, accept with conditions (and name them), or decline. Not "the committee should carefully weigh."
- Clause-by-clause reasoning that the Pell family's lawyer could respond to rather than just reject. I want to be able to hand this across the table.
- An honest treatment of whichever option you're not picking. If you're against the rolling seal, say plainly why the strongest version of it fails; if you're for it, say plainly what we lose and what the most likely way it gets abused is.
- A concrete time frame. What happens by when, and when whatever we decide gets reviewed and by whom.
- One thing I specifically want you to grapple with, because I think it's where Dana's Nietzsche frame quietly breaks: he's writing about how a person or a culture needs history in order to live, and needs forgetting too. But a college isn't a person and it doesn't die; the Board that accepts this gift will not be the Board that reads the twenty-year review. So what does "history in the service of life" even mean when the "life" in question is an institution that outlives everyone in the room? If the answer is that the frame just doesn't transfer, say that and give me something that does.
- And note that we are not short of memory here. There's already a campus museum, a Truth and Reclamation report that was recieved in 2019 and never acted on, an oral history project, and starting next fall a required first-year Legacy module. Factor that in instead of treating the Heritage Room as though it would be the college's only act of remembering.

Length: a memo, not an essay — call it 900 to 1,300 words, short sections with headers the Board can quote in the minutes. Plain English, and no citations or reading lists; we're not a faculty committee and half the Board won't follow them.

Then, at the very end, after everything else, one line and only one line, in this form:

\answer{bottom line in five words or fewer; then a semicolon, then the one condition you'd refuse to drop}
