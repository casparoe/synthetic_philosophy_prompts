I'm a journalist finishing a long piece on how non-consequentialist moral theories handle the aggregation problem, and the spine of it is a series of interviews I've done with a moral philosopher — call her Dr. Ellery — who works in the Scanlonian contractualist tradition: an act is wrong if it would be disallowed by any principle that some person could reasonably reject, where the strength of a person's complaint against a principle is meant to be a matter of how that principle treats her, not a unit to be pooled with everyone else's. Across four sittings I've collected her verdicts on rescue cases, always stipulating that everything else is equal, that the people are strangers to the agent, and that no one is responsible for the predicament: one stranger's certain death against five strangers' certain deaths, she saves the five; one death against one death, she says either is permissible; one death against a hundred people each losing a hand, she saves the one who would die; one death against ten thousand people each with a painful but fully recoverable injury, she saves the one who would die, and said the same when I raised the number to a million; one person losing both hands against nine people each losing one hand, she saves the nine; one person losing a hand against nine people each losing a hand, she saves the nine; and one person's certain death against a hundred people each facing a one-in-a-hundred risk of death, she saves the certain one, but more slowly than she answered any of the others, and with the remark that risk cases feel different to her in a way she couldn't quite articulate.

I have one interview slot left and I want to spend it well, so I need to know where her track record actually points. The case I mean to put to her is this: one identified stranger will certainly die unless you act, versus four thousand strangers each certainly losing a hand — with a wrinkle none of her earlier cases contained, since every one of those had harms that were uniform on each side: among the four thousand, most would lose a non-dominant hand, about three hundred would lose a hand their livelihood depends on, and forty would lose their only remaining hand. I've started calling this the granularity problem, the question whether contractualist complaints can be pooled when they are merely comparable in seriousness rather than, so to speak, the same complaint repeated, and I genuinely can't tell whether it is a real feature of the theory or a distraction I've talked myself into. I also mean to put a variant: the four thousand don't lose hands at all but instead each face a one-in-four-hundred risk of death, so that ten expected deaths stand against one certain death.

What I want from you is not your own position on aggregation, and not a defense of her view against utilitarianism — it is a reading of the record. Set out the two or three rules that fit her seven verdicts about equally well, say which of the candidates her verdicts eliminate and which survive, and be explicit wherever a rule's prediction depends on a quantity her earlier answers leave open, such as the rate at which she trades hands for lives or certain deaths for chances. My own candidates, which I don't trust, are a lexical rule on which the strongest individual complaint wins and numbers only break exact ties; a global sum of weighted complaints with a floor below which harms cannot be aggregated at all; and a tiered rule on which a harm of a more serious kind to one person defeats any number of harms of a less serious kind while numbers count freely within a kind — but if the rules that actually fit are different, use those. Then predict what she says about the four-thousand-hands case and about the risk variant, give the reasoning that gets you there, and tell me plainly how much confidence the prediction deserves. Finally, I can put exactly one further case to her after these two: name the single case that would do the most to separate the rules still in play, and say why it separates them better than the ones I already have.
