I write about science and ideas for a living, which means I spend a lot of evenings watching two people who each know a great deal about one thing talk past each other. Last week it was Yusuf, who does learning theory, and Bea, who works on philosophy of science, and the subject was whether the no-free-lunch theorems have anything to do with Hume's problem of induction. I couldnt tell who was winning, they asked me to referee, and I obviously can't. Can you?

Here's roughly how it went, cleaned up a bit.

Yusuf: The NFL theorems are Hume's problem of induction with a proof stapled to them. Fix any two learning algorithms, average their off-training-set error over every possible way the data could continue, and they come out exactly tied. That's "you have no reason to expect the future to resemble the past" as a theorem. Induction is done. And so is any appeal to simplicity: averaged over all target functions, a simplicity bias does precisely as well as a bias toward maximal complexity.

Bea: That's a parity result, not a skeptical one. Relabel the unobserved cases and any advantage vanishes, which is Goodman's grue riddle in a lab coat — a symmetry of the description, not of the world. And notice it gives you no reason to prefer randomness either. To get from "they tie on average" to "you aren't justified" you need an extra premise: that a choice of prior has to be justified a priori. That premise is Hume's, not the theorem's.

Yusuf: Maybe, but the parity framing hides what you have to pay. To get a free lunch you need the world to sit in the corner of function space where compression pays, and that corner is vanishingly small. Almost every Boolean function on n inputs has essentially maximal complexity; the fraction whose shortest description is dramatically shorter than the full truth table goes to zero as n grows. So "we have evidence our bias works" requires believing we landed in an exponentially unlikely corner. Worse, the domains where simplicity keeps paying off — cosmology, genetics, materials — are domains we study because it paid off there. The ones where it flopped have no phenomena, so nobody writes them up. Call it the survivorship of legibility.

Bea: I'll grant the selection point, I think it's your strongest, but it cuts the other way. If the selection runs all the way down, then "most of function space is structureless" isn't something you could have empirical evidence for either — it's an a priori combinatorial fact you're using to undermine a posteriori reasoning, and I want to know why that a priori fact gets to be authoritative when a simplicity prior doesn't. Also, the rarity of structure is what makes finding it informative. If structure were everywhere, finding it would tell us nothing. You don't get to charge rarity twice.

Yusuf: Fine, but suppose I concede all of it. You still don't get to say which bias. "The world has structure" is compatible with endlessly many structure-priors — simplicity, smoothness, sparsity, modularity, symmetry — and they come apart on real data. NFL says they tie on average, and inside the structured corner nothing in your argument picks the corner's shape. The history of the field is priors that worked for reasons nobody anticipated. So the honest verdict is: not justified, lucky so far, and the only policy with a track record is "generate a lot of biases and keep the ones that survive out-of-sample." Which is induction one level up. Reichenbach's vindication, not justification.

Bea: That last sentence is where we agree, but you can't have both halves of it. If "keep the biases that survive out-of-sample" has itself survived out-of-sample, you've got a vindication argument, and then you owe me a story about why vindication isn't a kind of justification — unless you're quietly reinstating the premise from your second turn, that justification has to be a priori.

One more exchange near the end, which I suspect is where the real action is. Bea said the NFL results are all about one problem at a time: you average over target functions for a single domain. But the fact that seems to matter is portability — the same bias that works in one domain works in a domain nobody tuned it for — and that's a fact about the joint distribution over many domains, which NFL says nothing about. Yusuf said sure, then treat all the domains together as one giant problem: the joint distribution is just another target function, and the theorem applies to it too unless you assume something about the joint, which is the same assumption one level up, plus interest. I can't tell if that's a real answer or wordplay.

So: who's right about what, move by move? Please rate each move rather than just declaring a winner, and tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing.
