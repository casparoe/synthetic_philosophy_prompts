Analyze the Flint, Michigan water crisis of 2014–2016 as a case of causation by omission, and then tell me what the case does to the theory.

I know the journalistic story and I don't need it retold as an indictment. What I want is a disciplined mapping — what exactly the omissions were, who or what bore them, against which baseline, with which contrast events, and over what time-window — followed by an honest account of which parts of the episode the standard accounts of omissions explain and which they misdescribe or simply fail to reach, and whether Flint ends up confirming, straining, or bypassing the theory.

I'll stipulate the facts; if one is wrong, correct it in a clause and carry on, because I don't want the argument to turn on a contested detail:

- In April 2014, with the city under a state-appointed emergency manager, Flint switched its drinking-water supply from the Detroit system (Lake Huron water) to the Flint River, as a cost-saving measure pending a separate pipeline.
- The river water was treated, but no corrosion inhibitor (an orthophosphate-based treatment) was added. Lead then leached into the supply from lead service lines and household plumbing; on the order of 100,000 residents were exposed, including roughly 6,000–12,000 children with elevated blood lead.
- Residents complained about colour, smell and taste almost immediately; there were boil-water advisories in 2014; in October 2014 a local General Motors engine plant stopped using city water because chlorides were corroding engine parts.
- The stated regulatory position was that corrosion-control treatment was not required under the federal Lead and Copper Rule until monitoring showed exceedances across two consecutive six-month periods, which pushed any requirement well into 2015.
- A 2015 paediatric study found elevated blood lead levels in Flint children; the city switched back to Detroit water in October 2015; a state of emergency was declared in January 2016; an outbreak of Legionnaires' disease in the county (twelve deaths) was linked to the water system, though the bacterial source was never identified.
- Afterwards: several officials resigned, there was a very large civil settlement, and the criminal prosecutions that followed largely failed to produce convictions.

Three things I want handled explicitly, because they are where I think the interesting pressure lies:

First, sorting the omissions. I want you to use a three-way distinction that I'll supply, since I don't think the literature's usual examples force it: *rescue omissions* (an agent fails to avert a threat that is already heading for the victim), *maintenance omissions* (an agent fails to sustain a protective condition — a passivated pipe scale, a buffer, a margin — that is quietly degrading, where no discrete threat-event ever arrives), and *procedural or safeguard omissions* (an agent fails to put in place the rule, test, deadline or monitoring regime that would have made some later omission by someone else impossible or visible). Sort the Flint omissions into these categories, allow that a single episode can contain all three at different levels, and then say whether the causal claim gets stronger, weaker, or just different as you move from the first category to the third — and whether, at the far end, we are still talking about causation at all rather than about the design of an institution.

Second, description. "Failed to add orthophosphate" can also be described as "supplied water that was chemically aggressive toward lead plumbing," or as "allowed the existing protective scale to dissolve." If re-describing an omission as an act makes the causal claim easy, is that a gain in understanding or a sleight of hand? I want you to say what the act/omission contrast is doing in this case: whether it marks a real difference in the world, a difference in how we pick out events, or a difference in what we want to blame — and what follows for the theory if the last of these is true.

Third, structure and knowledge. The relevant omission seems to belong to an office rather than to a person, and to be spread across offices such that no single individual's acting alone would have averted the outcome. Also, the officials maintained that the water was safe; whatever they knew, they arguably should have known, and there was an available and cheap precaution. So: can actual causation be located here at all, or only difference-making? Does causation by omission require the agent to have known, or merely to have been in a position to know — and if it requires the latter, has causation quietly become partly normative? And because the harm accrued over roughly eighteen months rather than at a moment, at what time does the omission do its causing?

Please work this out from the case and from the concepts themselves; I don't want citations, reading suggestions, or a tour of who has said what. Where an account of omissions has nothing to say about some feature of the episode, say that it is silent rather than stretching it. Argue in your own voice and give me a verdict I could disagree with, including on the question of whether the collapse of most of the criminal cases tracks a genuine causal difficulty in the case or only the law's separate requirements about intent and proof.

Use these sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths — about 2,000 words in total:

1. "The case on the table" (~200 words) — fix the outcome to be explained (be precise: elevated lead at household taps over a period, not "harm to the city"), and say what would not count as an explanation of it.

6. "Description and the act/omission contrast" (~250 words) — the re-description problem above, and whether the contrast is doing causal work.
