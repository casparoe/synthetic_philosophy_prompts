I'm in a graduate seminar on scientific realism and I've volunteered to put together a one-page "stress test" handout on structural realism for our reading group, which I then have to present cold in front of people who know this literature far better than I do. So I'd like your help getting the ordering right rather than just collecting objections.

Please answer as a professional philosopher of science who works on the scientific realism debate. Before you rank anything, brainstorm for a paragraph or two about what a consideration has to do in order to count as a genuine objection here at all — as opposed to (i) a restatement of the position, (ii) a worry it inherits from scientific realism generally and so does nothing to discriminate, (iii) a worry that only bites one version of the view, or (iv) a purely verbal dispute. Use whatever criterion you settle on explicitly when you rank. We're working with the two standard packages: the epistemic version introduced by Worrall in 1989 — the "best of both worlds" idea that what survives theory change and what we are entitled to believe is form or structure rather than content, with the furniture of the world left unknown — and the ontic version that Ladyman distinguished from it in 1998 and defended, on which structure is not merely all we can know but all there is. Here is my list, written in my own words; don't worry if my terminology isn't the standard one, and please don't send me a bibliography or citations — we already have the reading list, and what I need is the dialectic, not the references.

1. The meta-induction bites structure too. The pessimistic meta-induction is usually run on ontology: the ether, caloric fluid and phlogiston are gone, so expect our current posits to go as well. But it looks as though the structural claims of those discarded theories went with them — the equations of the caloric theory are not retained in later thermodynamics the way Fresnel's equations are said to be retained in Maxwell's theory. That raises the suspicion that structural realism rests on a handful of cherry-picked friendly cases, and that "continuity of structure" is survivorship bias in a sample of one or two.

2. Structure with nothing to be structure of. If you delete the objects and keep the relations, you may not have a leaner ontology; you may have nothing at all. Relations seem to need relata, and a structure whose "nodes" are themselves nothing but more structural relations looks either viciously regressive or empty.

3. Safety bought with content. Suppose the realist's claim is only that the world contains some collection of items that realize the right structure. Then the claim looks nearly empirically empty: given enough objects, almost any world will contain some tuples standing in the right relations, so there is nothing much left to be wrong about. On this reading structural realism escapes the historical argument only by making its central claim close to unfalsifiable.

4. Too many structures. Our best theories admit empirically equivalent formulations that are not structurally identical — different symmetry groups, gauge redundancies, dual descriptions of the same physics, position versus momentum formulations. If what counts as "the structure" depends on which formulation you write down, then there may be no unique candidate for the realist to be committed to, and the surplus structure has to be declared unreal. By what criterion, and why is that criterion not just the realism the view was supposed to replace?

5. The success story goes circular. The appeal of structural realism is supposed to be that it keeps the no-miracles explanation of predictive success while giving up the ontology that history has discredited. But if what is retained is exactly what is needed to generate the predictions — the mathematical structure of the equations — then "the predictions succeed because the structure is approximately right" threatens to mean only "the predictive machinery predicts because the predictive machinery is approximately right." Maybe the explanation is circular, or a relabeling of the phenomenon to be explained.

6. The unstable middle. The epistemic version is sold as a middle way: structure known, natures unknown. Yet knowing the structure of a causal network — what causes what, which quantities are conserved, what the symmetries are, what theModal profile of the theory is — already seems to be knowing a great deal about the nature of things, not merely about the shape of our equations. So either the view quietly expands into full-blown realism, or the structure/nature distinction is too vague to carry the weight the position puts on it.

7. Continuity of formalism, not of structure. The flagship historical case (Fresnel to Maxwell, or the Newtonian limit of relativity) is a formal relation: one theory's equations approach the other's in a limiting regime. But the terms change their interpretation across the transition — mass as an invariant quantity versus a velocity-dependent one, or an elastic solid ether versus field values in space. If "the structure" is read off the interpreted theory, then the structure changed and the example shows nothing; if it is read off the uninterpreted equations, the realist is preserving syntax rather than structure.

Then tell me which single item you would cut from the handout as not really an objection to this position at all, and which one you think is most overrated in seminar discussion.
