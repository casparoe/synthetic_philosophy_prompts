Last month I sat through a conference session on animal cognition in which the speaker showed footage of a New Caledonian crow bending a length of wire into a hook; the room applauded the dexterity and, as far as I could tell, entirely missed why the clip was supposed to matter. I'm a science journalist working on a long piece about how explanations of intelligent behaviour have migrated outward — out of the skull, into the body, the environment, and the accumulated scaffolding of tools, marks and other agents — and I would rather not write another article whose argument rests on wonder-footage. As part of the reporting I have agreed to run a ninety-minute participatory session at a science-communication workshop in the spring: about fifteen adults, mostly working journalists and editors, no assumed background in biology or cognitive science, and a room I expect to be allergic to being lectured at and quick to announce that any demonstration has been rigged.

What I want from you is the activity itself: something in which that room feels, rather than is told, the difference between the four rungs of the standard taxonomy of Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian and Gregorian creatures, where each kind has the capacities of those below it plus one more — selection over a population of blind variants; reinforcement of an individual within its own lifetime; the preselection of candidate actions inside the head, so that the bad ones can die in the creature's stead; and the offloading of cognition into artefacts, signs and other agents outside the creature. The design constraints I care about are these: everyone works on the *same* task in all four phases, and the only thing that changes between phases is a rule about which information may pass between attempts and between people — no new puzzle and no new props, so that whatever differs in the outcome is attributable to the mechanism and not to the material; and nobody hears the four labels until the last fifteen minutes, so that until then participants are simply operating under rules. Please give me the rules, the materials (paper, index cards, envelopes, string — no screens, and it has to survive two people not turning up), a payoff scheme built on points or standing rather than money, a minute-by-minute timeline with what the facilitator says at each transition, and the short log line participants fill in after every attempt, so that the comparison at the end is theirs rather than mine.

Two things I want the debrief to do, and they are the reason I am asking rather than adapting something off the shelf. First, I want the room to notice that the lower mechanisms are filters — they can only subtract from a supply of candidates — and that the ladder therefore does not by itself say where the candidates come from, so that the interesting move at the top is the acquisition of generators, things that propose better things to test. Second, I do not want people to leave with a tidy upward ramp: I would like a closing move in which the artefacts or notations produced during the Gregorian phase are carried back into the Darwinian phase, so that participants can see the arrow pointing downward as well as up — tools altering what the blind variation is being selected *for*, and thereby altering the costs and payoffs at the bottom. Build that in as an actual fifth phase if you can, not as a remark from the facilitator. Finally, be straight with me about failure modes and limits: tell me what will predictably go wrong — people smuggling deliberation and table-talk into the phase that is meant to allow only reinforcement, the Darwinian phase feeling like nothing at all is happening, someone deciding to meta-game the scoring and accusing me of having fixed the task, the two quietest participants never touching the materials — and tell me honestly which features of the four-fold distinction the exercise cannot reproduce and which I will therefore have to supply in words. I assume the timescale and population-size asymmetry between the rungs, and the fact that real animals run several of these layers at once rather than taking turns, but give me your own list rather than agreeing with mine.
