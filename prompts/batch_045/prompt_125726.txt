I've been going back and forth on Moore's paradox for a while and I want to see whether my gut actually adds up to a view, because I suspect some of it doesn't.

Quick version for anyone reading over my shoulder: "it's raining but I don't believe it" — said straight out, first person, present tense — is weird in a way that just "it's raining and I don't believe it" from me about you isn't. It's not a contradiction exactly; the world could clearly be that way. Something else is going on, and that's what I want a principle for.

Here are my verdicts:

1. Me saying "it's raining but I don't believe it" out loud: bad, distinctly odd.
2. Me saying it about you — "it's raining but she doesn't believe it": totally fine, nothing odd at all.
3. Past tense about myself — "it was raining, but at the time I didn't believe it": fine.
4. Buried under a supposition and negated — "if I were to say 'it's raining but I don't believe it', that would be strange, so I won't": fine, no weirdness leaks out.
5. "It's raining but I don't believe it" is clearly worse than "it's raining, though I might be wrong about that." Same neighbourhood, very different feel.
6. The reverse order, "I don't believe it's raining, but it is": also fine to my ear, or at least way less bad than (1), even though it seems to say the very same thing. This is the one that bugs me.
7. High stakes version — "the building is on fire but I don't believe it": still odd, but I hear it as *less* odd than the rain one, and I don't know why.
8. Swapping belief for something gradable — "the talk starts at four but I'm not confident it does": barely odd at all.
9. Swapping it to a moral case — "factory farming is wrong but I don't believe it is": sounds bad, but bad in a *different* way than (1), more like a complaint about my character than a glitch in the sentence.
10. A large language model printing "Paris is the capital of France, but I don't believe that": I honestly can't tell. Part of me says it's not odd at all, because there's nothing there to have the attitude; part of me says it's just as odd as (1) because the printed line is the same.

So: is there one principle that gets all ten? I had a candidate, which is that the oddness comes from breaking a rule about assertion — roughly, you're not supposed to put a claim forward while disowning the state that licenses you to put it forward — rather than from anything contradictory or self-defeating in what's said. If that's right it should handle (2), (3) and (4) for free, since those aren't assertions of the whole thing by the right person at the right time. But I can't see how it explains (6), where the order seems to matter though the content doesn't, and it puts me in an awkward spot on (10): either the rule applies to text-output systems, which feels like a stretch, or it doesn't, and then I owe you an account of why the model's line isn't strange when mine is. It also has nothing obvious to say about (9).

What I'd like from you:

- One principle, or at most two rivals, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case you haven't seen. Say which of my verdicts motivates each clause.
- Where my verdicts and your principle come apart, take a side: tell me which one to give up and why, rather than leaving me to choose.
- Give me the principle's verdicts on three cases I haven't listed, including at least one where it's a group rather than an individual — a committee report or a lab's public statement that says "p, but we don't believe p" — and one where the speaker is a system like the one in (10) but is described as having something belief-like tracking its outputs.
- Then a short honest note on where your own account is weakest, and specifically whether the (1)/(6) pair and the (10) case can both be handled without the principle getting so complicated that it's just a list of my answers.

Please commit to a view rather than surveying the options — I can read a survey anywhere.
