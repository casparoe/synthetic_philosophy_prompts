I work on, and defend, a version of the abolitionist project: the view that the long-term task of biotechnology is not merely to prevent suffering from the outside but to abolish it from the inside, by rewriting the neural machinery that produces it. A colleague in computational neuroscience has walked me into an argument which, if it goes through, shows that the maneuver on which the project depends also removes the project's point. I cannot accept the conclusion, and I cannot locate the broken step.

(1) Abolitionism is not the deletion of the mechanisms that carry bad news. The proposal is to preserve what those mechanisms do — representing damage, error, loss or threat, driving avoidance, updating expectations and policies — while removing how they feel. Call this the decoupling thesis: a state's functional role and its hedonic tone are separable, so the role can be kept and the tone deleted.

(2) The thesis is not fantasy. There are clinical reports of patients who retain the sensory and discriminative aspects of pain and who still withdraw from noxious stimuli, yet who deny that the pain bothers them. If the dissociation occurs naturally, there is no in-principle barrier to engineering it.

(3) This is what answers the indispensability objection — the objection that suffering does work we cannot do without, that without it we would not learn, correct our errors, or act. If (1) and (2) hold, the work survives the deletion.

(4) The decoupling thesis is stated generally. It says that tone and role are independent; it does not say that they are independent for pain only. So it licenses the same maneuver on the positive side: keep the role of reward — computing prediction errors, stamping in policies, calibrating behavior — and delete the pleasure.

(5) Suppose then a population of agents in which every functional role is preserved exactly as before and nothing feels like anything: no distress, no enjoyment, no tone at all. Call them decoupled agents.

(6) The abolitionist has no complaint against such a population. By the argument in (3), nothing of functional value has been lost, because the roles are intact. Nothing bad has been introduced, because there is no suffering anywhere in it. Every demand the program makes appears to be satisfied.

(7) But the program was motivated by the thought that felt tone is the thing that matters — that the sting is precisely what we are trying to remove, and that the positive tone which replaces it is the point of the exercise. If a world of decoupled agents satisfies every demand the program makes, then what we set out to remove was never what mattered, and the program had no object.

(8) Hence the dilemma: either valence is not separable from function, in which case abolitionism is unachievable, since we cannot remove the sting without removing the signal; or it is separable, in which case abolitionism is unmotivated, since a functionally perfect and affectively blank world would satisfy it completely.

I would much rather surrender (4) than any of (1), (2) or (3), and I want to keep the motivating thought in (7). But I cannot say what principled asymmetry makes tone detachable on the negative side and not on the positive one, and I am no longer confident that (4) is where the damage is. I have tried locating the asymmetry in the distinction between incentive salience and hedonic impact, but the distinction is drawn on both sides of the valence divide and I cannot see that it marks the difference the argument needs. A second thought pulls the other way: if motivational force without satisfaction amounts to a kind of frustration, then the decoupled agent is not neutral but bad — yet by symmetry the abolitionist's own proposal, deleting the sting while leaving the aversion in place, may leave something bad behind as well, which would tell against (3) rather than (4).

Which step should I reject? Tell me why that step looked sound, and what rejecting it commits me to. If you think the argument is valid and the cost must be paid elsewhere, tell me which of the surviving premises is cheapest to give up and what the resulting position looks like. In particular: can I consistently hold both that suffering must be abolished and that positive hedonic tone is worth producing, or does the argument show that these two halves of the project come apart?
