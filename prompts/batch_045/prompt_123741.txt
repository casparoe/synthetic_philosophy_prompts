hey — so I'm third-year in philosophy here, mostly philosophy of mind with a side of metaethics, and I co-run the grad center's sci-fi-and-philosophy film night. It's free and open to the public, which means the room is usally half grad students from our department and half people who wandered in because it was raining and there's cheap wine. Next month is Blade Runner (we're doing the Final Cut, or whichever cut we can actually get streamed) and I'm giving the ten-minute intro and then leading the discussion afterwards. I really don't want to do the standard 'but are the replicants persons, can a machine have a soul' thing that's been done to death; I want to put the film through Dennett's cranes-vs-skyhooks distinction instead and see how far it actually gets before it breaks.

The bit I've been chewing on, and the reason I think this might be more than an excuse to show a film: Dennett's target is always the skyhook at the *beginning* — the unmoved mover, the special ingredient lowered in from above that gets you design, or mind, or value without the cranes underneath having done the work. But there seems to me to be a structurally different move that the distinction doesn't obviously cover, which I've been calling a terminal skyhook: nobody denies the cranes, everyone cheerfully grants the whole bottom-up story (the thing was assembled out of dumb parts by dumb processes, no élan vital, no ghost), and then, once the crane has finished and the finished pattern is just sitting there, the result gets treated as if it had been lowered from above anyway — as if the achieved output now carried a non-derivable authority that nothing in the crane story predicted or explains. The skyhook is at the end of the process rather than the start, and it's doing justificatory work rather than causal work. Which is roughly what I think the film does: the replicants are literally manufactured, everyone in that world knows exactly how the sausage is made, and then at a certain point — Rachael finding out her memories belong to somebody else, Deckard's status left deliberately undecidable, Batty's dying speech — the film starts demanding that you respond to them as though the manufacturing story had never been told. Is that a confusion, or is it just what respect for a pattern looks like from the inside? And can Dennett actually say anything useful here, or is cranes-vs-skyhooks only a constraint on causal-explanatory stories and completely silent about normative status?

Things I'd want you to get into: Tyrell as the greedy reductionist (his company's whole pitch is that a person is a specifiable engineering problem, and then his death is staged so that the man who treated eyes as components gets his own destroyed), and the Voight-Kampff test, which strikes me as the film quietly undercutting itself — it's a machine for detecting the absence of the very thing, affective response, an emotional life, that a consistent reductionist thinks is perfectly mechanically implementable, so it's a test for a property the film's own ontology says you could just build in. Also where the film resists: my supervisor's line is that the whole Dennettian framing is a category error, that this is a romantic text about mortality with nothing reductive or anti-reductive in it at all, and I suspect she's half right. So I'd like a reading that goes both ways — where the framework genuinely illuminates a scene or a character's choice, and where it flattens something the film is doing and you have to say so honestly — and then, at the end, what if anything the film adds to this debate that the arguments on their own don't. Don't quote lines at me, I've seen it a dozen times and I'd rather have the shape of the scenes than half-remembered dialogue; and please keep it usable for a mixed room, since half the audience won't know what a skyhook is and shouldn't feel lectured at.
