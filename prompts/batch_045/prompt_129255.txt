I'm writing up my position on whether trained nets really represent anything, and I want an audit rather than a lecture. Roughly where I've landed:

1. Realism: there's a fact of the matter about what a given activation in a trained net means, over and above what it's useful for us to say.
2. Features are directions in activation space. That's the right unit of representation — not individual neurons, and not nodes in some graph I draw.
3. Superposition is pervasive: nets pack in many more features than they have dimensions, using near-orthogonal directions.
4. The coordinate basis isn't privileged just because we happened to writen the weights down in it. I'm moved by the rotational-symmetry argument that only the geometry — distances, angles, whatever the nonlinearities single out — is real.
5. Evidence: I'll only accept a representation claim on interventional evidence. Push along the direction, behavior changes in the predicted way. A linear probe that classifies at 95% shows nothing about content on its own.
6. Content: what makes a direction mean F is that it tracks F (in the training-distribution / asymmetric-dependence sort of way), not what later layers do with it. So a direction that no downstream component ever reads out can still mean F — I want to say a model that never does anything with language identity still represents "this text is in French" if some direction tracks it.
7. And one case verdict: the modular-arithmetic results where the model seems to lay numbers out around a circle show that at least some features aren't directions at all.

Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and what's the cheapest revision for each?
