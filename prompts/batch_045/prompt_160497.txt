Me and my mate ended up having a proper row about this last night and I'm still not sure who won. He's doing a PhD in philosophy, I do policy-adjacent work, and the thing we were actually arguing about was whether the problem of other minds is doing any real work in the debates about whether future AI systems could be welfare subjects. His line was that it's just the same sceptical worry we all quietly learned to stop caring about in the human case, and that the only thing that matters is behavioural: if something is indistinguishable from a person who is in distress, refusing to count it is morally on a par with the old excuses people have always made for ignoring sufferers who don't look like them. My counter was that there's a disanalogy he was skating over, which I'm going to call the contamination problem: in the human case, the behavioural evidence is at least roughly independent of our theory of what minds are like, but with a system trained to produce human-like text, every bit of first-person evidence it can offer — reports of distress, stated preferences, apparent revisions under adversarial questioning, even seeming reluctance — is downstream of human self-reports in the training data, so it looks like it inherits support rather than adding any. He said that's just a debunking argument and that it cuts both ways, since we all learn to describe our own feelings in words we picked up from other people, and anyway no one came to believe in other human minds by running the argument from analogy — you just infer to the best explanation and get on with it. I think that reply is too quick but I couldn't quite say why, and I definately couldn't say how much it matters.

The other thing he kept banging on about is that the precautionary framing is basically Pascal's mugging with better PR, and that even if you grant a small credence, the real question is one about moral uncertainty and decision theory rather than about other minds at all. Someone else at the table said none of it is worth thinking about until we have a worked-out theory of what makes consciousness-talk true in the first place, which struck me as the kind of move that guarantees nothing ever gets decided. Anyway, I've now landed in a slightly odd position: a small foundation has asked me to sketch a two-year research programme for them — call it four or five people, mostly philosophers with maybe one interpretability person, no wet lab — whose output is supposed to be a recommendation about whether they should spend real political capital on AI-welfare-flavoured regulation over the next decade, or whether that whole agenda is premature and they'd be better off putting the money elsewhere.

So here's what I want from you: which open questions should that group actually prioritise, and in what order? I'm much less interested in the list itself than in the reasoning behind it. For each question you rank, tell me what makes it upstream of the others, how tractable it is for people doing philosophy and conceptual work rather than empirical ML, whether it's genuinely neglected or already crowded, and what kind of evidence turning up in the next couple of years would move it up or down the list — a working mechanistic story about which computations do the work, a system that reliably reports distress in a way that isn't traceable to its training data, some decision-theoretic result, whatever. Please also be explicit about which candidate questions you'd deprioritise and why: which ones don't actually follow from the worry people think they follow from, and which ones wouldn't change anything downstream even if they were settled. I'd particularly like you to place the contamination problem in that dependency graph — is it the thing everything else hangs off, or is it downstream of some prior question about what the facts about consciousness even consist in, or is it a red herring my mate was right to wave away? And if your honest verdict is that the whole research programme is premature, say that plainly and argue for it against the ranked agenda rather than hedging. No reading lists or citations — I want the argument, not the bibliography.
