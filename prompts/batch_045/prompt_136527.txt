You are a philosopher of social epistemology who also knows the economics of information, and I want slow, careful conceptual work rather than a quick label. Please begin by sketching two or three candidate concepts that the pattern I describe might be an instance of, testing each against the details, and only then decide which fits; if none of them fits, say so plainly and coin a name, with a definition and with cases that are clearly in and clearly out. I do not need citations, papers, or a reading list, and I do not need anything looked up: what I want is the shape of the idea and the location of its edges.

Here is the pattern. I used to work in freight pricing and now sit in on a small reading group on markets and information, which is where this came up. Some institution is very good at pooling what a lot of scattered people know: a futures price, a forecast, a ranking, an odds line. Its success depends on participants continuing to bring it information they acquired independently — the trader who actually drove out and looked at the grain before the price moved, the analyst who actually read the filing. But the more trusted the aggregate becomes, the less point there is in anyone doing that work, and the more rational it is simply to read the aggregate and act on it. The institution slowly consumes the inputs that made it worth reading. Two features make this feel unlike anything I can put a name to. First, the aggregate does not thereby become inaccurate. It can go on being right for a long time, either because it is still living off the residue of earlier inquiry or because the few people who still do the work do it for reasons unrelated to the aggregate, such as needing the information for their own operations. So the decay is invisible from inside: accuracy is not a signal of grounding, and there is no observable difference between a well-founded consensus and a hollowed-out one until the day there is. Second, the failure is not that participants set aside private information they already have in favour of what everyone else appears to believe. It is that the private information never gets produced in the first place. That distinction matters to me, because the accounts I keep encountering in this area are about people who have information and then abandon it — which is not my case.

To show you the edge I am after, here is a case I think is not it: a hiring committee where every member privately thinks the candidate is weak but all speak in her favour, each inferring from the others' apparent confidence that they must know something, until one person breaks ranks and the whole thing collapses. That is a different failure, it turns on false beliefs about other people's beliefs, and a single defector bursts it. My phenomenon does not burst when someone defects, because there is nothing to defect with: nobody has anything independent left to say. So my question is this. Is there an established concept for the erosion of an aggregating institution's epistemic base by its own success, where accuracy outlives grounding? How does it relate, and where exactly does it differ, from the familiar result that a price which fully revealed everything would leave nobody with a reason to gather information (which, as I understand it, is a claim about impossibility in equilibrium rather than about slow, unobservable decay), from herding and informational cascades, and from the broader family of self-defeating or reflexive predictions? I will add, only for orientation and not because I need it answered, that the reading group arrived here from an older puzzle: if the price already contains what everyone knows, what exactly is the person who buys trading on? My question is a cousin of that one, not the same question. Where the nearest concepts each capture only part of what I have described, tell me which part each captures, which part it leaves over, and give me a name for the leftover.
