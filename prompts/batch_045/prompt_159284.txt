Over dinner last night I had a long and inconclusive argument with a friend — she's an economist, and her position was that the backward-induction verdict in the centipede game is neither a prediction nor really a recommendation, but simply what rationality means, with the empirical fact that people pass several times before taking being noise generated by imperfectly rational subjects. I disagreed and yet couldn't say what I wanted instead. We are both auditing the same graduate game-theory course — the centipede came up last week, together with the common-knowledge-of-rationality argument and the reputation-style story about a small prior probability of facing a non-maximiser — and I came away realising that I have no clear idea what I would count as an adequate account of rational play in that game, which is why the argument kept going round in circles.

So, before I read any further into the literature, I want the specification. Write me the desiderata that any adequate theory of how to play the centipede game would have to meet, where 'adequate' is fixed for two purposes at once: it must be able to advise a player who is actually sitting at, say, the fourth node wondering whether to take or to pass — that is, it must say something useful at nodes which the theory itself assigns probability zero — and it must be precise enough to be coded into an agent-based simulation whose output could be compared with laboratory play. For each requirement give the case or consideration behind it, i.e. why it is a genuine requirement rather than the taste of one camp, and mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are negotiable or merely local preferences. Please stress-test the list against three variants I sketched afterwards, because my friend's answer did not obviously survive them: (i) a 'leaky' centipede in which, at each node, Nature terminates play with a known small probability and both players receive a fixed fraction of the current pot — several people at the table said this obviously makes passing reasonable, and I want to know whether a good theory is obliged to agree; (ii) a 'linked' pair of centipedes played consecutively against the same partner, where the size of the pot in the second game depends on how far the first game got, which seems to reopen the reputation story but ought not, on a sound view, to change the verdict; (iii) a version in which you do not observe your opponent's move directly but receive a noisy signal of it, so the game is no longer one of perfect information — I would like a requirement about how the theory's verdict should behave as the noise tends to zero.

Finally, flag any pair or subset of the desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — that is the part I most want, since if the list is internally inconsistent then my friend and I were arguing over an imaginary good — tell me which single requirement excludes the largest number of otherwise attractive accounts, and add a one-line verdict on each of the usual suspects: the common-knowledge argument, the reputation story, level-k and cognitive-hierarchy models, quantal-response equilibrium, and social-preference explanations. Please don't give me citations, references or a reading list: I want the criteria themselves, not a tour of who said what. British English throughout, and please keep the whole answer, table included if you use one, to no more than 900 words.
