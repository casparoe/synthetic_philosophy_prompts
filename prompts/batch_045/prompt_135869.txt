I write literary fiction — my current novel is told in reverse, three interleaved timelines, and one of the characters dies in the very first chapter even though inside the story's chronology it's the last thing that happens. Every reading ends with someone in the audience saying some version of "so it was always going to end that way, the ending was already sitting there on the shelf," and last week a physicist friend went further and told me that's literally the block universe: the whole of spacetime is like a finished novel, all the pages exist at once, and "now" is just wherever your finger happens to be in the book. She was using it to back up the Rietdijk–Putnam line — no absolute simultaneity, so for any two events there's some frame where they're simultaneous, so tomorrow is as real as today — and then, in the same breath, to tell me that my character's death was "already written" in a sense that made my agonising over how to end the thing feel like a cozy illusion. I'd like the audit before I repeat any of this to a room full of strangers who've bought my book.

What I want is the mapping made explicit, then broken. In the novel picture, what plays the role of a time slice, of "now", of the future's existing, of my characters' deliberation, of the fact that the pages have a built-in order while the block's slices apparently don't (isn't the direction of time supposed to come from entropy or a low-entropy boundary condition rather than from the book's binding — and if so, what in the correspondence carries that)? Which features of a novel does the analogy quietly import that spacetime lacks — an author, intentions, a fixed linear order, a text that's about something only once someone reads it, a copy that can be reread out of order or skipped? And she also used the other one, that spacetime is like a landscape where all the places exist and "here" is merely indexical, which seemed to me to do the same work without the fatalism; so which of the two is carrying which conclusion? My worry is that "already written" collapses two different claims: that future-tensed statements have determinate truth values, and that nothing I do now makes a difference to what happens — and only the first of those looks like it follows from the relativity of simultaneity.

My own attempt at a fix was to say it's less like a novel and more like a finished film reel — the whole spool is there, but the projector lamp is what makes one frame present — and she said that just smuggles a moving spotlight back in and I'd reinvented a view she doesn't hold and made it sound deep. Is she right about that? And if the projector version is out, is there a better one: a musical score and its performance, a map against the terrain, a Choose Your Own Adventure book bound with every branch included, or the bit about two people walking past each other in the street who disagree by days about what's happening "now" in Andromeda? Give me the correspondence table, sort the disanalogies into the ones that actually matter for the fatalist payoff and the ones that are cosmetic, tell me whether any analogy can carry the step from "no absolute simultaneity" to "the future is real" or whether that step has to be argued for on its own with the analogy as decoration, and finish with a verdict on what I can honestly say at a Q&A — something that doesn't imply deliberation is pointless and doesn't pretend the physics is silent either. Skip the bibliography, I just need the reasoning, and its fine if you tell me my friend was half wrong.
