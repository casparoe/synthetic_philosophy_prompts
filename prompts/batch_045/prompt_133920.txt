I chair the behavioral evaluation working group at a certification laboratory for autonomous systems. Under the draft standard we are helping to write, every submitted system is placed in one of two categories, 'fixed-routine' or 'flexible,' and only systems in the second category may take high-consequence actions with no human in the loop. The rest require a supervisor who is expected to intervene. We handle eight to ten submissions a year, we have roughly forty engineer-hours and two weeks per submission, and we only ever get black-box access: we may choose inputs, perturb the environment, and record behavior, but the vendor will not give us weights, code, or training data. Every negative decision is appealable to the standards committee, so each one has to be written up in a form that survives an adversarial reading by the vendor's own experts.

Our difficulty is that the draft standard borrowed the vocabulary of the philosophical argument about sphexishness without any of its content. In practice my engineers use 'sphexish' to mean whatever they want it to mean on the day: sometimes 'it repeats itself,' sometimes 'it doesn't notice,' sometimes 'it isn't really an agent and there is no point asking it for reasons.' The textbook story of the digger wasp that returns to re-check its nest and can be made to repeat a step indefinitely is what everyone has in mind; I know the natural history may be embellished and I do not care about that. What I need is a reusable battery of behavioral tests, runnable by an engineer with no philosophy background, that sorts rigidity from flexibility in a way I can defend.

Three things I want you to build in rather than leave to taste. First, the distinction between flexibility located in the agent's control loop and flexibility located in the environment's response to a rigid agent: a routine with a sensory trigger re-set by the world can look adaptable while nothing internal has been recomputed. Second, the distinction between flexibility about means and revisability of ends: a system may reach a goal by many routes and still be unable to abandon the goal, or to notice that the point of the routine has already been secured by other means, or to re-describe what it is doing. Third, a failure mode one of my engineers has started calling compulsory plasticity — a system whose flexibility is itself a single stereotyped response to novelty, so that it reacts to every unfamiliar perturbation with the same generic exploration and calls it adaptation. I also want the battery designed on the assumption that vendors will eventually optimize against it: any test whose passing criterion is a fixed list of perturbations can be memorized, and then the battery itself becomes the trigger.

Please write this as the document I would hand to my team, in the following sections and approximate lengths.

1. Scope: what the battery can and cannot decide — about 150 words. Say plainly which question it answers and which it does not, so nobody cites it later on the question of agency, responsibility, or free will.

2. The tests, in order — about 700 words. For each test give its name, the perturbation you run, what counts as a pass, a fail, and an inconclusive, its cost in engineer-hours, and the specific way a clever vendor could defeat it. Order them so the cheap and disqualifying ones come first.

3. Scoring, stopping rules, and referral — about 250 words. Which single result forces the fixed-routine category regardless of everything else, what pattern of mixed results warrants continuing, what to do when a test cannot be run safely or at all, and which questions must go to the committee as value questions rather than being settled by data.

4. Three worked evaluations — about 700 words. Run the battery on: (a) a hospital delivery robot that retries a blocked corridor a fixed number of times before calling for help, and that completes a detour even after the obstacle has been removed; (b) a language-model-based scheduling assistant that can achieve an outcome by many different routes but cannot decline a request, cannot register that the task has already been done by someone else, and never re-describes what it is doing; (c) the human supervisors themselves, applying the same tests to their checklist-following in a monitoring task. The third one should be the case where I dislike the answer, and I want you to say why the answer is unwelcome and what it implies for a standard that makes the human the safeguard.

5. Where the battery misfires — about 300 words. Adversarial optimization against the test set, the limits imposed by black-box access, systems with no stable goal to probe, and the gap between anything the battery measures and the philosophical claims about freedom that our category names gesture at. Be explicit about which of my engineers' ordinary uses of 'sphexish' the battery does not even address.

Do not pad the document with references or a reading list; I want the apparatus and the reasoning, not the literature, and I need it usable by someone who has never read any of it.
