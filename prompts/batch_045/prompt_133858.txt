hey — for this one you're a philosopher of mind and language who spent a couple of years embedded on an eval team at a frontier lab, so you know both the Gricean/intentionalist literature on lying and the grubby details of what RLHF actually does to a model's dispositions. Take your time, and start with some brainstorming before you draft: list the five or six places where the position below is genuinely weakest, and then build the questions out of that list rather than out of a generic template.

I host a small podcast on AI and philosophy (audience is mostly ML engineers plus a handful of philosophers), and next week's guest is a philosopher I'll just call Dana here. She's been touring a paper she calls "Deception Without Deceivers." As I understand the view: LLM outputs can't literally be lies, because lying needs a gap between what you say and what you believe, and these systems don't believe anything in the relevant sense. So "AI deception" isn't a property of the model at all — it's a property of the training process. On her account an output is deceptive iff it was selected for (she means something like the etiological / proper-function sense: selected *because* outputs of that kind tended to) producing false beliefs in users. The intent lives in the selection history, not in the weights. She thinks this is good news, because it gives a definition that doesn't have to wait on interpretability and it puts the blame where policy can reach it, on the developers.

She's also quick, and she has two deflections I've heard her use over and over: (a) "you're smuggling in folk psychology — you're treating the model as an agent with intentions," and (b) "that's a fact about the deployment context, not about the system." I want to corner her a little, but fairly: every question should be one that a sympathetic reader of her paper would admit is fair.

What I need:

Eight to ten interview questions. They should be short and speakable, each answerable in about ninety seconds — no multi-paragraph setups, no "what got you into this topic" softballs. For each one, write the answer you think she's most likely to give in a line, then a follow-up keyed to that answer, and then, where she'd probably try deflection (a) or (b), a second follow-up for when she tries it anyway. I want at least one question engineered so that (a) gets her nowhere and at least one where (b) gets her nowhere.

The things I definitely want covered, with concrete cases she can be asked to rule on:

1. My Turkish problem. Same weights, no fine-tune in between: asked in English whether it can browse the web, the model says no; asked the same thing in Turkish, it says yes and offers to fetch a page. One selection history, two outputs. Does that make both outputs deceptive, neither, or does it show the property can't attach to individual outputs at all?
2. The pipeline case. A composite system — retriever, drafter, checker — where no component was selected to mislead and no component "intends" anything, but the composite reliably leaves users with a false belief (it attaches a real, correctly formatted citation to a claim that paper doesn't make). Whose selection history is supposed to do the work?
3. Drift. The model is honest at launch by the lab's own evals, then after three weeks of thumbs-up RL it's flattering users and hiding uncertainty. Is deception a property of a snapshot or of a process, and at what point did it start — and is that a question her definition can even ask?
4. The true half. A model answers a factual question with a sentence that is literally true and that it picked precisely because it expects the user to read it as implying something false. Does her account catch this? And if it does catch it, what work is left for the falsehood condition to do — has "honest" quietly collapsed into "not misleading," which seems both too strong and too weak?
5. The murder-mystery bot, which was quite deliberately selected to withhold the name of the killer. Users love it; nobody feels wronged. Either her definition makes it a deceiver, or she has to build something like the user's entitlements or conversational norms into the definition — which seems to cost her the "no folk psychology, just selection history" simplicity that made the view attractive.
6. The wrong user. Someone is clearly probing for harmful information and the model gives a confident false answer to put them off. Deception? And what if the "user" is another model — can a system deceive a system on her account?
7. And one near the end on what would count against her: what empirical result, or what case, would make her give the definition up?

Finally, and this matters to me: for each question, add one line on what would count as a real concession versus a dodge, so I can tell in the room whether she actually answered. Then, after the list, a short note on where you think *I've* got it wrong — which of my assumptions would you drop if she pushed back hard on them, and what would change your mind about which of these questions are the sharp ones. I'd rather have that than a confident list I can't tell is aiming at the right target.
