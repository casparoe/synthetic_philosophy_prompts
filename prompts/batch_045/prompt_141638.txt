Hey — I'm TAing a joint philosophy/economics undergrad course this term (18 students, one 75-minute slot, mixed group: half econ majors who are happy drawing indifference curves, half philosophy majors who are suspicious of the word "utility" but can't say why). Next week's session is on revealed preference and on what, if anything, the word "utility" is supposed to name. I really don't want to just stand up and explain the axiom and the representation result, because I've run this session before as a lecture and everyone nods and then writes essays that quietly treat utility as a feeling of satisfaction that choice is a symptom of. So: design me a runnable in-class activity — a game with rules and small real stakes — that makes them run into the problem themselves before I name it.

What I have in mind, roughly, and you should improve on it or replace it: they each face a sequence of four or five budget sets over three or four actual snack-sized goods (real goods, real consumption, chips or tickets as the medium, no money changing hands), I collect the choices, and then each pair gets a printed choice history — in round one their own, in round two an anonymous one I've cooked up to be weird — and is asked to write down a utility function that makes those choices come out as maximizing. I want them to *feel* the thing where rationalization is too easy: whatever the data is, you can always save the theory by attributing a preference over the act of choosing itself, or over the menu, or over "getting mango when mango is cheap" — I've started calling this menu-laundering, call it whatever you like, but I want them to notice they're doing it and to find it a bit fishy rather than triumphant. Then I want a moment where the theory actually bites: some history (or better, something that happens live in the room) that no utility function can fit at all, so the axiom turns out to be the only real content there is. And then a last beat where two students turn out to have produced exactly the same choice data for completely different reasons — one picking what they want, one following a rule they were secretly told to follow, one acting on a false belief about what the goods are — and we have to decide whether "utility" as the theory defines it can still do any work when we ask who is better off. Secret role instructions are fine; straight-up lying about what the activity is for is not, and I'd rather avoid anything that depends on my being able to predict which way the room will vote.

Give me: the materials list, the rules and payoff scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline, the exact choice histories to hand out, and a debrief that maps each moment in the game onto a specific part of the idea (representation vs. discovery, the axioms as content, menu-dependence, preference vs. rule vs. belief, and why the "as-if" reading is both a rescue and a collapse). Also: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — trading between students, people not caring about the snacks, allergy stuff, someone in the back who's read ahead and shouts the name of the axiom in week one of the module — and how I should handle each. And be honest about which part of the argument the game can't deliver and I'll just have to say out loud myself: I'd rather know now than discover it at minute 60 when half the room has checked out.
