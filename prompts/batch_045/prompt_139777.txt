I teach a second-year course called Minds and Machines — about 22 students, mostly philosophy and cognitive-science majors with a handful of comp-sci people, one 80-minute slot, and a room with a projector but no group whiteboards (I can print handouts, and I've got pads of paper and markers).

Next week we're doing the MU-puzzle bit in Hofstadter and the whole "jumping out of the system" idea (his phrase I think). I've run the MU puzzle itself as a class activity before and it always dies the same death: someone's older sibling told them the answer, or somebody googles it mid-session, and the "oh — you have to stop playing and start proving" moment evaporates. So I've built my own little formal system that I hope does the same job, and I want an activity built around it.

The system: a state is a pair of positive whole numbers, (a, b), starting at (1, 1). Three moves, and that's it:

- ADD-LEFT: (a, b) -> (a + b, b)
- ADD-RIGHT: (a, b) -> (a, a + b)
- DOUBLE: (a, b) -> (2a, 2b)

A target is just another pair. You've solved it if you can get from (1, 1) to it in some finite number of moves. Targets I was going to hand out, one per group of 3-4: (8,8), (7,7), (12,20), (6,6), (13,21), (9,15).

Two things before you design anything. First: I'm genuinely not sure which of those are reachable. I worked a couple of them out and then lost confidence, and I really dont want to be wrong in front of twenty people. So tell me which are solvable, give me a not-absurdly-long path for each solvable one, and for the impossible ones tell me what the actual proof is and how a second-year could find it in twenty minutes without me saying the word "invariant" first. Second: if my rule set doesn't do what I want — if everything is trivially reachable, or nothing interesting is unreachable, or the double move wrecks it — say so and fix the rules. I'd rather change the game then run a broken one.

What I want them to feel, not hear: that there's a difference between producing legal moves and knowing something about the whole space of legal moves; that no amount of staying inside the system settles the question; and that the move that does settle it is a change in what you're talking about (from pairs of numbers to properties of pairs of numbers). I'd like them to run into that themselves.

So: design me the session. Timeline for 80 minutes, materials, and roles — I was thinking of splitting each group into an "inside" player who may only write legal move sequences and isn't allowed to say or write anything about the game as a whole, and an "outside" player who can't touch the board but is the only one allowed to make claims about all reachable states. Tell me if that's a good idea or if it'll just annoy people. Also give me a scoring scheme — no real money — that puts pressure on the decision I actually care about: when to stop searching and start proving. And tell me whether to warn them up front that some targets are impossible, or let them find out.

No lecture at the start, and I don't want the name of the idea to drop until after they've hit it.

Then the debrief: map what happened at each stage onto the inside/outside distinction, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to recover, and be straight with me about which part of Hofstadter's idea the game can't deliver and I'll have to say out loud myself. My worry is that the thing that's unreachable here isn't even expressible in the system's own vocabulary, so calling any of this a mini-Gödel situation might be a stretch — am I right to be nervous, and what's the honest version?

Last thing: I've got 15 minutes at the end and the room has wifi. I'm tempted to have them open a chatbot, paste in the rules, and ask it to get to (7,7). Worth doing? What do you predict it'll actually do, and what do I say about what comes back?
