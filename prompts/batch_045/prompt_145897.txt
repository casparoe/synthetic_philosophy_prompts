I'm on this community care panel at a district hospital — we're the ones who get asked who counts as one of us once the decisions get uncomfortable. Our founding document says the usual thing about a person being a person through other persons, but nobody ever wrote down what that commits us to, so we've been deciding case by case, and I've started to suspect we're running two or three different ideas without noticing.

Here are our rulings so far, as short as I can make them:

- Baby girl, three days old, fatal diagnosis: full standing, no talk of potential persons, treat her as one of us.
- Woman in a persistent vegetative state for four years, family still comes and talks to her every week: full standing, we refused the request to move her to comfort-only care so a bed would free up.
- Man dead sixty years, still named and spoken to at family rituals: still part of the community; his stated wish about where he was buried beat the family's convenience.
- Foreign contractor here for six weeks, no local ties: owed real but thinner consideration, loses a scarce slot to a member.
- Company that's employed half the town for eighty years and gets talked about like an ancestor: not a person, no standing of its own.
- Local man who organised a massacre, unrepentant, refused every attempt at reconciliation: made himself less than a person, owed baseline humane treatment but not the same participatory consideration.

New one, and we vote Thursday. Mr N, 78, last fluent speaker of his language, everyone from his world dead, no family, in a care home in a city where nobody knows his people. He's alert, tells stories, but there's nobody who can recieve them; the staff are kind to him but it's all task-based. The proposal is to fund a "memory companion" for him — a system built out of recordings of his dead relatives and of his language that talks back to him in it, and he's started using a kinship term for it. Half of us think it puts back something real; half think it's a simulacrum that changes nothing about his standing, or is quietly insulting. It costs real money, and we said no to a music therapy programme for another resident last month on cost grounds.

What I actually want from you: the rules that fit all six of our earlier rulings (I can see at least two — one about really being in relations, one about being the kind of thing that can be — and they don't feel like the same rule), what each of them says we should do about this proposal, your best guess at what we'll end up saying if we care about being consistent with ourselves, which earlier ruling is the closest analog, and what we'd each be signing up for either way. Also: is the sixth ruling even consistent with the first two, or have we been quietly using two different notions of standing? And what single further case would actually tell the rules apart. I'd guess it's something about whether a relation has to be returned by something that could in principle return it, but you tell me.
