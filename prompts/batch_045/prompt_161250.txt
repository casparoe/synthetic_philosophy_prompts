we're putting together a position piece on overdiagnosis for our hospital's ethics committee, and the two people who've been arguing about it for a year and a bit — one is a value-free naturalist (disease is just statistically subnormal performance of a part-function, no value judgement anywhere in the concept) and one is a normativist (you need a failure of some bodily function *and* a judgement that it's bad for the person, and that second bit isn't optional) — have finally agreed to stop writing separate sections and do it as an adversarial collaboration. I'm the one editing it, so I need the document they'd actually both sign.

Give me that joint statement, drafted so each of them could put their name to it. I want:

1. the claims both can sign — about what the concept of disease is *for* (explaining? predicting? deciding what we owe someone?), about the easy core cases, and about which of their apparent disagreements turned out to be purely about words. If part of the fight is verbal, say which part and what the verbal bit was concealing.
2. the residual disagreements, each phrased in wording its own holder would accept, as a table.
3. the cruxes, and for each one a concrete case both agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance, plus what each would count as evidence *against* their own view.

The cruxes I want covered, plus any you think are actually bigger than these:

- Mismatch cases. Take a condition that is now statistically typical in this population but which, in the environment where the relevant system was calibrated — evolutionary or developmental, and I suspect they disagree about which, so make them say — would count as a clear malfunction. Candidates people throw around: myopia, metabolic syndrome, allergies. Is it a disease? I expect the naturalist to say it turns on which reference class you use and the normativist to say typicality is irrelevant because the disvalue is real. Say which of them has to give ground, and on what.
- The baseline problem: is the comparison class the population, or the person's own earlier trajectory? Nearly everything about an eighty-year-old is subnormal relative to a twenty-five-year-old, and that can't all be disease, so something else is doing the work. What is it, and which side pays for it?
- Incidental findings: a structural abnormality picked up on imaging that will never cause symptoms, disability, or shortened life. Both camps have a position here; the interesting question is whether they can agree on what would settle it.
- Looping: sometimes the label itself creates the disvalue. You get screened, you get a diagnosis, and now you're a patient, with insurance and employment consequences and a worse time. Can the value component arrive *after* the classification and still count? I think the naturalist has to say no and the normativist has to say yes, but I want to know whether either is genuinely forced.
- My own half-formed suspicion, which you should either use or knock down: that they aren't really disagreeing about disease at all but about whether the word has one job or three, and that once you split the entitlement question off from the explanation question each side gets most of what it wanted. Tell me if that's a cheap move.

Formating: numbered agreed-claims section first, then the disagreements table, then the cruxes with predictions. Start straight into the statement — first line is the one-sentence headline of what they agreed and where they split — then the rest. No preface, no restating what I asked for. Aim for something that'd fit in about two pages.
