I spent thirty-one years as a field ecologist, most of them running a long-term monitoring transect on a salt marsh, and since retiring I have been sitting in on a small philosophy reading group that meets at the local library. We recently worked through the 1998 Clark and Chalmers paper and the case of Otto, who keeps his appointments and directions in a notebook rather than in his head, and I came away both convinced by the parity argument and dissatisfied with the criteria on offer. What troubles me is that the standard glosses — that the external resource has to be reliably available, automatically endorsed, easily accessible, and consulted as a matter of course — seem to me to describe the good behaviour of a tool rather than the incorporation of a part. In my old discipline the question of what belongs to an organism was not settled by what a thing is made of or where it sits: a structure counts as part of the organism if the organism invests energy in building and maintaining it, if its condition is monitored and defended, and if its failure is registered and compensated for, as with a limb gone numb or a clutch of eggs abandoned when the cost of keeping them exceeds what the parent can sustain. So I have been toying with a different criterion for when an external object is genuinely constitutive of a cognitive process rather than merely causally upstream of one: first, the agent must actively maintain the object in a usable condition — recopying, reorganising, charging, backing up, repairing — at some cost, and the coupled performance of agent plus object must degrade when that maintenance is withdrawn, in something like the way performance degrades under sleep loss; and second, the agent must carry a working forward model of the object's dynamics, such that perturbing the object — moving the notebook, rearranging the kitchen drawer, changing the location of a menu item on a phone, deleting a file — elicits a measurable prediction-error signature, an orienting response or a moment of surprise, rather than merely an inconvenience. I find the second prong especially attractive because it is operational: I can imagine running the experiment, and I can imagine it coming out either way.

What I would like from you is an honest map of the exposure of this position, not the standard objections to the extended mind thesis generally. I have read the survey literature, so please do not spend your answer on the coupling-constitution fallacy, on the worry that the thesis lets in libraries and filing cabinets and collapses into promiscuity, or on the point that Otto's notebook is not much like Inga's biological memory in its implementation; I stipulate all of that. I want to know what would specifically defeat the maintenance-and-forward-model version I have sketched. Which results, cases, or arguments should I treat as fatal to it, and which as merely awkward? In particular: does the forward-model prong do any independent work, or does it quietly reduce to the trust-and-reliability conditions it was meant to replace, in which case I have merely relabelled the orthodoxy; does the maintenance prong predict verdicts that diverge from the standard criteria, and are there cases where it returns the intuitively right answer for what I take to be the wrong reason; what does it say about the hard cases I keep returning to — the ornithologist friend who has identified birds by ear for fifty years and says the recording in his pocket is part of how he hears, my own 1979 field notebooks, whose page-layout I can still recall while the contents have gone, a shared laboratory notebook maintained by nobody in particular, the photograph archive that a widow consults every evening, and an implanted stimulator that requires no maintenance at all from its user? What empirical findings, in the neuroscience of tool use, in human-computer interaction, in animal cognition or developmental psychology, would discriminate this view from its rivals, and which of them are we ever likely to get? And which of these defeaters would a sympathetic defender of the extended mind thesis concede as counting against the view, as opposed to those only a critic would press?

The thing I would personally find most persuasive is a methodological one, and I would like you to tell me whether it is a fair standard or merely a prejudice imported from my old field. I was trained to distrust any distinction that cannot be cashed out in two independent measurements: if the maintenance criterion and the forward-model criterion were to come apart on a real case — if, say, an object were clearly maintained at cost but generated no prediction-error response when perturbed, or were smoothly modelled but never maintained — and if there were in principle no observation that would settle which of the two tracks constitution, I would take that as grounds for giving up the whole project and concluding that the boundary between cognition and its scaffolding is drawn by our explanatory interests rather than discovered. Is that the right way to think about a dispute like this, or does it beg the question against the kind of claim the extended mind thesis is making? Relatedly, my grandson, who is in a cognitive science programme, assures me the debate has largely stalled into competing intuitions that neither side can move, and that the interesting work has shifted to predictive-processing frameworks in which the skull is not a boundary worth defending anyway. If he is right, I would like to know whether my version has been insulated by its own vagueness into unfalsifiability, and whether, for a claim of this sort, that is a defect or simply the normal condition of a metaphysical thesis.
