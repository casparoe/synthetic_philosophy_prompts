Last night I had a long argument with a friend about counterpart theory and transworld identity, and I came away unable to refute her and unable to reconstruct her replies. I'd like you to argue for her position: not to survey the debate, but to build the strongest case you can for her version of counterpart theory, including answers to the objections I raised and to the standard ones.

Common ground as we set it up. The counterpart theorist holds that ordinary things exist in only one world, and that a de re modal claim about an object is made true by there being, in some other world, things sufficiently like that object in the respects that matter. The transworld-identity theorist holds that the very object can exist in more than one world, and that identity across worlds is not to be analysed in terms of qualitative resemblance at all — on the strongest version, it is secured by a primitive non-qualitative thisness belonging to each object. I was defending the second view; she was defending the first. We agreed that the three stock complaints against counterpart theory do not settle the matter on their own: that on that account the modal claim about the losing candidate is not really about him but about someone else; that a counterpart relation which need not be transitive invalidates the principle that if possibly possibly p then possibly p; and that sufficient similarity in the relevant respects is hopelessly interest-relative. She did not deny any of them. She tried to use them as premises.

Her thesis, which she called two-track contextualism. De re modal ascriptions have no truth conditions fixed by a single context-invariant relation. There are at least two relations in play. On the resemblance track, an object's counterpart in a world is whatever there maximally resembles it in the respects the context makes salient; on the origin track, an object's counterpart in a world is whatever stands at the far end of the causal-historical chain that actually produced it, and where nothing does, the object has no counterpart in that world and de re claims about it there are false. Each track tolerates an object's having several counterparts in a single world, or none. Context selects a track; where context fails to select, the modal claim has no determinate truth value, which she took to be the correct verdict rather than a cost, because the underlying situation is itself indeterminate. Her clinching argument was that transworld identity cannot say what needs saying here: since identity is one-one, it can neither represent a single object as having two candidates in one world nor express the indeterminacy of which of them it would have been, without either contradiction or smuggling in the very counterpart apparatus it was meant to avoid.

The case that did the damage. Call it graduated origin. A cello is made by a given maker in a given year from one maple tree. Consider four worlds: same tree, different branch; a genetically identical tree grown from a cutting of the original, so the wood is qualitatively the same but there is no material overlap; the same wood, used a year earlier; and the same wood used to build an instrument of a different kind. My friend's claim was that speakers' willingness to say this very cello could have been the instrument in that world is high in the first case, unstable in the second and third, and low in the fourth, and that the instability splits along two dimensions: how similar the candidate is, and how much of the object's actual history it shares. Ask whether this cello could have had different wood and you get one pattern of verdicts; ask whether this cello could have been that other instrument and you get another. She said a view with a single one-one transworld relation must return a determinate yes or no for each world while having nothing to say about why the verdicts are graded and question-sensitive, whereas her two tracks predict exactly that split.

My objection, which she answered too quickly for me to follow. If each utterance selects one track, consider a sentence that embeds one modal operator inside another while apparently shifting tracks: this cello could have been made of a different plank, and then it could have been finished slightly differently. The first clause wants the resemblance track, since on the origin track it is straightforwardly false; the second operator then applies to a counterpart selected by the first. Her reply, as I half-remember it, was that the tracks are never composed: the inner operator inherits whichever relation governs the outer one, so the sentence has one reading on which everything downstream is resemblance-governed and another on which the outer clause is simply false, and never a hybrid; the appearance of mixing is produced by unmarked shifts at clause boundaries in flat discourse. I could not tell whether that was a genuine answer or a stipulation dressed as one, and I want you to make it precise and defend it, or to give a better answer in the same spirit.

What I am asking for, in about nine hundred words. Argue for her thesis against mine. Take on the three stock complaints in her own terms, especially the failure of transitivity under iterated modality. Reconstruct and defend the reply to my embedding objection. Say plainly what the view has to pay: what it gives up relative to transworld identity plus primitive thisness, how track selection is supposed to work without circularity, and whether the view can be stated if worlds are treated as abstract representations rather than as concrete pluralities, or whether it needs the heavier metaphysical package it is usually presented with.

Two constraints on how you do it. Do not cite or quote anything; I want the argument, not the apparatus, and I have no way to check a reference. And as you go, mark which moves are internal stipulations of the framework, which are claims nearly everyone working on these questions would grant, and which are live contested claims a reasonable specialist could reject. Do not present the contested ones as settled. If the case for her position rests on a premise that is genuinely up for grabs, say so and say where the argument breaks if that premise fails. Also tell me where my statement of the case is doing work the argument should not have to lean on.
