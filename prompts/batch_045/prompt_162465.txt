I am putting together the discussion notes for a graduate seminar session on recent work in epistemology, and I was planning to build the session around a survey article on closure and skepticism that a colleague recommended. Before I commit to it I would like to know how much of it I can actually trust. Its central paragraph reads as follows:

"The bootstrapping problem is just the problem of easy knowledge under another name. Cohen's version goes like this: you look at the table and thereby come to know that it is red. You also know, without looking, that if the table is red then it is not a white table lit by a red bulb. So, by closure of knowledge under known entailment, you deduce that it is not a white table lit by a red bulb — a fact about the lighting that no amount of staring at the table could have told you, since a white table under a red bulb looks exactly like a red one. Vogel's version is the same argument in a different costume: a driver glances at her fuel gauge, sees that it reads full, and concludes that the tank is full. The inference is circular, since the conclusion is nothing more than the very thing her premise rested on. That is why process reliabilism has no answer to it: reliabilism says that a belief counts as knowledge when it is produced by a reliable process, but the only way to establish that a process is reliable is to consult the process itself, which is circular all over again. The upshot is that Vogel has refuted Moore's two-hands argument: to know anything about the external world you would first have to establish that your senses are reliable, and the only way to do that is to rely on them. The standard repair is to give up closure, which is what Nozick's tracking account and Dretske's relevant-alternatives account were designed to do."
