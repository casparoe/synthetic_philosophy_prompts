I host a small interview podcast out of the LessWrong-adjacent corner of the blog world; my listeners are mostly people who read the Sequences years ago and now argue with each other in forum threads. Next episode's guest is an early modern specialist who defends a contemporary reconstruction of Berkeley's so-called master argument: the claim that the materialist's hypothesis is not merely false but cannot be conceived, because any attempt to think of the tree as existing unconceived just is an act of conceiving it.

I don't want an episode that walks through the slogan about being and being perceived and then arrives at God as the caretaker of unobserved furniture. Draft nine or ten questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give, that do the following: force her to say whether the argument's premise concerns conceiving or sensory imagination, and whether that collapses the argument into Berkeley's campaign against abstract ideas; make her say whether it stands on its own or is quietly riding on the principle that an idea can be like nothing but an idea; press the ambiguity between unconceived as perceived by no one at all and unconceived as not perceived by me; raise what the threads I follow call the parity problem, that the same structure seems to rule out conceiving of other minds or of my own existence through a night when no one was awake, so that she must either generalize into something close to solipsism or concede that this is not the topic-neutral argument from conceivability it advertises itself as, with the standard escape hatch about having a notion rather than an idea of spirits looking like a patch; and ask what licenses the step from I cannot conceive it to it is impossible, and whether the argument does any work at all on someone who has not already granted the theory of ideas.

Don't hedge across options: commit to an ordering of the questions by how much trouble each will actually cause her, say which answer you expect and which follow-up you would bet on, and name the single question you would cut if we run short, with your reason.
