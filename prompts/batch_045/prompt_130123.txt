I teach an upper-division seminar on the philosophy of punishment, and I would like a diagnostic tree to hand out rather than a reading: students should arrive at the name of a position by answering eight questions, none of which may use the vocabulary of the debate. The spine of the handout is the disagreement between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso over whether anyone ever morally deserves to suffer for what they have done, but I do not want a map of their exchange. I want a map of the logical space, with their two views located inside it alongside deterrence-only, communicative, expressivist, desert-capped, and abolitionist positions.

The entry commitment every student is asked to grant at the start is thin: the state may do something burdensome to at least some people who have committed serious offenses. Anyone who refuses that goes to an abolitionist leaf immediately.

Here are the questions I have in mind, roughly in the order I would ask them. Rewrite each as a concrete case wherever you can, and feel free to replace any of them with a better case that separates the same positions.

1. The replacement test. Suppose the same future goods — fewer future offenses, public reassurance, whatever you think the goods are — could be produced by an intervention nobody experiences as punitive: a quietly administered course of treatment, a monitoring requirement, a change to the environment. If the punitive-looking part could be subtracted without losing any of the benefit, is anything left that the state owes the offender, or should be withheld from him?

2. The secret, safe, undeterrable offender. An offense is known only to the offender and to the authorities; no one else will ever learn of it, he is not dangerous, and no general deterrent effect is available. Is there still a reason to impose something burdensome?

3. Explanatory luck. The conduct was produced by upbringing, neurology, and circumstance in the way a storm's path is produced by pressure and temperature. Does that remove the reason to punish, leave it untouched, or change what kind of reason it is?

4. The authorship question. Must the offender's control extend to how his own character was formed for him to be a fitting target of hard treatment, or is it enough that he acted from his own competent, uncoerced deliberation at the time?

5. Uptake. The offender cannot possibly recognize the sanction as a response to what he did — advanced dementia, or a framework in which the violated norm is unintelligible to him. Is the sanction then merely training or containment, or does it keep whatever it was doing before?

6. Desert accounting. Can desert be stored, overdrawn, expire, or run out? Two cases: a man convicted at ninety for a killing committed at twenty; and a woman who serves eleven years for an offense later shown to be someone else's. Does the second carry any credit against what may be imposed for anything else, or against what the state owes her?

7. State complicity. Housing, licensing, and policing policies that the state itself set caused a measurable share of the offense rate. Does a causal contribution by the punishing authority reduce what it may impose, reduce it proportionally, or make no difference?

8. The harmless recidivist. An offender is statistically certain to reoffend and certain never to endanger anyone — prolific regulatory or benefit fraud, say. Does certainty of repetition without risk to persons change what may be imposed, and in which direction?

A ninth candidate I am unsure about: whether it matters that the burdens fall on the guilty rather than on someone else, when the consequences are identical — the scapegoat and substitution cases. Tell me in the last section whether this earns a node or is already implied by an earlier one.

Constraints. No question may name a position, a theorist, or a slogan, and none may be answerable only by someone who has read the literature; each must be decidable from the case by a student who has never heard the words retributivism or consequentialism. Build the tree from the answer patterns the questions permit, then place the named views in the resulting space, rather than starting from a list of views. Leaves should include, at minimum: compatibilist just deserts of roughly Dennett's sort; the free-will-skeptical public-health or quarantine model of roughly Caruso's sort; deterrence-only; expressivist or denunciatory; communicative or penitential; desert-capped consequentialism, where desert sets a ceiling or floor on what forward-looking reasons may impose; and at least one abolitionist leaf. Add unnamed leaves wherever the space has room for a view nobody defends, and say so plainly. Assume no internet access and no library: do not cite, quote, or attribute anything, and do not refer to works by title or date; the handout must stand on its own.

Please return the following sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths:

1. Branching questions — the eight questions in presentation order, each in final handout-ready wording, with its two or three answer options and one line on which positions it separates. About 600 words.
2. The tree — the instrument itself, as an indented or ASCII flow from the entry commitment to every leaf, with each branch labeled by the answer that takes it. About 350 words.
3. Leaf glossary — one entry per leaf: name or unnamed, one sentence of core claim, the answer pattern that lands there, and one awkward commitment the holder has to swallow. About 600 words.
4. Cut points — which three nodes at most actually decide the Dennett–Caruso dispute, and why the remaining questions do not separate them. About 300 words.
5. Empty regions — the coherent-looking answer patterns no leaf occupies, with a verdict for each on whether the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. About 300 words.
6. Indistinguishable leaves — pairs of leaves my questions cannot pull apart, plus the further case that could pry them apart, and your verdict on the ninth candidate question. About 250 words.
