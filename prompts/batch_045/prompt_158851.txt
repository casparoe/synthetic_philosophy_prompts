I am one of three standing members of a regional Substitute Judgment Panel. We are an advisory body, not a court: we meet weekly, we see about sixty cases a year, and we produce a one-page advisory that the attending physician must either follow or explain departing from in the notes. The decisions are real ones — whether to treat or discharge someone over a present objection, whether to feed, whether to restrain — and the advisory has to be defensible afterward to a family and to an ombudsman, which in practice means that each step of our reasoning has to leave behind something recordable, a sentence we can put in the file.

Our governing standard is an idealized-preference standard: the thing to be respected is not what the person currently wants but what they would want under better conditions — better informed, less distorted by fear or pain or depression, more internally coherent among the values they already hold. (This is the sort of standard that gets discussed under the heading of coherent extrapolated volition, though our statute does not use that phrase.) I want a procedure, not an essay: a decision procedure my panel can run on a real case, in the order the steps should be run, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, an explicit stop rule, and — this matters more than anything else — explicit marking of the points at which the procedure stops and hands the question to a human judgment that I can then be held accountable for.

The procedure has to survive five ways we have already managed to break it:

**Order of idealization.** Correcting a patient's beliefs routinely destroys the very desire we set out to respect: a man refuses surgery partly because he falsely believes he will be a permanent burden, and once we correct the belief the refusal evaporates — which is fine, except that the same operation, applied to desires rather than beliefs, just installs the panel's own preferences under the name of the patient's. Which dimension gets idealized first — belief, desire, or the person's sense of who she is — decides the outcome, and I need that order fixed by the procedure rather than chosen case by case.

**Dossier gerrymandering.** Because the panel assembles the record of a person's earlier values, we can make the extrapolation come out almost any way by choosing which of their past statements count as really theirs — a letter written at sixty against a remark made last week. I need a rule that fixes the admissible data before the extrapolation step, and a rule for what to do when the dossier is thin or supplied entirely by an interested party.

**Convergence failure.** Sometimes a small change in how much idealization we apply flips the recommendation. Averaging across idealizations is not something we could ever defend to a family, so I need to know what the procedure does instead, and whether the answer differs when the instability comes from thin evidence rather than from the idealization itself.

**The identity cutoff.** Our idealizations are not neutral. They tend to strip out commitments that look to us like defects of information or nerve — a religious conviction, an ascetic discipline, a political loyalty — and to leave in the ones we already approve of. At some point the more idealized person is no longer recognizably the same agent, and deferring to her is no longer deferring to the patient. Where in the procedure is that tested, and who decides?

**Repudiation.** Sometimes the idealized person would reject the apparatus entirely: fully informed and fully coherent, she would say that what matters to her is that she decides now, not that we get it right. Does the procedure defer to that? Note that deferring there can look like the procedure dissolving at exactly the moment it was about to be useful.

Apply the procedure to three cases.

1. A seventy-eight-year-old man with mild fluctuating vascular dementia and untreated depression refuses a hip replacement that would end intractable pain; he says he has had his innings. A decade ago he wrote to his brother that he would never want to be kept going out of stubbornness. He also has a sixty-year self-conception as a self-reliant stoic, and uses it to describe this refusal. Clinicians say the depression is treatable and the refusal would probably dissolve with treatment.

2. A twenty-nine-year-old woman with severe anorexia refuses nasogastric feeding. She is informed, consistent over months, and describes the discipline as her central achievement rather than as a symptom; she says she values thinness above continued life. I am personally inclined to defer to her, and I doubt a forty-five-minute procedure should be in the business of coercing someone over a competent, stable, informed refusal. If your procedure overrides her anyway, I want to see it do so, and I want to see which step does the work rather than which of my sympathies.

3. A fifty-four-year-old man refuses a transfusion that would almost certainly save his life, on the basis of a religious prohibition he has held, stably and with full information, since adolescence. There is no cognitive impairment. The dossier comes almost entirely from representatives of his community; his estranged son, who would inherit, disputes it and says his father was privately ambivalent.

Close by telling me what the procedure cannot do: whether it can say anything about whether an idealized-preference standard is the right standard for us to be using at all, whether it can settle who counts as the patient, and which of its steps are really judgment calls that we have agreed to record as findings. About 1,000 to 1,400 words, procedure first, no survey of the literature.
