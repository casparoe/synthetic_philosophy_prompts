Quick one that started as a work thing and turned into a philosophy thing. I'm building a small tool that puts a number on the chance a given climber gets sick enough on a particular route to need a helicopter out of there. You hit the reference-class thing immediately: she's a 41-year-old woman, first time above 6000m, on this specific route, following one particular acclimatization schedule, mild iron definciency, and so on. Every class she belongs to gives a different rate.

My fix is to start broad — everyone who attempted that mountain — and then keep adding the next most specific attribute I have records for, stopping as soon as the class still has at least 30 people in it. Whatever rate that class has is my answer, and because I stop by a rule I get exactly one number out, so the problem is solved. My mate reckons that's more or less the standard narrowest-usable-class rule, and that the whole headache is only a problem for frequentists anyway — I'm a Bayesian, I've got a prior and I update on her features, so none of this touches me.

Are we right? If we're not, what exactly are we still getting wrong, and what should the tool actually do? Keep it under 250 words, and tell me what would change your mind plus where your own reasoning is most likely to break.
