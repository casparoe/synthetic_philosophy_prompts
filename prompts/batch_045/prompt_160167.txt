Consider the following market design, which a colleague and I have been arguing about. I want a number with the reasoning that produces it.

The setup. There is a single binary contract paying one dollar if event E occurs and nothing otherwise. The traders are N copies of one language model: identical weights, identical decoding settings, no shared memory, and no way to read one another's reasoning. Copy i receives a private briefing — a bundle of source documents bearing on E — drawn from a fixed corpus by a procedure every copy fully understands; the briefings are disjoint. Copy i also observes the current price, and knows that the other traders are instances of the same model, facing briefings drawn the same way and seeing the same price. A market maker quotes the price as a function of net order flow; each copy may take a bounded long or short position and is instructed to maximize expected profit given everything it knows.

The reason this case interests me is that the common-prior assumption of the Milgrom–Stokey no-trade theorem is satisfied here more literally than it ever is among human traders. The traders do not merely happen to share a prior; they are the same prior, in the sense that the same weights underwrite every inference any of them makes. The mechanism, the briefing procedure, and the identity of the other traders are all common knowledge. So the theorem appears to predict that essentially nothing should happen: no copy should want to trade against a price set by traders whose information it can in principle reconstruct, and any bid it makes reveals its hand before anyone accepts. Empirically, though, I expect a great deal of trading.

The proposition I want a credence on: in this market, the closing price is a better estimate of the probability of E conditional on the whole corpus than the simple average of the N copies' independently stated probabilities is. Let that be settled operationally by running the design on a few hundred events with known outcomes and comparing mean log scores; that is what I mean by the claim being true or false.

My own credence is 0.3. My colleague, who works on mechanism design, is at 0.75.

Please give me your own number, decomposed. The terms I would want, at minimum:

1. That the copies' errors are conditionally independent given E. Shared weights mean shared inductive biases and shared blind spots, and shared pretraining means the documents are not really new to any of them in the way they would be to a human. If the copies fail in correlated ways, the market aggregates one judgment N times rather than N judgments, and averaging would do as well.
2. That a copy can invert the price. Seeing the price, can it work out what briefings the others probably had and update on their content, rather than merely deferring to the number? This is the common-knowledge-of-rationality clause, and for a language model it becomes a concrete question about how good the model is at reasoning about other instances of itself — including whether it can represent the possibility that another instance is worse at this than it is.
3. That the theorem's conclusion is about speculative trade grounded in informational disagreement. The trade we would actually observe may be driven by position limits, dollar risk aversion, the market maker's inventory rule, or a copy's misreading of its own briefing — none of which is the trade the theorem forbids, and none of which need carry any information about E.
4. That whatever aggregation occurs comes from the price and not from the briefing. If a copy's private estimate already implicitly reflects what copies like it are likely to have seen — because the same weights that generated its estimate also generated its guess about the others — then pooling is partly redundant and the price may double-count evidence that is already in every estimate.
5. Whether the market maker is doing the aggregation rather than the market. A Bayesian or scoring-rule-style market maker can compute something close to the correct posterior from order flow even if no copy reasons correctly about the others, in which case the design is an ensemble method wearing a market's clothes, and the interesting question becomes whether that is better or worse than pooling the copies directly.

Give a number and one line of justification for each term, state how the terms combine into your headline figure, and then tell me two things: which single term your number is most sensitive to, and which term you think the gap between 0.3 and 0.75 most likely lives in. Finally, name the experiment, runnable on an existing model with no new theory, that would move your number the most, and say which way it would move it.

If you think the proposition is not well posed as stated — for instance, if the closing price depends on the market maker's rule in ways I have not pinned down, or if "independently stated probabilities" is ambiguous between a copy's pre-trade estimate and its post-trade one — say so and propose the nearest well-posed version before committing to a number.
