hey — treat this as a consulting question. You're someone who works in animal ethics / philosophy of biology but who's also spent real time on farms and reads the industry trade press, so please don't just give me the seminar version: brainstorm a bit before you write, then give me something structured, and flag where you're guessing vs. where you think the answer is forced.

Some context. I do data work for a certification body and I'm building a scoring tool for farmed fish and shrimp, and the thing I keep running into is that I cant tell what theory the existing rules actually come from. Here's the pattern as I see it. For salmon and trout, everything I have to audit against is inputs and physical condition: dissolved oxygen, stocking density in kilos per cubic metre, temperature, feeding regimes, plus a set of "operational welfare indicators" scored on a sample of fish at the kill — fin erosion, eye damage, gill condition, skin lesions, sometimes cortisol or just how the fish is swimming. Nothing in the standard is phrased as a claim about what the fish is experiencing. Where I am there's now a requirement to stun before killing (though plenty of places still go straight to ice slurry or CO2-charged water), but crowding the fish and pumping them through pipes for the hour beforehand is more or less unregulated, and if a pen loses a big chunk of its fish to disease or to a jellyfish bloom, that gets written up as a production loss rather than a welfare problem — even when the deaths were slow. For shrimp it's much thinner: broodstock females in hatcheries routinely have an eyestalk removed to bring them into spawning condition, the animals are generally killed by chilling, there's no agreed stunning method anyone uses at scale, and in most places they aren't covered by animal protection law at all, though a couple of jurisdictions have started extending it to decapods.

So: what view of welfare does this whole pattern presuppose? I don't want "they're speciesist" — I want the actual reconstruction. Is the only thing that makes sense of it a functional or health-based account (an animal fares well when its systems are working and it isn't damaged), with the hedonic language as a pious extra? Or is it equally consistent with a felt-experience view plus a lot of measurement caution — i.e., they'd say density and fin erosion are proxies for suffering and they'd accept my indicators as second-best? Is it even one theory, or are the slaughter rules (which really do seem to be about a bad experience at a particular moment) and the on-farm rules (which seem to be about functioning) coming from different places? For each part of the practice, tell me what it strictly presupposes, what it merely fits well with, and where it's philosophically underdetermined so that several theories would license it. And tell me what would concretely have to change in what I audit — and in how I'd have to write the standard — if we swapped the implicit theory for its main rival.

Two things I especially want you to take on. First, the thing that started this: the pilot version of our tool scores farms on something like "welfare-hours per kilo of product." Every hour of an animal's life gets an intensity rating (0 fine, 1 mild, 2 moderate, 3 severe), we sum the weighted hours over the whole life, add across the crop, and divide by tonnes harvested. It turns out this ranks some low-density, slow-growing, long-lived salmon systems worse than intensive short-cycle ones, and it makes a disease outbreak that kills a lot of fish quickly look better than a long drawn-out one that kills fewer. My colleague says that's just what the arithmetic does and we should publish it. I think it shows our metric is smuggling in a theory nobody signed up to. Which one — a specific claim about how intensity trades off against duration, or about whether death is itself a harm to the animal, or about whether we're aggregating over individuals or over lives, or something about animals that only exist because we bred them? Second, the objection I get from producers that I can't answer properly: fish don't have the brain structures mammals use for pain, so all of this is sentiment. I don't mean the empirical reply. I mean: what does the practice presuppose about the epistemology here? My standard treats fin erosion and cortisol as evidence of bad welfare but would presumably reject any direct claim about what a fish feels as unverifiable. What's the implicit rule for when a physiological or behavioural indicator is allowed to stand in for an invisible mental state, is that rule coherent, and is it one anyone would defend outside this context? Be honest if you think my colleague is right and I'm the one with the hidden commitment.
