I teach an upper-level undergraduate course in philosophy of medicine. I have one 75-minute session, 18–24 students, and I want them to feel the force of the dispute between naturalist and normativist accounts of health and disease from the inside, before anyone names the two families of view.

Design a committee simulation in which the class acts as a body deciding what to put in a revised disease classification. Split them into panels of three or four; each panel receives the same dossier of four candidate conditions and a private role card stating an institutional stake (insurer, patient-advocacy group, research funder, occupational-health regulator, disability-rights organisation, textbook editor). Roles are secret and must not be revealed during deliberation.

Build the dossier so that the naturalist and normativist pulls come apart, using cases I have not been able to find in the literature: (1) a trait present in roughly 70 percent of the population that lowers a measurable capacity relative to the individual's own earlier baseline while remaining statistically typical; (2) a variant that is statistically atypical now and impairs function relative to present peers, but was well suited to the environment most of the population lived in two generations ago and remains so in a sizable minority; (3) a state that causes no distress, no reduced function and no raised mortality by itself, but sharply raises the risk of a serious outcome only in combination with a common and easily avoided behaviour; (4) a condition that degrades one statistically normal capacity while enhancing another, so that net functional contribution is genuinely unclear.

Each panel files a two-part ballot for every case: first a descriptive judgment about whether there is a departure from normal functioning, then a classificatory judgment about whether it belongs in the manual. Panels also wager points on how the other panels will vote. Give me materials, a scoring scheme with no real money, and a timeline.

The debrief should map the divergence in ballots onto the two accounts, including the divergence in the supposedly descriptive answers and what that shows about reference classes and levels of functional description. Tell me what will predictably go wrong on a first run and how to handle it, and be honest about which feature of the dispute the game cannot stage and the facilitator has to argue for explicitly.
