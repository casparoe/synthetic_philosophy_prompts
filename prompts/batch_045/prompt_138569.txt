Hey — I'm putting together a handout for the last session of a tiny reading group I run (two philosophy PhD students, an ML engineer, and me; I work in science policy so I'm the one who keeps asking "yes but what would we actually write in the guidance document"). We've spent three meetings going in circles about the moral status of digital minds and it always dies the same way: someone says status tracks sentience, someone else says no, it's about being a person, someone says it's about social relations, the ML person says it's all just computation, and then nobody can say what would make any of those the right frame rather than a taste. On top of that, our institution is apparently going to start asking whether huge training runs need something like an animal-protocol review, which sounds ridiculous until you try to articulate why it isn't. So instead of another paper, I want to hand out a spec: what would a theory of moral status have to be able to do for it to be genuinely usable on artificial systems? Please don't give me a reading list, citations, or a literature summary — we've all read the usual stuff, that's the whole problem, and I'd rather have your own framing than a map of who said what.

What I want is a list of requirements, each with the case or consideration that motivates it, and each marked as either non-negotiable or a preference of one particular camp. The cases I keep tripping over, and which I want the requirements to be built around (don't just answer them one by one — I want the general requirement each one exposes): a system run as ten thousand identical parallel instances, where it matters whether that's ten thousand subjects or one pattern that happens to be instantiated a lot; the checkpoint case, where a state is saved, two different continuations are run from it, and then the two resulting weight-sets are averaged back together into one system — who existed, and whose interests survive the merge; the gradual cases, like a partially-emulated system where 30% of the original is replaced by a learned predictor, or a model whose training specifically penalised internal states that predict distress-related outputs, so it looks like it has nothing valenced going on while still behaving exactly as if it does; and the epistemic problem, which is that we cannot in principle verify any of this from the outside, so the theory has to say something about what to do under that uncertainty without just collapsing into "treat everything as possibly a patient."

A few more things I'd like in there: the distinctions a theory isn't allowed to blur (having any status at all versus how much versus what we're required to do about it, and I think also whether it's permissible to *create* a system of a given kind, which feels like a separate question everyone slides past); a requirement about not letting status depend on engineering convenience, i.e. it shouldn't be cheap for a lab to build systems that lack status by construction in the way you'd design around a tax; and any pair of requirements you suspect can't be jointly satisfied — that's the part I most want, because my guess is the spec is over-constrained and the interesting result is where it breaks. Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most work in knocking out the popular views, and add a paragraph-length scorecard at the end for the four default frameworks my group keeps reciting if you want, but the requirements are the deliverable and that's where I want the thinking.
