I'm writing up my position on persistence for a graduate seminar and would like an audit of whether it hangs together before I commit to it in print. I hold the following.

(1) Endurance: a persisting thing is wholly present at each time at which it exists and has no temporal parts. My reason is not ordinary language; it is that endurance is what lets the very thing that has a property at one time be the thing that lacks it at another — the subject of change is the changer.

(2) Eternalism together with the relativistic denial of absolute simultaneity: past, present and future times are all equally real, there is no privileged foliation, and so there are indefinitely many overlapping maximal spacelike hypersurfaces. I take the enduring thing to be wholly present at each of them.

(3) In the standard fission case — each cerebral hemisphere transplanted into a distinct body, both successors psychologically continuous with the pre-fission person — the pre-fission person survives as both successors. I mean this literally, not merely as a claim about what prudentially matters: I deny that there is some further identity fact settling which of them, or neither, is the original. But I also think the rival verdict, that there were always two persons sharing the pre-fission stages, is unacceptable.

(4) The principle I use to reject that rival: where two accounts differ only in how many continuants they posit at a given time, and the additional candidates are indiscernible at that time by any test that does not already presuppose one of the accounts, I count the extras as a theoretical cost. This is also why I reject unrestricted composition.

(5) Identity is never indeterminate; I accept the familiar Leibniz's-law argument to that conclusion. But in a case I call the Fade — fission brought about continuously over eleven months, so that the two streams diverge by degrees and there is no crossover moment — I want to say that around the middle there is no determinate fact of the matter how many continuants there are, and I want that to be indeterminacy in the world rather than merely indecision about our words.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look as though they are, ordered by severity; for each real tension, say what would have to be given up and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the set. Please discriminate rather than flagging all five as equally shaky — I don't know where the fault is. If the cheapest fix turns out to be abandoning endurance, spell out what the temporal-parts alternative would actually deliver on (3) and whether it really delivers it; I suspect it does not, but I cannot say why. If instead there is a single revision that cleans up the most, name it.
