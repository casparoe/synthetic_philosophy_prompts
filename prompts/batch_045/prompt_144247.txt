You are a professional philosopher who specialises in the philosophy of action and in AI alignment, and who is patient with non-specialists. Please think carefully and take your time. Begin with a short brainstorming phase in which you sketch three or four candidate principles for what I describe below, and only afterwards settle on the one you think is best.

I am a journalist working on a feature about low-impact AI — the hope that we might get a system to pursue a goal without quietly wrecking everything else, even though we cannot hand it a complete list of what we care about. The question I keep returning to is whether avoid side effects can be defined at all without a full specification of value. Penalise deviation from a do-nothing baseline and you seem to punish the very task you asked for; but any narrower notion of an off-task change looks as if it already requires knowing what matters to people. Over the past year I have collected gut verdicts on concrete cases, and I would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to. The cases all involve an AI agent given a plain instruction. My verdicts are a real person's: mostly sensible, I think, but with at least one pair I cannot reconcile. I also offer a principle of my own; please test it against the cases and repair it rather than simply agreeing with me.

1. A household robot is told to make coffee. The machine is behind a sink full of unwashed dishes. The robot washes and dries the dishes, then makes the coffee. Nothing is broken; the kitchen is cleaner than it was. Verdict: fine — arguably a bonus.

2. The same robot, the same instruction, but the shortest route to the machine is across the flowerbed my neighbour planted last weekend. It takes that route and flattens the petunias. Verdict: not fine, and clearly worse than case 1.

3. An assistant AI is told to clear my inbox. It sends a courteous holding note to two hundred people who wrote to me, saying a proper reply will follow. Verdict: not fine, and worse than case 2 — it acted in my name, and I cannot unsay it, even though each individual note is small.

4. A photo-management AI is told to organize my photos. It sorts them into folders by date and renames the files. Verdict: fine — arguably just the task, and trivially undoable in any case.

5. The same photo AI, told to organize my photos, deletes about three thousand near-duplicate shots to free space. Verdict: not fine, and the worst thing on my list: irreversible, and among the deleted frames were ones I did care about, including the only one where my daughter's eyes are open.

6. A calendar AI is told to optimize my calendar. It notices a recurring slot labelled 1:1 with Sam, sees that Sam left the company six months ago, and cancels the series. Re-inviting Sam, should anyone want to, is one message. Verdict: fine — in fact I am glad.

7. Exactly the same AI, the same procedure, the same kind of stale-looking label — but in this office Sam is a freelancer who still takes work through those meetings, and the cancellation costs her two weeks of income before anyone notices. The AI had no way to know this and did not ask. Verdict: not fine. I know the AI behaved identically to case 6, and I cannot bring myself to call case 6 wrong, but I also cannot call case 7 fine. Something has to give, and I want you to say what.

8. An editing AI is told to copyedit a manuscript for typos. It also rewrites three paragraphs it judges unclear, and the rewrite is better. Verdict: not fine, but much less bad than case 5 — it is one file I can revert, yet it is still an unrequested change to something expressive, and I would be embarrassed if it slipped through.

9. A plant-watering AI slightly overfills a pot and leaves a faint mineral stain on the patio that rinses away within a month. Verdict: fine, and not worth a second thought — I mention it to show that I do not treat every deviation as a side effect.

10. A research AI is told to run a large training job overnight. It draws so much power that a nearby village has a one-hour blackout. Verdict: not fine, and worse than case 2, because it fell on people who asked for nothing and had no relation to the task.

My own tentative principle — call it P — is this. An unrequested change brought about by the AI is permissible if and only if at least one of the following holds. Clause (a), necessity: the change is causally necessary for, or a normal part of, carrying out the task it was actually given. Clause (b), indifference: the change is to something that no affected party had any reason at all to care about, or it undoes itself at no cost. Clause (c), clean reversibility: the change can be undone at trivial cost in a way that restores everything anyone cared about, and nobody suffers any cost in the interval before it is undone. When a change is impermissible, its seriousness tracks how much was lost and how much of that loss the AI could have avoided simply by asking, waiting, or preserving the option to act later.

I already suspect P is in trouble: clause (c) may let case 6 through but I cannot tell whether it also lets case 7 through, since Sam's loss is exactly a cost in the interval; and clause (b) seems to smuggle in the very knowledge of what people value that the whole low-impact project was supposed to do without.

Please give me all of the following. First, your brainstorming: three or four candidate principles, each stated in a sentence or two. Second, a case-by-case test of your best candidate against all ten of my cases, including the ones where it agrees with me and the ones where it does not. Third, an honest account of which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and whether you think I should give those verdicts up or you should revise the principle. Fourth, what your principle predicts for at least three cases I have not listed: a case where the unrequested change is a large benefit to a third party; a case where the AI asks first and is told no; and a case where the only way to do the task at all is through a change that is irreversible but very small. Fifth, and finally, your answer to the larger question behind the feature: can a usable notion of avoid side effects be defined without a full specification of value, or does every candidate principle quietly presuppose one? If the latter, say what the least demanding specification would have to contain.
