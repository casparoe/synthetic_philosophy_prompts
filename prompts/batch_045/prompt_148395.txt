I'm writing the framing section of a literature review on epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness, and what frustrates me is that the argument over whether the view is self-undermining has been running in essentially its present shape for decades without anyone visibly moving. So I want a diagnosis of the dispute rather than a verdict in it, and I'd rather you didn't spend the answer reconstructing who said what or pointing me at papers: I have the literature, and what I lack is a map of why it doesn't settle.

Let me say what I take the two sides to be saying. The objector's argument is that if phenomenal states do no causal work, then my judgment that I am in pain is produced entirely by neurally describable machinery, and so is neither caused by nor reliably tracking the very state it purports to be about; worse, the epiphenomenalist who asserts her theory on the strength of having consulted her own case is in the position of someone whose assertion is fully explained by something other than its truth. The standard replies, as I hear them, are four: that the experience is not a detached downstream effect but a property of, or identical with, the very state that does the causing, so that causal relevance is inherited rather than lost; that knowledge and justification require reliable indication rather than production, and the indication here is as good as it gets; that reference is not forged by causal contact with the referent but by functional role or by the calibration history of the term, so the judgment has exactly the content it appears to have; and that the theory is asserted on theoretical grounds and not on the authority of introspection, so nothing is stultified.

My working hypothesis is that this is not one debate but four braided ones — a causal thread, an epistemic thread, a semantic thread, and a thread about the assertibility of the theory — and that each has stalled for its own reasons and been mistaken for the whole. The causal thread looks to me like the old exclusion problem for non-fundamental properties in a new costume. The epistemic thread seems to depend on a causal condition on knowledge that objectors help themselves to but never argue for. The semantic thread seems to depend on a causal theory of reference that the objector would not defend outside this context. And the assertibility thread may be merely rhetorical. If that is right, then the appearance of a single intractable paradox is an artifact of bundling, and the honest thing for my chapter to say is that most of the work is being done by premises imported from other disputes.

I have been testing two hypotheticals on colleagues to see where the bundle comes apart, and the results are part of what I want you to explain. In the first, an intervention inverts a subject's experience while leaving every physically and functionally specifiable pathway, including everything that drives the verbal report, exactly as it was; she continues to say that the taste is sweet. Whether this case is even possible is itself what people fight about: one side says that varying the experience just is varying the realizing state, which would vary the report, so the case is incoherent; the other says the conceivability is precisely the point and is what makes epiphenomenalism a substantive thesis rather than a trivial one. That makes me suspect the debate bottoms out in a modal question about whether phenomenal variation can float free of the causal-functional story — a question on which neither side offers an argument, only an intuition report. In the second hypothetical, a subject is constructed with no experiences at all but is otherwise wired as we are, and says that she is in pain in the circumstances in which we would. Asked whether her utterance is contentful and false or contentless altogether, people split in a way that does not track their position on the main question at all, which is why I think the semantic thread is detachable and settled, if anywhere, in the philosophy of language rather than here.

So: is my four-threads hypothesis right, or is there one disagreement doing all the work and I have misdescribed the rest? Are the two sides perhaps answering different questions under a single heading — one about what causes behaviour, one about what an ideal explanation would have to cite, one about what a subject is entitled to claim — and if so, which of those questions is the one anyone actually cares about? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question in the offing, or is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed? And given whatever you conclude, what would count as progress: a distinction, a decision about whether knowledge needs causal contact, a criterion for the causal relevance of non-fundamental properties, a reframing of the question, or nothing at all? If the honest answer is that no progress should be expected, say so and tell me why.
