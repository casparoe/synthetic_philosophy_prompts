taking this module on conservatism — Burke, Oakeshott, Hayek, that whole lot — and last week we did the Chesterton bit about the fence: don't tear down a fence until you can say why someone put it there. I basically like it but every time I try to use it on actual decisions at my uni my own reactions come out all over the place. so here they are, going purely on gut, no theory:

1. The registry still makes us hand in dissertations on paper, signed, in person, to an office that then scans the damn thing. Nobody in the registry can tell you why. I say scrap it tomorrow.
2. Formal hall, everyone wears the gowns. Half of us think it's ridiculous and nobody can give a reason past 'tradition'. I say keep it.
3. Only permanent staff can supervise dissertations, teaching fellows can't. I actually looked this up: introduced in 1987 by a committee made up entirely of full professors, right after teaching fellows started being hired, and it neatly reserves the paid supervision hours for them. I say scrap it.
4. City zoning rules: every new block of flats has to include one parking space per flat. From 1962, when obviously everyone drove. A third of households round here don't own a car now. Scrap.
5. PhD students aren't allowed to teach in their first year. The origin is on the record and it's a nice one — stop them being used as cheap labour. But now it means you graduate with zero teaching experience and don't get hired. Scrap, and weirdly I'm more confident about this than about 1.
6. College library shuts at 6pm on Sundays. Used to be that the staff wouldn't work Sundays. Now it's the one evening nobody expects you to be working. Keep — except I notice I only want to keep it for a reason that's got nothing to do with why it's there, and if I said that out loud the college would swap it for a wellbeing policy, which would be worse.
7. Two things nobody can explain that I want to keep *because* nobody can explain them: the outgoing SU president chairs the first meeting of the new year, and in our seminars nobody contradicts the professor in the first ten minutes. My sense is both of them stop working the second somebody asks what they're for. Like they only hold up while they're unexamined — which is awkward, because Chesterton's actual prescription is go away and work out what the thing is for.
8. The rule that any change to degree requirements has to go through a committee that meets once a year and wants a written rationale. Keep. I realise this is just the fence principle with a committee bolted on.

The two pairs I can't square: 1 and 2 look identical on paper (no idea why it exists, minor costs either way) and I want opposite things. And 4 vs 6, where in both cases I know the original rationale is dead, and again I want opposite things.

My own attempt at a principle: you may remove an existing practice only if you can say what it was for and show either that that thing is gone or that it was never worth having; and it gets harder to remove the more expensive it would be to rebuild, and the more its working depends on nobody asking why. Does that hang together? It seems to give the wrong answer on 1, or else I'm just wrong about 1. Also I don't know what to do with 3 — I feel *more* confident there because I found an origin I don't like, but Chesterton's whole point is about not knowing the origin, so why should knowing a bad one help me at all?

Two more things. Does any of this say anything about fences that haven't been built yet? My department is about to add a rule that every seminar starts with a five-minute recap of last week's, which nobody objects to. Is there a symmetric 'don't put up a fence unless you can say what it's for', or is the principle only about things that already exist? And does the presumption get stronger just with age? If it does, isn't that just status quo bias with a story attached to it?

The case I actually have to vote on next week: scrapping the year-long research colloquium for PhD students. No one remembers why it exists (someone thinks it was a condition of some 1990s grant), everyone hates it, and it's the only time the entire cohort is in a room together. My gut flips every time I think about it.

So: is there one principle that gets most of my verdicts, which of them does it force me to drop, which clause is earning its keep on which case, and what does it say about the colloquium and about cases I haven't thought of?
