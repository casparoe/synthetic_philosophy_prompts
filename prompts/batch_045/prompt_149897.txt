I've more or less come to believe that in Parfit's hitchhiker there's no way out except becoming the sort of person who actually hands over the money once you're in town — some kind of rational irrationality, a disposition you can't quietly revoke at the moment it costs you. And I think it generalizes: turning the dials on the setup doesn't dissolve the need for that move, it only changes how big the prize for being reliable is.

The dials I have in mind: the driver is a decent but not perfect judge of character, right maybe 90% of the time, and picking wrong costs her little; the hitchhiker will need rides again, but news of a default reaches the next driver only with some probability and gets discounted when it does; and instead of cultivating a disposition the hitchhiker could pay a third party up front for a commitment device that fails with known probability.

So tell me what would actually count against the claim that rational irrationality is required here. I want the things a defender of that claim would themselves concede as counting against it, not just the usual objections from outside — especially cases where the disposition can't be installed at all (you can't decide to be irrational on cue, or the ride is long and the hitchhiker's values shift enough that the disposition doesn't survive the trip), cases where the prize for reliability flips sign, cases where the commitment device at some failure rate plainly beats the disposition, and anything like a formal result showing that any rule that pays in the plain case loses in a variant where no disposition is detectable at all, say the driver chooses in the dark off a cheap signal the hitchhiker could fake at a cost.

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and give me an honest verdict on whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability — whoever pays was disposed, whoever doesn't just couldn't manage it — and whether, if it has, that's genuinely a problem for a claim of this kind or fine given what the claim is for.
