I run incident response for a moderately large distributed payments platform, and a solid chunk of my week goes into postmortems whose central claim is of the form *the incident happened because the retry was never configured*, or *the failover happened because the heartbeat stopped*, or — the case I keep coming back to — *the customer-visible outage lasted forty minutes because the alert that was supposed to page us had been silenced in a stale routing rule*. My working vocabulary is services, dependencies, retries and timeouts, circuit breakers, heartbeats and dead-man's-switch signals, SLOs and error budgets, alert routing, fault trees, causal DAGs over a single run of the system, and counterfactual replay ("if the flag had been off, would this still have happened?"). I'm comfortable reasoning as if I had structural equations and I use do-notation informally, but I have no philosophy beyond one undergraduate course. I've been arguing with a friend who does metaphysics, and she says the thing I spend my professional life asserting — that an omission, a failure to act, a missing signal, can be a cause — is deeply contested, and that whole literatures exist over whether an absence can appear in a causal chain at all.

So here is the job. Take the debate about causation by omission and restate it as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use, distinguishing at least the following competing accounts as you go: the pure difference-making account (an omission causes if, had the agent acted, the outcome would not have occurred); the account that demands a spatio-temporally continuous physical process carrying a conserved quantity, and which therefore seems to rule omissions out; the account on which omissions cause in virtue of departing from a default or from what is normatively supposed to happen; and the account that ties causal selection here to agency, control, or responsibility. Two stipulations about my system that I want you to take seriously rather than smooth over. First, some absences in my system are instrumented as first-class positive events: a heartbeat that stops firing emits a real log line with a real timestamp, and downstream services consume it; other absences were never instrumented and show up only as a gap in a trace, or not at all. Second, my postmortem template has a normative baseline built into it — the runbook says what should have happened — and it is genuinely ambiguous in our practice whether "normal" means statistically typical or prescribed-by-us, and our cause categories (trigger, contributing factor, root cause) come with a hard actionability rule: we only write down as a cause something we could have changed at deploy time.

Give me the translation and then the residue report, in three buckets: what maps onto my vocabulary without loss; what maps only if I add structure my tooling doesn't currently have (say exactly what I'd have to start logging or modeling); and what cannot be expressed in it at all. My own instinct, which I want you to pressure-test rather than flatter, is that the whole worry is a logging problem and not a metaphysics problem — that once an absence is instrumented it just *is* an event in the causal graph, and if it isn't instrumented then that is an observability gap, not a fact about causation.
