Background so you don't have to guess: the classical view, associated with al-Ghazālī and the Ash'arite tradition, says no created thing causes anything — when cotton touches fire, God burns the cotton, not the fire, and what we call a cause is really just God's habit of doing one thing after another. Hamdi thinks this is *good* news for science rather than bad news, and she's got three moves I want to get at:

1. She says people only hear occasionalism as "science is just astrology, patterns with no reason behind them" because they assume a law of nature has to be either a brute regularity or a mind-independent necessity, and she says both options are bad. On her view a law is a description of a personal habit, and because habits are facts about agents rather than about event-sequences, the regularity is anchored in something that can't just stop holding for no reason. Every time I've heard her pressed on why that isn't just renaming the problem, she answers: a habit is a fact about a person, not about a sequence of events. She says this roughly twice per talk.

2. On miracles she refuses to call them suspensions or interruptions of a law, since on her picture there's no difference at the level of mechanism between a burning bush and a candle flame. She says the difference is hermeneutic: a miracle is an event God is using to say something, and the very same event would be ordinary if it weren't. Critics point out that this makes "miracle" a matter of interpretation and she seems completely fine with that.

3. The chapter I actually want to spend time on argues that occasionalism is the natural or "home" metaphysics of any world whose physics is computational. If the universe updates in discrete steps according to a rule, then no state produces the next state — the rule does — so the causal oomph everyone says occasionalism can't account for was never in the picture to begin with. She calls this the frame argument and thinks it converts the standard objection into a motivation.
