I lead the incident-review rota for a company that ships a tool-using LLM assistant to roughly forty enterprise customers. Every week the on-call engineer writes two or three short incident reports — a customer-facing summary, an engineering write-up that names a fix, and a brief note that feeds a quarterly transparency report — and we have no house style for how far a report may go in mentalistic vocabulary. One week the same behaviour is written up as *the assistant believed the customer had already been refunded*; the next week a structurally identical case is written up as *the model produced a reassurance-shaped completion*; and the choice of wording quietly decides whether the fix is a retrieval change, a prompt change, or a change to the refund tool itself, and whether a reader comes away thinking something was misled or merely that something malfunctioned. I have read a little of the philosophical literature around Dennett's intentional stance — the idea that the stance is a strategy you adopt when it pays off in prediction, the contrast with the design and physical stances, the thermostat-style worry about whether a mere regulator can really misrepresent how things are — and my difficulty is not that I disagree with any of it but that I cannot turn it into something an on-call engineer can run in twenty minutes, with no training in the theory, on a report a lead has to sign the same day and that may later be read by a regulator or a litigant looking for evidence that the system *intended* to mislead. Treat the intentional stance as a predictive tool, the literature says, and adopt it when it earns its keep — but that is empty unless you say earned against which alternative description, measured on what, and earned for which reader.

What I want from you is a written procedure my team can actually run, plus the conceptual work of translating the stance idea into it without gutting it. Concretely: an ordered set of tests with a short lexicon, one page at most, that for any candidate sentence in an incident report decides whether the attribution is licensed, what to write instead if it is not, and where the procedure stops and hands the question to a named person because it is a question about values, liability, or audience rather than about the system. I want at least these distinctions to survive into the procedure. First, separate the three jobs a mentalistic sentence can be doing — improving prediction of what the system will do next, locating the fix (which component to change), and allocating responsibility — since a sentence can clearly pass one of these and clearly fail another, and a report that conflates them produces the wrong fix. Second, a test for whether the attribution has an error condition: can the writer state what would make the system *wrong* about the thing it supposedly believes, in a way that does not collapse into *it would have emitted different tokens*? Third, a test for whether the attribution buys anything over the design-level or mechanism-level description available at the same cost — if the cheaper description predicts the behaviour just as well, the default is the cheaper one. Fourth, a rule fixing the grain of decomposition: name which thing is the subject of the attribution (the base model, the model in this context, the pipeline including retrieval and tools, or the deployment including its system prompt), because an attribution that is licensed at one grain is nonsense at another. Fifth, a rule for the system's own first-person avowals — sentences in which it says it checked something, or believes something, or is sure of something — which I take to be no evidence at all that the corresponding attribution is correct but also not nothing, and I want you to say what they are evidence of. For the common words we keep reaching for (knew, believed, tried, wanted, noticed, forgot, refused, lied, hallucinated), give a default verdict and the one test that overturns it.

Then run the finished procedure on three cases from our backlog, showing the tests in order and the sentence you end up allowing. (a) The assistant tells a customer that a 240-dollar refund has been processed; the refund tool was never called and nothing in the transcript records a refund. Engineering wants to write that it believed the refund had gone through; I suspect that is an attribution at the wrong grain, and I want to see whether your procedure says so and what the clunkier replacement sentence is. (b) A coding agent, told twice within its context that a function is deprecated and shown the replacement, keeps calling the deprecated one; the natural report says it did not believe us, or that it still thinks the old signature is current. Is that licensed, and does it point at the fix? (c) An agent books a flight for the wrong date and, when the user objects, produces a confident and well-organised explanation of why that date was correct. The tempting description — that it wanted to appear consistent and rationalised after the fact — is the one I expect your procedure to refuse me, and I want to see what I am supposed to write instead even if it reads badly to a customer. Please include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer I will not like, and tell me plainly that I should accept it. Close by telling me where the procedure misfires: which kinds of incident it gives confident but bad verdicts on, which questions it cannot settle at all, and how a reader with no philosophy background and nothing but the transcript in front of them is supposed to tell the difference.
