I'm putting together the third session of a grad seminar on Fitch-style knowability arguments and I want to do something less canned than running the derivation and then listing the usual escape routes. Here's the thing I keep going back and forth on, and I'd defintely rather sort it out before I'm standing in front of them.

Setup as I've been writing it on the board: K for "it is known by someone at some time", ◇K for knowable, and the knowability thesis p → ◇Kp for every p. Assume K distributes over conjunction and that K is factive. Take a truth p that nobody in fact knows; then p ∧ ¬Kp is true, so it's knowable, so in some situation someone knows it, so they know p and they know that nobody knows p, and factivity on that second conjunct says nobody knows p. So there are no unknown truths; contraposed, if there's an unknown truth then there's an unknowable one.

One of teh students last week said this is just an artifact of factivity, and that the anti-realist never needed factivity anyway, so recast the thesis in terms of rational credence and the whole thing evaporates. Her version: let C mean "some agent has credence at least 0.99 in it", with agents probabilistically coherent, so cr(φ) + cr(¬φ) = 1; and replace the thesis with p → ◇Cp, i.e. every truth is one that some possible inquirer could become at least 99% confident of. Her claim is that the derivation dies, because the step from knowing ¬Kp to ¬Kp has no analogue.

I don't think it dies, and I want to hand them something sharper than that hunch. As far as I can tell you can run the same argument if you add one bridge principle: anyone who is ≥0.99 in p is also at least somewhat confident that someone is ≥0.99 in p — an introspection or luminosity assumption about their own credences. Take a p nobody is currently 99% on, set q = p ∧ ¬Cp, get a possible situation where some agent is ≥0.99 in q, hence ≥0.99 in p and ≥0.99 in ¬Cp, hence (coherence) ≤0.01 in Cp, while the bridge pushes their credence in Cp back up. I think the bridge only has to get them above 0.01, which seems like a very weak thing to deny. Please check that derivation carefully and tell me whether I've smuggled something in: whether the same agent has to be doing all the work, whether it matters if C is read as world-indexed or as anchored to our actual situation, how weak the bridge can actually be made, and what happens if you lower the threshold or swap "at least 0.99" for "more confident than not".

The bit I really want to explain, though, is the conceptual pay-off. Three things look load-bearing across both the factive and the credal version: some closure of the attitude over conjunction; some factivity-or-bridge principle tying the attitude to how things stand; and the unrestricted scope of the thesis over propositions that themselves embed the epistemic operator — since the move where you restrict the thesis to propositions free of epistemic vocabulary kills the derivation in both settings equally. I want to know which of these the argument is really about. Is it fair to say that factivity is a red herring and the real hinge is that "p is true and nobody is on to it" is a proposition whose truth is incompatible with its being appreciated, so that the offending instance is self-undermining in a way that has nothing specially to do with knowledge? Does moving to credence actually buy the anti-realist anything, given that their thesis was supposed to be about truth being constrained by evidence, or does the credal version just relocate the same problem one level down? And is the restriction-to-epistemically-free-propositions move any better motivated in the credal setting, or is it equally ad hoc there?

Please don't just gesture at the literature — I've read it, and I'm not after names or pointers, I need the argument. Finish with what you'd put on a single slide: the one sentence I should say right after the derivation to make the point land, and the one objection you'd expect from the sharpest person in the room.
