I work on a policy team that is weighing whether a selective-abstention design gets us out of the standard incompatibility result for group-fairness criteria. The set-up: two groups, with base rates for the outcome to be predicted at 20% and 40%; the risk score is imperfect; the decision is binary (grant/deny, release/detain). With unequal base rates, no such rule can have both equal false-positive and false-negative rates across groups ("equalized odds") and equal precision across groups ("predictive parity"), unless the score is perfect. Now modify the design: the system may abstain on up to 15% of cases in each group and route them to a human decision-maker, and the two criteria are then evaluated only over the cases the system actually decides on, with the deferred cases scored by whatever the human does.

Give me your credence, as a number between 0 and 1, that this design genuinely dissolves the conflict for the cases that get decided, as opposed to merely relocating it. Count it as dissolved only if both criteria hold to within a tolerance a regulator would not object to on the decided cases, with per-group deferral rates under 15%, and without the decided-on rule being made practically useless — not, for instance, degraded to the point where a group-blind threshold on the raw score dominates it. My own number is 0.4; a colleague who likes the design puts it at 0.75.

Decompose the number rather than defending it. At minimum separate: how much turns on whether the criteria are read as conditional on the outcome alone or on the outcome together with non-abstention; whether an abstention rule can shift the effective base rate of the decided-on population far enough to matter under a 15% cap; whether unequal deferral rates across groups are themselves a fairness failure of the kind the criteria were introduced to catch; whether the human track simply reproduces the disparity it was meant to escape; and whether the conflict reappears once deferred cases are scored by the decision they actually received. Give a number and one line of justification for each term, say how you combine them, then tell me which single sub-question your final figure is most sensitive to, what evidence would move it most, and which of the two of us at 0.4 and 0.75 is most likely wrong about that sub-question.
