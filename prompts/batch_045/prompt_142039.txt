I take part in a small, non-academic reading group that works through books on genocide and memory; I have no formal training in history or philosophy. In our last session someone ended a discussion by saying, in a tone that was meant to close the matter, that the Holocaust was a product of modernity rather than a relapse into barbarism — that it was bureaucratic, administrative and industrial, and that this is what sets it apart from earlier massacres. The line was made to do two jobs at once: to suggest that comparisons with other mass killings are somehow illegitimate, and to suggest something indicting about modernity itself.

Could you set out, as a list I can bring to the next session, the different precise claims that sentence might be making? I have tried to sort it out myself and got as far as three: an organizational claim, that the killing was carried out through division of labour, files, timetables and supply chains; a distance claim, that most participants never saw the killing and could think of themselves as processing paperwork; and a totality claim, that the aim was total destruction including women, children and the elderly, with no one inside the targeted category exempt and no useful outcome being sought. I suspect there are several further and quite different theses hiding in it — about causation, about technology, about what modern is even supposed to mean here, about whether the point is that this was worse or that it was different in kind — and I cannot tell which of them would do the work my interlocutor wanted done.

For each reading I would like a verdict: trivial, defensible, or false, plus a note on whether it actually supports refusing comparisons, or supports an indictment of modernity, or supports neither. I would also like each reading put to two tests. The first is a counterfactual one: if a pre-modern regime had achieved the same scale, thoroughness and intent using only hand weapons and local organization, would the claim be false, and would the person relying on it have to give up the point they were making? The second runs the other way: are there features of the event that are genuinely unusual but have nothing to do with modernity — a state aiming at the total destruction of a dispersed, legally emancipated and partly assimilated minority that it had itself defined by ancestry, for instance — so that modernity looks like the wrong word for what is actually distinctive? Finally, say which reading someone repeating this sentence in a general discussion is most likely to have in mind. Please do not cite books or authors; I would rather have the distinctions than the references.
