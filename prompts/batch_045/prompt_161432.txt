I am the product lead for a patient-facing clinical assistant that our hospital system is piloting in its portal, and I wrote the refusal policy it currently ships with. Six weeks from now I present a revision of that policy to the system's AI Oversight Committee, and I would like to walk in prepared.

The revision is partly personal. My mother, who has mild cognitive impairment, used the portal assistant last year to ask whether she could stop taking a blood thinner before a dental procedure. The assistant declined to engage and told her to speak with her doctor. She stopped taking it anyway, without asking anyone, and we found out after a bleeding scare. The refusal did not prevent the action; it only ensured that the action happened outside the conversation. That experience is the core of what I now want to defend.

The thesis I will defend is that a refusal is a steering resource, not a moral verdict, and should be governed as a scarce one. Concretely, I propose three things. First, a steering-loss asymmetry: because refusing generally does not stop a determined user but does remove the assistant from the loop, the value of a refusal must be measured against the value of the shaped answer it displaces, not against the value of silence. Second, an identification requirement: an override may never be grounded solely in population-level base rates about people like the user; it has to cite a user-specific reason, on the model that a base-rate-only intervention is a form of statistical discrimination against the user in front of you. Third, a refusal budget: because each intervention spends trust and pushes traffic toward unsteered alternatives, the policy should carry an explicit expected-budget per user, which turns the question of how paternalistic to be into an allocation question with a real constraint. Underneath these sits a single trigger for intervention short of refusal — what I call the reversal-cost trigger: friction, not refusal, when a self-regarding request has a tail-shaped downside, an irreversible one, and a plan whose success depends on the user's prediction about their own future behavior.

The committee is: an emergency medicine attending who believes every non-answer transfers risk back onto the clinician; a geriatric psychiatrist who thinks our biggest exposure is undetected capacity loss in exactly the users who present as competent; a clinical ethicist who is a pragmatist, distrusts autonomy as a conversation-stopper, and will always ask who bears the cost of a proposed rule; the system's risk counsel, who wants the strictest defensible policy and cares only about what is documented and discoverable afterward; a nurse manager who will count the extra messages each intervention generates; and a patient advocate who has opposed the pilot publicly and regards refusal as institutional condescension.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet for that room, not for a room of AI safety researchers. I want the eight or so objections I will actually get, in roughly the order they will arrive, phrased the way each of these people would phrase them rather than in my vocabulary, and ordered by expected damage rather than by elegance. For each: the reply I should give in about ninety seconds, where that reply is soft, and whether it is a place to concede or a place to hold. Tell me what I should concede in the first two minutes, before anyone asks, and which single reply has to land for the revision to survive the vote. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and say why it is the dangerous one. Finally, flag anywhere my own framing — steering-loss asymmetry, identification requirement, refusal budget, reversal-cost trigger — begs the question against someone in that room, and tell me which of those four I should quietly drop if the discussion turns hostile.
