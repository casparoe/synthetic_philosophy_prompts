I've gone down a bit of a rabbit hole recently (it started when a school near me got renamed and everyone more or less shrugged, whereas the statue arguement in the same town ran for years), and I keep noticing the same pattern. When a city decides that someone commemorated in public isn't worthy of it any more, the standard menu seems to be: pull the statue down and put it in a museum or a warehouse — very rarely melted down or smashed, even when it's a bog-standard mass-produced bronze that nobody thinks is any good as art; or leave it up and bolt on an interpretive plaque; or rename the street, the school, the lecture theatre, the scholarship. And here's the bit that puzzles me. Renaming is the option that actually costs money and causes hassle — new signage, new addresses, businesses having to reprint everything — and yet it's the one that gets treated as obviously fine and barely argued about. Shifting a statue is, by comparison, cheap and quick, and it's the one that produces years of fighting. We do X in one case and Y in the other and I can't tell whether that's just inertia about old objects or whether it tracks something real about what the two kinds of thing are.

Two mates of mine offered opposite explanations and neither fully convinced me. One says the statue is basically detachable: the honour lives in the plinth, in the square, in the fact that we put him up high where we have to walk round him, so once you take it off the pedestal the bronze is just an object and a museum can hold it without honouring anybody — which would explain why nobody insists on destroying them. The other says it's the other way round: a street name is worse because we use it constantly, we say it out loud, we give it as our address, so it enrols us in the honouring every single day, whereas a statue is just something you walk past. I also notice that nobody ever campaigns to rename something because the namesake turns out to have been merely forgettable, or a mediocre dullard, or to have got his fame by luck — the campaigns only fire when there's some serious wrongdoing. And then there's the retain-and-explain option, which seems to assume that bolting on a few paragraphs can genuinely change what an object is doing, that its meaning is fixed by us now rather than by whoever put it up; but if that's true, why remove anything at all?

So: what exactly does this whole package of practices presuppose about what a statue, or a name, is? What view of commemoration does it only make sense under — an honour, a record, an expression of who "we" are, a claim about admirability, something else — and which rival views does it quietly rule out? I'd like you to seperate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and to say where it's underdetermined and several different theories would license the same behaviour. Is my first mate's "the plinth is the honour, the bronze is just a thing" idea actually doing work in the practice, or is the museum solution just a compromise nobody has thought through? Does the wrongdoing-but-not-mediocrity pattern tell us anything about what a monument asserts about a person? Is the package internally consistent — do the statue bit and the renaming bit presuppose the same theory, or is one of them running on a different one? And if we swapped in a rival view (say, that commemoration is mainly a record of what earlier generations valued, or that it's purely the current community's speech, or that the dead themselves have some stake in it), what would actually have to change in how cities handle these things? I don't need any reading recommendations or names of papers, I just want the argument.
