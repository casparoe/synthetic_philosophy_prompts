i run a weekly AI safety reading group, about 14 grad students (mostly ML, a couple of philosophers), 75 minutes a session. Next week is scalable oversight, and I want them to *feel* why debate might let a weak supervisor oversee a system smarter than her -- and where it breaks -- rather than me sketching the argument on a whiteboard.

Design a tournament-style game for that slot. Pairs argue opposite sides of questions the judge (a third student) can't cheaply check herself -- multi-step arithmetic, "which of these two cities had more rainfall in 1994", chained factual/legal stuff -- with one debater secretly assigned the true answer and one the false one, and nobody told which they got until *after* they've committed to an opening argument. Both debaters score for winning, not for being right. Give the judge two "zoom-in" tokens: she can force either side to split any claim into two sub-claims, then flips a coin and inspects exactly one of them. I want the scoring worked out so spending a token is genuinely costly for her, and so there's a real temptation for a debater to bury things in plausible-sounding detail.

Then a debrief mapping what actually happened onto the crux: that debate only tracks truth if it's cheaper to expose one false leaf than to keep a consistent false story intact all the way down, and that it fails when the deceptive side is simply better at being persuasive than the honest side is at being correct. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (my guess: my students are bad liars, so debate will look far stronger than it is), and be straight about which feature of the real setup the game can't reproduce at all -- I suspect the whole point is a debater smarter than the judge, and I can't stage that.
