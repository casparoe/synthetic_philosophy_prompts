I'm writing the methods section of a term paper for a seminar on the history of violence, and I've realized I can't write another sentence until I fix what I'm measuring. My paper asks whether an ordinary person in, say, eighteenth-century Europe was worse off with respect to violence than an ordinary person in a wealthy country today. Before I commit to an operationalization, I want the specification that an operationalization would have to meet.

Please write the desiderata — the list of requirements an account of "how violent a society or period is" must satisfy, given that my question is a comparative, cross-epoch one and given the evidence that actually survives: criminal court records, coroners' rolls, parish and hospital registers, military rolls, and modern police and hospital data, with wildly different coverage, definitions, and reporting rates across periods. For each requirement, give the case that motivates it, mark whether it is non-negotiable for my purpose or merely a preference of one camp, and tell me which single requirement does the most to rule out the operationalizations people actually reach for.

Cases I want the list forced to handle:

- The unit problem. Per-capita rates and absolute totals diverge when population grows, and expected harm to a randomly selected person diverges from aggregate harm again. A period with a very large population and a very small individual risk has to come out somewhere determinate, and the account has to say which of these quantities is the one my question is about.
- Opportunity versus disposition. A person in a modern city passes more strangers in a week than a villager did in a year. A per-capita-per-year count can fall simply because contact fell, and rise because contact rose. I want a requirement that the measure be able to separate how disposed people are to do violence from how many chances they get — something like a probability of assault per encounter of a specified type, rather than per person-year — and I want a verdict on whether that separation is even coherent, given that the number and character of encounters are themselves shaped by how violent a place is.
- The relabeling problem. If violence is defined as unauthorized or illegitimate force, then a regime that reclassifies beatings as correctional discipline, or an invasion as a police action, lowers the number without anything changing on the ground. I want a requirement to the effect that no purely verbal or legal reclassification may move the quantity — and I want to know what that requirement costs, in particular whether it forces a purely outcome-based measure in which authorization, intent, and the agents' own categories drop out entirely.
- Conditional and displaced violence. One person refrains from assaulting another because a credible threat of punishment hangs over him; a society drives assaults down through surveillance, incarceration, and architectural design. Is the standing threat itself violence? Is the coercion removed or merely relocated? The account must say whether it counts expected harm from unexecuted threats, actualized harm only, or counterfactual harm absent the deterrent — and it must not count the same coercion twice.
- Substitution across means and distance. A knife, a shell fired from ten miles away, an automated targeting system, a poison that presents as natural disease, a famine produced by an export policy. Does the definition track the mechanism, the proximity, the intent, or the outcome? And if it tracks only the outcome, why keep the category of violence at all rather than tracking welfare directly? Please treat that eliminativist option as a serious candidate rather than as a aside.
- The harm currency and its scaling. Deaths, disabling injuries, credible threats, humiliation, chronic fear, animals raised and slaughtered for food. Whatever the account aggregates has to be aggregable in a single currency or it cannot answer a comparative question at all; say what that currency is and what it does to the hard cases above.
- Incommensurable evidence bases. If the underlying quantity is latent and only partly observed, and both the coverage and the classification of records change between periods, is there a fact of the matter about the trend? I want the list to state plainly whether it requires the account to make the trend well defined, or permits it to return an interval, a bundle of disaggregated trends, or an admission that the comparison is not well posed.

Two things I especially want flagged. First, pairs of requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied: my suspicion is that relabeling-invariance is incompatible with any definition that respects agents' intentions or local categories, and that outcome-based completeness is incompatible with measurability from pre-modern records, but I'd like your own candidates for the sharpest tension. Second, any item on my list above that is really a prejudice of one school rather than a genuine desideratum for this question. Please spend no more than a short paragraph on how the familiar operationalizations — per-capita homicide rates, battle-death counts, total-welfare measures — fare against the list; I'll do the scoring myself.
