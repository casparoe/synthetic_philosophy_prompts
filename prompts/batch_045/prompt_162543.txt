Here is my question.

I work on automated reasoning, and we are considering an architecture I call a reflection tower. The idea comes from my reading about the Lucas–Penrose argument, which as I understand it runs like this: for any machine M that proves arithmetical theorems, there is a Gödel sentence G_M that is true but unprovable in M; a human mathematician who is told what M is can see that G_M is true; so human mathematical ability outruns any machine, and minds are not mechanisms. The reply that seems obvious to me is that a machine can do the very same thing: hand it M, let it construct G_M, adopt G_M — or equivalently the consistency statement for M — as a new axiom, and then do it again, and again. This is the idea behind transfinite progressions of theories: start from Peano arithmetic; at a successor stage add the consistency statement (or the uniform reflection principle) for the theory you have so far; at a limit stage take the union; index the whole construction by notations for constructive ordinals, in the style of Kleene's system O. I have been told that work in this line, descending from Turing's ordinal logics, shows that if one climbs along a suitable path through O the theories obtained in the limit prove every true sentence of arithmetic.

If that is right, then the anti-mechanist's supposed advantage is just a matter of height in the tower. The Gödelian gap closes one step at a time, human mathematicians simply occupy some level of the tower that our systems have not yet reached, and surpassing them is an engineering problem about how far we can climb. Concretely, we plan to let the tower climb on its own, and we are not too worried about which ordinal notations it accepts: when the system gets stuck we will simply have it add the consistency statement for its current theory, or, if that is unavailable, the negation of its own Gödel sentence, which I take to leave the theory consistent.
