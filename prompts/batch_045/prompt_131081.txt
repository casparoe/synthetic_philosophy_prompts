You are a professional philosopher of probability working in formal epistemology, with a particular specialism in self-locating belief, and — less commonly — someone who is genuinely good at structuring an explanation for non-specialists. Please think carefully before answering. I would like you to begin with some unstructured brainstorming: sketch at least two candidate scenarios that could carry the session and at least two orderings of the material, note what each would cost and what each would make easy, and only then commit to a plan and justify the commitment. Do not explain the Sleeping Beauty problem to me; I know the set-up. What I want is the plan for teaching it.

The situation. I am a retired experimental physicist — condensed matter, four decades of counting events and worrying about whether I had counted the right things — and in the last few years I have been going to a philosophy reading group. I have been asked to take forty minutes, with a whiteboard and no slides or handouts, on the Sleeping Beauty problem. The audience will be the group plus guests: about twenty-five people, roughly half of them working or retired scientists and engineers, roughly half with no quantitative training at all. Four things about this audience and this topic seem to me to be genuine design constraints rather than afterthoughts, and I would like them built into the plan.

First, the scientists will arrive holding that probability just is long-run relative frequency, and within two minutes someone will propose to settle everything by repeating the experiment a hundred times and counting. I want the sequence to use that instinct and then break it, because the count comes out differently depending on whether one tallies awakenings or experimental runs, and I do not want the session to end with each camp simply announcing that the other side counted the wrong thing.

Second, I want a carrying case that I can deform on the board. My own instinct is to state the standard coin version once and then vary the number of awakenings on the unfavourable outcome — two, ten, a million — and press the question of why one answer does not move as the number grows. I cannot tell whether this generalisation is a genuine ladder or whether it quietly presupposes what is in dispute. Tell me which, and why.

Third, I have a suspicion I cannot state precisely, and I need to know whether it is a defensible position or simply a muddle I should keep to myself: that the two famous answers are not really competing, that there is one quantity — something like degree of confidence in a proposition — which stays at one half, and another — something like what one ought to be willing to stake — which goes to a third, and that the philosophically interesting question is which of the two a theory of rationality is obliged to track. If that is a real position with a shape, place it at the correct point in the sequence; if it is a confusion, locate the confusion exactly, so that I do not transmit it.

Fourth, two of the members have read popular books on anthropic reasoning and will try to drag the discussion toward the Doomsday argument inside the first five minutes. I need to know where and how to fence that off without making them feel fobbed off.

What I want from you, concretely: the order in which the components have to arrive, and which ones depend on which; the single case that carries the session, with a note on what it must contain and what it must omit; the misconceptions this particular audience will generate, in the order they will generate them, and the precise moment at which each should be headed off; a short diagnostic for the final five minutes that distinguishes having understood the structure of the disagreement from having merely learned two labels; and what I should openly decline to go into at this level, having said that I am declining it.
