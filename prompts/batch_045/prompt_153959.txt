hey — I'm putting together a short segment for a reading group on specification gaming (mostly philosophers, a few ML people) and I want one case that runs the other way: a case where leaning hard on a proxy genuinely improves the thing you actually care about, and keeps improving it as you lean harder. Everyone's seen a dozen examples of the opposite and I want to break the "proxies always break eventually" intuition without cheating. I've been useing the same three examples for years and they all point one direction.

The four failure modes I'll be working with, in case it helps to aim at them: (a) selecting on the top of a noisy proxy buys you regression to the mean rather than the real thing; (b) the proxy isn't on any causal path to the goal, so moving it moves nothing that matters; (c) the correlation is fine in the middle of the distribution and falls apart out in the tail; (d) someone downstream learns the proxy and reroutes around it.

So here's the ask. Build me one scenario — realistic, one paragraph, a hospital or a warehouse or a school or a call centre, nothing sci-fi, no futuristic AI — in which:

- the proxy is not the goal and is not perfectly correlated with it (tell me what the residual gap actually consists of in the case);
- whoever is optimizing it is competent, motivated, and not resource-constrained — the escape hatch can't be "they weren't trying hard enough to game it";
- cranking up optimization pressure (more weight on the proxy, more budget, more rounds of iteration) makes the goal better too, and not just at the margin: I want to be able to keep cranking.

Then show your work:

1. Sketch the causal structure — which way the arrow runs between proxy and goal, where the noise sits, and which variables the optimizer can and can't touch.
2. Walk through (a)–(d) one at a time: which are blocked, and what specific feature blocks them. Be honest about any that are only partly blocked, and say how much damage the remaining leak can do.
3. Name the single feature doing the real work — the one where, if I flip it, the whole thing collapses back into an ordinary Goodhart story. Then flip it and show me the collapse in two or three sentences.
4. Give me the strongest objection from someone who thinks the "proxies break" intuition is basically right. What do they say about your case, and is it a real problem or just a quibble?

I'd much rather have a case where the verdict falls out of the setup than one where you stipulate "and the proxy happens to track the goal perfectly here" — that's defintely the main thing I'm trying to avoid.
