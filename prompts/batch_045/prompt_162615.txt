Then give me the speech. No outline, no bullet points after that, no "you could also adjust the tone" — I want the text.

The situation: next month I'm giving the eight-minute keynote at the founding convention of a new party. Genuinely new, single-issue, formed by about fourty people from my corner of the internet — LessWrong-adjacent, EA-adjacent, a couple of them extremely online since the old blog days. The plank is one specific piece of legislation. The charter has a mandatory sunset: the party dissolves automatically when the plank passes, or after three election cycles, whichever comes first.

They built the whole thing as a rebuttal to Weil. You know the essay — the late one arguing that political parties as such should be abolished, that a party is a machine for generating collective passion and for pressing the thought of its own members, that it exists first of all for itself and therefore can't serve either truth or the public good, that every party is totalitarian in germ. The founders read her as making an incentive-shaped argument: members' judgment gets corrupted because their interests are tied to the party's success, so fix the incentives and you fix the corruption. Hence the sunset clause, a seperately-funded permanent internal dissent wing with equal convention speaking time, a rule that no member may ever be asked to vote or speak against their own judgment, and a norm that every public statement steelmans the opposition.

I think that rebuttal misses the load-bearing part of her argument, which isn't epistemic but about will: a group that can act at all has to be able to act as one; a will that can be effective has to be able to count on its members; so the never-vote-against-your-judgment rule is precisely the thing that gets spent the first time the party is actually in reach of power, and the dissent wing is worse than nothing because it licenses the appearance of internal contestation while making the real thing unnecessary. The sunset clause I'm less sure about — maybe an organization reinterprets its own survival conditions as it goes, maybe a charter does bind a group that wants to live, I've defintely been wrong about this kind of thing before — so don't let me sneer at them.

Room: about 400 people. A third are the founders and their friends, who've read the essay and will catch every dodge. If I say she was really only talking about totalitarian parties, or that she'd have endorsed us, or that the charter answers her, they'll know I'm lying and so will I. The other two-thirds are volunteers who drove six hours or quit jobs and think a party is just what you do to win; they've never heard of her and don't need a lecture. There's press in the room. And whatever I close on goes on the banner — so I can't close on a line I don't believe, which rules out most of the available material: not the party that isn't a party, not the first honest party, not we're a movement and not a machine (they registered as a party, everyone there knows it).

So the genre problem is the actual problem: a founding-convention keynote has to make people glad they came, and I can't end it by telling them to disband, and I can't tell them anything I think is false. My guess is that the honest move isn't reassurance but a tripwire — give the room something they'd have to betray in order to keep, aimed at the moment the dissent norm first costs them a winnable seat. That's my guess; argue with it if it's wrong. What I do know: no direct quotations from the essay (I'd be paraphrasing from memory in front of reporters anyway), no sneering at the volunteers, nothing that turns into a speech about how sincere we're going to be, and it has to land as affection for these people rather than as a seminar. ~1000 words, which is eight minutes read aloud.
