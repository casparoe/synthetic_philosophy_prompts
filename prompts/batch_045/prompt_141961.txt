Hey — I'm in a small reading group on collective responsibility, and we've spent the last three weeks stuck on who's to blame for the First World War: the old Fischer rows in German historiography, the war-guilt article at Versailles, and the more recent big narrative histories that spread responsibility around all the capitals. Everyone else seems to arrive with a theory and then bend the cases to fit it. I'd rather go the other way: I trust my verdicts on particular cases more than I trust any account of national blame, so I want to start there and see what principle, if any, falls out. We can't look anything up in the room, so please don't send me to sources — I want the argument, not the reading list. Here are my verdicts, roughly in order of how sure I am: Imperial Germany in July 1914 — blameworthy for the war, substantially, because of the blank cheque to Vienna and the march through Belgium. Austria-Hungary — blameworthy for *a* war, the one against Serbia, but not really for *the* war; that feels like a real difference even though I can't say what it consists in. Serbia — not blameworthy for the war, even though the assassins came out of a milieu with links to people inside its own military intelligence and it defied most of the ultimatum. Russia — some blame for turning a Balkan war into a European one by mobilizing, but less than Germany and less than Austria-Hungary. Britain in 1914 — no blame for the war, though I do blame "Britain" in some hazy background way for the naval race and the guarantee politics, a verdict I suspect is a different thing wearing the same word. Nazi Germany in 1939 — maximal blame, no argument, and I notice that here every test gives the same answer so the case can't discriminate between theories. Borealia, an invented case: starts a war of intervention that in fact stops a genocide — I don't blame it at all, which suggests I'm not tracking "who started it" or "how bad it turned out" directly. Atlantia, also invented: its ally starts a war of conquest, Atlantia could have stopped it by withholding loans and naval escort, doesn't, and profits enormously — blameworthy, complicit, but clearly less than the ally. Ruritania, invented: five men in the war ministry forge cabinet authorization and start a war; the public, told they were attacked, rallies; two years later the forgery is public, the country votes to keep fighting and later builds a national myth around the war — I blame the nation, but only from the point where it could have known, so the blame ratchets forward in time; and yet I also feel the ratchet reaches back and stains the whole thing retroactively, which contradicts that. Valoria, invented: a referendum, 51% vote for a war of conquest, it goes disastrously — blame, yes, but thinner per person than the Ruritanian cabal case, even though vastly more individuals there were at fault.

My own tentative principle, which I can't get to work, is a "last effective no" rule: blame a nation for a war in proportion to (i) how much causal slack it had at the last point where a decision of its own could still have prevented the war, (ii) whether that decision aimed at the war itself or at something else it valued more, and (iii) how far the reasons the decision-makers acted on were reasons the political community had already made its own — whether the public, knowing the real facts, would have backed it. Please test that against the cases above and repair it or replace it. Two pins I want you to feel: if enabling Austria makes Germany to blame, why doesn't enabling the assassins make Serbia to blame (my answer is that Germany's support was decisive and Serbia's wasn't, but that just smuggles in a counterfactual I can't run); and if blame dilutes across headcount, then big democracies get off lightly for wars they voted for, which I hate, but if it doesn't dilute then my Valoria verdict is simply wrong. Give me one or two principles stated precisely, each clause tied to the case that motivates it, verdicts on a couple of cases I haven't listed (pick your own, but include at least one about whether the blame survives into the next generation and whether an apology or reparations discharges it or merely acknowledges it), and an honest list of which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate. And the case I go back and forth on weekly: Meridia, which spent twenty years publicly declaring it would never tolerate a rival's naval base ninety miles off its coast, whose intelligence correctly said the rival planned to attack within a year anyway, and which then mobilized first and fired first — tell me what your account says there.

Last thing: I'd like you to say where you're most likely to be wrong. What would change your mind — what kind of archival finding about July 1914, or what argument about whether nations are the sort of thing that can be blamed at all rather than just collections of people who can, would make you drop the principle you've just given me? If a clause is defintely doing all the work and is really just a restatement of a verdict, say so, and don't hide the places where you're guessing.
