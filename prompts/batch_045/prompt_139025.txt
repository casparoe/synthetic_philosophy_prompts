I TA for an intro cognitive science course, and after last week's section on perception two of my students — Ines and Tobias — got into an argument about Molyneux's question, the old one about whether a man born blind and suddenly given sight could tell a cube from a sphere by sight alone. I'd tweaked the case in section to make it harder, they ran off with the tweak, and now I'm supposed to tell them who was right. I dont think I can.

In my version the subject has never simply been blind from birth. She's 30, blind since birth from dense congenital cataracts, and for the last eight years she's been a heavy user of a sensory substitution device: a head-mounted camera whose output is turned into a moving pattern of tingles on a grid on her tongue. She's expert with it — she navigates a building, reaches for the right thing on a cluttered table, and can tell a mug from a bowl by moving her head and sort of looking with her tongue. Now the cataracts come out and she can see. Cube and sphere on a table, no touching: can she tell which is which?

Ines said yes, straight off. Her argument: the device already delivers shape. It's distal (about the object, not the tongue), it's spatial, and it's perspectival in the way vision is — she has to move her head to get more of the object and the pattern changes as she does, so what she's really learned over eight years is to extract three-dimensional shape from a stream of sensor readings that shifts with her own movement. Vision is just another such stream. Tobias said that only works if you've already decided the two experiences share a content; what's shared is at best a label applied from outside — "cubical" — and that's not what the question was about. Otherwise it's settled by stipulation: pick a description abstract enough to cover both modalities, "represents a cube-shaped distal object," and of course it's shared. Ines said it isn't stipulation but a bet with a testable edge: she conceded that recognition at first sight might fail, and said that if it does, the interesting question is transfer — if her tactile experience of shape is doing any work, she should learn to match seen shapes to felt ones much faster than someone who had the cataracts out without those eight years, and she should fail in distinctive ways, getting shape while being hopeless at telling a shadow from a dark surface, or at faces. If the answer is no transfer, vision starting from scratch, then Molyneux's question was never really about shape. Tobias replied that now the yes-answer is too cheap: suppose she says "cube" correctly on the first go — that's a judgement, maybe a fast inference from edges and shading to a shape she knows by touch, and it doesn't show her visual experience is as of a cube; so if the criterion is behavioural the yes proves less than people want, and if the criterion is phenomenal then no experiment settles it and we should say the question is unanswerable and move on. Ines said "unanswerable" is then doing the same work as "no," and that the behavioural/phenomenal split is too crude anyway: what matters is whether her seeing is guided by her tactile knowledge in the right kind of way, and there are intermediate cases — she needs a moment, or one confirming touch — that neither box handles. Last round, on what's at stake: Tobias said Molyneux's question is a probe for whether perceptual content is modality-specific at all, and that if Ines is right then it's amodal and the whole sight-versus-touch framing in those old empiricism debates was a category mistake. Ines said that is exactly the conclusion, and the device case shows why: what she learned from the device isn't a tactile fact but a fact about how objects and your own movements go together, and there's nothing especially visual about it. Tobias: then why did anyone think the question was hard? Ines: because they were asking the immediacy version, which is a bad test.

Can you go through this move by move and tell me who's right about what at each step, where one of them has mischaracterised the other, and what both of them are missing — including whether my device tweak actually changes anything or just reframes the same old question? Please don't lean on the empirical literature about cataract patients or sensory substitution to decide it; I don't remember the details of any of it and I'd rather you reason from the structure of the case and from what each of them actually says.
