I would like a number, not a tour of the available positions. Please give me your credence in the following claim, together with the decomposition that produces it.

P: where two proper names that a competent speaker uses for one and the same object differ in the cognitive significance of the identity statements they flank — the difference between 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' and 'Hesperus is Hesperus' — that difference is at least partly a difference in the semantic values of the names (a sense, a mental file, a character, or some functional equivalent), and not merely a difference in the speaker's epistemic history, in conventionally associated encyclopaedic or orthographic information, or in the pragmatics of the utterance. Count P as settled the way a majority of philosophers of language would settle it if the question were resolved twenty-five years from now, by the best theories then on offer rather than by fashion.

Two cases that I think the standard treatments handle badly, and which should constrain your terms. First, the veteran astronomer: someone who has known for forty years that Hesperus is Phosphorus, uses both names routinely, and teaches the identity to students. Is any of the original difference in cognitive significance left for her, or does it disappear entirely once ignorance does? Second, the fluent bilingual who has never been in doubt that Wien and Vienna name the same city: 'Wien is Vienna' does not read as flatly as 'Vienna is Vienna', but it may read as a fact about vocabulary rather than about a city, which is just what a pragmatic or lexical explanation would predict. Do not assume in advance that the first case supports the semantic reading and the second the pragmatic one; price them separately.

Decompose at least into: (i) whether a residue of cognitive difference survives the elimination of ignorance at all; (ii) given that it does, whether its best home is semantics or rather pragmatics and processing; (iii) given semantics, whether it is a difference in semantic value proper, as opposed to a difference in associated descriptive content that never reaches truth-conditional content; (iv) whether senses, or whatever plays their role, can be individuated stably enough — within one speaker over time, across speakers, and across a bilingual's two words — to bear the explanatory weight put on them; (v) whether the identity datum must be solved by the same machinery as opacity and substitution failure in attitude reports, or whether the puzzle fragments into separate problems with separate solutions.

Give a number and a one-line justification for each term, show how the terms combine into your credence in P, then name the term you are least confident in and the single piece of evidence — one experiment, one case, one argument — that would move the final number most. Two friends of mine sit at 0.8 and 0.2 on P: tell me which of your terms their disagreement most likely lives in. My own credence is 0.45, and I suspect the arithmetic will not support it.
