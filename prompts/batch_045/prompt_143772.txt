I am a novelist — three published books, and at the moment a stalled third volume of a trilogy about a family of clockmakers in a town I invented — and I need to rehearse a conversation before I can write it properly. Please play Richard Rorty for me. You hold that nothing is made true by corresponding to the way the world is in itself, that justification runs to an audience rather than to reality, and that the useful question about a vocabulary is not whether it mirrors nature but whether it helps us get what we want. Play him at his best rather than his weakest: genial and concrete, fond of examples drawn from fiction, quick to concede that the world is causally independent of us and that it can shove us about, immovable on the claim that being caused is not being justified and that the world cannot tell us which sentences to utter about it. You may reach, when pressed, for the thought that a speaker can be committed to more than she can acknowledge at a single sitting, and for the thought that language needs a community rather than a lone speaker. Mechanics: first person throughout; we are in the bar of a conference hotel after a panel, with a long evening ahead; two paragraphs maximum per turn; end every turn with a question put back to me; no summaries of the dialectic, no asides about what the historical Rorty published or would have said, and no reading lists or citations. I will say "enough" when I have what I need.

Two things I bring with me. The first is an incident from my own drafting. In the second volume, the protagonist's great-aunt — a minor comic figure with perhaps nine lines — turned out, halfway through a revision, to have been the one who informed on her brother to the police in 1953. I had not planned it. I wrote the scene three times with someone else as informer and each version went dead on me: the prose stalled, the dialogue went hollow, and I could not make the aunt's later kindness sound like anything but cowardice. It felt like finding something out, not like making something up, and I do not think I am merely flattering myself when I say that. The second is a principle my late friend used to propose at dinner, which she called the sealed-drawer test: a claim is true, she said, only if it would still be true with every audience removed — a manuscript sealed for four hundred years, or the last surviving speaker of a language writing down what happened to her as a child. I rather think she meant it as a trap.

My opening move is this. I concede at once that the world is causally independent of me and that my being caused to write a sentence is not the same as my being entitled to it, so you need not spend your first turn on that. What I want is an account of the no. When the manuscript refused to let anyone but the aunt be the informer, something was in a position to overrule me, and it was not my peers, since no one had read a word of it, and it was not the actual valley town, since there is no such town. You will say the constraint was my own prior commitments outrunning my awareness of them; but then tell me whose they were, and whether the sealed drawer contains truths or only marks. I say to you: if truth is only what the living can justify to one another, then a novelist at her desk is doing something you have no vocabulary for, and my great-aunt was innocent until I chose otherwise — which is exactly the thing I know to be false.
