Two research programmes have spent several years arguing past each other about online echo chambers. The structuralist camp holds that digitally mediated fragmentation is primarily a product problem: engagement-optimised ranking plus homophily sort users into feeds in which counter-attitudinal claims rarely surface, the resulting harms are false belief and out-group hostility, and the effective levers are architectural — ranking objectives, feed composition, friction before sharing, provenance and sourcing signals. The motivationalist camp holds that the empirical case for widespread informational isolation is weak: exposure and trace data suggest most people encounter plenty of cross-cutting content, endorsement of false claims tracks elite cues and identity more than exposure, and interventions that increase cross-cutting exposure sometimes increase hostility — so the operative mechanism is identity-protective reasoning and affective polarisation, and the levers are educational and institutional rather than architectural.

Write the joint statement these two camps could both sign at the end of a well-run adversarial collaboration. It should contain:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. Include the distinction both sides now work with — an epistemic bubble as an environment from which outside voices are absent by omission, an echo chamber as one in which outside voices are present but their credibility has been systematically undermined, so that exposure alone does not correct — and say plainly what the existing exposure-diversity evidence can and cannot establish, and under what conditions exposure interventions plausibly backfire.

2. A disagreements table. For each residual disagreement, give the structuralist formulation and the motivationalist formulation side by side, each in wording that its own side would accept rather than a paraphrase its opponent would accept.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one side. Cover at least how chamber membership should be measured given that omission and discredit require different instruments; whether persistence of a discredited claim inside a community is better predicted by what members see or by whom they trust; and whether de-ranking engagement-optimised recommendations reduces polarisation or merely relocates it.

For each crux, specify one test, study design, or case both sides would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance, including the result that would count against its own view. Two candidate designs are offered for you to refine or replace: an insider-agreement probe, in which members of a community rate the trustworthiness of an unfamiliar outside source that either restates their own side's claims, restates the opposing side's claims, or states checkable non-political facts; and a repair-response test, in which a community's signature claim is decisively refuted by a source it had previously treated as friendly, and observers record whether the community updates, reinterprets the claim, or withdraws credibility from the source.

Where part of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive, say which part and why.
