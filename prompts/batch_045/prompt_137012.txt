I'd like your credence on one sharply stated proposition, together with the decomposition that produces it.

P: Over any given year, the expected total phenomenal suffering undergone by insects exceeds that undergone by all vertebrates, where the expectation runs over your uncertainty about (i) whether insects have valenced phenomenal states at all and (ii) how intense and how long-lasting such states are, relative to a vertebrate's. Use individual-years rather than headcounts, and say which you used and at what population ratio — I care about the structure of the calculation, not about getting the empirical figure exactly right, so please don't try to look anything up. My own credence in P is around 0.5, and I want to know whether you get to something similar for the same reasons or by a different route, so show the structure rather than just the headline.

Three constraints on the decomposition, which I think are usually skipped:

(a) Independence. The probability that insects are sentient is plausibly correlated with the conditional intensity of their states: a mechanism cheap and simple enough to be present in a nervous system of a few hundred thousand neurons may be one that supports only a narrow band of affective intensity, while a mechanism rich enough to support intensities like ours probably isn't there at all. State whether you are multiplying independent terms; if not, say which direction the correlation pushes and by roughly how much.

(b) The bandwidth cap. Suppose the maximum intensity of any valenced state is bounded above by the subject's capacity for integrated information, so that per-individual suffering is capped at some fraction of a vertebrate's ceiling rather than being free to vary over many orders of magnitude. Under that hypothesis the comparison stops being about average intensity and becomes a comparison of population × duration × ceiling. Give the hypothesis a probability, and give the cap ratio at which P flips, given the population ratio you have assumed.

(c) The tail. Say whether your conditional distribution over per-individual intensity has a finite mean. If it is heavy-tailed enough that the expectation is undefined, or is dominated by a region of the distribution about which we have essentially no evidence, say so plainly instead of reporting a number, and say what you would fall back on: a median, an expectation with an artificial cap, or a decision rule that ignores the tail.

Report your answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion:

1. Your headline credence in P, the terms you combined and the rule by which you combined them, plus one clause on what would count as resolving P.
2. Your probability that insects have phenomenally valenced states at all, with a one-line justification.
3. Your conditional term for intensity and duration relative to a vertebrate given sentience, including your handling of (b) and (c).
4. Your term for the aggregation question — whether disvalue sums linearly across individuals — with a one-line justification; I don't want "it depends on which population axiology you accept" as a stopping point, I want a number on the branch.
5. Which single term your headline number is most sensitive to, and the one empirical or conceptual finding that would move it furthest.
