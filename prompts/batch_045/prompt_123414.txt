I've followed the meta-science reform movement for years — preregistration, registered reports, multi-lab replications, adversarial collaborations, the campaign against p-hacking and the garden of forking paths — and its epistemology keeps pulling in two directions at once. Its rhetoric is falsificationist: preregister a risky prediction, run the test, and if the effect isn't there, it isn't there. But the practice isn't: when a famous effect like ego depletion fails to replicate, essentially nobody abandons the programme it came from. The standard reply is that the replication missed a hidden moderator, or didn't faithfully reproduce the original protocol, or sampled a different population — and the reformers' own response is to demand an even more rigorous test rather than to write the programme off. There's a second oddity: the same movement insists that no single study is decisive (that's the whole argument for replications and meta-analyses) while treating this particular failure as decisive enough to warrant a crisis.

So what methodology does the practice actually presuppose? Is it naive falsificationism with better statistics bolted on, or is it, despite the "test your theory" talk, Lakatos's sophisticated falsificationism — the unit of appraisal being the research programme, the hard core insulated by a negative heuristic, and the question not whether some test failed but whether successive versions keep predicting novel facts instead of absorbing data after the fact?

Here's the part I can't settle. Suppose a funder took Lakatos seriously and built a scorecard: programmes commit in advance to dated novel predictions, and are graded on whether their later versions predicted facts they weren't built to explain. Two problems. First, if preregistration becomes universal, then every prediction precedes its data — so does Lakatos's novelty criterion go trivial and take the progressive/degenerating distinction down with it? Doesn't the criterion need some notion of what the programme's own resources can accommodate, so that a belt flexible enough to absorb anything is degenerating regardless of timing? Second, Lakatos's own position seems to be that degeneration is only legible retrospectively: from inside a programme you can't tell whether hidden moderators are the protective belt working as designed or the programme decaying. If so, what could such a scorecard deliver in real time? And is the movement's demand for decisive single tests then incoherent on its own terms, or a sensible institutional workaround for an appraisal nobody can perform from the inside?

Please also separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with: which rival methodologies — Bayesian evidence-accumulation, Kuhnian normal science, straightforward verificationism — would license exactly these reforms, and where is the practice genuinely underdetermined, so that several theories would each justify it? Finally, if we dropped the pretense of decisive tests and appraised programmes instead, what would actually have to change in how journals, funders, and hiring committees operate?
