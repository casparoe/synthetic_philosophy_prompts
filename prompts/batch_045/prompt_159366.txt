I run a small type foundry, and I need help turning an idea into something my team can actually use. We maintain a variable superfamily covering Latin, Greek and Cyrillic, and for the past eight months we have been contracted to extend it to a language whose orthography was standardized only in the 1990s and which has, as far as we can tell, no typographic tradition beyond a few handwritten primers and some photocopied church bulletins. Our workflow is that a generative model trained on our existing masters proposes candidate glyphs and a human reviewer accepts or rejects them: about two hundred candidates a month, under fifteen minutes per review, and the reviewers are junior designers with no training in linguistics or philosophy. A rejection costs us roughly a week of regeneration; a bad acceptance ships to readers who notice at once that the letter was drawn by someone who does not read the language. Sign-off is mine, with a two-person advisory board of native readers I can consult maybe twice a month and who do not always agree.

A friend described to me a research project on letterform design whose central claim — I only ever got it second-hand, so treat my summary as hearsay and please don't lean on knowing the source — is that what makes every 'a' an 'a' is not a list of features you can check off. Roughly: the parts of the glyph (the bowl, the stem, the aperture, the terminal) each play a role; the same role can be filled in many different ways; and a letter is identified as much by its place in a system of contrasts with the other letters of its alphabet as by anything intrinsic to it, so that an 'a' is partly what is not an 'o', not a 'd', not a 'u'. Right now my reviewers have no procedure at all — they look at a candidate and say that's not an a, and when I ask why, they gesture at the shape. I want better than that, and I want it usable by someone who has never thought about categories.

Please write me an accept/reject procedure my reviewers can run, in words, in the order the questions should be asked: what to look at first, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where a candidate is rejected outright, where it is handed on to me because the remaining question is one of judgment or house values rather than fact, and how a reviewer should separate a deviation of style from a failure of category — the difference between a candidate whose stem is straighter than ours because the model learned a different model of the skeleton, which is fine, and one that will be read as a different letter or by nobody at all. It has to survive three complications I cannot design away. First, the new script has almost no tradition: there is no settled prototype to compare against, and the tempting move of importing the Latin contrast set may simply be importing an answer. Second, the superfamily includes a micro master for ten-pixel interface labels and an accessibility master aimed at readers with dyslexia, where our 'a' is deliberately exaggerated and the ordinary tests may not apply. Third, for nearly every candidate we can run no empirical test at all: a few thousand speakers, no eye-tracking, two advisors. Say what the procedure does when it cannot get an answer from a reader, and be explicit about which steps are judgment calls wearing procedural clothing.

Then run it on three cases. First: the model's candidate 'a' for the new script — a closed bowl, a straight stem on the right, a small foot serif — where two reviewers say it reads as an 'o' with a tail, one says it is fine, and the advisory board is split. Second: a candidate where, generating for the Cyrillic codepoint, the model produced something very close to the Greek alpha our family already ships, and which reads perfectly well to a monolingual Latin reader. Third: a candidate I drew by hand myself and would like to keep — a single-storey 'a' whose bowl opens very wide where it meets the stem — which the reviewers love, and which at the micro master becomes hard to tell from both our 'o' and our 'c'; I expect the procedure to tell me something I do not want to hear about this one, and I would rather hear it. Finally, tell me plainly where the procedure will misfire and what it cannot do, and in particular whether it can ever settle the question of whether two shapes are the same letter across two different scripts, or whether that question belongs to Unicode and to the community of readers rather than to us.
