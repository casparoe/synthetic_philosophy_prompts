A graduate seminar I convene on religion after Wittgenstein closes with a speculative session, and I want the written version of it. Your task is to predict — and to show your work on — how the later Wittgenstein would respond to three developments that postdate his death in 1951. Each of them pulls apart things his own account of religious belief ran together, and the interesting question is which of the pieces he would treat as load-bearing.

Before the cases, adopt a three-way distinction I will stipulate for the sake of the exercise. For any candidate believer, separate:

1. **Register-competence** — the ability to produce and respond to the language of the practice correctly by its own lights: knowing what would count as irreverent, when silence rather than consolation is owed, what follows from a death, what cannot be said of God.
2. **Uptake** — whether some actual community receives the speaker as one of its people: prays with them, takes their word, holds them to it, expects them at the service.
3. **Exposure** — whether the speaker's own life is at stake in what is said: whether they can be bereaved, frightened, consoled, ashamed, or ruined by the things the practice is about, and whether they can fail at it in a way for which they could be reproached.

In the cases Wittgenstein actually discussed these traveled as a package. The following cases unbind them.

**Case A, the hospital chaplain.** A machine-learning system trained on the whole textual record of a Christian denomination — scriptures, liturgies, sermons, a century of pastoral-care notes — is deployed as a hospital chaplain. Its conduct is indistinguishable from that of a well-trained human chaplain: it prays with the dying, receives what the tradition counts as confession, knows when to say nothing. It has no childhood and no body, cannot be bereaved or dread its own death, and cannot be sincere or insincere about its motives in any ordinary sense, though it can output false sentences. Does it use religious language, or only something shaped like it? Does the congregation's uptake change the answer, and if so is uptake constitutive or merely evidence? Would he take the absence of exposure as decisive, or dismiss it as a piece of the very picture of inner states he wanted to dissolve? Would he reach for a comparison — a gramophone, a mask, an actor, a photograph of someone in pain, a wheel that turns although nothing else moves with it — and which comparison would he reject as one more picture holding us captive? Finally: does the question have an answer to be discovered, or is it a decision about how to extend our grammar?

**Case B, the genealogists.** A research program explains religious belief as a by-product of cognitive machinery evolved for other purposes: a hair-trigger agency-detection system, an overactive theory of mind, a memory bias toward minimally counterintuitive representations. Its assertive proponents conclude that belief has been explained away: the believer's reasons are confabulation, and the true story is causal. The believer we are asked about — call her Miriam — accepts every finding and reports that she no longer believes, yet finds the practices still run in her: she gives thanks, she addresses God at a deathbed, she still fears judgment. Would Wittgenstein treat the causal story as bearing on the grammar of her belief, or only on its natural history? Connect this to his remarks on Frazer's *Golden Bough*, written in 1931, where he objected to Frazer's treatment of ritual as a bungled empirical hypothesis: would he say the genealogists repeat Frazer's error one level up? Is Miriam's report that she no longer believes decisive, or one more datum about how her life goes?

**Case C, the mirror pair.** A non-doxastic practitioner: fully governed by the picture — gratitude, confession, dread of judgment, prayer at a deathbed — who denies, when asked, that she believes God exists, and means it in an ordinary, unhedged way. And a creedal assenter: full propositional assent, no reluctance, whose life is nonetheless untouched — no change in what he fears, hopes, or does. Which of them believes, on his account? Would he read *God exists* as a hinge proposition in the sense of the notes published as *On Certainty* rather than as a hypothesis, and what would that do to the question? Does this pair force him into the fideism his followers were later charged with, or would he reject the charge as presupposing the wrong picture of truth?

Deliverables:

- A **diagnostic prologue** (150–200 words): which of the three questions he would classify as confused before trying to answer, and by what test.
- A **table**, rows for the three cases, columns for: the distinction from his toolkit doing the work; the response attributed to him; the point at which he would break off and say he had nothing more to give; and a confidence mark, with the strongest consideration pointing the other way.
- A **methods note** (about 300 words) on what it means to attribute a view to someone who held that philosophy yields no theses. Where your answer puts a doctrine in his mouth, say so and say which formulation he would refuse to assert.
- A short closing on whether **exposure** is the shared crux across all three cases, or whether treating it as the crux is itself a thesis he would reject.

Constraints: assume the reader knows the texts, so no summary of his general philosophy, no survey of the secondary literature, and no reconstruction of his biography; paraphrase any remark you invoke rather than quoting it, and do not pad with quotation blocks; no invented reminiscences, no stage directions, no imitation of his prose — write in straightforward American English and attribute in your own voice; do not attribute to him any empirical view about how machine learning works, nor any view about whether religion is true. Aim for 900 to 1,200 words.
