Please act as a professional philosopher whose main area is material constitution and persistence, and treat what follows as a consistency audit rather than a survey of the literature. Work through the items one at a time, in your own head, before you start ranking anything.

Background: I sit on the board of a small sculpture park. One of our bronzes was badly damaged last year, and the conservator we use melted it down and recast it from the same metal in the same mould. She says nothing was lost and that it is the same work; I said it is a replica. The argument pushed me to write down what I actually think about the statue and the clay, and I would like to know whether the package holds together before I inflict it on a discussion group.

Here is where I have landed:

(1) The statue and the lump of clay (or bronze) that constitutes it are numerically distinct objects: constitution is not identity. They nevertheless share all of their matter, all of their parts, and their exact location at every moment at which both exist.

(2) What shows this is the modal argument: the lump could survive being rolled into a ball, the statue could not, identity is necessary, so there are two things here.

(3) The statue is not anything over and above the clay: no non-material constituent, no second portion of reality, no extra form-stuff. What is in that region is matter arranged statuewise, and that is the whole story of what is there.

(4) None of this is hostage to how we find it convenient to speak or classify. It is a mind-independent fact that there are two things there and that this statue could not have survived being squashed; and I want the modal facts to be grounded in non-modal ones — the matter, its arrangement, and its causal history — rather than in brute modal differences. In particular I do not want them grounded in what anyone intended, or in anything about our practices.

(5) The recast bronze is a replica, not the original: the statue went out of existence when it was melted, even though every bit of the metal survived and the result is indiscernible from the original.

(6) I am a continuist about Theseus-style cases: replace the parts one at a time, keep the form and its going-on working, and the same statue persists.

Which of these actually pull against each other, and which only look as though they do — and for those that only look that way, what dissolves the appearance? For the real tensions, order them by severity, give me the cheapest revision in each case, and say what that revision costs me elsewhere in the list. I do not know where the trouble is, so please discriminate rather than flagging everything as contested or as merely a matter of which theory you like. Finally: if I could make one revision that did the most work across the whole package, what would it be?
