I'm a PhD student in philosophy writing on metaphysical indeterminacy, and I've been mugged by an argument that someone in my department wrote up as a provocation. I've reconstructed it below, together with the two things about it that bother me, and I can't find the exit. Here it is, with the steps I take to be doing the work.

(1) Set theory as it is actually practiced is formalized in first-order logic. That is why we have a complete proof procedure and a tractable notion of logical consequence, and it is why first-order ZFC is the axiomatization mathematicians invoke.

(2) Suppose ZFC is consistent. By the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem it has a model M whose domain is countable.

(3) By Cantor's theorem, ZFC proves that no bijection exists between the natural numbers and the reals. So M satisfies the sentence that we would render as there being no bijection between the naturals and the power set of the naturals — M satisfies the claim that the reals are uncountable.

(4) M's domain is countable. So the object r that M takes to be the power set of the naturals has only countably many members by M's own lights: there are only countably many objects that M counts as elements of r. In the background there is a bijection from the genuine natural numbers onto them.

(5) Hence a structure can satisfy every sentence of ZFC — can be, as far as the axioms can tell, the entire universe of sets — while the thing it calls the reals has only countably many elements.

(6) It follows that our sentence about the reals does not record an absolute fact about how many reals there are. What it excludes is only a bijection of the relevant sort existing inside the model. Cardinality talk in set theory is therefore relative to a model: nothing about the size of the continuum is pinned down by what we say.

(7) The mechanism is not special to cardinality. By compactness, no first-order theory pins down the natural numbers; by the Löwenheim–Skolem–Tarski theorem, no consistent first-order theory with an infinite model pins down the cardinality of its domain; and well-foundedness is not first-order axiomatizable at all. So natural number, finite and well-founded are relative in exactly the same way.

(8) Therefore our mathematical language, at least as formalized, does not determine its own subject matter. There is no fact of the matter, fixed by our usage and our axioms, about how many reals there are, and none about whether the continuum hypothesis is true.

Two things bother me. First, the argument looks like it proves too much. Steps (2), (4) and (7) themselves quantify over all models, all bijections and all cardinals, and they use an apparently absolute notion of countable and of the genuine natural numbers. But if (7) and (8) are correct, then the background theory in which the argument is conducted is itself a first-order theory with models of every infinite cardinality and with nonstandard natural numbers, so countable is relative there too — and then it looks as if the argument cannot so much as be stated in the form that made it compelling. It would follow, absurdly, that the theorem invoked in (2) does not say anything determinate. I can't tell whether this self-referential awkwardness is a real refutation or just a dialectical embarrassment that a relativist can live with by treating the metatheory as a fixed home theory or as a schema.

Second, I don't accept (8), and I don't know which step to give up. A logician in my cohort says the move from (3) to (4) quietly needs M to be well-founded so that it can be collapsed to a transitive model, and that the existence of such a model is strictly stronger than the consistency of ZFC, so my assumption in (2) doesn't deliver it. Someone else says the real moral is that the claim that a given set is uncountable is not absolute — that it has a logical complexity that makes it preserved downward but not upward across transitive models — so this is a failure of absoluteness, not a crisis about reference. I sort of follow that, but I don't see how it is supposed to answer (7) and (8), which generalize past the case of a single set.

What I would most like to keep, in descending order: Cantor's theorem and the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem themselves; the claim that there is a fact of the matter about the continuum hypothesis and that uncountable means something absolute; and the view that first-order ZFC is the right formalization of what set theorists actually do, so that I am not driven into full second-order semantics just to escape this argument.
