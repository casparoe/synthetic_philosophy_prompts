I chair the ethics committee at a large teaching hospital, and most of what we actually do is broker what our policy calls accommodation plans: packages that let a clinical team and a family who disagree about what must be done go forward together. We handle about thirty a year. Each one has to be assembled inside a single two-hour meeting, by a rotating group of clinicians, a chaplain, a lawyer and two lay members, and each needs the attending physician's signature and the family's before it goes in the chart.

The justification we give ourselves is gains from trade: the two sides care about different things, so a package can leave each better off by its own lights than either would be after losing a fight. I have come to think that many of our plans are what I now call phantom trades — packages both sides prefer to losing outright, but where at least one side's gain is not a gain by its own lights, only the other side's description of one — and that others load their costs onto the patient, who is not at the table and cannot consent. I also think we routinely mishandle currency mismatch: what a family needs is sometimes not a policy concession at all but recognition, a sequencing of events, or a ritual, and we keep offering things denominated in a currency they cannot spend.

Write me a decision procedure we can run in the room: the questions to ask about a proposed accommodation, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the point at which the procedure returns a no and we stop negotiating; and the points at which it has to hand the question to a person because it is a question of value rather than fact. Three things it must handle. Where it prices a concession by how intensely each side wants what it wants, tell us how to treat intensity that is cheap to fake or that rests on an empirical belief we take to be false. Where a concession would move the baseline for the next case, so that a series of locally acceptable trades is globally a loss for one side, say how to count that. And our clinicians distinguish sharply between being the one who does something and letting it happen, even when the outcome is the same; the procedure should not treat those as interchangeable.

Then run it on three of our recurring cases: a time-limited trial of intensive treatment for an infant the team judges to be dying, with a stop date fixed in advance and palliative care layered in from the start; a Jehovah's Witness family's request for surgery without transfusion, with the team offering cell salvage and volume expanders instead; and a plan we have used for years and are rather proud of, under which a seventeen-year-old who refuses a recommended transfusion may refuse, provided she signs a document recording that she understands she may die. I would rather not be told that the first or the third is a phantom trade, but tell me if the procedure says so.

Close by saying what the procedure cannot do. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the procedure and the applications. No preamble and no restatement of what I asked.
