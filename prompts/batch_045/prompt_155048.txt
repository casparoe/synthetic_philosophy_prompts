Longtime reader here. In Plato's Laws 1, the Athenian proposes supervised drunkenness as a test of character, and at 650b he concludes: "This then—the discovery of the natures and conditions of men's souls—will prove one of the things most useful to that art whose task it is to treat them; and that art is (as I presume we say) the art of politics: is it not so?" I am trying to read this the way a rationalist would read a proposed low-cost experiment: wine is supposed to be a safe, cheap, and fast stress test that reveals a latent disposition of the soul by temporarily disabling the golden cord of calculation and letting the harder cords of appetite and spirit operate unchecked. Is that the right way to reconstruct the argument—as an ancient version of load-testing or adversarial testing for virtue, where the value is high signal about stable underlying parameters at low risk? Or does the passage quietly depend on a stronger, more doubtful assumption: that there is a single stable nature or condition of the soul that the test measures, rather than drunk behavior being just another context-sensitive sample that we overfit into a story about the person's real self? I would also like to know how the cheapness, security, and speed praised at 650b fits with the earlier claim that no fear-inducing drug exists and that wine instead produces fearlessness and excessive confidence: if wine mostly inflates confidence rather than simulating real fear, in what sense is it a valid test of courage rather than merely a test of how someone handles lowered inhibition or inflated status? Please explain the argument as Plato intends it, and then say where a modern rationalist should accept it, refine it, or resist it.
