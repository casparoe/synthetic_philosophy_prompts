I have been asked to take a ninety-minute graduate seminar on Kavka's toxin puzzle. The group is mixed: mostly MPhil and PhD students in economics and psychology, with two philosophers sitting in. They are at home with expected utility, time inconsistency and dual-process models of judgement; none of them has done any action theory, and I expect them to reach for economic vocabulary first. Do not give me the seminar, and do not give me a summary of the literature or a reading list: give me the architecture of the session.

The set-up I plan to use, stated in my own words: a benefactor will pay you a million pounds if, at midnight tonight, you intend to drink tomorrow morning a dose of a toxin that will make you thoroughly wretched for a day and do no lasting harm. The money is transferred at midnight on the strength of the intention alone; the benefactor's detection of it is not your problem. Whether you drink the following morning is entirely up to you, and you know now that you will not drink it, since by then the money is already in your account and nothing else recommends the dose.

What I want from you:

1. The order in which the components have to be introduced, and what depends on what. My instinct is that nobody will hear this as a puzzle until intending has been prised apart from predicting what you will do, and until it is explicit that the case contains no change of preference, no temptation, no uncertainty about the future and no commitment device lying around. Tell me what has to be on the board before the fork appears, and why each item belongs where you put it rather than fifteen minutes later.

2. Whether to carry the session on the toxin itself or on a sanitised substitute. The toxin brings luggage: students will wonder whether you might drink it out of curiosity, hardness, self-punishment or a taste for the experience, and that noise is precisely what the no-reason clause is meant to exclude. A milder substitute weakens the clause that you know you will not perform the act. Recommend the carrying case, and say what it must contain and what it must lack.

3. The misconceptions, in the order this particular group will produce them, and the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off rather than merely answered. The ones I anticipate: reading the case as weakness of will or hyperbolic discounting; proposing a commitment device; treating intention as strong desire or as an avowal one can simply issue; concluding that the rational agent must therefore engage in self-deception or fool the machine; and the sharp version of the objection, that of course one can intend what one knows one will not do, since people intend to take up exercise every January. Say which of these are worth taking seriously as partial insights and which are category errors, and where in the arc each belongs.

4. Two variants I have devised, which I want to use as diagnostic instruments rather than as further puzzles. In the serial variant the same offer is made every week for a term, so that there is now a standing reason to be the sort of person who sometimes follows through. In the graded variant the benefactor's mechanism reads the strength of your intention at midnight and pays in proportion, so that a half-hearted intention is worth half a million. For each, tell me which of the two rival diagnoses it isolates — the diagnosis that the agent cannot intend what she knows she will not do, and the diagnosis that she merely has no reason, or no reason of the right kind, to form that intention — and where in the session, if anywhere, each should appear.

5. The check for understanding I should end on, and what a good answer to it would look like as opposed to an answer that merely sounds confident.

6. What to leave out at this level and to say explicitly that I am leaving out.

Finally, flag any point at which my set-up as stated already settles a question I ought to leave open: I would rather be told that a stipulation is doing argumentative work than discover it from the room.
