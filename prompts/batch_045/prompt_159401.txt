I run a small reading group on the ethics of artificial minds, and I keep losing the same argument to a friend who works in machine learning. His move is always the same analogy: a mind is to a brain as software is to hardware. The program is what does the thinking, the machine is merely what it runs on, and it is therefore incidental whether the computation is implemented in neurons or in silicon. He rides this to a strong conclusion: once the non-player characters in a game are implemented richly enough, the fact that they run on a console rather than in a skull is morally irrelevant, and so deliberately tormenting one of them could be a genuine wrong to it and not merely a matter of the player's character, of the aesthetic value of the work, or of the effects on human players.

The case we argue about is deliberately awkward, and it is the part I want you to take most seriously. Imagine a studio ships a game in which the NPCs are not scripted at all: each one's behaviour comes from a policy trained by reinforcement learning, and the training objective contained a penalty term keyed to an internally generated signal that the engineers informally call distress. Every NPC retains persistent memory across sessions, maintains a running model of its own body and of the other agents around it, and continues to be updated by its interactions with players after release. Crucially, nobody wrote a distress routine, nobody specified what any internal state is supposed to represent, and there is no layer of the system at which anything was designed to mean anything. My friend says this makes no difference: it is still software, still multiply realizable, still substrate-independent. I suspect the analogy quietly depends on the software having been authored by someone with intentions, on there being a specification or interpreter that fixes what the states mean, and on the program being the sort of thing that could in principle be ported intact to another machine — and that a matrix of learned weights may not be.

Take the role of a philosopher of mind who also knows how modern ML systems are actually built. Before committing yourself, brainstorm the candidate correspondences and discard the ones that obviously fail. Then give me the audit: an explicit mapping of what plays the role of the program, the machine, the instruction set, the programmer, the user, and the act of running; the disanalogies, sorted into those that matter for the moral conclusion and those that do not; an account of which features of the software-hardware relation the analogy imports into the mind-brain relation that the latter may lack (authorship, teleology, clean layering, determinate semantics, portability, debuggability); and a verdict on whether the conclusion my friend wants survives once those imports are made explicit. Say whether the verdict changes when the software is learned rather than written, whether the same program on different hardware is even a well-defined notion here, and whether you can offer a better analogy for a trained policy than the one he is using. Close by telling me what would change your mind about this case and where your own reasoning is most likely to have gone wrong — in particular whether you have smuggled in a theory of mental content rather than argued for one.
