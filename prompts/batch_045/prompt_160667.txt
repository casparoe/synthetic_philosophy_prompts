I've been trying to work out what I actually think about escalation -- when to keep pouring money and time into something that's going badly -- because I run a small software studio and I make these calls all the time, and my gut verdicts don't seem to line up with the rule I hand out to everyone else.

The line I give my team: everything already spent is gone, ignore it completely, and ask only whether the next dollar buys more than a dollar of value from here. I believe that line. But here is how I actually rule when the cases show up.

1. We're eight months into a platform migration, maybe 70 percent done, and finishing it will cost less than the new platform is worth to us. Keep going, obviously.
2. Same migration, except we've just learned the platform we're moving to is being discontinued in eighteen months. Kill it today, even though eight months of work evaporates. My rule agrees here, so far so good.
3. The dollar auction -- the party game where a one dollar bill is auctioned off and both the top bidder and the runner-up pay their last bid, so the winner gets the dollar and the loser pays for nothing. I'm the top bidder at 95 cents, the other person is at 90. My verdict: I bid a dollar, and I think that's right; handing over 95 cents for nothing feels worse than paying a dollar for a dollar. But here's the part I can't square with my own rule -- I only feel that way if I can make exactly one more bid and then stop. If I can't commit to stopping, I'd rather walk away at 95.
4. A product line that loses money every month with no route to profit. Shutting it down means laying off two people and dropping three integration partners, and if we ever wanted back in, rebuilding those relationships would cost more than a year of losses. Verdict: keep it alive at minimum cost, indefinitely. That isn't the next dollar buying value. It's the next dollar buying optionality.
5. A test that's been running six weeks and shows nothing. One more month wouldn't change the product, but it would tell us whether a whole research direction is dead or just slow. Verdict: spend the month. The money buys information, not product.
6. A friend five years into a PhD she doesn't like, two years from the end, who would use the degree: finish. Same friend, but she wants to open a restaurant and would never use the degree: quit. I'm confident about both, and I think the reason is the credential being a real forward-looking asset, not the five years.
7. Two verdicts that feel different to me and I can't say why. (a) I inherit a team, their flagship project is a dud, I kill it inside a week. (b) If I had started that same project myself, I suspect I'd want to give it one more quarter. I'm not proud of that difference. Is the right principle different depending on who made the original call?
8. Something I've noticed in myself with money: I'll sit on a losing position for months, but once I've sold it I won't buy it back at the same price, even though the exposure is identical. I'm fairly sure that's just a bug in me and not a principle.
9. Auction for a house I love: I blow past the ceiling I set for myself, because the other bidder showed me how much I actually want it. Verdict: fine, keep bidding. But if I were raising my bid mainly because I'd already spent four grand on surveys and legal fees, that's the fallacy. Same outward behavior, different reasons, opposite verdicts -- and my stated rule only looks at the forward-looking part, so it can't see the difference at all.

So: is there one principle that gets all of these, or nearly all? I'd rather have two or three stated precisely, each with the case that motivates its clause, than one vague thing that technically covers everything. Tell me which of my verdicts a given principle forces me to drop -- I'm guessing 3 and 7b, though maybe 4 and 5 aren't really escalation at all and shouldn't be on the same list.

Then the live decisions, which aren't in my list. One: a project where the next increment is cheap, the thing is probably dead, and the team's morale depends on not having been wrong. Two: a feature we publicly promised a client by December and now think was the wrong thing to build, where backing out is possible but expensive for the relationship. What does the principle say about each?

And the question I actually care about most: if sunk money shouldn't matter but I know it will matter to me, is it rational to tie my own hands? We now require a written 'continue' memo signed by someone who wasn't in the kickoff before anything passes its second checkpoint, and I've thought about literally paying a colleague two hundred dollars for a veto over my own decision to continue. Does any sane principle endorse that, or does it just admit the principle is really about decision procedures rather than about what to do?

If you want to name cases that would separate the candidate rules: a dollar auction where I'm already at three dollars and the other guy is at 2.95 -- my cap-the-loss-with-one-more-bid instinct says escalate, but does it, and at what point does that stop being sane? And a sealed version of case 3 where each of us writes down one number instead of bidding in the open, with the top two paying.
