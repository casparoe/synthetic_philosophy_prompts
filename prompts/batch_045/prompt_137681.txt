I am giving a paper next week to a joint session of the economic history seminar and the comparative-historical sociology workshop — about fifteen people, thirty minutes of talk and twenty of questions. Title: "Nothing Is Locked In: Against the Rhetoric of Path Dependence."

My thesis: the canonical lock-in stories — the keyboard layout, the videocassette format war, railway gauge, and the institutional stories told in the "why some regions and not others" register — establish at most that outcomes are historically sensitive, not that history is locked in. What actually gets locked in, I argue, is a coordination equilibrium within a functional class, not the particular technology or arrangement: for every allegedly locked-in outcome there is a 'functional shadow', a nearby counterfactual path that terminates in a materially different arrangement doing the same job at comparable cost. So the well-posed question is not whether the same arrangement would have arrived anyway (usually not) but whether the same functional outcome would have (usually yes). To keep that from collapsing into vacuity I add the 'migration test': a lock-in is genuine only if, at no point along the path, a historically admissible coalition — agents who actually existed, with the information and incentives they had, and with no exogenous shock — could have moved the system to a functionally superior arrangement. Crucially, the test must be failed at every node of the path, not merely at the origin.

The audience: mostly economic historians steeped in difference-in-differences and natural experiments, who will distrust any counterfactual I cannot identify; three comparative-historical sociologists who think of themselves as mechanism people and will care whether my account can discriminate between real cases; and two philosophers of social science who work on causation, counterfactuals and scientific modeling, and who will press on what kind of possibility the phrase "historically admissible" is doing.
