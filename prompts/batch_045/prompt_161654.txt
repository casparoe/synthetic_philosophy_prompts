I am helping a small foundation allocate roughly fifteen researcher-years over five years to the study of religion as a natural phenomenon: explaining religious belief, practice, and institutions with the same tools one would use for any other recurrent human pattern, never appealing to the truth of religious claims as an explanatory resource. The aim is that the work be usable within a decade by people who make real decisions — public-health officials deciding whether to route programmes through congregations, courts adjudicating religious exemptions, educators designing curricula about religion — rather than merely interesting to specialists.

Give me a defended agenda: three or four open questions in priority order, with the reasoning doing most of the work. For each, say what other questions it is upstream of, how tractable it is with methods that exist now rather than methods that would require a breakthrough, and what specific result in the next five years would move it up or down the ranking. Then say which currently fashionable questions you would decline to fund, and why, on the ground that their answers would change no one's mind and no one's practice.

Two constraints on your answer. First, treat the following as a live hypothesis about the field rather than as a methodological footnote: that its flagship findings were largely established on populations that are outliers by global and historical standards — wealthy, literate, post-Protestant, high-trust — so that some of what presents itself as a theory of religion is really a description of one unusual slice of it; if that is right, tell me which questions it re-ranks, and whether it makes any currently popular question unanswerable with the populations we can actually reach. Second, take a position on a distinction I want to impose: between questions about why human minds are receptive to religious representations at all, and questions about why particular religious packages spread, stabilise, outcompete, and decline. My suspicion is that the second family is far more decision-relevant while the field's prestige sits with the first; tell me whether that is right, and what would have to be true for the first family to deserve more of the money than the second.

Lead with your bottom line — the ordered list — in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
