I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on consciousness, and the week we cover illusionism I usually screen a film rather than assign another paper; this year I want to use Christopher Nolan's Memento (2000), because Leonard Shelby's anterograde amnesia looks like a made-to-order case for the user-illusion picture — the metaphor Daniel Dennett takes up from Tor Nørretranders's The User Illusion, on which conscious experience is a radically lossy desktop rather than a report on what the nervous system is doing — and also for the stronger illusionist claim associated with Keith Frankish and with Dennett's own work, that the apparent inner show is not merely simplified but in an important sense not there. I'd like a reading that takes both the film and the philosophical positions seriously, and that doesn't treat the film as a decoration for a view the students could get from the readings: assume your reader already knows the realism/illusionism dispute, and tell me where the film actually does argumentative work.

The distinction I want to organize the discussion around is between what I'll call the interface claim (experience is a simplified, user-friendly rendering of processes it does not report) and the user claim (there is no unified subject to whom the rendering is presented; the user is a posit of the system, something like Dennett's center of narrative gravity). My hunch, and I want it tested rather than flattered, is that Memento gives us something the papers don't have a slot for: Leonard's external prostheses — the Polaroids, the annotated photos, the tattoos, the habits and clothes he wakes up in — don't so much record a persisting self as manufacture the next one, so that the film depicts the user as something the system must keep re-rendering, a desktop redrawn each boot from its own leftover icons. If that's right, the film offers a third option: a user that is fully real but manufactured and externally maintained, rather than either a discovered inner subject or a mere illusion. Is that a coherent position, or does it collapse into one of the standard two? Does it help the illusionist or quietly remove the film from the illusionist's evidence base? Relatedly, Leonard retains an enormous amount of processing with the integration gone — he speaks, drives, fights, forms habits, can be conditioned — so I want to know whether the honest description of his case is a subject with no user at all or a user compressed into a few minutes, and whether anything in the dispute between realists and illusionists turns on which of those we say.

I also want the places where the reading fails, since I'd rather hand students an honest no than a forced yes. The Sammy Jankis material seems to me the crux: the ambiguity about whether Sammy is faking or genuinely failing, and the question of whether the clinic's conditioning test is measuring something felt or merely something trained, looks at first like a dramatization of the illusionist point that there is no deep fact behind report and behavior — but I worry it is really dramatizing the collapse of testimony and self-trust, which is a different thesis entirely. And the ending worries me in the opposite direction: once Teddy has told him the truth and Leonard burns the photographs and writes himself a knowingly false fact to aim himself at a new target, is the film vindicating the user illusion — we all maintain a partly fabricated interface, and that maintenance is what a person is — or is it staging a moral failure that only makes sense if there is a real agent there who could have stopped? I'd add the film's reverse-ordered color sequences, which put the viewer in a mild version of Leonard's epistemic position, and ask whether that structural device is doing philosophical work or is just rhetorical. Finally: which three or four scenes would you put on a handout for students to re-watch with these questions in mind, and what, if anything, does the film let them see about the lived stakes of this debate that argumentative prose doesn't?
