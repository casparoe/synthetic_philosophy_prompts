I'm a software engineer by training and I run a fortnightly internal reading group at the company I work for — fifteen or so engineers, all comfortable with probability and Bayes' rule, none with any economics or philosophy. I have forty minutes, a whiteboard and no slides, and I have to get across why a group of people each reasoning sensibly from their own evidence can settle on the wrong option and stay there: the information cascade case, as against herding that has some other explanation.

I don't want the explanation written out for me; I want a plan for delivering it. Specifically:

1. The order the components have to be introduced in, and what depends on what. My instinct is that "nobody here is being irrational" has to land before the pathology does, but I'm unsure where the sequential structure — people committing one after another, seeing predecessors' choices but not the evidence behind them — belongs relative to that, and whether the point that some evidence is private and some public needs to be explicit before anything else can be said.

2. The one example that should carry the whole session. I was going to use the urn setup and actually run it live: two urns with different colour majorities, one chosen by a coin flip nobody sees, each person privately draws one marble and then publicly commits to a guess, and we compare with the truth at the end. Tell me what an example like this has to have and what it has to avoid. I can see it must not have payoffs that depend on what other people choose, but I don't know what else would quietly contaminate it. And say whether the run-it-in-the-room version is better or worse than a worked example on the board.

3. The misconceptions, the order they will arrive in, and the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. The ones I'm expecting: "this is just groupthink, i.e. irrationality"; "the larger the crowd, the more evidence the group's decision rests on"; "if the cascade is on the wrong option, somebody's private evidence will eventually overturn it"; and a slide from "cascades are fragile" to "cascades self-correct". Tell me too which misconception I'm not anticipating.

4. Whether the analogy I was going to open with is a help or a trap: private evidence as writes that never reach the shared log, so the group ends up branching off a couple of commits' worth of information while everyone keeps producing more that goes nowhere. If it bakes in an error, say which one, and where I should put it instead.

5. What the audience should be able to do at the end, and a check I can run in the last five minutes that separates people who have the mechanism from people who have taken away only "crowds can be wrong and copying is bad". I'd like them to be able to look at a case from our own work — everyone adopting an internal framework after a well-known team did — and say whether it is a cascade, a case where the payoffs genuinely depend on converging with others, or a case about avoiding blame; and to say what would have to change in the setup for the group to do better. I don't want the session to become a talk about how to run meetings, though, so tell me where that belongs, if anywhere.

6. What I should explicitly decline to go into at this level, and say out loud that I'm declining it.

Please answer in British English.
