I sit on the standing committee that decides which research fields qualify for our national research foundation's long-term programme grants, and I have to draft the recommendation on the case we vote on next month. I would like the reasoning set out before I write it, and I am less interested in a general essay on demarcation than in a prediction about this case, the rules in play, and the evidence that would settle them.

Our precedents, with the notes our secretary kept on what seemed to move the committee:

1. *Tectonic stress mapping* (approved, 2004). Precise quantitative predictions; three of them failed; on each occasion the assumptions about rock plasticity in the models were revised, and each revision also implied a further, previously unexpected prediction that was then confirmed in independent data.

2. *Motivational field theory* (rejected, 2009). No observation could be described that would count against its central claim; every apparent failure was explained by the subject's or the researcher's unacknowledged resistance.

3. *The carrier framework* in systems ecology (approved, 2012). Its central claim is unfalsifiable: the quantity it says is optimised is defined by the optimisation, so the claim is true by construction. The committee explicitly declined to hold this against it, on the grounds that the framework has generated a long succession of falsifiable satellite models, many confirmed, and that a methodological convention in the field reserves the core from test.

4. *Ambient field epidemiology* (rejected, 2016). Its claims are sharply falsifiable and it has never been refuted in some thirty studies; but each study was small, the outcome variable was re-specified after inspection of the data, no study reported power, and the reported effect sizes have drifted toward zero. The note reads: the claims have never been put in jeopardy.

5. *The predictive phenology network* (approved 2019, with conditions). Registered its predictions in advance in a public registry, ran adequately powered tests, survived two failures by revising a secondary parameter, and preregistered the revised predictions before the next round of data came in.

6. *Coherence thermodynamics* (rejected, 2021). A well-institutionalised field: its own journal, a textbook, a conference series, shared exemplars, agreed puzzles. The committee found that its central quantity had never been measured independently of the effect it was introduced to explain, and that no test in its literature had a serious chance of failing.

The committee has never stated a general criterion. There is only a 2011 guidance memo saying that a field is scientific to the extent that its claims can conflict with observation and its practitioners treat sustained conflict as a reason for revision, and that revision of auxiliary assumptions is permitted except where the revision does no more than accommodate the data already in hand. Two members describe themselves as following quite different methodological traditions and both claim the precedents vindicate them; I suspect the memo is compatible with more readings than either admits, and that some readings available here belong to neither tradition.

The case before us is *phase-lattice dynamics*, an interdisciplinary programme of about two hundred active researchers, fifteen years old:

- Its core, the lattice postulate, is stipulative by the field's own founding convention; graduate students are taught that no observation bears on it, and members who have tried to test it are told they have mis-specified the mapping from theory to data.
- It has produced some forty belt models. When a model fails, practitioners re-specify the coupling kernel, a functional form they themselves describe as having on the order of thirty effective degrees of freedom, which is enough to fit any dataset of the kind the field collects.
- The re-specified kernels do have content beyond the data that prompted them: each predicts something not used in the fit. But the predictions are qualitative and directional, tested on samples of twenty to forty, with flexible exclusion rules and no power analysis. Roughly seventy per cent are reported as confirmed.
- A public prediction registry was introduced in 2019. In the preregistered subset the directional hit rate is nine of twenty-six, which is about chance; unregistered predictions still come out near seventy per cent.
- Two large multi-lab replications of its flagship effect failed in 2022. The community's response was to invoke site-specific coupling.
- Two of its belt models are in routine engineering use and do work, within a narrow and well-characterised domain.

What I want from you: the candidate rules that fit all six of our precedents jointly, stated sharply enough to disagree with each other; what each predicts for phase-lattice dynamics and with what confidence; your best guess at the committee's vote, with the reasons for preferring the reading of the memo you rely on; which precedent is the closest analog and why, and, where our precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, what principle we would be adopting under each option; and finally the single further case or piece of evidence that would best separate the surviving rules, with a statement of what each rule says about it. Please argue from the cases and the memo rather than from anything you would need to look up.
