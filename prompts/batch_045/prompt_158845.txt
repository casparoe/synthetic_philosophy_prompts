I have no formal training in philosophy and I run a fortnightly discussion evening at my public library: usually 14 to 18 adults, mixed ages and occupations, two hours in a room with a whiteboard, no homework and no assigned reading. I first met the gavagai puzzle in a popular book and I want to build one evening around it, but as something people do instead of something I explain. I would rather not even say the word indeterminacy until the final fifteen minutes.

Please design a runnable activity for that session. My constraints and hopes:

- One person plays the speaker of an unknown language and the rest try to construct a translation manual. Nothing beyond paper, index cards, and the whiteboard.
- The reaction I want them to walk into is the obvious objection to the standard example: that a real fieldworker would simply ask more questions. So include a round in which questioning is genuinely unlimited, in any form they like, with the speaker answering, and arrange the rules so that the extra evidence still fails to settle what the word picks out. I have in mind two or three manuals that fit everything observable forever: one whole-object, one mereological or stage-like, possibly one wildly scattered. Nobody should be told which the speaker is really using, because on the view I am trying to stage there is no such fact. Tell me how to make that felt rather than stated.
- If a version of the same move can be turned back onto English, so that it stops looking like a fact about exotic languages or careless fieldworkers, I would like it, but only if it actually lands in a room like mine.
- I also want a moment where the question of which manual is correct has some small stake attached: a score, a bet, a commitment made in writing, so that people notice they must choose with nothing to choose between.

Then give me the debrief: what to say and in what order, mapped onto specific moments that will have occurred; what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, including the participant who tries to game the rules, the one who demands the speaker's inner intentions, and the one who decides this is just skepticism about other minds and checks out; how to handle each; and an honest account of which part of the argument the activity cannot generate and I will have to supply in my own words.
