I'm putting together a short position paper for a reading group on the Everett interpretation, and my supervisor's comment on the draft was that it "reads like three different people wrote it," which I take to be a complaint even though I can't find the seam. So I want an audit of the package before I rewrite it. Here is where I've landed, stated as flatly as I can: (1) No collapse. The wave function evolves unitarily, every term of a superposition is realized, and the appearance of a definite outcome is explained by decoherence together with the fact that each copy of the observer ends up correlated with one result. (2) Nothing in any of this is objectively chancy. Since every outcome occurs, there is no propensity for one outcome rather than another to happen, so whatever the Born rule says, it is a norm: a constraint on rational credence or rational preference, not a physical mechanism. (3) The decision-theoretic derivations are basically right, and the objection that they are incoherent misfires. Concretely: impose rationality constraints on an agent's preferences over acts whose payoffs depend on measurement outcomes — transitivity, some dominance principle, some indifference between an act and the act that gives you the same payoff in every branch — and the only admissible way to weight outcomes is by squared amplitude. The objection that this is pointless because the agent knows every outcome will occur seems to me to confuse the agent's situation with the multiverse's; she is choosing for the copy of herself in a branch, and the payoffs to that copy are what she has a stake in. (4) The uncertainty reading is also right, and I don't want to give it up as mere metaphor: before the experiment I genuinely do not know which result I will see, and this is real self-locating uncertainty, structurally like not knowing which day it is in Sleeping Beauty. I want to be able to say that I have a one-half chance of seeing spin-up and mean by it something more than a report of my own betting policy. (5) I hold a broadly Parfit-style view of fission: branching is not death, I have no reason to fear it as such, and my concern for a future copy of myself is not diluted by the fact that there will be other copies; what matters is how things go for the people there will be, counted equally. (6) Parsimony is why I am an Everettian at all, and I insist that the Born rule be recovered rather than postulated — bolting on the squared-amplitude rule as a separate axiom would give back everything I took the interpretation to avoid. (7) The Born rule governs single cases, not merely long runs: betting at Born odds on a one-shot, never-repeated experiment is rationally required and not just sound long-run policy. And the confirmation story runs through typicality: what licenses belief in quantum mechanics is that, weighted appropriately, the overwhelming majority of branches exhibit Born-rule statistics, so finding myself in a branch with wildly deviant frequencies really would be evidence against the theory.

Here is the case that made me suspicious. Call it the duplication machine: run a measurement with Born weights of one-half and one-half, but then, in the down branch only and before any observer looks at the result, apply a further process that splits that branch into nine exact duplicate sub-branches — nine copies of the same environment, the same apparatus, the same observer, the same everything — with the branch's total amplitude divided equally among them, so that the amplitude measure is untouched: each sub-branch has weight one-eighteenth, summing to one-half. All ten branches then continue in parallel and the observers look. My verdict is that the right credence in seeing down is still one-half and not nine-tenths, and that a bet on down should not become nine times more attractive. But as far as I can tell, (4) as I have stated it is a rule about spreading credence over the branches or copies there will be, and taken at face value it delivers nine-tenths — so my verdict is being produced by something I have not written down, and I would like to know what.

Three objections from friends I think are wrong but cannot fully rebut, in case they are where the trouble is. First, that (2) dissolves the problem by stipulation: if probability is only a norm on credence, there was never a theorem to prove, and (3) is theater. Second, that (6) is in bad faith: to state the Born-rule problem I need a preferred basis, decoherence, a branching structure and a measure, which is at least as much extra machinery as a collapse postulate, so Everettianism is not the austere package I signed up for. Third, that (3) is redundant, because the numbers are forced anyway — the kind of uniqueness result people cite says that any assignment of weights to the outcomes of all possible measurements that adds across exclusive alternatives has to be the squared-amplitude measure, so structure hands us the measure and only its interpretation is left over. Could you go through the seven theses and tell me which pairs actually pull against each other and how badly, which of my friends' objections I can simply reject, and what the cheapest repair would be in each case? I am attached to all seven, which is presumably the problem, so please rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equals, be specific about which thesis has to move, and tell me whether there is a single revision that cleans up the most.
