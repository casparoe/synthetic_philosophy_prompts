I am a novelist, and for some years I have been writing a single long novel in four interwoven books. Each book retells the same decade in the lives of the same nine characters; the books differ from one another roughly as a story differs from a draft of itself. The cast carries the same names across the books, but the divergence is not cosmetic. In the second book Madeleine is born in a different city to different parents. In the third, the betrayal that structures the plot is committed by someone else, and the woman with all of Madeleine's history and temperament is a bystander to it. In the fourth there are two women, Cécile and Céline, whom no reader can tell apart and who are equally similar to the Madeleine of the first book. And in the first book there is a late disclosure that the governess and Madeleine are one woman, a disclosure the third book never makes.

My readers and my critics disagree, with great confidence, about which of these women is really Madeleine. I have come to think that what they are doing is metaphysics: that each of them is tacitly taking a position on whether the same individual can figure in more than one possible world, or whether individuals belong to one world only and are merely represented elsewhere by something very like them. What I want from you is an instrument that sorts them — a decision tree, or a short quiz with a scoring key, whichever does the work better — that begins from what someone says about my characters and ends at a named position in that dispute.

Please observe the following.

First, the audience. The instrument is for intelligent readers of fiction with no philosophical training, so every branch node must be a question about one of my cases that such a reader could answer without knowing what the answers are called. No node may simply ask whether two characters are the same person; sameness is what is in dispute. The nodes have to ask about something a reader can settle independently — whether a given variation is one the story could have contained, whether a claim is about Madeleine herself or about someone very like her, whether there is a fact of the matter at all, and so on. Eight or nine nodes, no more.

Secondly, the cases. Six must be used: the changed origin; the betrayal committed by somebody else while the woman with Madeleine's traits stands aside; the indiscernible pair, Cécile and Céline, both inside a single book; a variant differing only on the far side of the galaxy, in which Madeleine is qualitatively unchanged; a non-transitive chain, where the second book's Madeleine is very like the first's and the fourth's very like the second's while the first and the fourth share nothing recognisable; and the disclosure, made in one book and withheld in another, that the governess and Madeleine are one woman. You may add a variant in which Madeleine is never born, or one or two cases of your own, if they separate views mine leave together.

Thirdly, build the tree out of the logical space rather than out of a list of names. Start from the questions and the answer-patterns they permit; then place the positions that are actually held into that space; then tell me which combinations nothing occupies, and whether each is empty because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has got round to it.

Fourthly, these distinctions must be doing work somewhere in the tree: whether the cross-book relation can hold between two characters inside a single book or only across books; whether a difference in which one it is, with no qualitative difference whatsoever, can matter; whether the relation is fixed by similarity and how similarities are to be weighted, intrinsic traits against the part a character plays in the plot; whether the books are things of the same kind as the world we occupy or representations of it; whether the modal facts are primitive rather than derived from books and characters at all; whether a cross-book identity claim can itself hold in one book and fail in another; and whether the relation has to be transitive.

Fifthly, the leaves: between six and eight. Give a leaf the name the literature gives it where there is one, a plain one-sentence summary where there is not, and say plainly when a label is contested. I want at least: a leaf for concrete worlds with no individual in more than one of them and similarity, weighted by what matters, doing the work; a leaf for world-bound individuals where the worlds are representations rather than concrete; leaves for the same individual appearing in several worlds, split by whether bare which-one differences can matter; and a leaf for the view that nothing here needs analysing because the modal facts are primitive.

Sixthly, where two leaves cannot be separated by anything in the tree, say so, and say whether the difference between them is real. I have in mind similarity-based counterparts on the one hand and identity across worlds with nothing riding on which individual it is on the other: it looks to me as though a counterpart relation that weighted bare identity as heavily as possible would agree with the identity view about every case, and I want to know whether anything survives between them beyond how the relation is stated.

One further thing. My characters do not exist, so it may be that the tree I have asked for is the wrong instrument for them and that statements about Madeleine are made true by the text, or by a game of make-believe, or by her being an abstract thing of some kind, rather than by worlds and their inhabitants. Deal with this in a closing section, and say whether the tree transfers unchanged, transfers with a modification, or should be set aside for a different one.

Finally, be committed. Where the tree has to choose between two orderings of the questions, choose and give your reason. Where a cell is empty, give me a verdict on why. And at the end, place me: my practice is to write as though there is a fact of the matter, to deny that the bystander in the third book is Madeleine, and to have no view whatsoever about Cécile and Céline. Tell me which leaf that combination puts me on, defend the assignment, and if you think the three commitments are jointly untenable, say which one you would have me drop. Do not leave me with the observation that it depends what one means by the same character.
