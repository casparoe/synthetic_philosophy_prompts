I come to this from forty years of X-ray scattering rather than from philosophy, so my instinct is to ask what the apparatus was before asking what the answer is. Bertrand's chord problem reads to me as a question about a half-specified measuring device: name the generator of the chord and I can compute the probability; leave the generator open and there is nothing to compute. The philosopher in my reading group agrees, but concludes that the principle of indifference is refuted by this, not merely under-supplied.

I want to resist that, and I have cobbled together two constraints I think do the work without sneaking in a mechanism. Call a chord-producing scheme admissible if (i) it is isotropic about the centre, so no direction is privileged, and (ii) it is nested: having drawn a chord across a disk of radius R, conditioning on the event that the underlying line also cuts a concentric disk of radius r < R must give back exactly the distribution the scheme would have produced had I aimed it at the smaller disk directly. A third condition, which I am less sure I need, is scale covariance: the scheme brings no length scale of its own, so rescaling the disk merely rescales the distribution. As far as I can work it out, the three together force the chord's signed distance from the centre to be uniform on [−R, R], which makes the probability that the chord is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle equal to 1/2 — the same value one gets by imagining a homogeneous, isotropic rain of straight lines falling on the whole plane and keeping those that hit the disk.

So: what is your credence that 1/2 is the privileged answer — the one a properly constrained principle of indifference delivers — as against 1/3, as against 1/4, and as against no answer at all until a generator is named? Give me a number for each of those four, but mainly give me the decomposition: whether the bare question is well-posed; whether isotropy is really the only symmetry available once the circle is given, or whether the translation invariance of the ambient plane is also in play; whether my nesting condition is a genuine constraint or an assumption that already contains the conclusion; whether the three conditions jointly force the uniform-distance law as a matter of mathematics; and whatever else you would add. Then show how the terms combine and say which single one your final number is most sensitive to.

For calibration: I sit near 0.6 on 1/2, and a retired probabilist in the group sits near 0.05, on the ground that nesting is no more compulsory than scattering midpoints uniformly over the area. Tell me which of us is mislocating where the real disagreement lies.
