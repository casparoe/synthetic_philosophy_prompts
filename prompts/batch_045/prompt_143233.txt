Hey — I'm putting together a short teaching note on the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem for a mixed audience of philosophers and political scientists, and I need a worked example built to order rather than a stock one.

Quick reminder of the setup so we're using the same words. Voters submit full strict rankings of the candidates (no ties, no truncation), any combination of rankings is allowed, and the rule always spits out a single winner — no lotteries, and any tie-break has to be stated as part of the rule. Gibbard–Satterthwaite says: if the rule is resolute in that sense, can in principle elect any of at least three different candidates, and no voter ever has a profitable unilateral misreport at any profile, then the rule is a dictatorship. My angle for the note is that the theorem's notion of manipulation is very austere — one voter, one profile at a time, full knowledge of everyone else's ballot — and I want to show students a case where the rule looks safe at the actual profile but is one phone call away from falling over.

Here's what I want you to build. A number of voters and candidates of your choosing, a rule, and a sincere profile P such that:

- Nobody at P can gain by misreporting on their own. Profit is always judged by that voter's true preferences as given in P, comparing the winner at P with the winner after the deviation, everyone else held fixed.
- Some pair of voters — call them Maya and Sam — can both strictly gain by misreporting together: there is a joint deviation after which the winner is strictly better than the sincere winner for each of them.
- For each of them, going alone gets her nothing or leaves her worse off. Nothing is fine if that's all you can get; strictly worse is what I'd really like, and if you think the strictly-worse version is unachievable with any rule someone could actually advocate, say so and explain what blocks it.
- Contagion: after Maya has already misreported, Sam's move becomes profitable (or vice versa). I want to be able to say that the manipulation isn't just jointly available, it's sequentially inviting.

Constraints, because I know how easy it is to cheat here:

- The rule has to be stated in one sentence and be something a real person could advocate — plurality with runoff, Borda, Copeland, Black's rule, whatever. No rules whose behaviour away from P has been gerrymandered to protect P. It should be anonymous and obviously non-dictatorial.
- At least three candidates and at least five voters, so no single ballot is doing something absurd.
- The profile has to be competitive: no candidate ranked first by a majority, and the sincere winner shouldn't be a Condorcet winner — if she is, drop that requirement but tell me. Ideally she wins by the thinnest margin you can manage.
- The pair's move should be a move a campaign could actually coordinate: something like you two both put B on top, not a contrived permutation that lands on a tie-break. If a tie-break is doing real work in your case, flag it honestly.
- It has to be checkable by hand — I want to put the verification on one slide.

Deliverables: the profile and rule; the verification (a table is fine — for each voter, why no solo deviation pays, and then the pair calculation); which structural feature of the case is doing the work; what happens to the case if you remove that feature (e.g. if you swap the rule for plain plurality, or make the sincere winner a Condorcet winner, does the pair still have a move?); and then the honest bit — what someone who thinks Gibbard–Satterthwaite already covers all this would say back. In particular: since your rule is non-dictatorial and resolute, the theorem guarantees some profile where one voter profits alone; find the nearest such profile to P (how many ballot changes away is it?) and tell me whether that distance makes the theorem feel more or less alarming than the phone-call case.
