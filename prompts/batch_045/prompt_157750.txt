Setup: I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy, and next Thursday I'm giving a paper at my department's work-in-progress seminar (40 minutes plus maybe 20 of questions) on the third chapter of my thesis. The chapter is about what a very capable AI system should be built to aim at, assuming we can't just hand it a complete list of instructions. The best-known proposal in that corner of the literature is coherent extrapolated volition: roughly, aim at what we would want if we knew more, thought faster, were more the people we wished we were, and had grown up closer together. My argument is that this proposal quietly contains two separate moves that everyone treats as one — an idealization stage (correct our actual preferences for ignorance, sloppy reasoning, and manipulation) and an aggregation stage (fuse many corrected wills into a single will to be served) — and that almost every serious objection to it is really an objection to the second stage, not the first. So I keep the idealization and swap out the aggregation for something like a contractualist rejection test: I call the result *extrapolated non-rejectability*, or ENR. Roughly: aim at outcomes that no member of the idealized version of the affected population could reasonably reject, where the idealization is the same one CEV already uses and reasonable is meant to be read from each rejector's own standpoint rather than from an aggregate. I claim this (a) keeps the corrective bite, so I can still say no to preferences that only survive because of ignorance or adaptive deformation; (b) handles the many-people problem without quietly collapsing into utilitarianism; and (c) gets the right answer on the case I actually care about — a small religious or cultural minority whose practices probably wouldn't survive full-information idealization for most of its members, but where I want to say the system has to preserve their ability to say no rather than steer them toward whatever the majority's extrapolated will turns out to be. Fallback position if I get hammered: even if ENR is too indeterminate to serve as a maximizing target, it's still the right procedural constraint on how such a system should treat people while it remains uncertain. My supervisor thinks I should drop the AI framing entirely and pitch it as a paper about idealized preferences in general; I've ben going back and forth on that, and a friend who does ML research says the whole thing sounds unfalsifiable, which doesn't help.
