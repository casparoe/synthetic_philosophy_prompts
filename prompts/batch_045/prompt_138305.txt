I chair the serious incident review panel at a ~500-bed hospital — we meet every other week, about 90 minutes per case, and the output that matters is a short "causal findings" section that says things like "the delay in giving antibiotics caused the septic shock" or "admission on a weekend contributed to the worse outcome." Those reports go to the family, to the department involved, and to legal, and they trigger things: a new protocol, mandatory training, sometimes a budget line. Six of us: two nurses, two physicians, a risk manager, and me. I did some philosophy as an undergrad, which is why I'm writing this, and nobody else on the panel has any formal training in it.

I've become convinced we're making two specific messes and I want a procedure, not an explanation.

Mess one: we conflate two quite different claims. (a) "this patient would not have died if the delay hadn't happened" — a one-off counterfactual about this admission — and (b) "if we changed the system so this kind of delay can't happen, future patients like this one do better" — a claim about what would happen under an intervention. These can come apart in both directions: sometimes (a) is true and (b) is false because this was a fluke that our fix wouldn't touch, and sometimes (b) is true and (a) is false because something else in that admission would have killed her anyway and the delay just happened to be there first. The family reads the report wanting (a); the department is supposed to act on (b). We write one sentence that pretends to be both.

Mess two: we write findings about things nobody can say how to change. "Weekend admission," "frailty," "poor handover," "the patient's race." Nobody can tell me what we would actually do to set those to a value, or what else would move along with them if we did. Half the time the honest answer is that the intervention isn't specified, so we don't know what question we're even asking.

So: write me a procedure the panel can actually run in our 90 minutes, as an ordered set of tests — what to ask first, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step given what we have (the chart, maybe two interviews, occasionally registry data; we can't randomize anything), where it stops with "no causal finding," and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values and not facts. It has to do something useful with: counterfactuals that quietly rewrite earlier events ("if she hadn't been admitted Friday she'd have been seen by a different team entirely"), overdetermination, and causes we can't manipulate. Give us a small set of sentence templates for what we can defensibly assert at each exit point — the wording has to be readable by a grieving family and survivable in a deposition, and legal and I both sign off.

Then run it on three: the delayed-antibiotics/septic-shock case; a "weekend admission caused the worse outcome" finding; and a case where the panel wants to write that the patient's race caused the worse outcome and the only real evidence is a disparity in our own numbers plus some notes about how staff spoke to her. Include at least one where the procedure gives me an answer I won't like. And tell me plainly where it will misfire on us and what it can't do.
