I have been trying to work out what I actually think about metaphysical grounding, and my verdicts on cases keep failing to line up with any principle I can state. I would like help stating the principle — or being told, with reasons, which verdicts have to go.

My verdicts, as honestly as I can report them.

1. The ball's being crimson grounds its being red. Yes, comfortably.
2. Socrates' existing grounds the existence of the singleton {Socrates}. Yes, comfortably. But nothing grounds the existence of the empty set, and I do not want to call the empty set *fundamental* either — it is not among the things the world is made of. So "ungrounded" and "fundamental" have come apart for me, and I do not know what the third category is.
3. The ball's being red and its being round do *not* ground the conjunctive fact that it is red and round. For me there is no further fact there to be grounded.
4. Its being red *does* ground the disjunctive fact that it is red or square — and that fact is, to me, obviously a further fact. If the ball were both red and square I would say the disjunction is grounded twice over. I know 3 and 4 sit badly together: both are Boolean compounds of the same two facts, and I treat one as no fact at all and the other as an extra fact that costs nothing.
5. The parts' being arranged table-wise grounds the table's existing; not conversely. The same parts in a scattered arrangement: no table. So arrangement is doing work, which makes me suspect that what really grounds the table is parts *plus* spatial relations *plus* a composition principle, not the parts alone.
6. For that reason I reject the priority-monist claim that the whole cosmos grounds its parts. Except that when I think about the universe as a whole I feel the pull of that claim and cannot say what the difference is supposed to be.
7. The negative fact that there is no hippo in the room is grounded in the room's total positive state: yes, but grudgingly, because every way I have of stating the ground seems to contain the negation I was trying to ground.
8. A Humean mosaic of particular matters of fact necessitates the law-facts; I still deny that it grounds them, and I am not sure anything does. So necessitation is not sufficient for grounding.
9. The ball's being crimson is grounded in the microphysical state of its surface. Which makes crimson both a ground (in case 1) and grounded (here), and makes me suspect I have been sliding between "more fundamental than" and "fundamental."

The only thing of my own I have managed to formulate is a two-part test. *Distinctness*: F is a fact distinct from the facts in Γ only if a complete inventory of reality that already records Γ would still have something left to record — a further condition that reality must meet. *Ground*: F is grounded in Γ iff, once Γ obtains, F's obtaining involves no further work, where the "no further work" is supposed to come from what it is for Γ to obtain rather than from some additional item in the world. This test delivers 4 and 7, but it seems to misfire on 3 (surely there is a further condition in the conjunctive case: that *both* hold), to beg the question on 2 (whether set formation counts as further work depends on whether the principle of set formation is itself a worldly addition), and to be indeterminate on 1 (is "red" a new entry in the inventory, or just a coarser way of recording "crimson"?).

So: is there a single principle that captures all or nearly all of these verdicts — or should I be working with two or three principles for different kinds of case, constitutive versus Boolean, say, and if so, what makes a case belong to one kind rather than the other? I want it stated precisely: numbered clauses, each tagged with the verdict it is there to save. If you think one of my verdicts is simply mistaken, say which and why. Assume I know the standard logic of grounding (irreflexivity, asymmetry, transitivity, hyperintensionality); do not rehearse it, but do tell me if your principle requires revising any part of it. Please do not give me a literature survey or citations — I want the principle, and your own verdicts, not a map of who has said what.

Please structure your reply in these sections, in this order:

1. **Candidate principle** — roughly 250 words. One precise statement, with numbered clauses, each followed by a short note saying which of my cases motivates it.
2. **Run over my cases** — roughly 400 words. A table with columns: my case, my verdict, your verdict, agree?, and a sentence of comment where the two diverge.
3. **What I have to give up** — roughly 150 words. Which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, stated plainly.
4. **Three cases I have not thought about** — roughly 250 words. Cases your principle settles that I did not list, including at least one where you think its verdict is counterintuitive but still correct.
5. **Where the principle falls silent** — roughly 150 words. The kinds of case on which it delivers no verdict, and what you would need to add to get one.
