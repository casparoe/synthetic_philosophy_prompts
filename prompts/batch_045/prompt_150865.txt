hey — I moderate a critique group for novelists. Eight of us, we meet monthly, and between full manuscripts and thirty-page chunks I'm reading other people's work-in-progress most nights. The actual job I keep failing at isn't writing feedback, it's adjudicating it: two readers come back on the same pages with flatly opposite verdicts (one says it's the best thing she's written, one says he couldn't get past chapter two), the writer looks at me, and whatever comes out of my mouth next decides which way six months of revision goes. That's maybe forty of these calls a year, the readers are unpaid volunteers I can't give homework to, I have about twenty minutes with both sets of notes in front of me and the pages, and whatever I decide I have to say out loud in a way that doesn't make either reader quit the group.

I half-remember that Hume wrote something about whether taste can be objective at all — true judges, delicacy of sentiment, practice, comparison, being free of prejudice, we defer to critics who've done all that. There's also some story about two guys at a wine tasting who both complained the wine was off, one said leather and one said metal, everyone else said it was fine and laughed at them, and then when the barrel was drained there was an old key on a leather thong sitting at the bottom. I don't want an essay about any of this. I want a procedure.

So: give me an ordered set of tests — a checklist, a flowchart in words, whatever — that I can actually run in twenty minutes to decide which reader's judgment to back, or whether to declare the disagreement undecidable. For each step say what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure just stops with "no verdict" and I tell the writer to pick based on what she wants the book to be, and where it has to hand the question to a person because it's a values call and not a facts call. Every step has to be phrased so someone who's never opened a philosophy book can run it, and you should say plainly which of Hume's distinctions survive being turned into a checklist and which ones you're dropping and why.

Please steal, fix, or replace my own half-baked ideas:

1. The key-and-thong step. Before I decide who is right, I go looking for a concrete thing in the text that both readers are actually responding to from different angles — a tense shift, a narrator who withholds, a deliberately dull scene, a joke that lands as cruelty for one reader and as honesty for another. If I find it, the disagreement is evidence about the book. If I can't find it after a fixed amount of time, the disagreement is evidence about the readers. I don't know how to stop myself from finding something, though, so this step needs a way to fail.
2. Local reasons beat global verdicts. "On page twelve the tense shifts and I lost the timeline" is usable. "It didn't work for me" and "I couldn't put it down" are not. But our best reader writes almost entirely in global verdicts and is usually right, so I need to know what to do with that.
3. The inversion test. Have the champion argue against the manuscript and the detractor argue for it, and see which of their specific observations survive the flip. Observations that survive get kept; overall verdicts get discounted. I've tried this twice and once it felt rigged toward whoever is more articulate.
4. The borrowed-standard problem. Half our fights are really about a reader judging a quiet literary novel by thriller standards, or a comic novel by standards of sincerity, or a first-person voice by standards of likability. What's the test for "you're using the wrong standard for this book" versus "you just dislike it and that's allowed"?

Two more things about my group you have to build around. There's a retired editor in her seventies who is scary good and has been right more often than the rest of us put together, and there's a writer whose own books are unlike anything anyone else writes and who loves things the rest of us hate and is sometimes the only early reader of something that later wins prizes. I don't want a procedure that just says defer to the expert, and I don't want one that pretends track records are nothing.

Then run the procedure on three cases, including one where it tells me to back the reader I personally disagree with — I want to see that happen, because if the procedure never overrules me I'll never trust it. Make them cases I'd recognise: a debut literary novel everyone loves except one reader who says the narrator is unbearable; a genre manuscript where the group's praise is about the idea and the complaints are about the prose; and a case where the two readers disagree about whether a book is even finished. End by telling me what this procedure cannot do.

One format thing: lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give me the reasoning. No preamble and don't restate the question back at me. Casual is fine — I'll be reading it at midnight.
