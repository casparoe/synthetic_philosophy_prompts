I record an interview series on formal epistemology and rational choice. My next guest is the author of a recent monograph in the epistemic-programme tradition: her central claim is that solution concepts should be derived as theorems about what follows from explicitly stated epistemic conditions — each player's rationality, common belief in the structure of the game, and, where more than two players are involved, a common prior — rather than imported as assumptions from outside. She is a fast and agile speaker, and she has two habits I want to plan around: she meets objections by saying that they concern the model rather than rationality, and she slides without warning between talk of common knowledge and talk of common belief, treating the latter as the philosophically serious version of the former.

Three of her claims strike me as more contested than she presents them. First, she holds that a player's conjecture about what an opponent would do at an information set that has not been reached should be held fixed when surprising information arrives — she calls this the invariance of unreached conjectures. It lets her reject forward-induction reasoning, and she uses it to argue that backward induction in a long centipede game is not paradoxical, on the ground that the paradoxical version of the argument smuggles in a belief revision that rationality alone does not license. Second, in her own theorems she replaces common knowledge with mutual belief of finite depth k plus a tolerance ε for error, defines the play that survives iterated elimination at depth k, and asserts that for all but a class of games she calls fragile the depth-k prediction converges on the common-knowledge prediction as k grows and ε shrinks. On that basis she argues that the e-mail game — the coordination example in which agents can communicate only through a channel that fails with small probability and must confirm every message, so that near-common knowledge of the payoff state produces a discontinuity — is not a counterexample to common-knowledge reasoning but a fragile case whose pathology belongs to the channel. Third, she wants to supplement common belief in rationality with a caution condition, roughly that no player ever assigns probability zero to any strategy of any opponent, because this would license eliminating weakly dominated strategies as well as strongly dominated ones, that is, iterated admissibility.

Draft me ten to twelve questions for this interview, with the following constraints. Include at least one question on each of the five areas above — the knowledge/belief distinction, invariance versus forward induction, the depth-and-ε machinery, caution and iterated admissibility, and the status of the common prior — plus a closing question about what any empirical finding about real players could count as evidence against the programme. For each main question, add a follow-up written against the answer she is most likely to give, not a generic prompt to elaborate: where she will say the distinction makes no difference because her beliefs are probability-one, where she will say the centipede case is settled by her invariance claim, where she will say the e-mail game is fragile. Make her say precisely what her convergence claim concerns — strategies, probabilities of actions, or expected payoffs — and in what sense of convergence, and press her to state a criterion for fragility in advance rather than one that merely relabels the example she needs to explain away. Press likewise on whether rationality plus caution really delivers full iterated admissibility, given that eliminating weakly dominated strategies is sensitive to the order of elimination. On the common prior, ask whether it is a condition on the agents or a feature of the analyst's model, and what that answer implies for the results that agents with a common prior cannot agree to disagree and cannot both expect to gain from a trade each knows to be voluntary. Finally, build a short sequence of two or three questions that makes her "that is a fact about the model, not about rationality" reply progressively harder to fall back on. Keep every question self-contained — she will hear it cold, without her notation in front of her — and avoid anything biographical or about how she came to the view.
