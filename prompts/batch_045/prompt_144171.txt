I'm a retired biophysicist — thirty years of fitting rate constants to stopped-flow traces, so I know what it looks like when three different parameter sets fit the same data and you have to reach for a prior to pick one — and I've been going down a rabit hole lately on inverse reinforcement learning, the idea that you watch what someone does and infer what they want instead of being told. My grandson works in ML and says the underdetermination people worry about is a philosopher's toy; in practice, he says, the degeneracy breaks. I say it breaks because people quietly bake the answer in. I know the textbook point that any policy is optimal for some reward function, degenerate constant ones included, so don't hand me that — I want a case where the disagreement survives even after the trivial solutions are set aside.

So build me one ordinary domestic scenario, one paragraph, someone moving around a kitchen or a workshop for a couple of minutes, no robots and nothing that needs a lab. It has to work like this. (1) The observation is a short trajectory — a little table, maybe six or eight rows, columns for where she is and what she does; that's all any procedure gets. (2) Her true reward is boring and specific: something like wanting the coffee hot and not wanting to scrape the bottom of the enamel pan. (3) The behavior is genuinely ambiguous, but not by stipulation — she's not irrational, not senile, not confused about what she wants. Any suboptimality has to come from something a real person could have: a false belief about how the new stove works, a stiff wrist, a habit left over from a different kitchen. (4) Three procedures run on that exact same trajectory must return three different best guesses at the reward: maximum-entropy IRL treating her as noisily (Boltzmann) optimal with some fixed temperature, Bayesian IRL with a prior that favors simple or sparse reward functions, and a version that lets her be optimizing with respect to her own partly-wrong model of the dynamics. Tell me which one gets the true reward and which comes out worst. (5) Then give me the single extra observation — ideally one counterfactual, what she'd have done if one small thing in the kitchen had been different — that collapses the disagreement, and say honestly whether that datum carries information about her preferences or only about her opportunities. (6) If I delete the false belief and make her model of the kitchen correct, do the three converge? I want to know whether the belief-mismatch or the rationality assumption was doing the work.

Show the state–action sequence and enough of the dynamics that I can actually check the scoring — I want to see why each procedure ranks the candidate rewards the way it does, not just be told it does. And be straight about the soft spot: tell me how my grandson would attack the case, because I can already hear "you picked the temperature" and "you picked the hypothesis class," and then tell me how much of the disagreement is left if he's right about both. What counts as cheating, so you know what I'm after: don't build the right answer into the observation, don't let a procedure read her mind or peek at her true reward, and don't make the verdicts come from the story rather than from the formalism — the reward has to fall out of the scoring, from the same trajectory and the same dynamics all three procedures get. Keep the whole thing under 500 words, not counting the state–action table, and no citations or links; I can't check them and I don't care who proved what.
