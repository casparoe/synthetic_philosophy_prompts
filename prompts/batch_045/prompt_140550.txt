hi — I'm in year 4 of a political theory PhD and I'm finally writing the chapter where I try to work out whether the negative / positive / republican distinction is doing any real extensional work, or whether its just three vocabularies for describing the same pattern of gut verdicts. So I sat down and wrote out my own verdicts on a pile of cases before going back to the literature, and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to. Here they are, roughly in the order I thought of them:

1. Income tax at 30% to fund a health service I use and like: reduces my freedom, but only slightly, and doesn't make me unfree.
2. A licensing board that must approve my renovation plans and has never refused anyone in living memory, and everyone knows it never refuses: I wait three weeks, file some forms, get approved. I feel noticeably less free than in a regime where no approval is needed at all. This is the one I keep circling back to.
3. Same situation, but no approval needed: instead there's a small random audit afterwards with a retrospective fine if I got it wrong, expected cost about the same as the three-week delay. Barely registers as a freedom loss for me.
4. My employer can fire me at will, for no reason, with no process. I feel unfree / dominated — even though my actual boss is lovely and has fired nobody in years.
5. I'm addicted to something and can't stop even when I want to. I resist saying this reduces my freedom, but I feel the pull.
6. I'm too poor to fly to see a dying parent. Not unfree. I really don't want the positive-liberty people to be able to say it is — but I can't say why I resist here and not in #4, where also nothing has actually happened to me.
7. A friend talks me out of a career move with a long, loving, entirely non-deceptive argument that I experience afterward as fully my own: no freedom loss.
8. The same outcome produced by an ad system that's been microtargeting me since I was fourteen: I want to say yes, that is a freedom loss.
9. A law I voted for and would have chosen anyway, backed by a criminal penalty: I feel no freedom loss at all. I know the negative-liberty people will say that's just wrong, since it's plainly a threat-backed removal of an option.
10. The one I actually go back and forth on: a precommitment arrangement I set up myself — I lock myself out of something for six months and hand the override to a friend with instructions not to give it back no matter how much I ask. My earlier self constrains my later self at my own earlier request. I cannot tell whether that's freedom or unfreedom.

The thing I keep reaching for is something like: what matters isn't whether an option is actually removed, but the relation some agent's will stands in to yours — whether they could have hindered you, and whether they were in any way required to track your interests in deciding. But then #6 starts to look just like #4 with the agent removed, and I can't tell whether my could-have-hindered / cannot distinction is doing real work or just restating my verdicts in fancier words. I've also been toying with a phrase for the #2 vs #3 gap — the shadow of a veto — but I can't say what property it names, and I'm suspicious it's only about how it feels rather than about freedom.

So: what I want is a principle, stated precisely enough that I could sit down and apply it to a new case, with the clause-by-clause motivation (which case forces which clause), its verdicts on two or three cases I haven't listed, and an honest account of which of my verdicts it makes me give up. If no single principle gets all ten, give me the two best candidates and say what each one costs me. Please also say plainly which of the three traditions your principle ends up closest to and where exactly it departs — and no reading lists or citations, I've got the literature, I want the principle.
