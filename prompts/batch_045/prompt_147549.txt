I'm a PhD candidate in comparative archaeology, and I'm putting together the methods appendix for a dissertation chapter on what, if anything, "collapse" picks out as a cross-cultural comparative category. Before I commit to a typology I want the logical space laid out properly, and I would rather have a working instrument than an essay about the debate.

What I need is a decision tree — or, if a tree can't carry the structure, a short diagnostic quiz with a scoring key — that takes a concrete case description and sorts it into a named position on whether that case is a collapse, a transformation, or something the category simply doesn't apply to. The audience is a mixed graduate seminar (archaeologists, two historians, one anthropologist), so every node has to be answerable by someone who has the case in front of them but has not memorized any verdicts about it, and no node may ask "do you think it was a real collapse?" or any other version of the question the instrument is supposed to answer.

The distinctions I want doing the branching work, roughly in the order I currently think they bite:

1. What the subject of the predicate is: a governing institution, a cultural repertoire (literacy, monumental building, elite material style), a population, or the complexity of a regional settlement system.
2. Whether "collapse" is partly evaluative — whether things have to have gotten worse for someone, and for whom: the elites, the median person, or the people living two generations later.
3. Rate: whether a change of a given magnitude spread over, say, three centuries is a collapse or merely a gradual change of the same size.
4. Agency: whether it matters that participants appear to have chosen, welcomed, or brought about the change.
5. Reversibility: whether it is still a collapse if comparable complexity reassembles a few centuries later.
6. Whether collapse needs a bounded unit at all, or can be predicated of a network or region whose "society" was never a discrete thing.

Requirements on the instrument:

Eight to ten diagnostic questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options. Include schematic versions of the usual three — the eastern Mediterranean around 1200 BCE, the terminal Classic centuries in the Maya lowlands, Rapa Nui — but where the empirical details of those are disputed, use the schematized version; I want the structure of the instrument tested, not the facts. At least four of the questions should use invented or idealized cases instead, including: a polity whose entire population is replaced over two generations while its administrative apparatus, archives, and tax system continue without interruption; one that is absorbed intact into a neighboring polity so that nothing observable changes locally except who receives the surplus; one where complexity, monumentality, and long-distance exchange all drop sharply while skeletal indicators of health, life expectancy, and interpersonal violence all improve; and one where the participants themselves describe the change in celebratory terms while outside observers file it as a catastrophe.

Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: state the axes, say which combinations of answers they permit, and only then locate the actual positions in that space — including, as leaves at minimum, the transformationist position (nothing really collapses, it only reorganizes); an institutional-collapse minimalism that restricts the term to the disappearance of a governing apparatus; a demographic version that makes population decline the criterion; a complexity-loss view in the marginal-returns spirit; the view that the term is irreducibly evaluative and so never purely descriptive; an eliminativist position on which the category does no comparative work at all; and a position on which what collapses is specifically a society's capacity to describe itself (loss of literacy, bureaucracy, or administrative legibility). If one named position turns out to occupy several cells, say so and treat it as a family. Each leaf should carry one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Then: name at least three unoccupied cells in the space and diagnose each as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; and give me a short closing section flagging any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, with an assessment of whether some further question could separate them or whether they are the same position under two descriptions. I am also genuinely unsure whether my third axis (rate) is doing independent work or collapses into the second (the evaluative one); if it does, tell me and restructure rather than leaving it in as decoration.

I don't need citations, a bibliography, or a reading list — I know that literature and my committee will supply the rest. What I need is the structure: the questions, the key, the empty cells, and the places where the instrument breaks. Please write the answer in American English, and keep the case descriptions schematized enough that they don't depend on contested site-level detail.
