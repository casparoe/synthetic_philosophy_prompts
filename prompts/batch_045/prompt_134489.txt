You are advising the research director of a human-rights advocacy organization on how to spend a small conceptual-research budget: about eight commissioned papers over two years, from legal philosophers, political theorists, and historians, with no new fieldwork, no litigation, and no original data collection. The organization's real lever is language — what its advocates and the journalists who quote them call an atrocity, and how international bodies classify it. So the question is which lines of work on the category of genocide and on the politics of naming atrocities deserve that money, and in what order.

Two distinctions I want you to use, because they organize how I already think about the problem. Call a label's gravity the extra attention, funding, and perceived obligation to intervene that attaching it buys. Call its elasticity how far advocates can stretch it to cover new cases before courts, journalists, or publics begin to resist. My working assumption is that these trade off: each loose application buys gravity now and spends credibility later, so that advocates face something like a commons problem, where the label is worth more to each user than to all of them. The trade-off is sharpened by a second fact: Lemkin's own concept was considerably wider than the one that was eventually codified — it reached the destruction of a group's institutions, culture, and cohesion and not merely the killing of its members — while the codified version turns on proof of an intent to destroy a group as such. So the elasticity question is in part a question about how much of Lemkin to restore.

Give me the three lines of work you would fund first, in order. Candidates I can see, which you should feel free to rank, combine, or replace: replacing the intent requirement with something like a destructive-pattern standard, and what would be lost in moral signal if the law stopped asking whether the destruction was aimed at the group's existence; moving the organization's public vocabulary to crimes against humanity or some broader umbrella term, and what that costs in gravity; the archives problem, on which an intent-focused test systematically favors bureaucratic killing with paper trails over chaotic or opportunistic mass killing, and whether that is an argument for changing the test, changing the vocabulary, or neither; the dilution question, whether loose application really does spend gravity and whether anyone could establish that without new data; whether there is a distinctive moral kind here at all, that is, whether the destruction of a group is worse than the same number of deaths not aimed at any group's existence, and whether our label tracks that kind or merely a legislative accident; and whether early-warning and prevention work should be reframed so the category can be applied before intent is provable.

For each of your three, say why it is upstream of the others, what a paper could actually establish in two years without new data, and what evidence or event during that period would move it up or down your list. Then name one prominent line of work in this area you would decline to fund as overrated — something advocates reach for whose answer, even if settled cleanly, would not change what anyone does or says — and explain why it fails that test. Be explicit about the criteria you are trading off, such as upstream-ness, tractability, neglectedness, and downstream effect on practice, and be explicit about where those criteria conflict.

If the framing is wrong, say so before you answer rather than after. In particular: if the gravity/elasticity trade-off is a muddle, if there is no genuine commons problem, if the archives point is overstated, or if the premise that conceptual work here can move institutional practice is simply false, tell me plainly and tell me what I should fund instead. I would rather have the objection than a polite answer to a bad question.

Please write in American English. Finish with a single final line, in exactly this form and with nothing after it:

\answer{the line of work you would fund first, named in ten words or fewer}
