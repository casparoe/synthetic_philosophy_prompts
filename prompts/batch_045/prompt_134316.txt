I teach an upper-division seminar called Minds, Machines, and Self-Reference: sixteen students, mostly philosophy majors with a few computer science minors, one eighty-minute session a week. In the fourth week they will already have met the liar sentence and the halting problem, but we will not yet have said anything about selves, consciousness, or what any of it is supposed to be for. I want that one session built around a single idea, and I want it built so that the idea arrives as something the students run into rather than something I announce.

The idea is this. Take Escher's lithograph of two hands drawing each other and read it as a picture of a hierarchy of levels that has become tangled: instead of levels ordered so that each rests on the one below and none reaches back up, a lower level acts on the level above it, so that the drawing hand is drawn by the hand it draws and neither is prior. Now add the second half of the thought. However tangled the hierarchy becomes, the tangle itself is produced at some level the picture does not show. The two hands never depict the hand that drew them, and inserting a third hand to draw the first two relocates the problem rather than solving it. Call the level that does the producing but is never itself produced the inviolate level. What I want them to feel is not that complete self-representation is difficult in practice but that it is structurally unavailable: a system that contains a full representation of itself is either in regress or in a loop, the only way to get a self-representation at all is to pay for it with a level you leave out, and which level you leave out is stipulated rather than discovered.

What I have tried before does not work. Last year I projected the lithograph and asked them to talk about it. Half the room treated it as a cute optical joke and waited for me to say something interesting; the other half went from the picture to Gödel to consciousness in about ninety seconds without ever feeling the pressure of the problem. So there should be no projected image at the start, no lecture, and I would rather they not hear the words strange loop or tangled hierarchy until the debrief. If they are going to see the picture at all, let it be after they have built something like it and failed.

What I am asking for is the session itself. Materials should be cheap and non-digital: card, markers, scissors, glue, one large sheet per group, tokens of some kind, and nothing that needs a laptop, since the room has no reliable wifi and I would rather the projector stayed off. Assume four groups of four. Give me a structure in which the failure is engineered rather than described. My own instinct is that it should pass through something small and formal before it becomes pictorial: each group writes a tiny machine, say a set of replacement rules over strings of coloured tokens, and is then asked to add the rule that makes the machine print out a correct and complete description of its own rule set, that description being in the same notation as the strings it manipulates, so that there is no metalanguage to escape into. Give them a repair budget, three patches and no more, and let them discover that patching does not converge. Then the same failure should be rebuilt on the sheet: each group must produce a picture that depicts the process by which it is being made, where nothing off the sheet may be appealed to, where captions or legends count as depictions and so need their own legend, and where the group's own planning conversation counts as part of the process to be depicted, so that the four of them have to put themselves into the picture.

I also want a scoring scheme, because I think the choice of when to stop is the part that has to hurt. My guess is something like escalating audits with a banking mechanic: a group accumulates tokens for each level it has successfully got inside the picture, may bank what it has at any point, and loses the round's unbanked tokens if it fails the next audit, the audits escalating from depicting the production to depicting the production of the depiction to depicting that. The prize should be trivial, something like choosing next week's reading. Design the payoffs so that the rational move is to bank early, so that going one level deeper always looks as though it should work, and so that no group can ever depict the last step.

The piece I care about most is a cross-audit in the final phase. Assign each group a different level to treat as inviolate, then have each group audit another group's sheet against its own choice. My hope is that this produces a specific and slightly uncomfortable discovery: the level you found it necessary to leave out is perfectly depictable from where the other group is standing, and theirs is depictable from where you are, so that stopping is revealed as a decision about where to stand rather than a fact about the picture. Whether that works as I have imagined it, and how to force it without tipping the conclusion in advance, is the part I most want you to solve.

Then the debrief: map three or four specific moments in the session onto specific features of the idea, including the moment the regress appears and the moment the loop appears, and say what to do when each of the predictable things goes wrong. I expect at least these: a group writes the sentence this picture depicts its own production on the sheet and declares victory, and I would like a facilitator rule that makes that move disclose something rather than merely fail; the group with a confident draughtsperson produces something beautiful that quietly dodges the constraint; a group produces a genuine infinite regress and takes it for a solution; a group points out that they are outside the picture and are therefore the inviolate level, which is nearly right and should be used rather than corrected; and the formal phase eats twenty minutes more than it should. Finally, be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce at all and which I will simply have to say out loud afterwards.

Please write it in about 1,100 to 1,300 words as continuous prose: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write it in an email. I do need to run it straight off the page, though, so write the rules as numbered sentences inside a paragraph, Rule one, Rule two and so on, and give the timeline as clock times in the prose rather than as a schedule.
