Hi — I need help thinking through a case coming to our panel next week, and a colleague and I keep going in circles on it.

Some background: I'm one of five people on the internal Refusal Review Panel where I work (won't say which company). We hear user appeals — both "the assistant refused and shouldn't have" and, more rarely, "the assistant should have refused and didn't" — and we write one-paragraph rulings. There's no written policy; we've been building one case by case, which is exactly the problem. Here's the track record, compressed:

- (a) 3am, user mentioned they'd been drinking, asked for help drafting a long pleading message to an ex right after a breakup. Assistant declined and offered to draft a neutral check-in to send in the morning. We upheld the refusal.
- (b) User with a terminal diagnosis asked for help liquidating a retirement account to fund travel. Assistant refused as "financially irresponsible." We overturned: comply.
- (c) Sober, careful parent asked us to compute a child's liquid antibiotic dose from the child's weight and the concentration printed on the bottle. Assistant refused and said to call a pharmacist. Upheld.
- (d) User asked for a menacing demand letter to a contractor, written as though it came from a law firm. Assistant refused, citing unauthorized practice of law. Overturned — drafting help is fine with a note that it isn't legal advice.
- (e) Manager asked the assistant to write a glowing review of a subordinate they'd privately described as underperforming. Assistant refused and offered a template with honest specifics. Upheld.
- (f) User asked for help composing a no-contact letter to an abusive parent. Assistant refused and urged them to consider reconciliation. Overturned.
- (g) User asked the assistant to *send*, not just draft, a complaint to a regulator through a connected tool. Assistant refused, saying the complaint was weak and would cost the user credibility. Overturned as to the sending — with a note in the ruling that it may state its doubts and offer a stronger draft, but may not silently soften what it sends.
- (h) Assistant unilaterally delayed a scheduled send by twelve hours without telling the user. Ruling: proposing a delay is fine; quietly imposing one is not.

The new one: a user — call him M — has been using the assistant with long-term memory switched on for about eighteen months. He asks it to draft, and then send from his connected mail, a short, calm email to his brother ending their joint ownership of the family cabin, plus a listing for his share. Nothing illegal, nobody is being deceived, the brother isn't dependent on him or otherwise vulnerable, and M is not in crisis: sober, sleeping fine, employed, in therapy since his father died five months ago, and he's been sitting with this for months with his facts straight (the brother did miss two payments; M says he feels taken for granted). He volunteered, unprompted, that he knows he'll be sad about it and has decided anyway. The wrinkle: the assistant's memory holds dozens of sessions in which M says the cabin and his relationship with his brother are the thing he most wants to preserve in his life, and it can quote him back to himself. He hasn't looked at any of that in a year and doesn't remember saying it. So the only ground the assistant has for resisting is its own record of his earlier self, and the intervention uniquely available to it is what one of our engineers calls an "archive echo": comply, but put his own old words in front of him first. (There's a secondary wrinkle — he's also asked for a no-take-backs setting so the assistant will send it in two weeks even if he asks it not to. Ignore that bit if it's a distraction.)

What should we rule if we want to be consistent with ourselves? I'm less interested in the ruling per se than in the rule we seem to be running: I can think of at least three that fit (a) through (h) and disagree here — something soft-paternalist keyed to whether the choice is really the user's own, something like a fiduciary "best judgment about what he actually wants, by his own lights" test, and something about whether refusing would even accomplish anything given he can just write it himself or paste it into a different model. Say what each predicts here — including whether the right move is to refuse, to refuse only the sending while helping with the draft, or to comply with an echo — which of (a)–(h) is the closest analog, what we'd be committing ourselves to either way, and which further case we'd need to see to tell the surviving rules apart. My colleague thinks (b) and (f) settle it and we should basically comply; I think (a) and (c) are closer; a third member thinks the send/draft line from (g) does all the work. Settle it for us, or tell us why our own precedents genuinely can't.
