I'm trying to work out what emotions are, and I'd rather start from verdicts I'm confident about than from a theory I'd have to defend. The trouble is that the verdicts don't line up.

(1) Miriam has a severe spider phobia. She will tell you, and fully believe, that the spider on the wall is a harmless house spider; she still cannot make herself cross the room, her heart pounds, and she wants it gone. I say she is afraid of the spider.

(2) A bomb-disposal technician judges that the device in front of him is live and may kill him, and sets calmly to work: no racing heart, no churning stomach, no urge to flee, nothing he can report except absorption in the task. I want to say he is not afraid — but I am far less sure of this than of (1).

(3) I wept at the end of a film last week, knowing throughout that no one had died and that the whole thing was invented. I say what I felt was grief: the same state, not a pretend or weakened version of it.

(4) A friend of mine was born unable to feel her own bodily arousal. She can be told that her heart is racing, but feels none of the pounding, tightness, or churning the rest of us report. She says she is terrified of public speaking, organises her life to avoid it, and is wretched at the prospect of it. I say she is afraid.

(5) Last Tuesday I was snappish and irritable for an entire afternoon with nothing in particular that I was annoyed about. I don't think I was having an emotion. But it doesn't feel like a wholly different kind of thing from being angry at someone, either.

My problem is this: (1) makes fear possible without the judgment that one is in danger, while (2) makes that judgment insufficient on its own, so a judgment account seems wrong twice over. The obvious repair — build the feeling into the emotion — is blocked by (4). Meanwhile (3) says an emotion can be genuine while its object doesn't exist, and (5) says something can be emotion-like while having no object at all.

Is there a single account of what emotions are — a judgment account, a perceptual one, a feeling theory, or something else — that recovers all five verdicts, or does one of them have to go? If one has to go, tell me which, and why that one rather than its neighbour. Please state the account precisely enough that it also yields verdicts on cases I haven't listed: in particular, on fear of one's own death, and on the anger people feel toward someone who wronged them long ago and is now dead.
