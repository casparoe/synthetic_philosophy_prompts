I chair the pest-management advisory committee for a mid-sized agricultural co-operative, and I also produce a short recorded interview series that goes out to our members and to extension staff. The ethics of insecticides stopped being a seminar topic for me this spring: two confirmed human West Nile cases in our district have put an aerial adulticide for mosquitoes on our agenda, and in the same month we have to decide whether to renew our neonicotinoid seed-treatment contract or pay substantially more for a sterile-insect and mating-disruption programme against the same pest complex. I am not looking for reading recommendations — I want the argumentative structure, because I have to put something in front of a board that is voting in three weeks.

Next month's guest is a moral philosopher who will defend what I'll call the counting argument. As I understand her position, it has four parts: first, that any serious non-zero credence in invertebrate sentience, multiplied by the very large numbers of insects killed per hectare per season, yields an expected welfare cost large enough to swamp the welfare gains from crop protection, so that routine broad-spectrum insecticide use is close to a moral catastrophe rather than a regrettable necessity; second, that the harm that does the work is not death but the sub-lethal course of neurotoxic poisoning — the hours or days of incapacitation before death — so that the relevant ranking is over methods of death rather than over whether animals die; third, that she rejects the replaceability-style reply (roughly, that most insect lives are short and end badly, so killing them costs little) on the grounds that it generalises to conclusions nobody accepts; and fourth, that she nonetheless supports gene-drive and sterile-insect programmes against malaria vectors, and denies that this commits her to any general duty to police suffering in nature, resting the difference on our causal role in creating and maintaining agricultural systems. She also says the costs of restraint should fall on regulators and buyers rather than on smallholder growers, which is the part my board will care about most.

Draft eight to ten questions I can ask her that go beyond having her restate that position. In particular I want at least one question on each of the following, and for each, a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give:

- Whether the swamping claim has a threshold at all — what magnitude of human benefit would outweigh it, what sets that magnitude, and whether her argument is really about insecticides or about agriculture as such.
- How her framework ranks the alternatives we could actually switch to: tillage and cultivation, which kill enormous numbers of soil arthropods by crushing and desiccation; classical biological control, where parasitoids consume hosts from the inside; the microbial and botanical substances approved for organic production; and the sterilisation-and-release approaches. I especially want to know whether the ranking is stable across different assumptions about whether an insect's life is on balance good or bad for the insect, since that seems to flip the verdict on methods that prevent future lives rather than ending present ones.
- Why replaceability fails here, given that the population-ethics questions it depends on are unsettled, and what empirical finding would make her take it seriously.
- How the malaria-vector case is supposed to be consistent with the crop case: if averting a human death licenses mass suppression of mosquitoes, what exactly blocks the parallel claim about averting crop failure, and is her appeal to causal responsibility doing more work than it can bear?
- Who is supposed to bear the cost of restraint, and what she would say to a committee that has to decide this month with no regulator or buyer standing behind them.

I would also like one question on the scope or stopping point of the view — whether it extends to soil nematodes and the arthropods killed incidentally by harvest, transport and storage — and one on what evidence or argument would count against it. End with the question you think she is least likely to have a prepared answer for.

Finally, and this matters as much as the questions: I need to know which of the background claims her position rests on are settled among people who actually work on this and which are live controversies. My understanding is that it is near-settled that neurotoxic insecticides produce extended sub-lethal incapacitation rather than instant death, that insect counts per hectare are enormous, and that juvenile mortality in most insect populations is very high; and that it is genuinely contested whether insects have valenced phenomenal states at all, how much welfare any individual can realise, how to aggregate across very many individuals with very small welfare ranges, and whether death is a harm to them. Please keep that distinction visible — mark in your notes where a premise of hers is contested rather than established, and do not present a contested commitment as the consensus. If I have misdescribed any part of her position, say so and tell me which question then needs rewriting.
