I'm working through a recent trade book on the limits of machine learning, and one chapter argues that the no-free-lunch theorems settle the problem of induction. I can't tell how much of it to believe, and I'd like an accuracy audit before I repeat any of it to other people. The chapter says, more or less:

> The no-free-lunch theorems finally settle Hume's problem of induction, and the verdict is harsher than the philosophers feared. Pick any learner you like — a nearest-neighbour rule, a deep network, a human research team — and then consider every possible world: every way of assigning outcomes to the cases you have not yet seen. Average each learner's expected error over all of those worlds and you get exactly the same number for every one of them. So no learner is better than any other, and none is better than guessing. Induction has no justification. The expectation that unobserved cases will resemble observed ones is only a bias we happen to have; nothing in the data and nothing in logic could favour it. In particular, our track record so far is no evidence that induction will keep working, because under the uniform average how well you fit the data tells you nothing at all about how you will do on the next case. Science looks successful only because we quietly confine ourselves to the handful of worlds in which it succeeds and then congratulate ourselves on the fit — which is circular.
>
> One clarification makes the result properly airtight. A standard objection is that the average is unfair: real worlds are structured, compressible, law-governed, and if you restrict the average to those worlds then lunch reappears. But this fails. Specifying a relabelling of the outcomes costs almost nothing, so a compressible world relabelled is still a compressible world; the symmetry that drives the proof therefore survives the restriction, and the tie remains even when you average only over simple worlds, so long as you weight them equally. What actually breaks the tie is not simplicity but favouritism: a prior that already weights some worlds more heavily than others. And since such a prior simply assumes what was to be shown, it is not a reply. The lesson is that any bias is as good as any other, and that the uniform distribution is the only unbiased assumption available.
