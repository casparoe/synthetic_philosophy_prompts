The question (30 points total)

Let Ω be a finite state space, μ a prior with full support, and let agents 1 and 2 have information partitions P₁ and P₂. The entire model (Ω, μ, P₁, P₂) is common knowledge; each agent observes only which cell of her own partition contains the true state ω. For an event E ⊆ Ω write qᵢ = μ(E | Pᵢ(ω)) for agent i's posterior.

(a) [8 points] State Aumann's agreement theorem for this setting and list the assumptions it requires. Distinguish carefully between mutual knowledge and common knowledge of the posteriors, and say whether the conclusion concerns a single event or the agents' entire posterior distributions.

(b) [12 points] Now suppose the agents announce their current posterior for E to each other over a noiseless channel, revising after each announcement, and suppose the process reaches a fixed point: the announced values stop changing and the two agents announce the same number q. Does it follow that q = μ(E | P₁(ω) ∩ P₂(ω)), i.e. that the settled value is the posterior that someone holding both agents' evidence would assign? Prove it, or give a finite counterexample.

(c) [10 points] Keep the setup of (b), but suppose the channel forces every announcement to be rounded to the nearest 0.01, with ties rounded up; honesty and this protocol are common knowledge, and announcements lie in [0, 1]. Suppose the process stabilises with both agents announcing 0.50.
 (i) Does the agreement theorem imply that the agents' unrounded posteriors are exactly equal? Identify precisely which premise holds and which fails.
 (ii) Give the tightest bound you can on |q₁ − q₂|, and say whether that bound is attained.
 (iii) Does the practical significance of your answer depend on μ(E)? Discuss the case μ(E) = 10⁻⁶.

The student's answer

(a) Aumann's agreement theorem says: if it is common knowledge at ω that agent 1's posterior for E is q₁ and agent 2's is q₂, then q₁ = q₂. Equivalently, agents who share a prior cannot "agree to disagree": they cannot have common knowledge of a difference of opinion about E.

The assumptions are: (1) a common prior μ over a finite Ω — both agents start from the same probability function over the same space; this is what does the work, and it is weaker than requiring that they start from the same evidence, since each still sees only her own cell. (2) Bayesian rationality: each agent conditions μ on the cell of her own partition containing ω. (3) The model is common knowledge — Ω, μ, both partitions, and the fact that each agent updates by conditioning; only the realised cell is private. (4) The posteriors themselves are common knowledge in the full set-theoretic sense: for the announced pair of numbers, the event that those are the two posteriors must contain the cell of the finest common coarsening of P₁ and P₂ that contains ω. Mutual knowledge at every finite depth is strictly weaker than this, and the theorem requires the full hierarchy; that is why the theorem is not automatically satisfied after a single round of announcements, and why the standard two-agent case in which each knows the other's posterior but the posteriors are not common knowledge is no counterexample to the theorem.

As to scope: the conclusion concerns the single event E. It says nothing by itself about other events. If the agents' entire posterior distributions are what gets announced and becomes common knowledge, then applying the theorem event by event yields agreement on every event.

(b) Yes, it follows. Suppose the announcements have stopped changing at the common value q. Consider agent 1. She knows her own cell and the whole history of announcements. The fact that agent 2 also announces q, and does not revise in the following round, is itself evidence: agent 2 would have revised only if agent 1's announcement had moved her posterior, and it did not. So at a fixed point each agent's announcement carries no news to the other, which means that each agent's private information is already contained in what the other has learned. Iterating this argument, at the fixed point each agent's information must include the other's, and so the number each assigns is the posterior conditional on the meet cell P₁(ω) ∩ P₂(ω), i.e. μ(E | P₁(ω) ∩ P₂(ω)).

So in the limit, iterated exchange of posteriors is informationally equivalent to pooling the two bodies of evidence: sufficiently many rounds of communication give you the same number you would get by merging the datasets. This is really the content of the theorem — communication substitutes for shared evidence, which is why the result is often put by saying that rational agents cannot agree to disagree once they have talked long enough.

(c) (i) Yes, the unrounded posteriors must be exactly equal. The rounding rule is a deterministic function of the exact posterior, and the protocol, including the tie-breaking convention, is common knowledge. Because the announcement is a deterministic function of the quantity announced, it transmits that quantity up to the resolution of the code, and since the code is known to both agents nothing is lost: hearing "0.50" is informationally equivalent to being told that the exact posterior lies in [0.495, 0.505), and the receiver can invert the code. Hence the premise of the theorem is satisfied at the level of the exact posteriors, and q₁ = q₂.

(ii) The bound is therefore |q₁ − q₂| = 0, and it is attained trivially, since both agents' exact posteriors are equal.

(iii) No, the size of μ(E) is irrelevant. The theorem is a consistency requirement on degrees of belief and its conclusion, equality, is scale-invariant; it makes no difference whether μ(E) is 0.5 or 10⁻⁶. Any residual discrepancy of at most one rounding unit would be negligible in either case.
