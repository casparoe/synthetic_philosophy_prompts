i'm 71, retired three years ago after 35 years in atmospheric chemistry (ozone sonde retrievals, and an ungodly amount of arguing about whether a record was accurate or merely precise), and since then I've been going to a philosophy reading group with five other ex-lab people. Last month we did testimony — whether taking someone's word for something needs its own justification, or whether it's fine by default until something counts against it — and we got nowhere, becuase everyone just restated their own slogan a little louder. So I'm making them a quiz for next month, and I'd like you to build it.

What I want: a "which position are you actually committed to?" instrument, eight or nine items, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, phrased so that someone who's read nothing can answer it. No item may simply ask for the label ('do you trust strangers?', 'is testimony a basic source of knowledge?') — sort people by what they say about cases.

The cases I want in there (substitute one if you have a better version, but keep the underlying idea; fill up to eight or nine with cases of your own in the same spirit):

- a seven-year-old who has never been lied to, and whose only track record is that the adults around her have been right about the weather.
- the one I care most about: a colleague who is right about 90% of the time, but only because the answer is 'no' 90% of the time and she always says 'no' — she has never once said yes. I genuinely can't tell whether trusting her is the same phenomenon as trusting someone who actually sorts the cases.
- a claim that reaches you through a chain of about twelve people, none of whom you can check, where each link is one colleague vouching for the next.
- someone who has been right about everything you were ever able to verify independently — and then you notice you only ever checked the easy cases.
- a generally scrupulous person testifying about the one topic they're known to be unreliable about (the teacher who is careful about everything except this).
- an expert from a field adjacent to yours telling you about something in your own field that you think is impossible.
- directions from a stranger in a city whose language you don't speak.

Build it from the logical space rather than from the list of names: lay out the questions and the combinations of answers they actually allow first, and only then drop the named positions into that space. Leaves should include global reductionism, the local / positive-reasons-in-each-case version, the default-and-defeat or entitlement view, the trust-or-assurance style view, at least one hybrid, and the view that the whole dispute is verbal; name a leaf if it has a name, otherwise write 'unnamed'. I also want to know which answer-combinations are empty — nobody occupies them — and whether that's because the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored. And flag any pair of leaves my cases can't distinguish from each other; don't paper over them, tell me what question would separate them.

The reason I actually care, in case it shapes which distinctions you put on the tree: I spent a career taking other people's numbers on faith, and I want to know whether that habit had anything going for it beyond laziness.

Notes on the audience: all ex-scientists, they will smell a leading question instantly, and none of us will look anything up — so no reading lists, no citations, no 'see so-and-so 1994'. A name in parentheses next to a leaf is fine. Where a case leans on an empirical claim (how often people lie, whether anyone is good at detecting lies), just say what you're assuming.

Give it back as JSON rather than prose. Schema:

- "questions": list of {id, case (the text exactly as I'd hand it to the group), options: list of {label, text, leads_to (a node or leaf id)}, splits (which leaves this item is meant to separate)}
- "leaves": list of {id, name (or "unnamed"), core_claim, answer_pattern (the pattern that lands here), hardest_case (which of the cases this leaf handles worst), defenders (who, if anyone, or "nobody I know of")}
- "empty_combinations": list of {pattern, diagnosis ("incoherent" / "unattractive" / "unexplored"), why}
- "indistinguishable": list of {leaves: [id, id], why, question_that_would_separate_them}
- "scoring_notes": string, how to score it when answers don't land cleanly on one leaf — I guarantee half the group won't.
