I should say at the outset that I have no academic background in philosophy. I work as a volunteer mediator for a neighbourhood dispute service, and over the past year I have been reading about the Twin Earth thought experiment on my own and turning it over in my head. I have ended up with a variation I have not seen anywhere, and I would like to know what it really shows before I try to make use of it.

In the version everyone knows, the lakes, taps and rain on the twin planet are filled with something whose microstructure differs from ours, the stock example being XYZ rather than H2O, while the speakers there are internally just like us; and the conclusion drawn is that their word picks out their stuff and not ours. My variation separates the two things that move together in that story: the microstructure of the liquid, and the history by which the word came to be applied to it.

Call it Foundry Earth. The liquid in the lakes, the rain, the taps and the veins of the inhabitants is straightforwardly H2O, indistinguishable from ours by any test you like. But it did not get there from nature. The whole hydrosphere is manufactured, and has been for as long as anyone can remember, by an industry working to a set of engineering specifications drawn up quite independently by Foundry Earth's own chemists, who hit on the same formula our people did. Nobody there has ever encountered naturally occurring water, and no chain of use runs back to our planet, our word, or our scientists. They call the stuff water, they have all the beliefs and recognitional abilities about it that we have, they use it for exactly the things we use it for, and they defer to their chemists as we defer to ours. (There is a second version I also worry at, in which the specifications were instead reconstructed from intercepted broadcasts of our own chemistry textbooks, so that there is a chain of transmission running back to us but still no causal contact with our water.)

The reason I care is this. Several of us on the panel have noticed that our hardest cases are ones where both sides use the same contested term — antisocial behaviour, say, or neglect — with what look like identical criteria in the head, while the two uses have quite different institutional histories behind them, and where neither side will accept the other's definition. We have been arguing among ourselves about whether we should treat that as a real disagreement over one shared subject matter or as two people talking past each other, and about what we would even have to find out in order to decide.

Please describe the most important implications of how you think Foundry Earth should be settled for how we should handle cases like those. In particular, I would like you to be explicit about whether it is the microstructure of the stuff, or the history of the word's transmission, that does the referential work on your view, and what follows from that for whether a dispute can be made genuine by the parties agreeing on criteria. It would also help us to know what the same reasoning implies about a speaker — or, for that matter, a computer system — whose entire command of a term comes from text rather than from contact with the things it is used of. Please be plain where you can, tell me frankly if the standard view does not settle the case, and give the strongest version of whatever you end up rejecting.

Please write your answer in British English.
