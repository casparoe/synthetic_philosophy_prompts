I'm putting together a chapter — well, a chapter-shaped mess at the moment — on what economists are actually measuring when they write down a utility function, and I've got a reading group in a few weeks where I want to push the line that utility read the revealed-preference way can't do the job policy people quietly want it to do. What I want to seperate are three things that keep getting run together: utility as an index of choice (numbers that just encode which option she'd take), utility as a measure of how much she gains, and utility as a currency for comparing gains across people. So I need a case that prises the second apart from the first, and I'd like it to be single-agent, so nobody can drag interpersonal comparison in and change the subject.

I already have the easy versions of this argument and I don't want them. The usual move is to make the agent faulty in some way — she's badly informed, or weak-willed, or her tastes have adapted to what she can get, or she's choosing for someone else, or the options were framed one way rather than another — and then point out that the utility function recovered from her choices misdescribes her. Every one of those lets the revealed-preference person say 'fine, but that's a broken agent; fix her and the choice data is all the meaning utility needs'. So build me a case where she isn't broken. Constraints: one ordinary paragraph — a hospital waiting list, a housing allocation, a commute, a shop, whatever — nothing sci-fi, no hypothetical lotteries or gambles, no thought experiments about machines. She's well informed, stable over time, picks what she most wants, doesn't get muddled or tired, and would give the same answers with a week to think about it. Money is in the picture and she's perfectly willing to trade money against whatever the good is: no corner solutions, no infinite valuations, no rule or guardian banning cash changing hands — so a willingness-to-pay, money-metric reading of 'how much did she gain' is well defined and in principle observable, and assume we've watched her choose from every budget set you like, so nothing hinges on missing data. Now make the 'how much' question come apart anyway: show that the money numbers available don't settle how much she gains, and that the reason is the shape of her situation rather than anything about her psychology or her behaviour. Then the bits I actually need: state what the choice-index reading says and what the benefit reading says; name the single feature of the set-up that's doing the work; show what happens to the case if you remove that feature (and tell me honestly if removing it just collapses things back into the ordinary money-metric story, because then I haven't gained anything); and give me the strongest line of defence available to someone who thinks utility simply means an index of choice and that there's no further fact about how much she benefits — I need to know whether this case really puts pressure on that person or just begs the question against them. Whatever makes the numbers fail should be the sort of thing that would plausibly be true of the situation anyway, not something switched on to manufacture the verdict, and please finish with an honest verdict on whether this is a genuine objection to reading utility off choices or just a reminder that large, non-marginal changes are messy.
