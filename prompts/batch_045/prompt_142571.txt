I'm writing a paper in political theory on whether the urban-professional outlook and the rural-working-class outlook are best understood as rival moralities or as a single morality operating under very different material conditions, and I want to test the framework on a case rather than only on arguments. The distinction I've been working with is what I'll call the substitutability condition: call an outlook substitutability-friendly when it treats the main objects of a person's obligations — a place, an industry, a craft, a set of people — as in principle replaceable, so that leaving is the default and staying is what requires a justification; call it substitutability-hostile when it treats them as non-fungible, so that staying is the default and leaving is what requires one. My working claim is that the professional-class moral vocabulary of merit, self-authorship, and developing one's gifts wherever they lead is substitutability-friendly in a way that is not a neutral moral default but a commitment fitted to lives with high mobility and low dependence on any single place or workplace, whereas the rural and working-class vocabulary of loyalty, solidarity, and inherited obligation is substitutability-hostile. The obvious reply is adaptive preference: people valorize whatever they happen to be stuck with, and the hostility is a rationalization of exit costs. I don't think that reply settles it, and I also don't think it settles it to say that the substitutability-friendly outlook simply has no vocabulary for losses that aren't opportunity costs.

I want to try the framework on Billy Elliot — the film set in a County Durham mining village during the miners' strike, in which an adolescent boy's talent for ballet is in tension with his family's and his community's commitments.
