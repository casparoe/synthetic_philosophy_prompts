I do volunteer work with a secular end-of-life group, and whoever put together our reading packet tucked in a page on Zhuangzi. The line everyone latches onto is that dying is like a caterpillar turning into a butterfly: the caterpillar is gone, but nothing bad has happened to it, it's just in another form now. The packet uses this to say that grief is basically a category error — that we're standing at the chrysalis wailing over something that has already moved on. People love it. I think it's doing way more work than it can.

Can you audit it? What plays the role of what here: the caterpillar, the chrysalis, the butterfly, the fact that it's the same animal the whole way through, the fact that it comes out with wings. Then where the mapping breaks. What I really want is what the analogy smuggles in that Zhuangzi probably wouldn't grant — a single thing that persists across the change, a direction (things getting better, getting wings), somebody's plan behind the process, the idea that the later stage is what the earlier one was for.

The reason I'm suspicious: one guy in the group has read more of this than me and he pointed out that in the butterfly bit the transformation runs both ways — the man wakes up and doesn't know whether he's the man who dreamed he was a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming it's a man — and that that's symmetric, there's no upward direction and no "real" stage, which is nothing at all like a caterpillar becoming a butterfly. I can't tell if he's right or just being clever at me. If he is right, does it kill the packet's version, or can both be in the text without too much violence?

And then the practical bit. If the caterpillar one is bad, is there a better analogy that does the job without lying — day turning into night, spring turning into autumn, moving house, handing over a shift, water freezing? For each, what does it get right about what Zhuangzi is actually after, and what does it quietly give up. Because my worry is that any analogy nice enough to comfort a room full of grieving people is going to sneak in exactly the persisting-self-and-a-plan bit that the "things transform" picture doesn't obviously have room for, and that the comforting conclusion is coming entirely from the analogy rather than from him.

Which makes me wonder whether it'd be more honest to say that his equanimity is a claim about your own death and not about other people's, and that the story where he's found drumming on a tub after his wife died is him talking himself into it rather than already being there. Is that fair? And does any of it need the cosmology to hold up — the one-stuff-that-keeps-rearranging-itself background — or does the attitude survive if you drop it? Half the room is atheist and will bounce hard off anything that sounds like "energy is never destroyed." If it does need the cosmology, say so, and tell me what the secular version of the view actually amounts to, because I have a feeling the secular version is just the old Epicurean point, or just habituation, and if that's what I'm really handing out I'd rather drop Zhuangzi and teach that instead.
