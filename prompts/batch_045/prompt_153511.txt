Hi — I'm a science writer (column on evolution and animal behaviour, mostly), and I've been asked to give the toast at the retirement dinner of a professor who retired this spring after forty-odd years as an evolutionary biologist at a big state university. She was also, for about fifteen years, the person I called whenever I had to write anything about adaptation, or altruism, or whether some animal was really "thinking". The room will be roughly half her colleagues and former grad students — people who will catch a whiff of a skyhook from three tables away — and half her family: her two kids, four grandkids between eight and fourteen, and her brother, who's a pastor and with whom she had a friendly, never-resolved forty year argument about exactly this stuff. He's doing the blessing; I'm doing the toast.

Things about her that constrain what I can say:

- She was an anti-skyhook person to the bone, Dennett-style: no cosmic purpose, no "she's watching over us", no spooky irreducibility, no "the universe is slowly waking up". A well-meaning colleague said something very close to that at her sixtieth and I watched her face.
- But she spent just as much of her career attacking what she called greedy reductionism — the move where you "explain" something by exhibiting its parts and conclude it was never really there. Her stock examples were cooperation, meaning, promise-keeping, the content of a theory. She thought "emergence" was usually a skyhook in a lab coat, and that "it's just neurons" / "it's just inclusive fitness" / "it's just memes replicating" was the same error wearing different clothes.
- Her positive view, which I'm definately going to mangle: she had a name for the middle case — I think something like "nonlocal cranes"? — for mechanisms made of nothing but the lower-level stuff, no magic at all, but which you literally cannot see from the bottom-up description, because the thing doing the work only shows up at a wider scale or over a longer time. A ratchet, a currency, a norm, a research tradition. Reduce them immediately and you lose the thing you were trying to explain; posit them as unexplained explainers and you've quietly built a skyhook. She thought most of biology is the study of these and that most of her field went greedy on them.

One story I want in there, and it's true: a student who'd already failed her class came and asked for help rewriting the thesis, and she spent something like six weeks on it over the summer, unpaid. Someone asked her why, at a department party, and she said "because she asked" and then refused to elaborate — wouldn't give a deeper story either way, not about her own goodness and not about the evolutionary roots of helpfulness. I think that refusal is the whole thing she was on about, and I want the toast built around it. Her family should hear a story about kindness; the other half of the room should hear a claim about the level at which explanation properly stops.

So: you're a professional philosopher who also writes for general audiences. Think carefully, take your time, and don't rush the drafting. Before you write anything, brainstorm: make me a list of every slot in a retirement toast where you'd naturally reach for a skyhook, and every slot where it naturally collapses into greedy reduction, and work out what goes in each slot instead. Then write the toast — five minutes, spoken, no lecture tone, grandkids shouldn't be lost. Constraints: don't define "crane" or "skyhook" out loud, and don't say "ontology" — she had a rule that if you have to say ontology at a dinner table you've already lost, and I'd rather honour the idea than name it; nothing that plays as an argument with her brother, and no ironic God-talk either, which she'd have found condescending; and it has to end on a real toast line that doesn't cheat in either direction. If it's useful, add a short note afterwards flagging which two or three lines you think the philosophers will hear differently from the family.
