I am a PhD candidate in political theory and this year I am the instructor of record for a third-year seminar, "Money, Markets and the Good Life," whose final quarter turns on Keynes as a moralist rather than as a macroeconomist. I am writing the in-class final (90 minutes, closed book) and I need one 20-mark essay question on Keynes's conception of the good life, together with a model answer and a marking scheme.

The difficulty I keep running into is that almost every question I draft on this material can be answered by reciting two things: the distinction between needs that are absolute and satiable and needs that are relative, insatiable, and satisfiable only if others go without; and the claim that the economic problem is not the permanent problem of the human race, so that solving it only reveals the real task of learning how to live well. I want a question that makes those two items the *starting point* of the answer rather than the whole of it, and that separates students who have understood the architecture of Keynes's view from students who have memorised its labels.

The crux I would like the question to force is one I have not seen handled in the secondary literature I know. Keynes holds (i) that what ultimately matters is states of mind rather than consequences — the Bloomsbury inheritance from Moore — and (ii) that the money-motive, however sordid, has been defensible because it canalises dangerous human impulses into comparatively harmless channels, so that abolishing it may release impulses that find worse outlets. These two commitments pull in different directions once abundance arrives. Two readings of "living well" can be distinguished:

- The Conversion Reading: the motivational energy formerly spent on money-making is to be redirected onto intrinsically worthwhile objects — art, friendship, knowledge, personal relations — so that the good life is continuous with the old striving, only differently aimed, and society still needs outlets for the impulses that the money-motive used to absorb.
- The Abstention Reading: the point of abundance is that activity no longer has to be justified by its product; the good life consists in contemplative, affectionate and aesthetic states of mind enjoyed for their own sake, and the virtue of the short working week is precisely that it releases us from having to show what our time produced.

The Conversion Reading handles the canalisation worry but seems to reinstate the relative, competitive, insatiable striving that Keynes thought abundance was supposed to liberate us from. The Abstention Reading avoids that but appears to leave the dangerous impulses unchannelled and to presuppose capacities for enjoyment that, on Keynes's own admission, we have not been trained to exercise. A further complication belongs in the question: in the late memoir-essay on his early beliefs, written for the Memoir Club in the late 1930s and published after his death, Keynes criticises his younger self and his circle for over-rating the power of reason and under-rating the role of habit, convention and inherited tradition in making a decent life possible. If the capacities the Abstention Reading needs are habituated rather than chosen, then the later self-criticism both rescues the earlier programme (the good life needs a cultivated tradition, not just leisure and income) and subverts it (a programme of deliberate social engineering cannot deliver a form of life that depends on unchosen conventions).

So I would like the following deliverables.

1. The question itself. Please make it self-contained: any textual point students must use — the absolute/relative contrast, the canalisation claim, the states-of-mind doctrine, the later self-criticism about reason, habit and convention — should be supplied or paraphrased in the question rather than assumed. It should be split into parts worth 8, 7 and 5 marks respectively: (a) which of my two readings is better supported by the absolute/relative distinction together with the Moorean doctrine, and why; (b) whether the later memoir-essay's self-criticism supports or undermines the programme of the 1930 essay, answered from the standpoint of the reading defended in (a); (c) an application to a stipulated case. For (c), please use this scenario, refining the wording as you see fit: two generations after the economic problem is solved, in a society with a fifteen-hour week, a generous unconditional income and universal access to education and the arts, time-use studies show that nearly all freed time is absorbed by ever-finer competitive ranking of achievements and experiences — obscure sports, collecting, micro-credentials, connoisseurship scored against peers — while self-reported satisfaction is flat; a reform movement then proposes "positional disarmament," the abolition of public ranking and comparative scoring outside the market. Does Keynes's account predict this outcome, and would positional disarmament help, hurt, or make no difference?

2. A model answer of roughly 800 words. It should take a definite position rather than survey options, and it should show how the three parts hang together: the answer to (c) should follow from the reading defended in (a) as qualified in (b).

3. A 20-mark marking scheme built around five must-hit moves, with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on how candidates typically get each wrong — the specific conflation or missing step, not a generic "lacks detail." Please also state, in one or two sentences each: the discriminator that separates an A from a strong B (the single move a B answer will not make), and the feature that caps an answer at C even when it is accurate as summary.

Two constraints. Please keep the question answerable by a student who has read only the 1930 essay on economic possibilities for our grandchildren, the late memoir-essay on his early beliefs, and Moore's ethics in outline: no further readings, and no specialist scholarship. And please do not let the marking scheme reward mere exposition of the absolute/relative distinction; if a candidate could earn full marks without engaging the canalisation claim, the question has failed.
