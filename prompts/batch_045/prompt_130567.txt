Analyze the periodic reconstruction of the Ise Grand Shrine (Ise Jingū) through the lens of the endurantism versus perdurantism debate about persistence over time. The episode is the ritual called shikinen sengū, most recently completed in 2013. The salient facts, which you should take as given, are these: the shrine is a Shinto complex dedicated above all to the sun goddess Amaterasu; since the seventh century its main sanctuaries have been rebuilt every twenty years to an identical plan, with interruptions during the fifteenth- and sixteenth-century warring-states period; in a given cycle a new sanctuary is erected on a site adjacent to the standing one; once the new structure is finished, the enshrined deities are ritually transferred from the old sanctuary to the new one, and the old sanctuary is afterwards dismantled, so that only the new sanctuary remains; the building materials for each new sanctuary are specially harvested cypress from the shrine forests; and the tradition is therefore more than a millennium old even though no single material sanctuary persists across a full cycle.

Before beginning the analysis, adopt the following stipulation, which I introduce in order to make the ontological question precise. Call a persisting artifact architecturally anchored just in case its identity over time is fixed not by continuity of matter, nor by continuous occupation of a single spatiotemporal location, but by an externally specified architectural form together with a prescribed ritual that transfers the artifact's occupancy from one material realization to a numerically distinct successor realization. On this stipulation, Ise Grand Shrine appears to be a paradigm architecturally anchored artifact.

Now carry out the analysis in both directions.

First, map the episode from the standpoint of each theory. Assume endurantism: a persisting object is wholly present at every time at which it exists, so persistence is a matter of one and the same object existing at different times despite change. Assume perdurantism: a persisting object is a four-dimensional entity extended in time, and is present at a time only by having a temporal part, or stage, at that time. For each theory, say what the shrine is and what is happening during three phases: the interval before the transfer when both the old and the new sanctuaries stand; the moment of ritual transfer; and the subsequent dismantling of the old sanctuary. Does the endurantist have to identify the shrine with something other than any individual building—a social institution, a ritual practice, or a sacred presence—in order to maintain that one and the same enduring object is wholly present throughout, and if so does that concession threaten to make endurantism about the building trivial or to collapse it into a disguised perdurantism? Does the perdurantist, who can treat the whole cycle as a single space-time worm with the old and new sanctuaries as temporal parts, owe an account of why the two sanctuaries existing together before the transfer are not simply two numerically distinct shrines, or two temporal parts of two different worms? What does each theory say the shrine authorities are doing when they transfer the deities: are they relocating one enduring continuant, or instantiating a new stage of a perduring continuant?

Second, test the theories against two further features of the episode. The first is the two-sanctuary interval: for a period, two complete sanctuaries coexist. The second is the interruption of the tradition during the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, when the rebuilding cycle lapsed. For each theory, say whether the lapse means the shrine went out of existence and was later replaced by a merely similar shrine, or whether it can allow the same shrine to persist across a gap, either as an enduring object that temporarily ceases to have a material realization or as a four-dimensional object with non-adjacent temporal parts. Be explicit about which resources each theory has to call on and where it is silent or strained.

Third, assess what the case shows about the endurantism/perdurantism dispute itself. Is the Ise Shrine a confirming instance for one side, an anomaly for both, or a kind of case the standard debate was never designed to handle? In particular, if architecturally anchored artifacts are possible, does the episode show that the standard menu is incomplete—that we need, say, a third category of reenactment, formal recurrence, or ritual re-instantiation, distinct from both endurance and perdurance? Or can one of the two standard views absorb the case without doctrinal cost, and if so, what is the cost? Where the theory does not apply or has nothing to say, say that plainly rather than forcing a verdict.

Throughout, keep the mapping disciplined: specify what counts as the persisting entity, what the relevant times and parts are, and what evidence in the episode would discriminate between the theories. Do not appeal to outside sources or citations, and do not assume any facts about the shrine beyond those supplied above.
