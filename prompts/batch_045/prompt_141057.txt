I'm in a little reading group doing metaphysics and we got completley stuck on material constitution this week — the statue-and-the-clay stuff and everything hanging off it. I want to work out what I actually think rather than just what the readings say, so here are my gut verdicts on a pile of cases, and I'd like the principle behind them if there is one, plus what it says about the case I keep flipping on. (1) Lump of clay on Tuesday, sculptor works it into a statue by Friday, someone squashes it back into a ball on Saturday. My verdicts: the lump is there the whole time, before and after; the statue only turns up at some point on Friday and is gone by Saturday; and on Friday evening there are two things on the table, not one thing with two names, because one of them would survive a squashing and the other wouldn't. (2) Ship of Theseus, planks gradually replaced, old ones reassembled into a ship: I lean toward the continuously repaired one being the original and the reassembled one being a newcomer — but it's a much weaker pull than in case 1 and I don't trust it. (3) A cat, and the thing that is the cat minus its tail, which was allegedly sitting on the mat all along: I want to say yes, there really were two things there sharing all their parts bar one, and after the tail comes off there's just one left. But I absolutely do not want to say there are three hundred cat-ish objects on that mat differing by a whisker each, so I need a cutoff somewhere and I have no idea where it goes. (4) Here's the pair I can't reconcile: a bronze statue that's melted down and recast from the same metal in the same mould — I say the original statue is destroyed and the new one is a different statue, even though nothing you could detect distinguishes them. But a Lego statue taken apart brick by brick, posted to another country and rebuilt exactly — I want to say that's the same statue reassembled. Both involve a gap in which nothing statue-shaped exists, and the same matter coming back into the same arrangement, and I give opposite verdicts. (5) A coral-ish thing that someone grows over years by depositing mineral onto a frame until it's statue-shaped, versus a carved one with the identical final shape: the grown one I want to say isn't a statue at all, just a lump with an odd history; but if you then sand it into a different shape, I think that object survives, whereas sanding the carved one into the same new shape feels like destroying it.

My own stab at a principle, which I'm sure is broken: two coinciding things are distinct only if their persistence conditions come apart, and a thing's persistence conditions are fixed by the sort of thing it is, where the sort is fixed not by its shape or matter but by how it came to be and what it's for — lumps tolerate any reshaping, statues tolerate only a bit, and things that came to be by being assembled tolerate coming apart and going back together but not being dissolved and recast. Please test that against the cases above, repair it or replace it, and give me: no more than three candidate principles, each stated in one precise sentence; for the best one, a clause-by-clause note on which of my cases forces each clause; its verdicts on two or three cases I haven't listed (a caterpillar and its butterfly? a river? a company?); what it says about the one I go back and forth on — a clay statue put in a kiln and fired into ceramic, which feels to me like the same statue but also like the clay has been destroyed; and an honest account of which of my verdicts it can't save. Write it in British English and keep the whole answer under 500 words.
