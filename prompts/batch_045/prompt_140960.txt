I'm about eight months into a conversion master's in archaeology (my first degree was in philosophy, so I keep annoying the dig-side people by asking what everything means), and there's a habit in the literature that I can't work out whether to be impressed or appalled by. The same writers who'll infer, from grave goods in a Neolithic burial, that the community definately had some belief about an afterlife — a belief claim, not just a behaviour claim — will insist in the next chapter that belief is invisible in the archaeological record, that you can't get from pots to thoughts, and that when they can't work out what an object was for they'll file it under "ritual", and then turn around and use that filing as positive evidence that there was a religious institution on site. And there's the slogan everyone repeats at conferences, that absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence, which gets wheeled out to defend one inference and quietly dropped when the arguement is "no palaces, no writing, so no state". My supervisor says this is just ordinary evidential caution and I say isn't absence of evidence simply evidence of absence, that's more or less what Bayes' theorem gives you, and she says I'm being crude about it, and I genuinely can't tell whether she's right.

So what I want is the implicit epistemology of the whole thing reconstructed. Does the practice, as it actually stands, presuppose a causal or trace-based theory of evidence, where the only legitimate move is from an effect back to its cause, so that beliefs as such are out of reach and only the behaviour that leaves traces is in reach? Or does it only presuppose something thinner — that detection and preservation probabilities in real sites are usually so low that absence inferences aren't licensed — something a Bayesian, an error-statistician worried about underpowered tests, and an inference-to-the-best-explanation person could each sign up to for quite different reasons? And where does the "ritual" bucket sit in all this: is calling a functionally unexplained object ritual strictly circular in a way that matters, or is it just a labelling convention that does no real evidential work? I especially want to know which of these commitments the practice strictly needs and which it merely gets along with, because I suspect several quite different theories of evidence would all generate exactly the caution I'm being told to have.

Here's the bit I'd like you to push on. A couple of us on the course drafted what we call the Parity Protocol, and I want to know whether adopting it would wreck the discipline or just make it honest. It says: (1) any inference from absence has to come with an explicit detection model — if X had been true, what would we expect to find, how likely is it to survive, how likely is it to be recognised and published — with numbers you'd actually defend; (2) the word "ritual" is banned from reports except where there's independent positive evidence, and unassigned objects get reported as "function unknown"; (3) every belief-ascription has to be tagged with what would falsify it, on pain of not counting as a claim at all. Is the protocol coherent, does any clause of it collapse into a scepticism nobody could work under, and which ordinary archaeological inferences would actually fall over if you imposed it? And if you were writing it up: would you say the discipline needs a thick, dispositional, behaviour-linked notion of belief to make its practice work, or can it get everything it wants with a deflationary one? Please write in British English, and sorry if this is a bit of a ramble — I've been staring at it all week.
