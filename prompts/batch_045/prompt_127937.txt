Me and my mate Dave got into a massive argument about this in the pub last night and its still bugging me, so I want a second opinion.

Background: my mum died in the spring, and the hospice that looked after her was genuinely incredible — the nurses there, the whole place. I've got about 18k put aside that I'd decided to give away — not life-changing money but not nothing — and I'd more or less settled on splitting it between that hospice and one of those malaria bednet charities where you can see roughly what a fiver does. Then Dave tells me I've got the whole thing backwards.

His argument, as near as I could follow it through three pints: every option sets off chains of effects that run centuries out — the kids who don't die of malaria grow up and have kids, those kids shift the economy, emissions, elections, who knows what — and those downstream effects are orders of magnitude bigger than the bit I can actually see. And we have basically no idea whether they're good or bad; for every story where the extra people make things better there's an equally plausible one where they make things worse. So the part of the calculation I actually have evidence about is a rounding error, and I'm congratulating myself on doing arithmetic while the real number is invisible and could have either sign.

Then he pushed it two steps further and that's where I lost my grip on it. First: if that's right, then the bednets aren't obviously better than the hospice, or than my nephew's school, or than blowing it on a boat — once the huge invisible part dominates, the differences between the visible bits stop mattering, and what's left is just "do something decent you can stand behind and don't pretend you've calculated it." Second, and this is the bit that felt sneaky: he said the hospice money is actually *safer* precisely because it's small and local. Five grand to one hospice in one town sends out a smaller ripple than five grand that changes whether a bunch of villages have a malaria problem at all, so there's less unknowable stuff stuck to it. I said that seems backwards — tiny differences in a messy system can end up enormous — and he said sure, but the size of the unknowable part still scales with the size of what you actually did, so if you're guessing about the rest anyway you should just rank the options by the part you can see, and that's an argument for bednets over the hospice, not the other way round, which is when I realised I'd lost track of whose side he was on.

I've got three objections and I can't tell if any of them land. One: if the invisible part really is huge and fifty-fifty good-or-bad, then not giving, or giving to the hospice, has exactly the same invisible part hanging off it — so his argument can't be a reason to prefer one option, it's a reason to shrug at all of them, and then I still need some other way to choose. Two: even if I grant him that the invisible bit averages out to zero, the spread doesn't have to be the same — deliberately picking the option with the biggest possible ripple feels like buying a ticket where the prize might be a catastrophe, and I don't see why "I can't predict it" should make me neutral between a small gamble and a massive one. Three: his fallback, "do what a decent person would do and stop pretending to calculate," sounds like giving up — except it doesn't sound crazy either, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's a real answer or just what people say when the answer is inconvenient.

So: which of us is closer to right, where exactly does his reasoning break (if it does), and what should I actually do with the money? I'd rather have the argument than a pat on the head.
