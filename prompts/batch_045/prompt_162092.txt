I co-convene a fortnightly methods seminar that sits across a biology department and a philosophy department, and two of its regulars have been circling each other since the first session: one is a behavioural ecologist who treats optimality modelling and reverse-engineering as the correct default strategy for explaining any trait, and the other is a philosopher of biology working in the tradition of Gould and Lewontin's 1979 critique of the adaptationist programme, who thinks that default is a licence for unfalsifiable storytelling. I want to stop them talking past each other by asking them to produce one text they could both put their names to. Please draft it: an adversarial-collaboration joint statement between a self-described methodological adaptationist and a constraint-centred critic in the spandrels tradition. Do not cite anything or point me toward the literature — I want the arguments themselves, and the empirical case below is invented precisely so that nobody can win the exchange by citing a study.

Format it as a numbered section of claims both can sign, followed by a disagreements table whose columns are: the disagreement stated neutrally; the formulation each side would accept for its own view; whether the dispute is genuine or merely verbal; the crux; a test, case or argument both accept as bearing on that crux; and each side's prediction recorded in advance. I want the agreed claims to include some that do real work rather than the usual both-selection-and-constraint-matter platitude: what each side grants about the evidential status of a well-fitting optimality model, about whether a non-selective null model for trait evolution is even formulable, and about the origination-versus-maintenance distinction, on which a trait can be currently useful and still have arisen as a byproduct. I also want the document to sort out the frequency question, since the claim that most traits are adaptations is ill-posed until you say how traits are individuated and sampled and until you pry apart architectural byproducts, later co-optations, developmental constraints and drift-fixed noise. Have the table say explicitly which disagreements are verbal — I suspect the word adaptationism is doing at least two different jobs — and which are substantive.

Use this invented case as the shared test: a clade of 190 arboreal lizard species, 181 of which carry a helmet-like bony expansion of the skull roof. One hypothesis is that the helmet was built by selection for defence against conspecific biting; the other is that it is a spandrel of selection for bite force, later co-opted for display, because skull-roof area is geometrically entailed by jaw-adductor attachment area. Both parties accept as bearing on it: phylogenetic comparative correlations between helmet volume and adductor attachment area with body size controlled; the handful of species in which the two dissociate, with large adductors and no helmet or the reverse; whether helmet shape fits the optimum predicted by defence demands or by the geometric entailment; and a developmental-ablation result showing whether helmet formation depends on the muscle precursor. Record what each side predicts for each, and what each would concede if the result went against it. Finally, add a short closing section on where this document is most likely to be too tidy: which signed claim is load-bearing and would collapse under which finding, which side is more likely to rationalise away a disconfirming result, and whether any disagreement you marked as verbal is really substantive after all.
