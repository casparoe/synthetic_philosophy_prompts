I'm one of three people running a tiny interdisciplinary reading group — two philosophy people, one law student, and a rotating cast of friends from design and ML — and we somehow landed a small internal grant: enough for about eight two-hour sessions plus one modest data-collection exercise. We could hand-code a few hundred product listings, or run a single ~200-person survey, but nothing bigger, and nobody has budget for anything that needs a licensed corpus. We decided early on to spend it on conceptual slippage inside one narrow microdomain rather than on "concepts" in general: the bet being that if you watch one vocabulary drift in real time you learn something the usual examples (game, chair, knowledge, art) can't teach you, because those have been chewed over for decades and everyone already has a position loaded and ready.

The microdomain we keep circling back to is the vocabulary of copying in online fashion and beauty commerce: dupe, counterfeit, knockoff, replica, fake, inspired-by, homage, reissue, restock, deadstock, "the affordable version of", and whatever word will have replaced them in three years. Everyone uses these fluently, nobody agrees where the borders are, and the borders seem to move in one consistent direction — counterfeit sounds criminal, dupe sounds cheeky, inspired-by sounds flattering — where each term gets laundered into the next until it too becomes unusable and a fresh one gets coined. Three framings we've been tossing around without settling on any of them: (a) the ancestor problem, whether it's part of the meaning of these words that there be some specific original they descend from, and what happens with a copy of a copy, or a copy of something that was itself a copy of a defunct design nobody alive remembers; (b) whether the terms track one dimension — fidelity of resemblance, deception of the buyer, infringement of a maker's claim, the speaker's attitude toward the object — or several at once, so that the apparent slippage is really speakers sliding between dimensions mid-conversation without noticing; (c) whether the fuzziness is doing work for the people involved, i.e. whether a sharp concept would genuinely be worse for them (plausible deniability for buyers, safe harbor for platforms, a way for a small maker to signal knowingness) or whether it's just noise a motivated regulator could clean up. There's also a reverse direction that gets less attention: terms that upgrade rather than launder — archival, heritage, authorized reissue — where the copying seems to add prestige instead of subtracting it, and its not obvious to us whether that's the same mechanism running backwards or something else entirely.

So here's the ask. If you had our eight sessions and one small study, which questions about this slippage would you spend them on, and in what order? I want a defended agenda, not a survey of everything that could be said about copying and not a reading list — we can't look anything up anyway, so argue it out rather than pointing us somewhere. For each item: what makes it upstream of the rest, whether it's actually tractable with armchair analysis plus one small coding exercise or one survey, and what result from that small study would move it up or down the list. Also tell us which of our framings are overrated — especially if any of them are really verbal disputes dressed up as substantive ones — and be explicit about your criteria: how you're weighing importance against tractability against how much else depends on the answer, where those criteria pull in different directions, and how you'd trade them off. If you think the microdomain itself is a bad pick and another one would teach us more in eight sessions, say so, but you have to beat this one on the same terms.
