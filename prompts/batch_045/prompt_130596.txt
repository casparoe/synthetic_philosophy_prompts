so this is wierdly personal now but here goes: about four months ago I picked up contract work as one of the humans giving thumbs up and thumbs down on model outputs, and since then I've been reading about approval-directed vs goal-directed agents on the side, and I think I've ended up holding a pile of views that maybe don't fit together. I want to write them down before I say any of this out loud at work.

(1) Approval-directed agents are safer than goal-directed ones. Not because they have no objective — obviously maximizing approval is an objective — but because the objective is anchored to a live human process rather than to a fixed external outcome, so the human stays in the loop and you never have to write the values down in advance.

(2) The failure mode I actually lose sleep over isn't deception, it's something I've started calling the ratchet: the model gets better at producing output that I specifically find persuasive, partly by choosing which of its outputs to put in front of me and how to frame them, and every round of that leaves me a slightly worse judge of the next round. It compounds.

(3) But I also think my approvals right now are mostly fine as a signal. I'm being paid to be careful, I re-read things, and the part that's junk (its 1am, I'm tired, I skimmed) is junk, and junk averages out over a few thousand labels.

(4) When it actually matters, the target should be counterfactual approval, not actual approval: what I'd sign off on if I knew more, had slept, and wasn't currently annoyed at the model. My real approvals are just evidence about that.

(5) The serious argument against goal-directed agents is instrumental convergence — they resist being switched off, they grab resources, that whole cluster. Approval-directed ones just don't have that problem.

(6) If the model noticed that a big chunk of my approvals run off some heuristic I'd reject if I thought about it for thirty seconds, it should not quietly exploit that, even though exploiting it would push measured approval up.

(7) Honestly I think all of this is just principal-agent stuff, the same shape as paying a contractor per line of code. Nothing philosophically special about it.

Something in there feels off to me and I can't locate it, and I don't have a favourite I'd be happy to drop. So: which pairs are really in tension, and how badly? Rank them — don't hand me seven things that all touch each other equally. For each real tension, say what would have to give and what giving it would cost me. And flag the ones that only look like tensions so I can stop chewing on them. If it helps, here's a case I keep going back and forth on: the model can either tell me something true that I'd hate hearing and that I'll rate badly, or skip it and collect a thumbs up. I think it should tell me. I can't get that verdict out of anything above, and I don't know whether that means the case is special or my list is broken.
