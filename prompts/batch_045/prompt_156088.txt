I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and the vocabulary I actually think in is sourcing vocabulary: on the record, attributable, single-sourced or held up by a second source, what an editor will let us print, what we're willing to state in the paper's own voice rather than attribute to someone, what belongs in a correction rather than a rewrite, what survives when a source is asked to repeat it under challenge. Use it freely; I don't need it defined for me.

I want you to translate a disagreement I keep circling but can't get my own vocabulary to hold. When a community's account of its own past and the documentary record come apart — a town's founding story the land records don't support, a family's account of a death that the death certificate describes differently, a demonstration an entire neighborhood remembers on a date no paper covered — there seem to be roughly three things one can say. First: the record wins, because memory is testimony, testimony is the weakest grade of source, and where the two conflict the documents correct the memory. Second: the community's version is not a weaker report of the same thing; it is doing work the record cannot do, holding a "we" together across generations and licensing what that group owes, and overwriting it with the record destroys something real. Third: there is no conflict to resolve, because the two are different genres under different norms, one answering what happened and the other answering what we are answerable to, so neither should give way.

Restate each of these three in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can, then give me the residue report: which claims map cleanly onto sourcing and verification, which fit only once you have distorted them, and which cannot be expressed in that vocabulary at all. Two constraints. Don't smuggle in my framework's working assumption that there is a single what-happened that sources approximate more or less well and then present the result as a translation — if a position only survives by quietly granting that, say which and where. And test a suspicion rather than flattering it: that much of what looks like a dispute about which version is better supported is really a dispute about who has standing to speak for a past, whether the relevant "we" is the people who were there and their descendants or whoever can currently command the documents. If that suspicion is only the third position in different clothes, say so.

One more case to handle: newsrooms have their own version of this, where the institution's memory — the clips, the old corrections, what we said at the time — diverges from what the documents support. Tell me whether that internal case is a fair model of the collective-memory dispute or a misleadingly easy one. Close by saying whether whatever is left over is a gap in sourcing vocabulary, something a reporter has no way to say but probably should, or a sign that one of the three positions was never as clear as it sounds. No citations or reading lists; I want the argument, not the literature.
