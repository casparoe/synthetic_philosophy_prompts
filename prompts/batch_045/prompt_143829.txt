I'm applying for a one-year dissertation fellowship and need a two-page proposal for a project on Kripkenstein's skeptical argument about rule-following. I have a half-formed idea and I want it turned into a proposal, not gently affirmed.

The standard presentation of the argument leans on the finitude of a speaker's past usage: however many additions I have performed, both plus and quus fit them all, so nothing in my history, dispositions, or mental states settles which rule I meant. Call this the underdetermination argument. My thought is that there is a second, structurally different route to the same skeptical conclusion, which does not use the finitude of past usage at all. Take a rule with a bounded domain: the rule a shop uses to price an order of 1 to 99 identical units, say, where the domain is fixed by what the shop sells and never extends past 99. Now take two rules that agree on every case in that domain but differ in how they would generalize beyond it: add two, versus add two unless the order exceeds ninety-nine, in which case charge five. A speaker who has applied the rule to every single case in its domain — who has used it exhaustively, with no case left over — still appears not to have settled which of these two rules she is following, and no further application can help, because the domain is used up. Call this the collapse argument. My suspicion is that the standard taxonomy of responses — those that look for a meaning-constituting fact and those that reinterpret meaning in terms of what is assertible — is built to answer the underdetermination argument and silently misfires on the collapse argument, and that some proposed solutions, especially ones appealing to dispositions or to what a community would go on to do, address only one of the two.

What I want is the proposal document itself. Give it a precise statement of the question — I take it to be whether the two arguments are genuinely independent, and, if they are, whether the available responses divide differently across them than the usual taxonomy suggests — then say why the existing positions leave that question open, describe the method (what I would actually do across twelve months, and why that method rather than the obvious alternatives such as a critical survey or a new solution of my own), set out the possible conclusions and what would decide between them, and add an honest note on whether this is a year's work or three years' work.

Two constraints. First, no bibliography, no citations, no literature review by name: refer to positions and arguments only, and I will fill in the references. Second, commit. State a definite hypothesis about whether the collapse argument is independent of the underdetermination argument and about which kinds of response survive it, choose one deliverable rather than offering a menu, and give the reasons the proposal would actually defend. In particular, address the objection that worries me most: that the collapse argument is the underdetermination argument in disguise, because the domain restriction is itself rule-governed and my bounded rule is secretly an unbounded one, so that quus-style moves can be run on the boundary too. If that objection is fatal, say so inside the proposal rather than writing around it.
