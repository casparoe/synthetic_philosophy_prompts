I write fiction, and a draft of my current novel has got me into an argument I cannot settle, so I am writing to ask for an audit rather than an opinion. My method as a novelist is to keep, for each character, what I privately call a ledger: the rows are situations I might put her in, the columns are the things she might do, and I fill the cells long before I write the scene. I also impose on myself a sealed-scene rule — I draft the situation, then let the character act, and I do not go back and delete or override what she did in order to make the plot work. I mentioned at a reading that this seemed to me to be exactly the Molinist picture of middle knowledge: I know, prior to any decision of mine to write the scene, what Beatrice would freely do if I placed her in a burning cathedral with the relic unguarded; my knowing it is not what makes her do it, and the knowledge is what lets me choose which scenes to write. A philosopher in the audience said that this is both the best available analogy for middle knowledge and the one that quietly destroys it, because what licenses my knowledge of Beatrice is that I invented her — maker's knowledge, which is precisely the sort of thing a Molinist cannot attribute to God without collapsing creaturely freedom into authorship.

The parts where I suspect the analogy is doing real work, and where I suspect it is cheating, are these. Most of my ledger cells I fill from the inside: I ask what Beatrice is like and the answer comes, and those cells feel like knowledge grounded in her character — which, I take it, is what a Molinist would classify as natural knowledge of a creature's dispositions rather than middle knowledge proper. But some cells I can only fill from the story: I simply see that she says yes, I could not derive it from her psychology, and if I alter one apparently irrelevant detail of the scene — whether the letter arrived that morning, whether the door was already open — the cell flips in a way I cannot explain. It is those brute cells that feel like the counterfactuals of creaturely freedom, and I do not know whether my ability to fill them is a genuine analogue of God's middle knowledge or just an authorial intuition that would not survive being asked for a truthmaker. There are further disanalogies I can list but not weigh: I am a cause of Beatrice and God is a cause of us; Beatrice is not morally responsible for what she does in my scenes, or is only fictionally so; I stand outside the book's time and can redraft, whereas God's knowledge is supposed to be eternally and non-successively possessed; my ledger is finite, vague at the margins, and revisable in a way divine knowledge is not; and there is a whole class of cells whose antecedent includes something the character does not bring to the scene at all — an offer she did not earn and could not have produced — which in the old Jesuit and Dominican quarrel about grace was the hardest case, and which my ledger seems to handle least well.

What I would like is the following. Give me the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of God, of the free creature, of creation, of a feasible world, of the trichotomy between natural, middle and free knowledge, and of the grounding objection. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the use I am making of the analogy from the ones that are merely decorative — I care about whether authorial causation, fictional moral responsibility, and the possibility of redrafting are load-bearing, and not at all about whether my ledger is finite. Say whether the analogy, honestly audited, supports the Molinist or the anti-Molinist: in particular, whether the brute-cell phenomenon is a real illustration of what middle knowledge would have to be, or whether it is just a disguised form of inference from character plus a writer's willingness to stipulate, in which case my ledger shows only that middle knowledge is either reducible to natural knowledge or ungrounded. If there is a better analogy in the vicinity — a theatre director who does not write the script, an improvisation partner who knows your style, a composer of chess problems — say what it buys and what it costs. Finally, tell me how confident you are in that verdict and where your reasoning is most likely to fail: which feature of Molinism, or of my own practice, would have to be different for you to reverse it, and which step in the argument you would look at first if someone told you the whole audit was wrong.
