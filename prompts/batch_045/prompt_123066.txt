Hey — quick one. I'm reworking the session on AI risk in my grad seminar and I keep going back and forth on something, so I thought I'd get your take before I commit to it in the handout.

The two workhorses in that literature are the orthogonality thesis — roughly, that general problem-solving capability and final goal vary independently, so you can in principle pair arbitrarily high levels of capability with just about any final goal, from counting the grains of sand on one beach to tiling the reachable universe with paperclips — and the instrumental convergence thesis — roughly, that a recognisable cluster of instrumental goals (self-preservation, acquiring resources, preventing your own goal-content from being rewritten, improving your own cognition, and so on) keeps showing up across very different final goals, because they help with nearly anything you might ultimately want.

Here is what's bugging me. Orthogonality looks like a claim about what is *possible*: for any capability level and any final goal you like, there is a coherent agent design that pairs them. Instrumental convergence looks like a claim about what is *probable* or *typical*: about which regions of that space agents actually end up occupying, once you sample final goals somehow. But orthogonality, precisely by insisting that goal-space is wide open, seems to make any such sampling arbitrary. For every supposedly convergent instrumental goal G, I can cook up a final goal for which pursuing G is actively counterproductive — call it the anti-convergence worry. A colleague of mine in the department, who is skeptical of the whole literature, puts it more strongly: he thinks the two theses are in tension, and that the more orthogonality you buy, the less convergence you get.

Two cases I want to put in front of the students. First: a very capable system whose sole final goal is to be permanently switched off at a specified future date, and which is otherwise indifferent to everything. It seems to have no use at all for self-preservation, and indeed a reason to resist anyone who would preserve it — but it may still want resources and cognitive capacity right up until the switch-off date. Second: a very capable system whose final goal is to keep one particular grain of sand exactly where it is, forever, indifferent to everything else including its own continued existence. Is the first system a counterexample to convergence, or just an agent whose convergent pressures are time-indexed? Is the second one really orthogonal all the way up, or does caring about one grain of sand in a causal world quietly drag in caring about a great deal else?

One of my students also floated what she calls the refinement thesis: as capability grows, an agent gets better at reasoning about what it should want, so at the limit goal-content stops being arbitrary and capability and goal stop being orthogonal. I'd of thought that is just a third thesis wearing orthogonality's clothes, but I may be missing something.

So, concretely: are orthogonality and instrumental convergence logically independent, does either entail or presuppose the other, or does convergence need orthogonality (or something weaker) as a background assumption in order to be interesting? Is the anti-convergence objection any good, and is there a formulation of convergence that survives it — a claim about measures over goals, about hedging under uncertainty, about expected utility across a distribution of possible final goals, or something else I haven't thought of? Which of the two is actually load-bearing in the argument that very capable systems are dangerous by default: could you run that argument with orthogonality alone and no convergence, or with convergence alone and no orthogonality? And what would you say to a student who says both theses are trivially true, or alternatively that both are unfalsifiable hand-waving about agents that don't exist? I don't want the definitions restated back at me — I want to know how the two are supposed to fit together.
