I convene a graduate seminar on personal identity, and our last meeting is on Hofstadter's strange-loop account of the self: the *I* as a self-referential pattern thrown up by a symbol-bearing substrate — a real pattern, not a mere façon de parler, but one whose edges are loose enough that something of a person can persist, in graded amounts, inside the loops of the people who knew them. I read his willingness to grade souls by their extent as a way of measuring how far a loop has spread, and I would rather the poem rested on no more exegesis than that. I want to close the session by reading a poem instead of a summary, and I have a fairly exact idea of what it must do.

The case is this. Ray is dying slowly; over forty years of marriage his wife Marian has become a second home for his patterns — she finishes his sentences in his cadence, reaches for the brand of tea he always bought, hears his side of an argument form itself inside her own head. Ray, for his part, finds his own loop thinning and, in the thinning, finds that a good deal of what he calls himself now seems to sit outside his skull. Two first-person voices, in other words, with a disputed boundary between them.

Please write a sestina: six unrhymed six-line stanzas plus a three-line envoi, in a five-beat line varied enough to speak aloud, taking roughly two and a half minutes to read, British spelling throughout, no archaic diction, neither speaker named or tagged anywhere in the poem. The constraints that matter to me are these.

1. Six end-words, unchanged throughout, rotated by the standard sestina permutation, so that no word belongs to any one stanza: I want the form itself to be the argument that a pattern can be wholly present in each of several places without any of them owning it. Two of the six are fixed — *mine* and *part*. Of the other four, one must name the physical substrate (brain, skull, nerve, head) and one must do double duty as both noun and verb (loop, leak, hold, name, turn). No end-word may be one that could only plausibly be spoken by one of the two voices.
2. The first three stanzas should alternate between Ray and Marian, and the reader should be able to tell which is which from concrete detail alone. Each voice needs at least two physical particulars — a gesture, a domestic habit, an unfinished sentence — since the whole point is that this is a claim about patterns in matter, not about ghosts.
3. By the fourth stanza the voices should have converged, so that it is genuinely unclear who is speaking; the envoi should leave it undeterminable whether one person or two is speaking. That convergence is the leakage claim, and I want it earned rather than asserted.
4. This is not a fission case, and it must not drift into one. We spent week three on Parfit-style branching, where two full continuer-candidates make identity indeterminate because each is equally the person. Here what is in Marian is a fragment, a dilution, the tail of a loop — not a rival complete continuer. If any line implies two whole persons where there was one, the poem has stopped being about Hofstadter and gone back to week three.
5. One stanza must ask a yes-or-no question about whether he is still there, and must answer it with a quantity rather than a yes or a no. Survival on this view is a matter of more or less, and I want the reader to feel the discomfort of wanting a verdict and being given a measure.
6. One stanza — I would put it fifth — must give the deflationary reading its due: that what Marian carries is only a model of Ray, a simulation, a very good impersonation by her own machinery, and not him at all. Neither refute nor concede it. The difficulty is that if the *I* just is the pattern, it is unclear what could make the difference between the pattern's continuing and a copy of the pattern running elsewhere; I want that unclarity registered honestly, not resolved by a rhetorical flourish.
7. No consolation, no mysticism, no merging-into-the-one, no bereavement cliché. The last three lines should leave open whether this is one *I* in two brains, two *I*s, or one *I* plus a model of it.

Give me the poem first, then a single section titled **Notes on the choices** (250–350 words) covering: which six end-words you chose and why; where the boundary between the voices falls and where, precisely, it stops being trackable; what the envoi settles and what it leaves unsettled; and any point at which the sestina's rotation forced you to compromise the philosophy — and, if it did, whether you would rather have kept the philosophy and broken the form.
