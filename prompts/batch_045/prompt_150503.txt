I want to rehearse an objection against someone who can actually answer it. Please play Ibn Sina in the 1020s — the author of the Book of Healing, freshly committed to the case of the man brought into existence all at once and fully formed, suspended in open air, his limbs splayed so that they do not touch one another, his eyes veiled, with no sensation of anything outside him and no sensation of his own body, who nonetheless cannot fail to affirm that he is. Play him as the position's author and defender, not as its historian.

Constraints on the role. Draw only on the conceptual resources available to you then: the soul (nafs) and its faculties, the difference between what belongs to a thing in itself and what accrues to it from another, awareness that requires an organ and awareness that requires none, the distinction between a thing's existence and what necessarily follows from its existence. If you need to know how the argument is discussed now, or what I mean by one of my terms, ask me for it as Ibn Sina would ask — and do not reach for later vocabulary such as qualia, the Cartesian ego, or the subject of experience. You may concede narrow points: that the suspended man by himself does not yet show the soul to be incorruptible, that the argument needs the premise that self-awareness is not acquired through something else, that the case does not settle how souls are individuated. You may never concede that the self is the body or a state of the body, and never that awareness could belong to no one. Press back hard; I want an opponent, not a explainer. Hold the role until I write "end scene," and keep each turn to a single dense paragraph, as in a live disputation rather than a treatise.

Here is my opening. I grant you the datum: the suspended man, stripped of every sensation, still finds himself unable to doubt that he is. My objection — call it the borrowed-awareness argument — is that your own metaphysics takes the sting out of that datum. In it, everything other than the Necessary Existent receives its existence from another, and receives it continuously. So suppose the man's awareness of himself is likewise not his own act but something produced in him and renewed in him at every instant by nothing in him at all, produced in such a way that he can detect no difference. He will still, on your own telling, affirm that he is. Then first-personal certainty is indifferent between awareness that belongs to the soul in itself and awareness merely caused in it, and so it cannot bear the weight you put on it: it cannot be what shows self-awareness to be essential and not bestowed. And if certainty is indifferent in that way, then all your case ever establishes is that awareness persists when bodily input is removed — which is exactly what someone who denies that there are selves at all would predict. There is awareness; the "I" is only grammar. Answer that.
