Below are a question from an upper-level undergraduate formal epistemology exam and one student's answer to it. Please grade the answer.

**Question (three parts, roughly equal weight).**

1. State the Dutch book theorem and the converse Dutch book theorem, and explain what each is supposed to establish about degrees of belief. Be explicit about which of the probability axioms the argument secures.
2. Set out the diachronic Dutch book argument for conditionalization. What exactly must be assumed about the agent's foresight of her own updating dispositions, and what becomes of the argument if that assumption fails?
3. Assess the objection that the argument, even if it works, yields at most a requirement of practical rationality and not a requirement of epistemic rationality. Is that objection answered by restating the bets in units of utility rather than dollars?

**Student's answer.**

Degrees of belief can be measured by betting behavior. Call the price an agent regards as fair for a bet that pays $1 if H is true and $0 otherwise her betting quotient q(H). The Dutch book theorem says that if an agent's betting quotients violate the probability axioms — non-negativity, normalization, finite additivity — then a bookie who is free to choose which side of each bet to take can assemble a set of bets, each of which the agent accepts at her own fair prices, that guarantees her a net loss whatever happens. The converse, de Finetti's coherence result, says that if her quotients satisfy the axioms no such set exists. Together the theorems show that the probability axioms are exactly the consistency constraints on partial belief: incoherence is not a small flaw but a kind of practical self-defeat, since the agent will predictably hand over money for nothing. Note that only finite additivity is delivered; countable additivity would need a separate argument, and de Finetti himself doubted it.

The same construction yields an argument for conditionalization. Let the agent have credences at t1 and then learn E at t2. Her t1 and t2 credences can be treated as one package: if P2(H) is not equal to P1(H given E), that package violates additivity somewhere, so a book can be made against her — a diachronic Dutch book. Conditionalization is therefore rationally required, and required however the learning happens. It is sometimes objected that the bookie must know at t1 how the agent will respond to E; but this costs the argument nothing, since a real bookie can simply wait and see which way she jumps and then take the other side.

It follows that coherence is the whole of what rationality demands of credences. Once an agent is coherent, no betting system can be run against her, so there is nothing further for a Bayesian to require.

One objection should be dismissed. Critics say the argument is merely pragmatic, tying rationality to money. But that objection really amounts to the observation that dollars have diminishing marginal utility, so an agent may decline a bet she judges fair. The reply is standard: denominate the bets in utilities instead of dollars, or let the stakes be arbitrarily small, and the argument goes through unchanged. Coherence is therefore a genuine requirement of epistemic rationality, established by the same argument.
