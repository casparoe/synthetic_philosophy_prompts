So I host this tiny philosphy podcast — mostly two people talking on a walk, half the episodes have wind noise — and next week's guest defends a neo-Cartesian line about animal minds, and I'd like questions that actually bite instead of the usual "but surely your dog feels pain" routine.

Her position, as I understand it: she denies that animals have phenomenal consciousness at all — nothing it is like to be a cow — but she *does* grant them what she calls bodily sensation, the kind of state Descartes was willing to allow the beasts: a bit of the animal's machinery registering damage, organizing avoidance, without there being a subject that the damage is happening to. She then uses this to keep saying that factory farming is morally atrocious, on the grounds that it wrecks a teleologically organized organism, and she says the harm doesn't need anyone to be home for it to be real harm. And because she thinks Descartes' language-and-reason test was a bad test, she replaces it with what she calls the unity requirement: a mind is a thing whose states all show up in one standpoint, where a desire can be outweighed by a memory or a pain, and she argues animals and current AI systems both fail it while they go on behaving exactly as if they didn't.

Draft me eight questions in the order I should ask them, with a follow-up to each one keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. I want at least one each on:

- the unity requirement eating its own children — human newborns, people with severe aphasia, someone mid-seizure: do they lose their minds until they can report again? Her likeliest move is that they're the kind of thing that has the capacity even when it's not exercised, so build the question so that reply costs her something.
- whether "bodily sensation with no subject" is just pain with the lights off, and how it differs from a thermostat or a Venus flytrap registering damage. She'll probably say the difference is teleological organization, so I need the follow-up to make her say what work the organization is doing.
- how the moral horror survives: if nothing is bad *for* the animal and its death isn't a loss to anyone, what exactly is the wrong-making property, and is she committed to saying a painless death in a well-run sanctuary is fine?
- the design worry: why would the machinery be built to register damage so exquisitely if there's no one for the registration to matter to — and, related, why our instinct that animals suffer is so universal if it's simply false. She's fond of saying that instinct is just the very prejudice she's dissolving, so give me a way to press that without letting her repeat it.
- something that could actually count as evidence *against* her view, given she's suspicious of behaviour as evidence for minds.

She also has a habit of answering hard cases with "that just restates the intuition I'm explaining away." One question near the end should make that move unavailable to her. If we're running over, tell me which two you'd cut and why.
