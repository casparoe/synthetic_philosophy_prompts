I'm a journalist — I cover science and ideas — and I'm working on a piece about what a *true* conspiracy does to the credibility of the false ones growing right next to it. I keep circling back to Tuskegee and I want you to think the case through with me properly, not hand me the standard take.

Facts as I've got them, so we're on the same page: from 1932 the US Public Health Service ran a study in Macon County, Alabama, on 600 poor Black sharecroppers — 399 of them with latent syphilis, 201 as controls. They were never told the diagnosis; they were told they were being treated for "bad blood". Penicillin had become the standard treatment by the mid-1940s and none of the infected men got it. It was meant to run six months and ran forty. People who knew included doctors, nurses, a Black nurse who was the study's main point of contact with the men, local officials, a historically Black college, and eventually the CDC. It ended in 1972 when, on the version I've read, someone inside the service who had been getting nowhere with internal complaints for years finally took it to a reporter. Afterwards: a lawsuit and a settlement, a presidential apology in 1997, the Belmont Report and the whole IRB apparatus, and a durable collapse of trust in medicine among Black Americans.

Here's the lens. The standard skeptical toolkit for conspiracy claims leans on two moves, as far as I can tell. One is the leakiness move: secrets get harder to keep as the number of people who'd have to keep them goes up and the cost to any one of them of defecting goes down. The other is the self-sealing move: you diagnose by what the claim does with counter-evidence — if denials, retractions, silence and absent evidence all get metabolised as further confirmation, you're not dealing with a testable claim anymore.

I want to propose a third, because the first one looks wrong to me here, and I want you to stress-test it. Call it the audience condition: what keeps a conspiracy secret isn't how many people know, its whether any of the people who know has an audience — standing, a platform, and a reason to act. Headcount buys you nothing if nobody who knows can get a hearing; a handful of knowers with sympathetic listeners is fragile. On the Tuskegee version, several hundred people must have known something over four decades, but the people who knew had no audience (poor, rural, Black, Jim Crow Alabama, with the local medical establishment on the other side of them), and the people who did have audiences didn't know. It broke when one insider found a reporter.

So: run all three tests over the case and tell me where each one works and where it doesn't.

- Does Tuskegee break the leakiness heuristic or only refine it? If a secret involving several hundred people can hold for forty years, what's left of that heuristic as a rough guide — anything?
- Is the audience condition doing real explanatory work, or is it just leakiness relabeled?
- Which test, if any, should have let a careful person in 1955 put "something is badly wrong with this study" clearly above "this is a paranoid fantasy" — and would any of them have licensed the specific claim, rather than a generalised suspicion?
- And the bit I actually need for the piece: once the real thing is exposed, what does it do to the epistemic status of the false claims that grew in the same soil — the belief that HIV was engineered to kill Black people, and the versions of that which turned up around COVID vaccines? Does one true conspiracy rationally raise the prior on the whole family, and by roughly how much? I'd like an honest answer on whether the particularist line (judge each claim on its own evidence) is actually usable for somone who has to decide what to run by Thursday.

Where the theory is silent, say so rather than stretching it. And if my audience condition is just wrong, tell me that too.
