I'm trying to work out what I actually think about what matters in survival, and my case-by-case verdicts won't line up behind any principle I can state.

1. My body is scanned destructively and a replica is rebuilt on Mars forty minutes later, with an interval in which nothing exists: I survive, and I've lost nothing but the travel time.
2. Same machine, but the receiver is wrecked before the replica is built; nine centuries later archaeologists repair it and build me from the stored specification: I balk. This feels much less like survival, or not like it at all — though the gap is again one in which nothing exists, and the causal chain is the same in kind as in 1.
3. A non-destructive scan is taken at fifty; I live to eighty and die of cancer; the stored specification is then activated: I died. The person who wakes up is someone else who inherits my memories.
4. The machine fails to destroy me, a Martian replica is built, and I die of the resulting damage two days later: I survive.
5. Two replicas are built simultaneously, one on Mars and one in Moscow: I survive, but divided — worse than 1, far better than death.
6. A replica built at synaptic grain from a scan: me. A replica whose internal structure is quite different but which reproduces every disposition I have in every possible situation: not me.
7. My neurons are replaced one by one over twenty years with functionally identical prostheses: me, and I don't think this raises any question.
8. A replica is built, lives a day, is scanned and rebuilt, and so on daily for a year: me, roughly like sleep.

My tentative principle: what matters is being carried forward by the most recent complete specification of one's psychology, realized at the right grain, the physical substrate being irrelevant — with division being a distinct bad. That handles 3 and 4 neatly (the stale specification in 3 is superseded by thirty further years of me) and 6 and 7 (grain matters, matter doesn't). But it predicts that 2 is straightforwardly survival, and I don't feel that, while every repair I can think of — requiring an unbroken process, or a short interval, or an unbroken stream of consciousness — starts eating into 1 and 8.

So: state the principle, or two or three serious candidates, precisely, and say for each clause which of my cases motivates it; say honestly which verdicts each candidate forces me to give up, and whether the cost of saving all eight is a principle so baroque that I should rather overturn a verdict; and tell me what each candidate implies about the two cases I haven't settled — a replica built at the right grain and immediately, but with one traumatic memory replaced by a neutral one and the rest of the psychology adjusted to cohere with it; and the two branches of 5 living ten years and then being merged into a single person by reconciling their divergent memories. If it matters to the answer, I'd also like to know whether the principle gives me any special reason to care about what happens to the duplicate in 3.
