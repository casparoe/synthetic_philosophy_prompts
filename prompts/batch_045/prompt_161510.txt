Writing a dialogue for my fourth-year seminar on atrocity crimes and I keep producing drafts where one side is definately a strawman, so I want you to have a go. The topic: what the word "genocide" is *for*, and what we lose or gain by the way it got narrowed.

Two characters, both adults about it:

**Neve** — intellectual historian of the concept. She thinks the word was coined to name something bigger than mass killing (the disintegration of a group, not just the killing of its members), that the narrowing in the 1948 convention was a diplomatic compromise rather than a piece of analysis, and that we pay for that compromise every time a government works out that you can dissolve a group without shooting anyone. She's suspicious of how much moral and political work gets done by whether a label sticks.

**Halvard** — legal theorist. He thinks the specific-intent requirement is doing real discriminating work, that without it genocide collapses into ordinary nasty nation-building, and that the distinctive wrong is a wrong about a group ceasing to exist *as such*, not merely a very large pile of rights-violations against individuals.

They agree on the facts of this invented case (keep it invented — no real countries, no real atrocities):

The Kelthi are an ethnic minority of about two million in the Republic of Maraven, with their own language; another three million live across the border in a neighbouring state, where they're doing fine. After a separatist insurgency in Maraven's northern highlands, the government spends roughly fifteen years doing the following: relocating Kelthi from the highlands into mixed towns (housing is adequate, nobody starves, families are not split up, children stay with their parents); dropping Kelthi from schools, courts and public signage while leaving it legal at home and in religious services; paying a cash grant plus public-sector job preference to anyone who re-registers their ethnicity as Marvene; and deleting "Kelthi" from the census. Two leaked internal memos argue that the "Kelthi problem will be solved within a generation if dispersion continues." Nobody anywhere says "destroy." About nine hundred people die over the period, nearly all in counterinsurgency operations the state claims were lawful. After two census rounds, self-identification as Kelthi among the under-25s is under twenty percent.

Both agree the programme is gravely unjust. The only question between them is whether it is genocide. Here's the definition they're arguing about — Article II of the Genocide Convention:

"In the present Convention, genocide means any of the following acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such: Killing members of the group; Causing serious bodily or mental harm to members of the group; Deliberately inflicting on the group conditions of life calculated to bring about its physical destruction in whole or in part; Imposing measures intended to prevent births within the group; Forcibly transferring children of the group to another group."

Give me 900–1200 words of them genuinely arguing, and make sure:

1. Real punches, not shadow-boxing. Neve has to answer Halvard's *best* version of "on your reading, compulsory schooling in a national language is genocidal, and so is half of nineteenth-century Europe." Halvard has to answer Neve's best version of "your reading just teaches states which buttons not to press."
2. The intent problem, properly. The memo doesn't say destroy. Halvard should say what kind of evidence would actually distinguish aiming at the group's dissolution from merely foreseeing it — and Neve should say why we should or shouldn't care about that difference in the first place.
3. The denominator mess. Every Kelthi inside Maraven is affected; the Kelthi worldwide number five million. Is "in part" measured against the group as such, or against the part the state can reach? Both of them should notice the consequence they don't like: on the first reading, a state that destroys every last member inside its own borders walks free; on the second, "part" becomes trivially satisfied and intent has to do all the work alone.
4. Halvard offers Neve a deal: keep the legal category narrow and condemn this as something else — crimes against humanity, persecution, ethnic cleansing — with exactly the same moral condemnation attached. Neve has to say what is actually lost. If the honest answer is "only the rhetoric," she should either explain why the rhetoric matters here or admit it.
5. Somewhere in the middle, have one of them float a proposal a student of mine made last year that I can't get out of my head: stop treating genocide as binary and score cases on a profile instead — how far the group's physical survival, its biological continuity, its cultural transmission and its political standing are each under attack, each on its own scale — and then set the legal trigger separately, the way you'd seperate a diagnosis from the threshold for treatment. Halvard's problem with it should be about what law can actually *do* with a graded score; Neve's reply should be about what the binary does to our attention. Don't let either of them just announce the idea — it should come out of the argument as something one of them needs.

Don't wrap it up in a tidy agreement, but don't let them talk past each other either: I want each to end up holding something they didn't arrive with, and I want a student to be able to see, concretely, what each position costs. No case names, no tribunals, no citations, no footnotes — it has to work on its own in a room with no internet.
