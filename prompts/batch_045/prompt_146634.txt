hey — I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background beyond one undergrad elective I mostly slept through, so bear with me.

At work we have this habit I definately can't tell is deep or self-deluding. We build performance models of our services that are obviously false. Concretely: to decide how many instances to run and to promise a p99 latency number, we model each service as a single M/M/1 queue — arrivals are Poisson, service times are exponential, one server, infinite buffer. Everyone in the room knows real traffic arrives in bursts (there's a daily shape, plus retry storms, plus the occasional noisy neighbor), and real latency has a fat tail because of GC pauses and lock contention. The model's assumptions aren't approximately true; they're just false. And we ship the capacity numbers anyway, and it mostly works, or at least we don't get paged. We also load-test in staging with a synthetic traffic generator, and everyone agrees those numbers are directional at best.

Meanwhile, in the same repo, a code reviewer will block a PR if the unit test mocks the payments client so it always returns 200 instantly — the objection being that the real thing can time out and the test therefore shows nothing. Which, fine, but isn't that the same move? We replaced a dependancy with a fake, idealized version of itself and drew a conclusion anyway.

Third data point: we refuse to mock the clock. Every service takes an injected Clock; tests advance it by hand, and if you call now() directly, CI fails. And for anything genuinely risky we don't model or stage it at all, we ship it dark behind a flag and watch real traffic.

A friend with a philosophy degree mentioned something about Milton Friedman arguing in some essay from the fifties that assumptions don't have to be realistic as long as the predictions come out right, and called it the F-twist or something. That sounds like us. But it also sounds like a license to never check anything, and we clearly do check some things but not others, so idk that it's the whole story.

So: what does our actual practice presuppose about when a false assumption in a model is acceptable? I'd like the implicit rule reconstructed and then assessed for coherence. Is it really just "only predictions matter," and if so why the fuss about the payments mock? Is something else doing the work — e.g. whether the assumption is one you could relax later and recover the messy version as a limit, versus an assumption that deletes the very failure mode you were modeling to find? Is part of it that some assumptions are wrong in a direction that flatters you (everything succeeds instantly) while others aren't? Is the clock rule a separate principle, or the same one wearing a hat?

Also please separate what the practice strictly commits us to from what merely fits comfortably with it — I suspect several different theories of models would all tell us to do exactly what we do, which would mean our workflow is evidence for nothing much. And then: if we swapped in the strict view, the one where a model's assumptions should be true or at least close, what would concretely have to change on a Tuesday? My guess is that it collapses into "test everything against real production traffic," which is impossible, and if that's right then maybe the practice isn't evidence for the F-twist at all, just evidence that we're resource-constrained. I'd like that spelled out either way.

Answer in American English, please, and skip the reading list — I want the reasoning, not things to go read.
