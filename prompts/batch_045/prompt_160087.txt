I'm in the second year of a philosophy PhD with a concentration in philosophy of physics, and my reading group has started a habit I have come to both enjoy and dread: once a term, each of us writes down our current commitments on one topic on a single page, and the others spend the session trying to find where the page contradicts itself. Mine is on Bell's theorem and locality, and I would like an outside audit before I hand it in. Here is roughly where I stand.

(1) The loophole-free Bell tests of the last decade settle that no theory satisfying Bell's locality condition can recover the observed correlations, and I read this as a real discovery about the world: nature is non-separable, or nonlocal, in a sense that any successor to quantum mechanics will have to preserve. I do not think it is merely a fact about one formalism or about our ignorance. (2) I nonetheless deny that there is action at a distance. I keep no-signalling and parameter independence; quantum mechanics and special relativity peacefully coexist. When pushed I would rather say that the two wings of a Bell experiment form a single non-separable system than say that the setting on one side reaches across and alters the outcome on the other. (3) I reject superdeterminism and retrocausality: I do not want to say that the measurement settings are correlated with the hidden variables, or with some unknown common cause, in a way that makes the violation an artifact of how the experiment happened to be set up. (4) I am a determinist about the fundamental physics; I think the settings themselves were in principle fixed by the initial conditions of the universe. I have always assumed this is compatible with (3), but someone in the group said it is not, and I could not say exactly why not. (5) I lean Everettian: no collapse, unitary evolution only, and this is a large part of the appeal, all the dynamics is local, interactions are local, the Hamiltonian is a sum of local terms, and the correlations come out of decoherence and branching without any spooky action. This is the main reason I reject Bohmian mechanics, whose dynamics strikes me as flagrantly nonlocal, with a preferred slicing in its relativistic versions. (6) I am not a realist about the wavefunction as a thing inhabiting a high-dimensional space. I am drawn to nomological readings of the quantum state, and more generally I think a fundamental physical theory ought to be about local beables, particles, fields, events, in ordinary spacetime rather than about an object living in configuration space.

Please audit this package. Answer the way a professional philosopher of physics would: start by brainstorming freely about which of these commitments constrain which of the others, and only then give me the finished verdict. Go through the pairs and tell me which are genuinely in tension and which only look that way; I would much rather have discrimination than a list in which everything gets flagged. Rank what you find by severity, and for each real tension say which commitment would have to give and what giving it up would cost me. Then commit: tell me which single revision you would actually make if you were me, and why, instead of leaving me a menu. Where you are relying on a contested claim about what Everettian or Bohmian theories are really committed to, say so and say how confident you are, but do not hedge the overall verdict; and please do not spend your first two paragraphs re-explaining Bell's theorem to me, I have read the derivations.
