I've spent the last year or so falling asleep to Roman history audiobooks while doing dishes, and I've ended up on the "it didn't fall, it transformed" side — the idea that 476 was mostly administrative theatre, that Odoacer and his successors kept a lot of the machinery running, that what actually changed is who pocketed the taxes and wore the diadem, and that "the fall" is partly us taking the losers' own rhetoric (plus a lot of later nostalgia for a unified Mediterranean) at face value. I'd like you to try to talk me out of it. Not with a reading list — I don't care who said what in which book, I care about the shape of the arguments — but with the specific things that should move me.

Because I'm a software engineer and I don't trust a claim I can't turn into a check, here's the operationalization I've been playing with, which I need you to pressure-test as much as the view behind it. Call it the three-generation test: a polity has fallen rather than merely transformed if, within roughly three generations (say 75 years), at least two of these stop being true. (1) The central fiscal apparatus still levies and collects taxes in roughly the old units, and pays a standing professional army out of that revenue rather than out of land grants, booty, and personal followings. (2) The senior administrative and military elite is still recruited and promoted through the same formal career ladder as before, and that ladder is what makes someone a person of consequence. (3) Whoever is running the place still claims — and is still widely granted — the same legitimizing office, rather than ruling as a king of a people who happen to be sitting on Roman soil.

So: what would count against the transformation view, and what would count against my test? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and split into things that arguably allready happened — archaeology of settlement abandonment and shrinking tax rolls, the loss of the North African grain and revenue base, plagues, a cooling climate, armies that stop being paid and start being billeted — versus things that would have to newly turn up or be newly argued to break the view. Please tell me which of these a committed transformationist would concede as counting against them and which they'd dismiss as changing the subject, because I suspect a good chunk of what I'd hear from the "fall" side is really a fight about what the word Rome picks out: the city, the empire, the office of emperor, the tax system, a language, an identity people felt. Say whether that makes the dispute verbal, or whether there's a non-verbal version that still risks something.

And be honest about the last bit: does my three-generation test rescue the transformation claim, or rig it? If I've set the threshold so that no real collapse could ever register as a fall — because any successor who wants the revenue will keep the machinery and claim the title — then tell me that plainly, tell me what I'd have to change to make the test actually bite, and then tell me whether the view survives the fixed version. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence: which of my two things, the transformation view or the three-generation test, is more exposed, and roughly how badly — then give the reasoning, no preamble and no restating the question back at me.
