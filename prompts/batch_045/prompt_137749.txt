I've recently been appointed, as the junior member, to the Animal Care and Ethics Committee at my university — we review protocols from the veterinary school, a psychology department that runs operant-conditioning work with rats, and an agricultural research station with a large herd — and I've run into a problem I didn't anticipate. The welfare arguments I arrived with (that suffering is what matters, that sentience is the threshold, that one should reduce the numbers used and refine the procedures) keep being ruled out of order by one of the senior members, a Kantian moral philosopher who insists that we have no direct duties to animals at all: animals are not rational, they cannot set ends, they are not persons and not members of the kingdom of ends, and so nothing we do to them can be a wrong done to *them*. Anything the committee wants to say has to be said instead as an indirect duty to humanity — he is drawing on the ethics part of the Metaphysics of Morals and the ethics lectures, where the thought is that cruelty to animals coarsens the agent and tends to harden him in his dealings with human beings, that an animal which has laboured long and faithfully is owed something close to gratitude although strictly no duty is owed to the animal itself, and that an animal may be used and disposed of as our property provided we are not cruel. I find the position genuinely disturbing, and I also find that nobody on the committee has ever worked out what it would actually require in practice — so rather than argue with him in the abstract, I'd like to hand him his own view in writing.

So: write the document he would be forced to write — the committee's new animal-welfare guidance, drafted entirely from inside the indirect-duty framework, in roughly 850–1000 words, laid out as a real institutional compliance policy: a memo header (from the committee; to principal investigators and facility managers; subject line drily procedural), a short definitions section, and then eight to ten numbered clauses with sub-clauses, in the flattened, euphemistic register of university administration. The humor has to come from the actual content rather than from jokes bolted on, and the clauses should exaggerate real features of the Kantian view instead of inventing ones. Please build in at least the following: a Definitions entry establishing that no duty is owed to any non-rational being and that all provisions protect persons only; an 'Analogy Schedule' ranking species by their closeness of resemblance to human nature — dogs and horses attracting full protection because the analogy to human loyalty and service is unavoidable, rodents being subject to mandatory measures that suppress the analogy (no naming, no photographs, no keeping of animals after use, mandatory terms like units and cohorts), fish getting an abbreviated review, and insects, molluscs and invertebrates falling outside scope altogether for want of any recognisable resemblance; a 'hardening register' under which staff are assessed for coarsening at induction and annually, with the consequence that once an operative is certified as fully hardened — or where a procedure is performed unwitnessed by any person of unhardened sensibility and is unlikely to reach public knowledge — the character-based ground of the restraint lapses; a clause on painful experiments that distinguishes purposes of substantial importance to humanity from merely speculative curiosity; a publicity clause dealing with what is to be done when the objection is really that the public would be distressed; and a long-service recognition scheme that pensions the retired facility dog while saying nothing at all about the fate of animals that were never of service. The document should never once appeal to the animal's own experience as a reason for anything.

Two further constraints. First, keep it legible and deadpan: a reader with no philosophy should be able to tell what is being ironized without the drafter winking, and I'd like a handful of bracketed marginal annotations in a dissenting utilitarian voice — each answered in two or three lines, still in the Kantian register — so the reductio is visible as it goes; where a clause follows the logic to a place where a real Kantian would want to resist, let the annotation register the resistance rather than softening the clause. Second, make it fair: someone who works on Kant's ethics should recognise the view rather than a strawman, and one clause should let the view show its genuine strength — that cruelty corrupts the agent in a way that matters independently of its effects on others — without breaking character. No citations, footnotes or reading suggestions anywhere; close with a compliance attestation or review clause that lands the final joke.
