I've got a bet going with someone in my philosophy of science seminar, and I want help winning it. Our prof has been running a bit of a parlour game: she keeps a list on the board of predicates and rules on whether each one is projectible, i.e. whether its positive instances actually confirm the generalization that uses it. Her verdicts so far:

- grue, in the usual Goodman setup (a thing is grue iff it's green if first examined before some fixed future date T, blue if first examined after): not projectible.
- bleen, the mirror image: not projectible.
- hemigreen (green if the sample was collected in the northern hemisphere, blue if in the southern — and every sample anyone has actually examined so far happens to be northern): not projectible.
- cryoverd (green below 20 C, blue at or above 20 C, and we do have data on both sides of the threshold): projectible — she said that's just a law about temperature dependence, nothing fishy about it.
- marbite: a mineral term that's been in continuous use for two centuries, and is a straight disjunction of two unrelated crystal structures with no single microstructural story behind them, but geologists have projected from it the whole time and been right: projectible.
- someone pointed out that you can define green as grue-before-T-or-bleen-after, so green is no better off than grue as far as the shape of its definition goes. She said the rewrite is irrelevant and green is still the projectible one.
- a term coined about fifteen years ago in a brand new subfield, which is again just a disjunction of two rather different microstructural states, and which has so far made three successful novel predictions: projectible, but she flagged it as provisional and said to ask her again once its had a few decades.

Next week's case is this one, and it's the one we're betting on. In conservation science there's a term, seral, in continuous use since the 1960s: a paint sample is seral iff it was laid down before 1900 and its binder fluoresces under UV, or laid down after 1900 and its binder doesn't fluoresce. People have used it for sixty years to predict how a sample will behave in treatment, and the switch has earned its keep: samples laid down after 1900 were tested later and came out non-fluorescing, exactly as the seral generalization had predicted back when nobody had checked. Then last year somebody worked out that the date isn't doing the work at all — what matters is which of two store-rooms a sample sat in, because the humidity in one of them destroys the fluorescence, and the pre-1900 samples mostly sat in that room. The date is just a stand-in for where the sample was kept.

So: is seral projectible, on her ruling?

I want the rule she seems to be running — entrenchment in actual projective practice, a ban on predicates that essentially mention particular times or places, a requirement that the extra structure in a predicate earn its keep against the evidence, something about mechanisms and mere proxies, whatever else fits — and what each of the rules that fit her record equally well predicts about seral.
