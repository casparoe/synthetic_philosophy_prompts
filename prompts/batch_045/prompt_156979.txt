I'm a doctoral candidate in modern European history, finishing a dissertation chapter on the eleven weeks of the July Crisis of 1914 — how a chain of decisions in Vienna, Berlin, St Petersburg and London produced a general European war. My supervisor's central objection is that the chapter is written with the outcome in view, and therefore exhibits hindsight bias; she adds that the narrative form itself is the vehicle of the narrative fallacy, which she treats as the same defect scaled up from single judgments to whole stories. Her remedy is that I strip the chapter down to a de-narrativized causal graph and attach to each transition an ex ante probability estimate produced before I consult the record of what happened next, reporting the discrepancy between that estimate and what actually occurred.

I want to comply, and I have built an instrument for it: a baseline-anchored counterfactual audit. For each decision node I reconstruct the alternatives that were live at the time, score how foreseeable each outcome was from materials available before it happened, then score how it reads once the later record is in; the gap is my bias score. My instruction is to treat any positive gap as error to be engineered out, in part by generating explicit counterfactuals at every node.

Four things make me hesitate, and I would be grateful for your view on which of them is the real problem.

First: as I understand the standard measure, a historian is hindsight-biased to the extent that she assigns high probability to the outcome that in fact occurred. But I will assign probability near one to much of what happened simply because I know it happened. That looks like conditioning rather than bias. What exactly is the quantity I am meant to be measuring?

Second: at some nodes the gap seems justified rather than inflated — a mobilization timetable that was genuinely binding, a commitment entered into years earlier. If I drive every gap toward zero, I get a chapter in which war looks like a coin flip at every step.

Third: my ex ante baselines must be reconstructed from the surviving diplomatic record, which was itself weeded, copied and retained by people who already knew how things turned out. Doesn't that contaminate the baseline, and if it does, what does my discrepancy score measure?

Fourth: the de-narrativized graph still requires me to decide which of the thousands of things that happened in those eleven weeks count as nodes and which get arrows between them. On what principle am I supposed to do that?

And the framing question beneath all of this: is the narrative fallacy really just hindsight bias applied to whole stories, so that de-narrativizing handles both at once?
