For a fourth-year seminar on the separateness-of-persons objection, I need a decision tree that sorts students by their structural commitments rather than by the label they happen to favour: at most eight branch questions, ten to twelve leaves, each leaf a named position in the debate together with one sentence on its least comfortable commitment.

Every branch node has to be a question about a concrete case, phrased without the vocabulary of the debate — no aggregation, utility, impartiality, side constraints, or reasonable rejectability — so that a student who has read nothing can answer it. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: fix the questions and the combinations of answers they permit first, then place the positions at the addresses their answers yield. Name any combination that no one occupies and give one clause on why it is empty (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unclaimed). Where two positions land at the same address, say so plainly instead of inventing a further question to pry them apart.

The angle that matters to me: the tree must separate two grounds for the objection that the literature tends to run together. One ties separateness to whatever unifies the stages of a single life, so that compensation within a life is permitted while compensation across lives is not. The other ties it to each person having their own standpoint from which to press a claim, so that what matters is whether someone is left with a complaint, however unified or disunified the lives involved are. To force these apart, at least two nodes must be cases where identity and standpoint come apart: a patient whose later stages share no memories, desires, or character traits with their earlier ones, and a fission case in which one person's claim is inherited by two successors. Each leaf should then state which ground it rests on, or flag itself as compatible with either.

Leaves that must appear: total-utilitarian aggregation in the Harsanyi style, prioritarianism, telic egalitarianism, the Rawlsian contractarian argument, Nozick-style constraints, Scanlon-style contractualism, and the anti-aggregation view associated with Taurek. Flag any of these that your tree cannot place, and note where the levelling-down case or the numbers case, if you use them, fail to do the discriminating work people expect of them.
