My friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about whether the shift from foraging to farming was, for the people who lived through it, a mistake. She made the case I take to be the standard one — short working weeks, a broad diet, fewer crowd diseases, no one in a position to give orders — and I found myself conceding nearly every sentence of it and still refusing the conclusion. Before we gave up we agreed on two things. First, we were not really arguing about the evidence; we were arguing about which yardstick to apply to it. Second, we would set aside the question of how many lives a landscape can carry and ask only about the shape of a typical life inside it. We did not agree on which yardsticks are admissible at all, and I said I would get an outside verdict.

Here are the seven we wrote down. Three are hers, two are mine, and two we invented together and can no longer attribute to either of us.

1. The somatic ledger (hers): whatever the skeleton keeps — adult stature, enamel defects, joint wear, healed and unhealed injuries, signs of anemia and infection. Her claim is that this is the only evidence about these people that was not produced by the descendants of the farmers.

2. Exit elasticity (mine): how much a household loses by leaving. A camp you can walk away from with what you carry is not like a village whose fields, stores, debts and graves you cannot take with you; I claim a regime you cannot exit has to be judged by a stricter standard, whatever its averages look like.

3. The ratchet discount (joint): weight a benefit by how soon it arrives. If the real payoff of the first fields only materialises after many generations of crops, animals and immune systems adapting to each other, then it counts for very little to the people who planted them. She replies that in some places people did go back to wild foods, so the ratchet is not absolute; I reply that a ratchet you can release for a season is still a ratchet.

4. The silver standard (hers): judge a regime by what it does to the people it has no use for — the lame, the old, the orphaned. A band that carries them and a settlement that lets them sink are not merely unequal, she says; they disagree about who counts. I suspect this one flatters her side, which is not a reason to exclude it.

5. Option space (mine): how many distinct lives are available to someone born into the regime. A camp offers nearly everyone the same handful of lives; a village offers priest, soldier, potter, beggar. I claim a wider menu counts for something even when most of the new items on it are grim; she claims that adding bad options to a menu is not a benefit to anyone.

6. Unrecorded harms (joint): some of what a regime can do to a person — boredom, humiliation, being ordered about, grief with nowhere to go — leaves nothing in the ground. The question is whether a harm that leaves no trace should count as much as one that does, or whether the fact that only some harms fossilise means we should distrust any ledger built from what survived.

7. Downside concentration (joint): judge by the floor rather than the average — by the worst tenth of lives in each regime rather than the typical one.

Two conditions. Please do not give me the names of scholars, book titles or reading suggestions: I can look those up, and I want your reasoning rather than your memory. And I would rather be disappointed by the ranking than flattered by it: if a yardstick I dislike deserves real weight, or one I invented deserves almost none, say so in its own line instead of quietly dropping it.

Drop the two you think deserve least, give the remaining five shares of a hundred points for how much weight they should carry in settling the argument, and answer as exactly five numbered items in descending order of share — each item giving the name, its share, and one or two sentences of justification. No introduction and no concluding paragraph.
