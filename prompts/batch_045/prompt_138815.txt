I'm giving a talk at a political theory workshop in three weeks — 20 minutes of paper, 40 minutes of questions — and I want to walk into Q&A with most of it already answered.

Here's the paper. Everyone's favourite complaint against Rawls's duty of civility is that public reason is rigged in favour of secular citizens, because it's just easier to translate secular-philosophical reasons into public ones than religious ones. I think that complaint is definately a dead end. My argument is different: even if the content of public reason were perfectly neutral at a given time, the burden of complying with it still isn't distributed fairly, because how much it costs you to produce an acceptable public reason depends on how far your comprehensive doctrine happens to sit from the political conception right now — and that distance drifts. The political conception is supposed to be drawn from the overlapping consensus, and the consensus moves; look how fast things like dignity or public health have become load-bearing and contested public values. So a doctrine can be a comfortable fit in 1995 and a real strain in 2025 without its adherents having changed anything at all. I call this the drift problem. My constructive suggestion is that the duty of civility has to be indexed to something like each doctrine's current distance from the consensus, which means Rawlsians owe us an account of who bears the cost of movement, not just who bears the cost of translation. I also argue that the proviso doesn't rescue anything: the bit where he says non-public reasons are fine provided public ones get supplied in due course solves the timing of admission, not the diachronic distribution of the burden.

The room is about fifteen people. Mostly mainstream Rawlsian political theorists; two of them have written on public reason themselves and will be quietly annoyed by any hint that they haven't read the proviso carefully. Plus a Catholic theological ethicist who thinks the whole public-reason project is an imposition on religious citizens and who will therefore be suspicious of anything that looks like a friendly amendment to it. Plus one political scientist who runs surveys on how religious voters actually describe their own reasons, and who will want to know what any of this predicts.
