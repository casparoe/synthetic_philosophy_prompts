Hey — I'm TAing a historical-methods seminar for first-year PhD students this term, and I want to hand them a decision tree on one question: given that a body of surviving sources is silent about some set of people, what (if anything) does that silence license you to say? Not "here are the four schools of thought on archives" — I want an actual instrument, where the student starts from a commitment they already have and walks down to a named position. Say 7–9 branch questions and 8–10 leaves, and please build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: lay out the questions first, note which combinations of answers the questions allow, then place the real positions in that space, and mark the combos nobody occupies with one line on why (incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored).

The distinctions I care about, and I'd like most of them to be branch nodes: whether a silence can be evidence at all or is simply missing data; whether the mechanism is someone actively suppressing or destroying records versus the record-producing apparatus simply never having had a category for these people (I've been calling this the legibility threshold — the level of property, legal standing or institutional entanglement at which a person starts generating paperwork at all, so the absence is built into the machinery rather than chosen by anyone); whether the inference target is the people and events themselves or only the machinery that produced the record; whether quantitative reasoning from the shape of the gaps — abrupt stops in a run of registers, missing sequence numbers, sudden changes in the categories used — is admissible as evidence about the unrecorded population; whether reading the surviving documents for traces of what they had to presuppose in order to say what they do say counts as recovering the unrecorded or only as describing the record; whether filling the gap is an epistemic move (imaginative reconstruction, explicitly flagged as not a knowledge claim) or an institutional one (go build a counter-archive, change who records); and whether the same rules apply to elite silences, e.g. a family's correspondence that never once mentions the estate's finances. I also want the difference between a group that is simply absent from the sources and a group that appears only as a line item in someone else's document — named in an inventory, counted in a levy, never a speaker — to show up somewhere in the tree, and I'd like you to decide whether it deserves its own branch or is handled by an existing one, and say why.

Every node has to be phrased as something a student could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, so no node like "do you think the archive is an instrument of power". Please anchor the questions in concrete cases rather than abstractions — I'd suggest at least these three: tax or parish rolls where entries for landless cottagers stop dead in one year while landholders' entries run on unchanged; a colonial court record where an enslaved person appears only as an entry in a property inventory; and the estate-correspondence case above. My supervisor's objection, which I half agree with, is that the deliberate-suppression branch and the legibility-threshold branch yield exactly the same conclusions in every case we've looked at, so if your tree can't actually separate them, say so explicitly rather than pretending a node does work it doesn't — and flag any other pairs of leaves that collapse into each other. Each leaf should get a name (or "unnamed" if nobody defends it), one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and one of the three cases it handles worst. Its fine if its a bit scrappy, I'd rather have the disagreements visible than a tidy diagram.
