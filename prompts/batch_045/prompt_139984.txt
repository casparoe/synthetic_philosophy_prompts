Next week I'm presenting at an interdisciplinary workshop on consumption and climate — I've got abou 25 minutes plus questions. My thesis is that the inefficacy objection runs on a snapshot counterfactual: it asks whether one person's single purchase changes anything with everything else held fixed. But for stuff sold through automated replenishment — supermarkets reordering in fixed case sizes off a demand forecast — the right counterfactual is a sustained small change in one household's buying pattern. That nudges the forecast baseline down a fraction, and because reorder quantities are lumpy and discrete, there's a real chance a threshold gets crossed a few months later and one fewer case gets ordered. So individual food choices can be non-negligibly difference-making via baseline drift even though each one looks pointless in isolation, which also means the duty is a duty of sustained pattern change rather than of individual acts. I plan to concede up front that none of this obviously carries over to voting.

The room: mostly ag economists, including one guy who works with supermarket scanner data and will know how these forecasting systems actually behave; two philosophers from the effective-altruism-ish side who think consumer change is a distraction from policy and tech work; one sociologist who has written on moral licensing and crowding out.
