I had a long argument about this over dinner last night with a friend who is about to start litigating, and I have been turning it over since. We started from the familiar complaint that the lessons of history mislead in foreign-policy argument — Munich gets wheeled out to mandate confrontation, Vietnam to forbid it, not infrequently by the same person a decade apart — and I said the standard thing: that these analogies are rhetorically powerful and evidentially nearly worthless, because a likeness can always be found and a difference can always be found, and nothing in the practice constrains which one a speaker reaches for. He replied that I had described a lazy version of the practice rather than shown the competent version to be defective. Reasoning from history, he said, is like reasoning from precedent in a common-law system: you never have a covering general law, you work by comparing cases, and the whole discipline consists in likeness-judgments that are answerable to reasons. He then used the comparison to do real work rather than merely to redescribe. In law, he pointed out, there is a presumption that like cases be decided alike, and the burden of argument falls on the party who would distinguish — so the Thucydides-trap framing, read properly, is not a slogan but a default: it sets a prior expectation that a rising power displacing an established one tends toward war, and the burden is on whoever makes the this-time-is-different argument. When I pressed the usual complaints — that the case base is a dozen or so famously reported episodes, most of them counted as ending in war, that the sample is tiny and its membership contested, and that we mostly remember the dramatic ones — he said these are precisely the pathologies that legal reasoning has institutional machinery for, and that I should say what the machinery is doing before I declare the analogy broken.

So I would like to audit the analogy properly, for a short write-up I am doing for a seminar on analogy and evidence. Please lay the correspondence out explicitly — what plays the role of the court, the holding, the ratio decidendi, the adversarial brief, the act of distinguishing, the appeal or overruling, and the doctrine that settles which features count as legally relevant — and then go through where the mapping breaks, separating the disanalogies that actually damage his argument from the ones that are merely colorful. My own candidate list, which I do not trust: that in law the relevance of a feature is fixed by an authority and a doctrine, whereas nothing fixes whether nuclear weapons, regime type, trade interdependence or the memory of 1914 is the relevant feature of the Athens-and-Sparta case; that in law there is a professional adversary whose job and fee depend on distinguishing the precedent, while in policy the distinguishing is done by whoever is already inclined to act; that a precedent is normally applied to facts it does not itself alter, whereas invoking Munich or Athens is a public speech act that the other side also hears and also exploits, so the analogy sits inside the causal situation it describes and can be self-fulfilling in a way a cited case is not; that legal error is correctable by later courts, while acting on a false positive here is not appealable, which matters because his burden-shifting repair quietly assumes errors that are symmetric and reversible; and that case selection in law is disciplined by a bounded, doctrinally indexed corpus, whereas the historical case base is curated by salience and by who won the peace — rather like a case reporter in which only the most theatrical decisions survive and the headnotes were written by the winning side.

The part I care most about is whether his conclusion survives the audit, and I suspect he may be running two conclusions together: that a good historical analogy can carry a prior probability or base rate, and that it can carry a mechanism sketch worth checking — status anxiety, preventive incentives, commitment traps, the domestic politics of backing down. My suspicion is that the precedent model can support the second and not the first, and that the honest repair is to treat the analogy as generating hypotheses and a checklist of things to go and look for, which makes it closer to a case report in medicine than to a binding precedent. But I also suspect I find that appealing because I already wanted to say these analogies are over-used, so please tell me where I am getting the better of the argument unfairly. And one further question I could not settle: if the disanalogies turn out to be institutional rather than logical, could a process supply what is missing — a standing devil's advocate, a written likeness-and-difference table produced before the decision rather than after it, a register of analogies previously relied on and how they turned out — or is the missing element not institutional at all, in which case no amount of procedure will make historical cases behave like precedents?
