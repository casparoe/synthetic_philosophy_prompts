My wife and I are buying a house on a river flat and we've completely stalled on the flood risk, and the more we go round in circles the more I think the thing we're stuck on isn't really about floods.

What we actually know: the map puts the house in the band that's officially a 1%-chance-in-any-given-year band. Insurance is on offer at $3,900 a year against a payout of about $260,000, which I worked out means that even if they were charging us their expected loss and not a cent more, they're behaving as if it's 1.5%, not 1%. The guy selling has lived there 31 years and says the water has never been over the bank, though he had the whole ground floor raised a metre in 1997 and goes vague when you ask why. We were flooded out at our last place four years ago. My wife says that settles it — that was our one, it can't happen twice to the same family. My brother says the reverse, that they come in runs because it's the same weather pattern doing it year after year. The architect we hired says that over a 30-year mortgage, 1% a year is more like a one-in-four chance. And when I phoned the engineer who built the map, she said that for our stretch of river there aren't enough gauged floods to check the model against, so they filled the gaps by asking local engineers how bad they thought it was, and that the whole thing assumes the next 30 years look like the last 100, which nobody she knows actually believes.

Three different friends have now told me three different things about what to do with the 1%, and I cant tell whether they're disagreeing about the world or about what kind of thing the number is.

One: ignore any number about this house. Go find the biggest pile of real cases you can — every river house, every year, whatever — and count. If you can't count it, it isn't a probability.

Two: there is no fact of the matter about whether this house floods next year; it will or it won't. The number is just how sure we should be, so the only thing that can go wrong is holding numbers that fight each other. Decide what odds you'd actually take, and if you won't bet at 1% then say a different number and act on that. On this view the insurer's price is interesting, but only as one more opinion with money behind it.

Three: the 1% is a real feature of this house on this river, the way a bias is a real feature of a loaded die — a die can be weighted even if it gets rolled once and then melted down, and equally you can get a long run of sixes off a perfectly fair one. The map, the engineer's guesswork, and the seller's 31 years are all just instruments of differing quality for measuring that one real thing.

They can't all be right.

So: what are we actually arguing about, and how would I tell whether it's a factual disagreement or a disagreement about what the number even means? Under each of the three ways of looking at it, what's the right thing to do here and why does it follow from that way of looking at it — buy the policy or not, buy the house or not? Which of the three handles the awkward bits worst: the 31-years-and-a-raised-ground-floor thing, the "they come in runs" thing, the melted die, and the "the next 30 years won't look like the last 100" thing? And if I've got one afternoon to go find out one thing — one phone call, one document — what should it be, and why does that particular thing matter on the view you think is right?

Please don't give me a survey of named positions or a reading list, and please don't just tell me to commission a surveyor and sleep on it. I'm asking the conceptual question because the practical answer seems definately to depend on it.
