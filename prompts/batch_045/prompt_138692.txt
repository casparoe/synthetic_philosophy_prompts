Hi — I'm the staff person for a city historical marker commission, and I could use some help turning something into a checklist.

Quick setup: we maintain about 140 markers and plaques around town — buildings, a couple of statues, a demolished neighborhood, a splashy one about the 1898 water filtration plant. We revise maybe 20–25 a year. Committee of five, appointed by the city council, two of them think any edit is "erasing history," one is a retired high school civics teacher, none of them have history degrees. I don't either. We meet two hours twice a month, so realistically ten minutes per marker, and every revision has to survive a council vote where someone will always ask "why are you changing it." Markers have to come in under about 90 words and be readable by a twelve-year-old standing on a sidewalk.

The problem: almost all of these were written in the 1920s–70s in this voice that assumes the town has been climbing a ladder and we're standing on the top rung. Words like "finally," "at last," "brought the city into the modern era," "paved the way for," "a pioneer who." I've read enough to know this has a name — historians call it Whig history, writing the past as a march toward us — and that it's a trap you can fall into in both directions (there's a version where every marker says things used to be better and everything since is a fall). But I can't hand the committee an essay about teleology. I need a procedure.

So: give me a decision procedure I can actually run out loud, in order, on a draft marker. For each step, what question do I ask, what counts as a good enough answer, where does the procedure just stop and say "this line has to go," and where does it throw up its hands and say "this is a values call, committee votes." I especially need it to distinguish three things I keep getting tangled: (a) sentences that claim something genuinely got better, which is often just true and checkable, (b) sentences that claim we have *arrived* — that the present is the point the story was heading for, and (c) sentences that quietly make people in the past look foolish for not already knowing where things were headed. And whatever you build has to not collapse into "delete every evaluative word," because then every plaque reads "in 1898 the city built a plant" and the council stops funding us and nobody stops to read them. I'd rather the procedure name that failure mode and have a guard against it than pretend it can't happen.

Two tests I've been using by instinct, tell me if they're junk or how to fix them. One: the 1975 test — would a sentence in exactly this tone, written in whatever year the marker was put up about that year's settled assumptions, now make us wince? Two: the losers' bet — could a sensible, well-informed person at the time have bet against this outcome without being stupid or wicked? If the marker implies no, it's doing the thing I'm trying to avoid.

Then please run the whole thing on three real drafts, and don't go easy on me:

One. The water plant: "For years the city drank its own filth. At last, in 1898, science and civic pride prevailed, and Cedar Heights joined the modern world." (I think this one's easy but I want to see what survives — the typhoid numbers are real and I don't want a procedure that can't say so.)

Two. The one I'm attached to, about Dr. Alice Renner, the first woman licensed to practice here: "Dr. Renner broke through the barriers of her day and opened the door for every woman who followed." I like this marker. I suspect your procedure is going to make me cut most of it, and I know the historical society that paid for it will be furious. If that's what it says, say so plainly, and give me the one or two sentences I can read out to the committee that aren't theory-speak.

Three. A proposed marker for Brightmoor, the Black neighborhood bulldozed for a highway in 1961: something like "Brightmoor was a place where families thrived, until the city destroyed it and built a road." I worry this is the same mistake with the sign flipped, but I also don't want to end up with nothing to say about it.

Last thing: tell me what the procedure can't do. Which markers are just unfixable in ninety words, which ones are really a fight about what the town wants to be rather than about the past, and where ten minutes isn't enough and we should just defer. And could you write it as continuous prose — no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables, just an email-length explanation I can read on my phone before a meeting. Thanks.
