Hi — I run a tiny podcast for software engineers (mostly infra folks, and I have no philosophy background beyond stuff I picked up from blog posts). Next week's guest is a philosopher who publishes under a pen name and has just put out a short book on Émilie du Châtelet's book on happiness. Her angle, which I recieved as a blurb and quite like: read du Châtelet's advice as a fault-tolerance manual. On her reading, the whole thing is about de-risking your hedonic dependencies — don't let one source of happiness be a single point of failure (someone else's moods, your reputation, a court appointment you don't control), put your weight on the reliable sources that live inside you and can't be revoked by anyone, and prune the dependencies on everything else.

I half buy it, and I want to press her on where that metaphor starts to creak rather than let her just explain the view again. So: draft exactly five interview questions, numbered, with no intro and no closing line. Each item should be one question of a sentence or two, followed in parentheses by a single-sentence follow-up aimed at whatever she's most likely to answer — I want the follow-ups to be the thing that makes the question hard to deflect. One question each on:

- The parts of du Châtelet's book that seem to go the other way: she's pretty hard on the unreliable stuff, but she also seems to want to keep some of it — love, ambition, the desire to be known for something. A dependency-minimising reading says drop all that. Ask her what her reading does with those passages.
- The tension between unlearning and self-programming: unhappiness is supposed to come from opinions we inherited without examining them, but the cure is to retrain your own imagination and desires until they aquire the shape you want. If we're allowed to rewrite our preferences on purpose, why isn't that just installing a prejudice with extra steps — and where does she draw the line between the two?
- The fact that du Châtelet is writing from inside a life where almost every public avenue was closed to her. Is "build the resources inside yourself" liberation, or is it hardening the component instead of fixing the environment — and does the fault-tolerance framing make that worse, by turning an adaptation to being shut out into a general recipe?
- Her claim that you basically need virtue, because remorse and self-reproach wreck happiness. Is that a moral claim or a prudential one in moral costume? Press her on the person who follows the rest of the program, is genuinely content, and is thoroughly rotten.
- Verification. There's no data, no control group, and I work with people who'll ask about this. Ask her what outcome would actually count as the program failing — someone who does everything right and is still miserable, or someone who is only happy because they never examined anything — and what she'd say to them.

Keep every question in plain language, no jargon my listeners won't follow, and don't let any of them turn into "so what did du Châtelet mean by X" — I want her pushed, not lecturing. Also assume neither of us can look anything up while we're recording: no reading lists, no citations, no "as so-and-so argues in their 2019 paper." Five items, that's it.
