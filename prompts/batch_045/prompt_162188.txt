I am drafting a paper on the ethics of lending, and I keep running into the tag "money is barren" (money does not breed money), usually traced back to Aristotle's treatment of interest in the Politics, where the point is made through the word for interest also meaning offspring: money returns as money, the child resembling the parent, and so this is called the most unnatural mode of acquisition. In a seminar last week the tag was used to end an argument rather than begin one. The speaker's claim was that charging for a pure loan is charging for nothing: the lender transfers a sum, receives that same sum plus a surplus, and has done nothing to it in the interval; "money is barren" was offered as the reason why no service has been rendered and why interest is, in the older vocabulary, usurious. I want to know what that sentence would have to mean for the argument to go through, and I suspect it is doing several different jobs at once.

Could you lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses the tag might be expressing — ordered roughly from the trivial to the demanding — and for each: what exactly it asserts; what further premise it would need in order to yield the conclusion that a pure loan involves no service and no product; whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, a piece of definitional fiat, or something false; and whether it actually supports the use my interlocutor made of it.

I would also like the readings tested against three complications rather than merely stated.

First: does the tag's force depend on what the borrower does with the money — seed corn bought and sown, a consumption good, or a sum simply kept idle and repaid out of unrelated income? If the claim is genuinely about money as such, it should not; if it is really about what money finances, the seminar use looks very different, and I want to know which of your readings covertly smuggles in a premise about the borrower.

Second: what happens when the lender is the kind of institution that creates the balance it lends, so that the sum advanced did not exist beforehand? For each reading, say whether the barrenness claim is even about the same object in that case, or whether it silently assumes a lender who already holds a stock of tokens.

Third: Aristotle also holds that money exists by convention rather than by nature, that those who use it can invalidate it, and that its work is to serve as a measure rendering unlike goods commensurable. Show how your readings interact with that. What kind of thing must money be, on each reading, for it to have a nature rich enough to be barren, or for its proper use to exclude a return? And where it matters, distinguish barrenness of the token, barrenness of the lending relation, and unnaturalness as a verdict on a practice of acquisition rather than on a substance.

Conclude by saying which reading the seminar speaker was most likely invoking, and whether that reading survives the three complications. Please argue from the distinctions themselves; I do not want bibliography, sources, or citations.
