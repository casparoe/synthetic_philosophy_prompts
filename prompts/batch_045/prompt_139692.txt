Bit of an odd one, and I'm not even sure it's a real thing rather than me being sloppy. I teach a first-year metaphysics module where I do the statue and the lump of clay on week three, and I also sit on the committee that looks after the university's art collection, so a few times a year I'm in a room with conservators and registrars. I keep noticing the same pattern in both places and I don't have a word for it.

Take any case where two things are made of exactly the same matter at the same time and everyone agrees they aren't the same thing: a lump and the statue it's shaped into. Whenever this comes up, the two of them never come out as equals. One gets treated as the serious one, the one that does the work, and the other gets waved off as nothing extra, or as just a manner of speaking, or as something you could drop without losing anything. Here's the part I actually want a name for: which of them plays which role flips depending on what's being discussed, and nobody seems to mind, and the same person will flip within a single conversation. In a conservation meeting the alloy is what's real — they talk about chloride corrosion and the 1970s restoration, and when I ask about the statue the conservator says, very kindly, that strictly speaking there isn't one, there's this bronze with a history, and the shape is the part you can lose. In the catalogue essay my art-history colleague wrote it's exactly reversed: the work is the thing made in 1968 and shown in Milan, and if you melted it down and recast it in the same shape she'd say it was the same work, and the metal is a footnote. Same object, same people, opposite demotions, tracking the conversation rather than the object.

It's not only the matter-versus-form flip. I made up a case for a seminar last term: a cast that two sculptors both worked on, which the museum has catalogued twice, under two titles, two dates, two entries in two different catalogues, while the conservation file for it is a single file because physically there's one thing on the plinth. Now the flip is between two candidates at the same level, and whether there are really one or two works here seems to track whether you're asking the registrar or the curator. And the bit I can't shake: whichever one gets demoted, you can't actually delete it. The conservator still needs the work in order to say what counts as damage as opposed to patina; my colleague still needs the metal's history to date anything. So the roles aren't really the real one and the idle one, they're more like two different jobs, and the same item does one job in one conversation and the other job in the next — and the metaphysical story people then give (that really there's just one thing here, or that there are two but one of them is derivative, or that it's all in how we describe it) looks like it's tracking whichever job happens to be salient, not the other way round.

I don't need the label for the overall puzzle, every textbook has one of those. I'm after the narrower thing: this allocation of seriousness, and the way it moves around. Does it have a name? Please don't just hand me a word, though — I want the carving, not the label. What exactly is the phenomenon; what are the nearest candidates in the literature and how does my thing differ from each (I know there is an argument somewhere that coinciding things would be causally redundant, and people who say disputes like this are merely verbal, and the whole nothing-over-and-above line — none of those seem to catch the flip, but tell me if one of them actually does once you spell it out); is it one phenomenon or have I bundled three; and if there's genuinely nothing established, coin a term, define it, and tell me what would be a borderline case that a good definition would have to settle either way.
