I've been going round in circles with a friend about Lewis Carroll's little dialogue where Achilles tries to get the Tortoise to accept a conclusion from two premises, and the Tortoise keeps insisting that the rule of inference itself be written down as another premise — so the list of premises keeps growing and you never actually arrive at the conclusion. My friend thinks it's basically a parlour trick that dissolves the moment you notice there's a difference between rules of inference and premises, and that you don't need to believe a rule in order to follow it. I'm much less sure. I feel like that move works, but I can't say why the Tortoise's demand is *illegitimate* rather than merely inconvenient, and every way I try to spell out the difference seems to help itself to the very thing that was supposed to be in question. (Also, I noticed the regress seems to come in two flavours that people run together: the bare fact that it never terminates, and the stronger complaint that every new premise is just as much in need of support as the original conclusion was. And I think it matters that the added premise could be read either as a truth-functional conditional or as a normative claim about what one is permitted to infer — those don't obviously generate the same regress.)

So here's the concretes claim I want a number on: that there exists a justification of deductive inference which is non-circular in the specific sense that it does not rely on deductive inference anywhere in its premises or in its steps. If you think that's ill-posed, say so, but then give me a number on the nearest claim that is well-posed and tell me how you repaired it. Decompose it for me: sub-questions with a rough probability and one line of justification each, then the combination, then which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to and what single argument would move it most. My own credence is about 0.35, my friend's is about 0.85, and I'd really like to know where you think that gap actually lives.

Two things I specifically want in the decomposition, because they're where I get stuck. First, I've been playing with a kind of "scope test" of my own: once you've got a candidate justification, ask whether it would work just as well for a rule that isn't deductively valid but still looks like decent reasoning — say a statistical syllogism, where you accept that most Fs are Gs, and that this particular thing is an F, and infer that it's a G. If your justification for modus ponens would also "justify" that, then I want to say it was never a justification of deduction in particular, only of inference in general, and probably a lousy one at that. So: how much of your credence rides on a justification passing or failing that test? Second, there's a distinction I keep returning to and can't get straight: is the thing that needs justifying (a) the truth-preservation of the rule, (b) my entitlement to move from accepting the premises to accepting the conclusion, or (c) the rule's content-neutrality, the fact that it works no matter what P and Q happen to be about? I suspect a good deal of the apparent disagreement in this area is just people answering different ones of those three — so give me a probability for each being the real question, and tell me which of the two of us at 0.35 and 0.85 is most likely answering a different one.
