I spent most of last night arguing with a friend from my old philosophy of mind reading group about higher-order theories of consciousness, and we ended up stuck somewhere I'd now like to make fun of. My claim was that the theory's deepest problem isn't the orphan-state objection — the possibility of a phenomenally conscious state that no higher-order state happens to target — but rather the worry that a higher-order thought directed at a first-order state seems merely to add a second mental state next to the first, rather than doing whatever it is that is supposed to make the first one conscious; there's a "lighting up" step that nobody has made intelligible, and I think that's where the view actually breaks. She answered that the orphan worry is worse than I allow, because a defender can't simultaneously hold that being targeted by a higher-order state is what constitutes a state's being conscious and allow that we can be wrong about which states we are in — so either misidentification is impossible or the constitution claim fails — and she also thinks the familiar complaint about the rock is a red herring, since the view can say the rock's thought isn't the right kind of state in the right kind of way. Half-seriously, she floated a fix she called dispositional higher-orderism: a state is conscious just in case the subject is disposed, under ideal reflection, to judge that they are in it. That rescues orphan states, keeps rocks out, and, I pointed out, ruins the theory's whole point, since it makes consciousness depend on a counterfactual about a thought nobody ever has. We also went in circles about whether any of this machinery handles transitive consciousness — being conscious of the coffee cup rather than merely being in a visual state — and neither of us had a line that felt right.

I want a joke out of this. Please write it in British English, and I'd like it to be a joke I could actually tell to someone who knows these theories: something in the form of a short exchange (a seminar room, a bar, a job interview, whatever works), whose punchline turns on the real machinery of the higher-order view — orphan states, the lighting-up step, the rock, the dispositional variant, creature versus state consciousness, transitive consciousness — rather than on the generic fact that philosophers are abstruse people. A punchline that someone innocent of the debate would read as merely odd, and someone fluent in it would laugh at, is exactly right; a pun on the word "higher" is not. After the joke, add two or three sentences at most saying which of the moves above the punchline is exploiting and why it lands, so that I can defend the joke if my friend claims it rests on a misreading.
