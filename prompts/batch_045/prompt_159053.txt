I'm putting together a one-page handout for the modeling course I TA, on when an unrealistic assumption is excusable and when it isn't, and I realised I have a lot of verdicts and no principle. Here they are, going by gut.

- Planets as point masses, Earth as a perfect sphere, in orbital mechanics: fine, obviously.
- Galileo's frictionless plane: fine.
- Ideal gas: point molecules, no intermolecular forces, perfectly elastic collisions: fine, even though it's false in a way that matters, since real gases condense.
- Hardy-Weinberg: infinite population, random mating, no selection, no mutation: fine, but for a weird reason — the whole point of the model is that its assumptions are false and the interesting quantity is how far the real population departs from them.
- Schelling's checkerboard segregation thing, where agents are dots on a grid with only a mild preference for same-colour neighbours: fine, genuinely illuminating.
- Friedman's billiard player, who plays as if he were solving the Newtonian equations for the shot: I want to say fine, but the as-if move makes me uneasy in a way the frictionless plane doesn't.
- Perfect rationality and expected-utility maximisation in a model whose conclusion is that markets are efficient: not fine. The conclusion is basically already in the assumptions.
- Representative agent in a macro model used to size a fiscal stimulus: not fine. Different households have different propensities to spend out of a transfer, and that heterogeneity is the mechanism.
- Coase: zero transaction costs: fine, and again the content seems to live in what happens when you relax it.
- Infinitely-lived agent with a transversality condition in an overlapping-generations model: not fine. This one bugs me because Hardy-Weinberg is also an infinite idealisation and I said that one was fine.
- The firm as a profit-maximising black box, in a theory-of-the-firm model: not fine. But the cell as a well-mixed bag of chemicals, in a model of a metabolic pathway: fine. Both swap a messy interior for something simple and I come out opposite ways.
- My own case, the Domino model of eviction cascades: I assume every household faces an identical eviction probability that it knows, and I assume landlords never renegotiate an arrears schedule. I feel fine about the first and queasy about the second, and they're equally false.

The one I keep flip-flopping on: homogeneous mixing in compartmental epidemic models. It's false, contacts are structured, and R0 and the herd-immunity threshold depend on it, but its also the basis of basically every useful result in the field.

My tentative rule, which I don't think survives: an unrealistic assumption is OK iff (a) the model's result is robust to de-idealising it, i.e. you put the friction back and the qualitative result survives, and (b) the assumption isn't about the very thing the model is supposed to explain.

Problems I can half see: (a) seems to condemn the ideal gas, since near condensation the law just fails, and maybe Schelling too, since I'm not sure the segregation result is robust at all. And (b) looks circular in a different way: in Schelling the assumption is about preferences and the model is explaining segregation from preferences, yet I want to allow it. But I cant see what should replace (b).

So: run the rule against my list, tell me which verdicts it can't deliver and which verdict it forces me to drop, and repair it into something precise, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it. I'd rather have a three-clause principle that saves everything than a tidy one that tells me the Domino model is fine after all, but say which you'd actually pick and why. And tell me what yours says about two cases I haven't listed: a model of citation networks in which papers cite uniformly at random among all earlier papers, and a model of academic hiring in which each candidate's quality is a single scalar that the committee observes perfectly.
