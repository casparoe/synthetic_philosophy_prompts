I have been going back and forth with a colleague about vagueness for months, and I would like to locate where our disagreement actually lives rather than continue trading intuitions. So I am asking for a number together with its derivation.

Fix a standard sorites series for a paradigmatic vague predicate — say a sequence of piles of sand in which neighbouring piles differ by exactly one grain — and define the target proposition as follows:

P: Every case in the series is either determinately a heap or determinately not a heap. No case is, in reality, neither. There may well be cases whose status we could not know even in principle, but ignorance is not indeterminacy.

P is what an epistemicist affirms and what truth-value-gap views (supervaluationist or otherwise), degree-theoretic views, and views positing ontic indeterminacy deny. It is deliberately neutral on the further question whether we could ever know where the cutoff falls.

My credence in P is about 0.4. My colleague's is about 0.75. Please give me your own, decomposed. I do not want a survey of the positions or a reminder that the answer depends on which theory of vagueness one accepts; I want a number per term and then a combination. The terms I have in mind, each with a probability and one line of justification:

1. How much cost actually attaches to giving up classical logic for vague language — rejecting excluded middle, or restricting classical inference. Put differently: how much of the case for P is really just a case for classicism rather than for determinacy?

2. Whether the "there is no sharp boundary" intuition is evidence about semantics at all, or merely a report of our ignorance and of our inability to discriminate neighbouring cases.

3. Whether higher-order vagueness is genuine: is there really no determinate boundary between the definite cases and the borderline ones, or does that appearance collapse back into the first-order question?

4. Whether our total linguistic practice — every judgement, disposition, and comparison that speakers would make, actual and counterfactual — could in principle determine a unique cutoff, or whether it is necessarily silent somewhere. Call this the determination question.

5. Whether contextualist or dynamic treatments, on which the extension shifts with the comparison class or the standards in play, undercut P entirely or merely relocate the cutoff to a per-context level.

6. Whether the margin-for-error style of argument — that any competent judgement about a case would have to hold throughout a small neighbourhood of similar cases, and so cannot track a sharp cutoff — is sound, or whether it presupposes what it is meant to establish.

7. Whether the two variants below are genuinely new problems or merely the standard sorites in a costume, and how much weight they deserve either way.

The two variants:

(a) The distributed-judge series. Arrange the same sequence of piles, but have each adjacent pair compared by a different speaker, at a different time, with no speaker comparing more than one pair and none aware of any other's verdict. Each judge reports the two piles indiscriminable; the conclusion that every pile is relevantly like the next, and hence that the first is like the last, is then assembled from separate reports rather than arrived at in any single mind. Does this alter the argument? In particular: does it suggest that vagueness is a feature of an aggregated practice rather than of any individual's discriminative capacities, and if so does that raise or lower the probability that the practice fixes a unique cutoff?

(b) The branching series. Instead of a single linear ordering, arrange the cases in a partially ordered structure: vary two dimensions at once — number of grains and size of grains, say — so that each case has several immediate neighbours and there are many distinct paths from the clear-heap region to the clear-non-heap region. Tolerance holds along every edge, but now every path must contain a cutoff and there is no single chain along which to locate one. Does the cutoff then have to be scattered across mutually incomparable branches, and does the awkwardness of that picture count as evidence against P?

Finally: combine your numbers into a single credence for P; tell me which individual term your final number is most sensitive to, in the sense that changing it would move the total most; tell me which of these sub-questions you think my colleague and I most likely disagree about, given that we sit at 0.4 and 0.75; and say what single piece of evidence or argument — empirical or philosophical — would move your number the most, and in which direction.
