My friend and I have been arguing about right and wrong for maybe ten years now, and the other night I finally did something I should of done ages ago: I wrote down every verdict she's ever given me, in a list, to see if there's actually a rule in there or whether she's just making it up as she goes. Here's the list, more or less verbatim from what she's said:

1. Lying to a friend about a surprise party: fine.
2. Lying to a friend to get her to come to a party where everyone is going to laugh at her: not fine.
3. Lying to police who would torture your friend, about where he's hiding: fine, obviously.
4. A doctor hiding a terminal diagnosis from a patient who'd previously said "always tell me the truth": not fine.
5. A doctor hiding the same diagnosis from a patient who'd said "don't tell me, tell my wife": fine.
6. A coffee company's ad implying its beans are shade grown and bird friendly when they aren't: not fine, even though no individual buyer is made worse off in any way you could measure.
7. Telling your six year old that the tooth fairy is real: fine.
8. Not mentioning to your partner that you ran into an ex at a party: fine.
9. Not mentioning that your ex has been texting you every week for a month: not fine.
10. Secretly recording a contractor who is overcharging your elderly neighbour, without telling him: fine.

She insists she's not running any rule, that each case is its own thing and the same feature can count for something in one case and against in another. I think she's wrong and that she's running something she hasn't said out loud, but when I try to say what it is I end up with three or four different formulas that all fit the ten verdicts above perfectly.

Here's the new case I want to throw at her. My mother has dementia and is in a home. She asks most days whether her sister is coming to visit, and her sister died three years ago. The staff go along with it — "soon, she's busy, maybe next week" — and they say it's kinder, and honestly it probably is: when someone has told her the truth, she grieves the whole thing again from the beginning, and then an hour later she asks again, so it isn't one sad conversation, it's the same sad conversation every day for the rest of her life. But she can't consent to being managed like that, she'd defintely refuse if she understood, and she never said anything in advance about how she'd want to be handled.

What I want from you: what is my friend going to say about this case, and what are the different rules she might be running that all fit her ten verdicts but disagree here? Give me your best guess with the reason you prefer it, and don't just tell me "it depends on the details" unless you think that genuinely is her answer — and if you do think that, tell me whether that's a real position or a way of avoiding the question, because that's the part of the argument I actually care about.

Also: is there one extra case I could put to her that would cleanly tell the candidate rules apart? I was going to try a version where the same woman, two years before the dementia, while perfectly competent, told my friend "if I'm ever like that, never lie to me, even about something that will upset me" — good consequences still point one way, prior consent points the other. Is that the right case, or is there a cleaner one?
