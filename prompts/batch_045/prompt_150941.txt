I'm helping put together the film night for a historiography seminar I TA for, and we're using Ford's The Man Who Shot Liberty Valance (1962) as the one non-book text on the syllabus — the module opens with Butterfield's The Whig Interpretation of History, so I want the screening to do real argumentative work instead of just being a treat at the end of term. The basic question I keep going back and forth on: is the film a critique of whig history, or is it one of the more sophisticated specimens of it? Butterfield's complaints — narrating the past with reference to the present, turning a sequence of events into a line of causation, abridging out whatever doesn't lead anywhere, casting the whole thing as a morality play with heroes of progress against a dummy foil — map onto this plot uncannily well, and yet the film seems to know all of that and to be staging it deliberately, which is exactly what makes me unsure how to place it.

Two distinctions I've been playing with, which you should feel free to junk if they don't hold up. First: I've started calling one variant elegiac whiggism — a progress story that openly concedes the violence and loss at the origin and mourns it, but where the mourning is precisely what licenses the celebration, so that the regret functions as a ratification rather than a criticism. Ford's ending (Stoddard's whole career disclosed, the editor burning the reporter's notes, Doniphon buried poor while the senator rides back east, Hallie quietly putting the cactus rose on the coffin, the conductor congratulating the wrong man) looks to me like either the sharpest possible indictment of that move or a textbook instance of it, and I can't tell which. Second: Butterfield mostly treats whig history as an *epistemic* failure — the historian mis-abridges, anachronises, can't see past people as strangers. But the editor in this film knows the facts, has them written down, and suppresses them on purpose, and Doniphon colludes; so the film raises a different question, whether the whig narrative can be a piece of political technology rather than a mistake, and if so whether that rescues it, damns it more thoroughly, or just leaves Butterfield's critique aimed at the wrong target. I'd like you to work that through, and to say what the film adds here that the prose argument doesn't give you.

Please be concrete about scenes rather than giving me a plot summary: the schoolhouse in the back of the Star's office and Stoddard teaching Hallie to read; the delegate election in Shinbone where Pompey puts his hand up and the cattle barons' man objects; Doniphon burning his own half-finished house after the shooting; the nested flashback at the territorial convention where he tells Stoddard what actually happened in the street; and that last long train ride. And please be honest about where the whig lens stops being useful — I'd rather you tell me the reading distorts the film (or distorts Butterfield) in some particular place than smooth it over, since I'll be running this in a discussion section and the students will find the seams anyway. If it's helpful, I'm also mildly interested in whether the film's own form — a Senator's flashback narrated from the vantage of the career it produced — is itself a whig structure, i.e. whether any story told backwards from an achieved present can avoid teleology, or whether the film is quietly conceding that it can't.
