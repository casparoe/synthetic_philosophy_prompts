I write for a magazine that covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about the current fashion for grading aesthetic quality in settings that used to resist it: a scientific society that now asks peer reviewers to score figures on visual quality, architectural juries required to give written reasons for findings of visual merit, and several labs building models trained on pairwise human judgments of images. In an interview, a psychologist who studies aesthetic preference dismissed the whole enterprise with the line there's no disputing about taste, and I could not tell whether she was making an empirical claim about how much people diverge, a conceptual claim about what sort of thing a judgment of taste is, or a normative claim about the legitimacy of grading at all.

Please set out the distinct precise theses that sentence could be expressing, ordered from the ones nobody denies to the ones that would be a genuine discovery if true. For each, say whether it is trivial, contestable but defensible, or false, and whether it would actually entitle her to the conclusion that these grading practices are illegitimate rather than merely noisy, biased, coarse-grained, or in need of calibration. I am most interested in the distance between the observation that responses vary and the conclusion that no response is more correct than another, and in what Hume's essay on the standard of taste commits a person to, since my impression is that he concedes an enormous amount to variation while still holding onto something like expertise; I want to know which of the readings he would be rejecting and which he could live with. Please also keep questions of reliability separate from questions of whether there is anything for a measurement to be reliable about, and flag any reading whose truth would leave the scoring practices perfectly intact.

Give me your answer as a numbered list of exactly five entries, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
