I spent most of last night arguing with a friend who works on the team that builds and maintains the automated triage system at a public benefits agency — the one that ranks incoming claims by risk and decides which ones a human caseworker reviews at all. We didn't get anywhere, and I think it's because we were disagreeing about something philosophical without knowing how to say it, so let me describe the practice and ask what it commits you to.

The system is trained on the verdicts of a rotating panel of experienced caseworkers who read anonymized files. Four features of the setup struck me as pulling in different directions. First, cases on which the panel splits are kept, and the model is not trained on the majority verdict but on the whole spread: at deployment it samples from the distribution the panel produced, so two identical files can get different risk scores, and the team's stated rationale is that a system which always picks a side is pretending to a certainty the case doesn't have. Second, panelists whose verdicts are statistical outliers across a batch are removed from the panel before training, on the ground that they aren't reliable judges. Third, the model has to emit a written rationale before it emits a verdict, and the team tests whether editing that rationale flips the verdict; a model that reaches the panel's own verdicts by a route the team dislikes — the claimant's postcode, the number of days since the last claim — is treated as defective and retrained, even though its verdicts agree with the panel's. Fourth, a human caseworker can override the system with no explanation required, and no one has proposed letting the system override the human even in the cases where it is demonstrably better than any individual caseworker at predicting what the panel would have said.

So: what does this bundle presuppose about what it is for a normative judgment to be correct? Does training on the spread of opinion commit you to thinking there is no single right answer in hard cases, and if so, what is the outlier-removal supposed to be tracking — is it expertise, or just agreement? Does the insistence on the right *route* rather than the right *outcome* commit you to something realist-sounding, namely that reasons and not verdicts are the primary bearers of correctness, even though the verdicts themselves are defined by what the panel happened to say? And what is the asymmetry in the last feature supposed to rest on: that human judgment has an authority that doesn't need to be discursive, that only agents who can be held responsible owe reasons, or just that we distrust whatever a model's rationale is evidence of?

I'd like more than a verdict on whether the practice is coherent. Tell me which of these features strictly presuppose a metaphysical or semantic view about normative judgment and which are compatible with several — my friend's position was that the whole thing is philosophically indifferent and just engineering, mine was that it doesn't hang together. Also tell me what would actually have to change in the design if the team adopted the opposite view at each point: if they treated the panel as fallible evidence of a fact of the matter rather than as the thing that settles it, or if they treated the panel's verdicts as the whole of what there is to get right.
