Hey — I need a hand turning a half-formed idea into an actual proposal. I'm applying for a small six-month independent-research grant (part time, I have a day job doing forecast evaluation for a climate-adaptation unit, and the deliverable is basically one journal article plus a short policy memo). I got into this after arguing with my brother-in-law, who's an economist, about whether Malthus was "right but early." He says obviously yes, population did break out of the trap only because of the very pressures Malthus described; I said that's exactly what you'd say no matter what happened. Then I realized the same argument gets recycled for the 70s-era limits models, for peak-resource forecasts, and for that 1980 bet about metal prices between an ecologist and an economist — and that nobody seems to have a non-hand-wavy way of scoring it. So: my half-formed idea is that "right but early" bundles together at least three quite different situations, and the interesting work is in prising them apart. Call them (1) dormancy — the theory says X happens once some trigger variable crosses a threshold, the threshold genuinely hasn't been crossed in the window we've observed, so there hasn't been a test yet; (2) what I've started calling the Cassandra dividend — the prediction was believed, acted on, and thereby averted, so the failure of the predicted outcome is evidence of uptake rather than of error; and (3) mechanism-swap — the original mechanism gets quietly replaced (positive checks replaced by a demographic-transition story, say) while the old vocabulary is kept for continuity. My guess is that only dormancy is really "early," that the Cassandra dividend is a genuine but wildly over-claimed category that needs a hard evidential test before anyone gets to invoke it, and that mechanism-swap is nearly always degenerative. But I don't trust that guess yet and I want the proposal to be able to survive being wrong about it.

So write me the thing: a precise statement of the question (something like: under what conditions is the "right but early" verdict rationally available for a long-run quantitative prediction whose test window hasn't closed, and can those conditions be stated so they give determinate verdicts on real cases?), and then why the obvious resources don't settle it as it stands — the coarse progressive-versus-degenerating framing gives you a vocabulary but no rates and no way to tell preemption from dormancy; naive Bayesian updating treats every missed date as just another update and has no notion of a rescue costing you something extra; and the forecasting literature's scoring rules only apply once an event has resolved, whereas this whole question lives precisely in the unresolved-window case. Then the approach, and why it rather than the alternatives: I'm imagining (a) building the taxonomy off a handful of concrete cases, (b) trying to say something principled about the credibility cost of a postponement — when a predicted date slips or a trigger gets re-specified, how much should that cost you, and is there any non-arbitrary answer, (c) a move I think is being underused: instead of waiting for the global case, probe a dormant theory on the units that got closest to the trigger — the regions or periods where the ceiling actually bound — and ask what evidential weight those near-misses carry, and (d) a hindcast check, running the finished framework as if it were 1800, 1900, 1975 and 2000 and seeing whether it licenses the verdicts we'd now accept, which is my main guard against building something that just rationalizes whatever I already believe. The policy memo side should fall out of this: a "rescue protocol" that an institution issuing long-run forecasts could pre-register, saying in advance which auxiliary changes count as legitimate refinement and which close the book.

Please also give me the honest parts, because my referee will ask: what would count as this project failing (e.g. the taxonomy collapses into case-by-case judgement, or the postponement cost turns out to have no principled motivation and has to be set by stipulation, or the hindcast check shows every historical case was actually decided by non-epistemic factors like who had tenure), and what gets cut if six months part time turns out to be too small — tell me which piece you'd drop first and why. Don't give me a literature review or any citations, I'll fill all that in myself, and please don't just conclude that it depends on the case; if you think it does depend, tell me what the dependency is tracking.
