I have been asked, together with two colleagues in economics, to design the call for a three-year research programme on moral trade. The budget is four postdoctoral positions, and the remit from the funder is unusually concrete: they want the programme to end with results that would change how large philanthropic funders, and perhaps eventually states, allocate resources in the presence of deep moral disagreement. My colleagues want a call organised around application areas. I think that is premature, because I do not think we yet know which of the open questions in this area are load-bearing and which are decoration.

Let me fix the object. By moral trade I mean the arrangement in which parties who disagree about what matters morally each perform the action the other's view ranks highly in exchange for the reverse: I fund the intervention your consequentialism prizes, you fund the thing my traditionalism or perfectionism or nationalism prizes, and each of us expects, by our own lights, to have done better than we could have done acting separately. Assume for this exercise that the parties are sincere, that their moral views are stable rather than up for revision, and that they are not trading under ordinary empirical ignorance about consequences. I want the conceptual questions, not the measurement ones.

The questions I can currently see on the table are these. Enforcement and credible commitment: how parties bind themselves when there is no external enforcer, and what the difference is between a moral trade and a mere assurance game. The unit of account: what exactly changes hands, and whether the currency of trade can be specified without presupposing a common standard that the parties by hypothesis reject. Adverse selection: agents holding the most permissive or most aggregative views can offer the most in any bilateral trade, so a mature market may be dominated by precisely the theories the trading parties least want to empower; call this Gresham's law of moral trade. Endogeneity: if trade is available and widespread, each party's effective normative position drifts toward whatever view is most willing to trade, which in practice means toward something consequentialism-shaped, and it is unclear whether that convergence is a defect of the institution or its point. Trade with parties who cannot or will not recognise themselves as trading: future people, nonhuman animals, agents whose commitments are structured as protected or sacred values that resist being placed on any common metric, and agents whose views we represent only by model or simulation. Aggregative effects: many bilateral trades, each rational by each trader's own lights, may leave every trader worse off by those same lights, a market-level analogue of a prisoner's dilemma. And finally the relation between moral trade and the other things we already do under moral disagreement: moral hedging, bargaining toward a shared second-order theory, legislative compromise, vote trading, and the accommodation and deference that pluralist societies already practise.

What I want from you is a defended ordering of the call, not a survey and not a reading list; I can find the literature myself. Be explicit about the criteria you are using, how much weight each carries, and where they conflict: importance to the funder's goal of changing institutional allocation, tractability in three years for people trained in philosophy with some decision theory, neglectedness, and how much of the rest of the agenda depends on the answer to this question rather than the reverse. Tell me which of the questions above are genuinely upstream, which are downstream and can wait, and which are pseudo-questions or would not change anything downstream even if settled. Tell me also which of them are really questions for moral philosophy rather than for institutional design or game theory, since I will have to defend the programme's share of the money to my economist colleagues. If there is a question I have not listed that matters more than any I have, say so and say why.

My own hunch, offered as a hypothesis you should feel free to attack, is that the unit-of-account question is upstream of everything else, including enforcement: until we can say what is being traded and in what it is denominated, the rest of the agenda has no clear object, and the enforcement literature is tacitly assuming an answer. My second hunch is that the endogeneity question is the one with the most surprising implications for how institutions should behave, and the one most likely to be resisted by people attracted to moral trade in the first place. Argue me up or down on both.

Finally, tell me what would change the ranking. If over the next three years one of these questions were answered, or shown to be unanswerable, or dissolved by a distinction, which others would move, and in which direction? What would you put in the call as the intended falsification test for the wager that this programme is worth funding at all?
