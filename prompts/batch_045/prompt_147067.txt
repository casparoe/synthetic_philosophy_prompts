Something about the way institutions talk has been bugging me and I think it's a philosophy thing, but I can't get the shape of it right. A friend said "oh that's Grice, implicature" and then couldn't explain either, so here I am.

Here's the pattern. On the one hand: a lot of jurisdictions have passed so-called apology laws, and hospitals have built whole "disclosure" programs around them, on the assumption that if a surgeon says to a family "I'm sorry this happened" after something went wrong, a jury will hear that as an admission of fault and hold it against her. The whole point of the law is to switch that off. So the practice seems to presuppose that what a speaker communicates outruns what her words literally say, that hearers will reliably draw the extra inference, and that the speaker can't stop them just by privately meaning something else. Very Gricean so far, I guess.

On the other hand: the same institutions, and every insurer and comms consultant advising them, behave as if the opposite were true. A company issues a statement saying it "regrets that some customers were disappointed" or that "mistakes were made," everybody can see exactly what that's doing, and it's still treated as having satisfied the demand for an apology — the box gets ticked, the story moves on. Here the practice seems to presuppose that you're only on the hook for what the sentence strictly says: no form of the words contains "we were at fault," so nothing has been communicated, so nothing has been conceded. Lawyers apparently tell clients to apologise only in the passive voice and never with "I" in the sentence.

So it looks like the same legal culture buys two flatly incompatible theories of speaker meaning, and picks whichever one is convenient. But maybe it doesn't look that way to someone who knows the machinery, which is what I want from you.

Concretely: reconstruct the implicit theory (or theories) here. What does each practice strictly presuppose about the gap between what is said and what is communicated, and what does it merely sit well with? Are they actually inconsistent, or is there a single view — something about uptake, or about what a reasonable hearer would take you to be doing, or about assertion as taking on a commitment rather than as expressing a mental state — that makes both practices sensible at once, just aimed at different audiences? Does Grice's own apparatus (what is said versus what is implicated, cancellability, the idea that implicatures are worked out from the assumption that the speaker is cooperating) actually explain the asymmetry, or does it tell against one of the two practices? And what would those speech-act people, or the relevance-theory crowd, say differently?

Then the bit I really can't figure out. Some risk-management offices apparently ban the word "sorry" outright but require clinicians to say something like "I wish this hadn't happened to your family." Is there a principled line there, or is that just a superstition about individual words? And relatedly: if we replaced the implicit theory with one where you're responsible for what a reasonable hearer would infer, full stop, no cancellability escape hatch — so that bolting "and of course I'm admitting nothing" onto the end of an apology changes nothing — what would these practices have to look like? Would the apology laws become pointless, or would the non-apology become impossible, or both? And which of the two would we lose more by losing?
