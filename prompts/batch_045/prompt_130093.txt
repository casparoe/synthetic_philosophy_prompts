Suppose John Stuart Mill, in the last decade of his life, were shown the following arrangement and asked to pass judgment on it: a modern industrial economy in which nearly all corporate equity is held by a single public fund; every adult citizen receives an equal annual dividend from the returns on that capital, which may be spent but neither sold nor bequeathed; firms continue to compete in ordinary markets under appointed managers rather than being governed by their workers; and wage labour continues much as before.

Mill's late commitments pull in several directions at once. He held that the laws of production are fixed by nature while distribution is a matter of human institution; he expected industrial co-operation among the workers themselves, spreading by example and competition rather than by legal compulsion, to be the form industry would gradually take; he was hostile to any scheme that made government the universal employer or concentrated economic direction in one bureaucracy; he wanted great fortunes effectively barred from passing by inheritance; and he judged institutions partly by their effect on the character, intelligence, and foresight of the labouring classes, including their prudence about family size.

In roughly six hundred words, take a definite position: would he greet this dividend fund as a genuine step toward the society he came to hope for in his later years, or as a counterfeit of it? Identify the one feature on which his reaction would land first and hardest — the extinction of inherited fortune, the absence of self-government in the workplace, the concentration of capital management in a single fund, or the effect of a guaranteed birthright income on prudence and population — and argue why, for him specifically, that one outweighs the others. Then name the smallest single change to the arrangement that would make the most difference to his verdict.
