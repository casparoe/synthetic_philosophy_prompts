I'm revising a paper on the Born rule problem in the no-collapse (Everettian) interpretation, and before I commit to the package in print I want someone to audit it. Below are the commitments I'm working from, listed in roughly the order I arrived at them rather than in order of confidence. I don't know which one is the weak point, and I'd rather find out now than in the comments section.

(a) The interpretation is deterministic and strictly no-collapse: every outcome with nonzero amplitude is actual, and there is no collapse, no hidden variables, and no additional ontology beyond the quantum state and its unitary dynamics.

(b) Probability in this setting cannot be a matter of chances, in the sense of the chance that *this* outcome rather than *that* one will occur. All the outcomes occur. So the probabilistic question is a self-locating one: what credence should an agent have, before and after branching, in being located on a branch of a given kind?

(c) That said, the weights are not up to us. There is an objectively correct credence function for an agent in a branching situation, fixed by the physics rather than by the agent's tastes; two agents sharing a total physical state and the same evidence should converge on it, and an agent who deviates from it is making a mistake, not expressing a preference.

(d) I don't want to buy the weights by postulating extra structure — no primitive thickness, size, or measure that branches carry over and above their amplitudes. The Born rule should fall out of the dynamics together with constraints on rational agency. The decision-theoretic program of the Deutsch–Wallace sort still seems to me the right shape: branching indifference, diachronic consistency, and value read off the agent's own preferences over branch-relative outcomes.

(e) Within that program, I want the equal-amplitude case to do the foundational work. If a branching yields n branches of equal amplitude, each gets credence 1/n; the general case is then obtained by decomposing unequal amplitudes into equal-amplitude pieces, or by a symmetry argument of the kind that turns on entanglement-assisted invariance. The equal-amplitude case is the one place where I have an intuition I actually trust, and everything else is supposed to be derived from it.

(f) Branching itself is emergent and approximate: it is decoherence, not a primitive. There is no exact, once-and-for-all fact about how many branches exist at a time or about what the fine-grained decomposition into branches is. Any such decomposition is relative to a coarse-graining, and it becomes sharp only at the level of description that matters to agents like us.

(g) On self-location specifically: there is no deep further fact about which of my successors is *me*, beyond that successor's subjective state and the physical state. So I should spread self-locating credence by indifference across my subjectively indistinguishable successors, treating none of them as privileged.

(h) Two case verdicts I'm attached to. First: suppose an experimenter installs a "multiplexer" in front of her apparatus — a device which, conditional on outcome A, splits the lab into a thousand decohered but subjectively indistinguishable copies, each carrying one thousandth of the outcome-A amplitude, and which, conditional on outcome B, does nothing. I'm committed to saying that this changes nothing about what anyone should expect: the credence in A is exactly what it was before the multiplexer was switched on. Second: the quantum-suicide or quantum-immortality line of reasoning is fallacious. An agent facing repeated branching in which she has a surviving successor on only one branch should not expect, from the first-person point of view, to find herself surviving indefinitely.

Please audit the set. Which of (a)–(h) are genuinely in tension with which others, and which pairs only look as though they are? Order the real tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad. For each, say precisely what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the package. Then say which single revision would clean up the most. Where one of my commitments cannot deliver one of the verdicts in (h), say so plainly and say which commitment has to be weakened. I'm not looking for a survey of approaches to the Born rule; I want to know whether this package is consistent, and if not, where the cheapest repair is.
