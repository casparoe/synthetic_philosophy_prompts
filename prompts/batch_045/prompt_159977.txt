I'm in a small reading group — two NLP people, a roboticist, and a philosopher who wanderd in one week and never left — and we've spent the last three sessions going in circles about whether a system trained only on text can be said to mean anything by the words it uses.

The roboticist has one move he makes every time, and he clearly thinks it ends the discussion: a text-only model, he says, is like a person locked in a room who learned Chinese from a Chinese–Chinese dictionary, no pictures, no Chinese speakers, no way to point at anything. Every lookup hands you more Chinese words, the whole system floats free of the world, and it doesn't matter how many entries you add or how fast you flip between them — which, as he keeps saying, is just the symbol grounding problem restated. He takes it to license something pretty strong: language models don't understand anything, the fix has to be sensors and actuators (or at least images, and letting the system pick out its own categories from what it sees), and scaling up text training isn't a slow road to understanding but a category error. One of the NLP people pushes back and I can't tell how good her pushback is: real text isn't a closed circle of definitions, dictionaries do say things like the colour of blood or of a ripe tomato, and corpora are stuffed with people recording what they saw and did — field notes, repair logs, recipes, trial transcripts, endless descriptions of weather — so the training data is less like a dictionary and more like an enormous pile of testimony from people who were actually there, and his analogy quietly assumes the thing it was supposed to show.

Last week I tried my own analogy on them and it didn't settle as much as I'd hoped: a text-only model is like someone who has read every restaurant review ever written but has never tasted food. They'd be extraordinary at predicting what gets written about a dish, could turn out a flawless new review, could tell you a kumquat is small, orange and sharp — but they don't know what one tastes like. The roboticist loved it; the philosopher said it changes the subject in a way that helps the roboticist unfairly (I think he means it sneaks in a private what-it's-like where the real issue is whether the system's symbols are causally hooked up to anything at all), and I can't tell who's right.

So can you audit both analogies properly? Give me the correspondence table for each — what plays the room, the dictionary, the entries, the learner, the world, the moment of contact — then say where each mapping breaks and which of the features each analogy imports from its source the target genuinely lacks versus only seems to lack. Crucially, sort the disanalogies by whether they matter for the conclusion he wants (no grounding, no understanding, scaling can't fix it) or whether they're just decoration. And tell me whether there's a better third analogy — something like a thermometer, or a chain of hearsay, or a colour-blind botanist — and if there is, whether it supports anybody's conclusion or just dissolves the argument. I want a straight verdict at the end on whether the analogies were doing the arguing for him.
