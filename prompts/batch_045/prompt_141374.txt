I recently read an essay on a philosophy-and-rationality site that presents the lottery paradox and the preface paradox as a single puzzle. I would like an accuracy audit of it before I rely on it, because I need to use its conclusions in a setting rather different from the ones it discusses. The piece says:

Both puzzles come from the same place: high probability is not certainty, and rational acceptance is not closed under conjunction. In Kyburg's lottery — a fair thousand-ticket draw with exactly one winner — it is rational to accept, of ticket 1, that it will lose; likewise for ticket 2; and so on through ticket 1,000, since each ticket has a 99.9 percent chance of losing. It is equally rational to accept that some ticket will win. But those thousand acceptances jointly entail that no ticket wins, which contradicts something else you accept. The only premise that can be surrendered is the rule that lets you conjoin, so rational acceptability is not closed under conjunction — which is Kyburg's own conclusion. Makinson's preface paradox is the same argument in academic dress: substitute the nth claim in my book is true for ticket n will lose, and this book contains at least one error for some ticket wins. The author has checked every claim, so each is rationally acceptable, yet experience tells her that a book this long will contain an error — if each of a hundred claims is 99 percent likely to be correct, the conjunction is worse than even money. So there is one paradox here, not two, and one moral: stop treating belief as a yes-or-no matter. Think in credences and the contradiction vanishes: 0.999 for each ticket, and the conjunction gets whatever the product of the individual credences yields. Where a threshold is unavoidable, let it rise with the number of claims you accept — the more you accept, the better supported each acceptance must be. The practical upshot follows directly: in any large collection of claims, no individual entry can be trusted on its own, and the honest writer hedges every one.

My instinct is that the unification is too quick. In the lottery I am handed the probability distribution and the existence of a winner is guaranteed by the setup; in the preface the error claim seems to be an uncertain, track-record-driven belief about my own fallibility, and it is itself one of the book's claims, so it could be the false one. But the essay is written with enough authority that I would like the audit done properly rather than trusted to my instinct.

Please give me a one-line verdict on the passage, then work through it claim by claim, separating (a) what someone working in formal epistemology would accept without qualification, (b) what is a legitimate simplification that a defender would still stand behind, and (c) what is subtly wrong or overstated, with the reason. I am especially interested in whether the identity claim — one paradox, one solution — survives, and in whether the credal treatment of the conjunction is right as stated, given that the lottery's thousand outcomes are not probabilistically independent. I would also like to know whether the closing practical moral follows from anything that survives the audit.

Then I would like the corrected apparatus applied to my own case. I sit on a committee that maintains a standing set of practice recommendations: roughly four hundred at any time, each of which has passed our endorsement bar on its own evidence. The foreword, which all of us sign, states that at least one recommendation in the document is wrong. The document is revised continuously — recommendations are added, revised, or quietly withdrawn — so at no point does it have a determinate, closed membership, and none of us knows how many recommendations it will contain in a year. The errors are also not independent: several recommendations typically rest on the same study or the same measurement convention, so when one falls its neighbours tend to fall with it.

Does this case behave like the lottery, like the preface, like both, or like neither, and which features settle the classification? Does the rising-threshold fix survive when the corpus is open-ended and the number of accepted claims is unknown to the acceptor? Does positive correlation among errors make our position better or worse, and why? And on the corrected account, is signing both the foreword and each recommendation incoherent — if not, what exactly licenses us to do it, and if we reformulate the foreword to avoid the difficulty, does the difficulty reappear for the reformulation?
