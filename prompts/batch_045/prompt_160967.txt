I've been sitting with one account of Moore's paradox for a while and I want to know what would actually break it. The account is basically the transparency story: the weirdness of p, but I don't believe that p isn't semantic, and it isn't really a rule about speech either -- it falls out of the fact that, from the inside, the question whether I believe p gets settled by settling whether p itself. Asked whether it's raining, I look out the window, not inward at my own mind. So in the Moorean sentence I'm using one and the same stretch of reasoning both to settle that p and to deny that I've settled it: a clash in the standpoint of deliberation, not a contradiction in what's said. And the commissive version (I believe that not-p) is the same clash, with me treating p and not-p as both settled.

What I like about it is that it explains why the third-person case is completely fine and why the oddity shows up in thought and in a diary you're going to burn, not just in things you say out loud.

But I'm worried I've picked the account that's hardest to test. So: what would count against it? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and please separate the ones a defender of this account should concede as genuine from the ones they'd swat away -- and then say which of those swattings is legit and which is a dodge.

Cases I keep coming back to; use them or replace them:

1. The calibration case. I have excellent evidence that my own belief-formation on some topic is currently broken -- a medication, say, that reliably leaves me convinced nothing is wrong when something is. I look at the rain, I check the gauge, and I say: it's raining, and I don't believe it, because my rain-beliefs are worthless right now. Does that still sound Moorean? And here's the twist I can't shake: if I really have that defeater, shouldn't I also stop flat-out asserting the first conjunct? Is the case just unstable, and does the account get credit for predicting that?

2. The outsourced self. Imagine nearly everyone carries a readout that reports their own confidence better than introspection does, and people routinely say: p -- the readout says so -- but I don't believe it yet. If that became ordinary and unproblematic, then the account looks committed to saying the paradox would vanish if the architecture of self-knowledge changed. Is that a concession a defender should happily make, and does it quietly turn a constitutive claim into an empirical one?

3. The old self-blindness thought experiment: someone who can only find out what they believe by watching their own behavior, the way they'd watch someone else's. If such a person could say the Moorean sentence without absurdity, is that a counterexample, or is it ruled out as impossible, and on what grounds?

4. Obligatory source-marking. In a language where every statement is grammatically tagged for whether you saw it, inferred it, or were told it, which tags keep the paradox and which lose it? In English, apparently p but I don't believe it is fine while the untagged version isn't -- is that difference about hedging, about source, or about something else?

5. Unasserted Moore: in a diary, in silent inner speech, in the antecedent of a conditional. And the iterated versions -- p but I don't believe that I believe that p, versus p but I believe that I don't believe that p. If one is fine and the other isn't, which account predicted that in advance?

6. No-subject cases. A system with no first-person authority at all -- a large predictive model, a committee, a firm -- that reliably pairs p-reports with denials of belief. If nothing there strikes us as incoherent, does that count against the story or just show nobody's home? And if such a system did strike us as incoherent, what would that show?

7. The half-belief family: grief, self-deception, the glass-floor flinch, and the neurological and psychiatric cases where someone seems to both report that p and sincerely deny believing it. These are often said to be fine, or at least not absurd.

Also: the commissive version feels worse to me than the omissive one. Does the account owe me an asymmetry there, and is its inability to produce one evidence against unifying the two?

Be honest about the big worry. If every apparent counterexample gets reclassified -- as loose talk, as a different sense of believe, as not really an avowal -- then what exactly does the account predict, and has it been qualified into unfalsifiability? If so, tell me whether I should care: is that acceptable for a constitutive claim about the first person, or does it collapse into a rival (avowal-as-expression, a norm on assertion, or a flat-footed semantic contradiction)?

For what it's worth, I trust my own gut reaction to the drug case and the readout case definately more than I trust my reaction to any armchair variant, so weight those accordingly.
