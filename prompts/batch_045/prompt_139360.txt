I'm putting together a short talk for a general philosophy audience on whether translation systems understand anything, and I want to rebuild the argument around Hofstadter's "shallowness" complaint instead of just rehearsing his examples. The distinction I've been chewing on is a three-way one: shallowness (the system keeps no situation model — no representation of who is doing what to whom, what's physically possible, what's already been established earlier in the text), rootlessness (no perception, no body, no action in the world — everything it knows arrives as text), and stakelessness (nothing rides on getting it right; no cost to being wrong, no commitment to one reading over another). Old-school statistical machine translation was all three at once. I'd argue today's systems still are all three, but the question I actually care about is which of them is doing the work in the cases that move people.

So here's what I want you to build. A minimal-pair translation case: two short English source texts (same genre, ordinary register, under about 60 words each, no proper nouns that need looking up, no rare vocabulary, and crucially no idioms or proverbs whose translation is just a memorized equivalent — those get waved off as lexicon lookup, which is exactly the dismissal I'm trying to get ahead of), differing in one small way, plus a target language with some grammatical feature that forces an explicit choice: gendered pronouns, honorifics, evidentiality, classifiers, whatever. Constraints:

1. The two sources are near-identical at the level of word choice and multiword-chunk statistics, so anything that works the way Hofstadter said shallow systems work — phrase-level regularities, no situation model — has to produce basically the same translation for both.
2. The correct translations differ, and the difference turns on a fact about the world that is never stated in either text.
3. That fact isn't the kind of thing perception or embodiment hands you. A system with excellent vision, a body, and a lifetime of ordinary physical experience, but no ability to assemble a situation model across a discourse, also gets it wrong. This is the bit I care most about: I want grounding to be idle in the case.
4. Any competent human translator resolves it in a couple of seconds without noticing there was anything to resolve. If the case reads as a puzzle or a brainteaser, it's a bad case.

If you write the target-language forms in a real language, gloss every form and stick to morphology you're actually confident about; if you'd rather specify a tiny invented language with one clearly defined feature, that's fine and probably safer.

Then the check. For each of three systems — the shallow statistical translator, the grounded-but-still-shallow one, and the understanding translator — say what it outputs for each source and why, and make it clear the verdicts fall out of what each system is defined to have rather than from you stipulating who understands. After that: which single feature of the case is doing the work; what the case collapses into if you delete that feature (i.e. does it just become a case were corpus statistics are enough and the whole debate deflates?); and the two attacks I should expect — the statistical-system defender saying the unstated fact is already there in distributional statistics, and the grounding theorist saying the fact bottoms out in embodied experience anyway so grounding was never really idle. Tell me briefly how damaging each one is and whether the case can be patched against them.
