My friend Sam and I spent about three hours last night arguing about whether the July Crisis of 1914 is basically a commitment race, and by the end I think we were both just repeating ourselves. His version: the mobilisation timetables were irreversible self-modification bolted on years before anyone knew what the crisis would look like, once the trains started nobody could stop them, so you get a race to be the side that's locked in first and everyone ends up somewhere nobody wanted. (The "commitment race" frame is from that decision-theory corner where the worry is that agents self-modify while still ignorant of each other, and that being smart is a losing position because you win by making yourself rigid and dumb fast. That's roughly how I understand the term, so correct me if I've got it wrong.) My version: the race framing is rational-choice cosplay, because most of the people in charge either wanted the war or at least preferred it in 1914 to later, given Russian rearmament and the closing window — so what looks like a tragic structural trap is mostly revealed preference.

What I want is the mapping done properly, and then an honest account of where the model stops working. Who the "agents" actually are — are Berlin, Vienna, Petersburg, Paris and London unitary agents at all, and what do you do with the fact that inside Germany the civilians and the General Staff wanted different things and the plan belonged to the second? What the "commitment" is supposed to be: the ultimatum to Serbia, the Austrian declaration, partial versus general mobilisation, the deployment plan itself. What the real option set looked like in July rather than in hindsight. And what the observability channel was — ambassadors, telegrams, military attachés — because a commitment that the other side can't see in time shouldn't do any work in the model.

Two hunches of mine, offered as hunches. First, a principal/agent inversion: the theory has an agent modifying itself, but here the war plans modified the principals. The plans were written in peacetime by people who didn't know the situation they'd be used in, they were treated as unchangeable, and when the Kaiser reportedly tried to switch to an eastern-only deployment after that telegram from London saying Britain might stay out, he was told it couldn't be done (I've probbaly garbled the details, so use it loosely). That's a different failure mode than a race: you delegated to something you can't revoke. Second, a distinction I made up on the walk home: a credibility race, where you want to be legibly locked in first so the other guy has to fold, versus a blame race, where everyone is trying to look like they moved second — Russia's partial mobilisation, Austria framing it as a local punitive expedition, Britain waiting for Belgium — because being the visible aggressor cost you at home and with the British cabinet. Those two pull in opposite directions. Is that a real distinction, or is the blame race just the credibility race under partial observability, and does that mean the "race" part dissolves into something more like a signalling failure?

Here's the bit I most want you to push on. The race only bites if the other side folds when faced with an apparently committed opponent — the sane-agent claim is that you shouldn't be exploitable that way, that you fold with whatever probability makes the commitment not worth making. But nobody in July folded. So either the commitments weren't credible, or the game wasn't shaped the way the model needs it to be. And there's a multi-player wrinkle: the one actor that flatly refused to commit, Britain, whose cabinet wouldn't say what it would do until Belgium was invaded, is also the actor usually blamed for failing to deter anything. If refusing to play is what the theory recommends, and refusing to play is what made it worse, is that evidence against the "don't be the kind of agent that folds" prescription, or is two-player commitment-race theory just silent once there are five players and I should stop forcing it to speak?

One thing about how I want the answer: I'm not a historian, and I can tell the difference between the wikipedia version and the real argument. So please seperate what people who actually work on 1914 agree on from what is still live disagreement — how rigid the plans really were, whether the timetables were a genuine constraint or a convenient excuse, how much Berlin wanted a war, whether British ambiguity mattered, whether the whole "trapped by the timetable" story has been overstated — and flag where you're confident versus where you're reconstructing. Don't smooth a running historiographical fight into consensus. And if the July Crisis is just a bad test case for this model, say so and name the kind of case the model does fit — Sam and I also got into whether any of this applies to AI systems having incentives to lock in their own behaviour early, so tell me if that's where the model actually bites.
