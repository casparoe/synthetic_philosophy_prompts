I work in model risk at a mid-sized lender, and I'm putting together a twenty-minute self-assessment exercise for the compliance team at our UK office — a decision tree that takes an analyst from answers about concrete cases to the fairness criterion they are actually, implicitly defending, with the technical name attached only at the leaf. The setting they will be reasoning about is our own pipeline: applications get a risk score from a model; scores landing in a middle band go to a human underwriter with override power; the audit only ever observes the final decision and the reason recorded on the file; and the same score is also fed into pricing, so a criterion satisfied at the decision boundary can be quietly undone downstream. On top of that, our internal policy (not, so far as I know, a legal requirement) says that every declined applicant must be given a recorded reason naming a change to their own file that would have flipped the outcome, and that the reason may not advert to a protected characteristic — call it "reasons-on-record fairness." I'd like that wrinkle treated as a candidate position in its own right rather than as a footnote.

A few constraints, because my first attempt went wrong in a predictable way. No node question may use a term of art — nobody should have to answer a question containing "calibration," "separation," "sufficiency," or "counterfactual" — and every node should be a concrete case or claim a compliance analyst with no formal training can answer in a sentence. I'd like the tree built from the logical space rather than from a list of names: pick the six or seven questions that actually do the separating first, then locate the real positions among the answer combinations. Seven branch questions maximum, eight to ten leaves. The following must appear as leaves: demographic or statistical parity; equality of error rates; a score-conditional or calibration-style view; a counterfactual-causal view; individual or similar-treatment fairness; "fairness through unawareness," which is where most of my colleagues will start; a welfare-consequentialist view of the kind that says no parity criterion is fundamental because what matters is the long-run effect on the group; and my own reasons-on-record view. Each leaf needs its usual name plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

The three questions I currently have are, I suspect, useless, and I'd like to know why before I rewrite them: "Should the model ignore protected characteristics?"; "Is 85% accuracy on the validation sample good enough?"; and a case in which two groups have identical distributions of everything that matters, where I ask whether the two groups should be approved at the same rate. Please do not include any of them in the tree unless you think one can be repaired. One more thing: a colleague claims that requiring (i) error-rate parity at the decision, (ii) that the score remain usable as a probability for pricing, and (iii) that recorded reasons be actionable and monotone generates a contradiction even when the two groups' base rates are identical — which would make it stronger than the familiar result that calibration and equal error rates cannot both hold once base rates differ. I don't believe him and won't put it in the handout unless it's right.

Please write in British English, in these sections and in this order: "Design notes" (about 150 words, on which questions you chose and why they do the separating work); "The decision tree" (about 500–600 words, nodes and leaves, each node with its one-line concrete case); "Leaf key" (about 200 words, one line per leaf: name and most awkward commitment); "Empty cells" (about 150 words, the answer combinations nobody occupies, with a sentence each on whether the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored); "What the tree cannot separate" (about 150 words, pairs or clusters of leaves your questions genuinely cannot prise apart); "My three draft questions, diagnosed" (about 200 words); and "The trilemma" (about 150 words, whether my colleague's claim holds and, if it does, where it belongs on the tree).
