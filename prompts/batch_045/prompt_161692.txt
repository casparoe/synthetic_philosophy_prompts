Hi. I run a small team, which means I write a lot of reference letters and job ads, and I keep having the exact same argument at work and I have no word for the thing we're arguing about. Hoping you can tell me whether philosophy has one.

Here's the pattern. Somebody says something perfectly ordinary, everyone who hears it comes away with an extra claim that nobody actually said out loud, and then a fight breaks out about whether that extra claim was *communicated* or whether the listeners just decided it. The bit I can't get straight is that it keeps feeling communicated even in cases where the speaker had no intention whatsoever.

Some instances:

- A colleague writes a reference for someone: "Priya is unfailingly punctual and her handwriting is lovely." Everyone who reads it concludes that Priya is a weak candidate. But suppose the writer is just a sweet, slightly clueless person who writes like that about absolutely everybody and never actually read Priya's work. Did the letter *say* she was weak?
- My boss sends round a job ad saying "we're especially keen to hear from people returning to work after a break." A younger person on my team reads it as "they don't want someone like me." In fact HR copied that line off a template and nobody thought about it at all.
- I reply "K." to a message because I'm busy, and the recipient is sure I'm furious with them. I type that to everyone. It means nothing.
- The reverse direction too: sometimes someone insists they *did* mean the extra bit and nobody believes them. A manager announces "we're a family here" and later swears it was sincere. Everyone heard it as a warning about overtime.

So the thing I want a name for is: the extra claim that attaches to an utterance in a context, that a reasonable hearer will take away, but that the speaker didn't intend and maybe couldn't have intended because they didn't know or hadn't thought about it. Is that just one phenomenon or have I lumped three together?

I've come across the word "implicature" online and it seems close, but as far as I can tell it gets used for the deliberate cases too — the winking, nudge-nudge ones — and I've read that the classic account ties what someone means to what they *intend* the listener to think, which is exactly what breaks in my cases. "Reading between the lines" doesn't work as a label because it implies someone deliberately hid it there. "Dogwhistle" is no good, that's about hiding a message from one audience on purpose. There's also the kind where the extra bit rides on a particular word and happens no matter who says it or what they meant, which feels different from all of mine. And separately, people tell me that saying something literally true but misleading isn't *lying* — I want to know whether that's the same distinction I'm reaching for or a different one that just lives nearby. (I vaguely remember from a marketing training that regulators use something like "implied claim" and don't much care whether you meant it. If that's right, is it the same idea in a legal costume?)

What I actually want is the carving, not a label. If there are established concepts nearby, tell me what each one is, exactly how my cases differ from it, and which one fits best or fits worst and why. If there's genuinely nothing, coin something: give me a definition, and then tell me where the edges are. Specifically, I want to know: does it matter whether the hearer's takeaway was *reasonable*, or is any takeaway that a hearer happens to have enough? Does it matter whether the speaker would have endorsed it if you'd asked them? Can it be cancelled — if the letter writer adds "and she's also brilliant," does the earlier impression vanish, or does it linger in a way that "she's punctual, and brilliant" never had? And what happens when the "speaker" isn't a person at all, like a statement put out by a committee or a brand account, where there's no single mind to have intentions?

One more thing: please say how confident you are in each of the main things you claim, and flag the single point you're least sure about. If the honest answer is "philosophers disagree about this and here's where," I'd much rather have that than a confident story that turns out to be one side of a fight.
