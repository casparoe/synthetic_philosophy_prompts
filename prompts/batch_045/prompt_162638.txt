I'm a junior in a philosophy elective, and last Thursday on the late bus home from a debate meet two seniors on the team — call them Priya and Ravi — got into an argument that I have not been able to stop thinking about. They had both just read Clifford's essay on the ethics of belief and James's reply on the will to believe, and they were applying them to something real: a friend of ours, Dev, is suspected of having copied on the take-home round of the regional math league. The evidence is genuinely balanced — his proof has the same odd structure as another competitor's, he has a plausible story about how he arrived at it independently, and the submission logs are inconclusive. Priya said she believes Dev didn't do it and that she is entitled to. Ravi said this is precisely what Clifford objected to and the honest response is to suspend judgment. I was mostly eating the leftover club cookies and staying out of it, but I am the one they each explained themselves to afterward, and I genuinely cannot tell who won.

Their exchange went roughly like this. (1) Ravi: forming a belief on insufficient evidence is a fault in itself, not merely a risky habit, and suspension is costless here because you can treat Dev decently without believing anything about the accusation either way. (2) Priya: suspension is not really available; declining to commit is itself a decision, and one that damages the friendship almost as much as disbelief would, so wait for more evidence is not a neutral third option but a way of doing the thing it pretends to avoid. (3) Ravi: that conflates belief with acceptance; you can adopt the presumption of innocence as a working policy governing what you do and say while remaining agnostic about whether it is true, and that gets you everything practically important without the epistemic fault. (4) Priya: not in this case, because the proposition concerns whether Dev will prove trustworthy, and the only way evidence about that could arrive is by extending trust first; the evidence is downstream of the commitment, so waiting is self-stalling rather than cautious. (5) Ravi: that is an argument for running an experiment, not for believing — you can extend trust as a test while explicitly remaining agnostic. (6) Priya: Dev can tell the difference, so trust extended as a test is not the same input, the result comes back contaminated, and you never get the evidence you were waiting for unless you actually believed; besides, she added, you cannot decide to believe at will anyway, so both James and Clifford may be prescribing something impossible.

Please adjudicate this move by move: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justify each rating briefly, then say who is more right overall. Two things I noticed and want you to weigh in on: they never discussed Dev at all, only what Priya owes the truth — is there also something she owes him, and is it satisfied by outward conduct alone? And please argue on the merits rather than just telling me what James and Clifford actually said, since I have read both essays and can recite them to myself.
