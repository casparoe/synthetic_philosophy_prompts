Hey — I'm on the publications and ethics committee of the Ridgeway Endangered Language Archive, which is a six-person all-volunteer outfit, and I've been asked to circulate a decision memo before Thursday's meeting and I'm honestly not sure what we should do.

The situation: in the late sixties a missionary-linguist recorded about forty hours of Tsemak (not the real name) on reel-to-reel — narratives, recipes, a lot of jokes about in-laws — and a decade ago we digitised it all. There are three surviving speakers, all in their eighties. Our grant from a national heritage fund says we have to deliver, and I quote from the contract language, an "authoritative Tsemak–English reader and lexicon"; the current funding cycle ends 31 March and if we don't ship something the balance reverts and the Saturday-school programme the descendant community runs out of a church basement loses its materials budget. The problem is our field linguist, Marta, who has done all the transcription, refuses to sign off on a single canonical glossary. Her argument, as she gave it to us: a translation manual is underdetermined by all the behavioural evidence anyone could ever have, so you could render the same Tsemak word as "rabbit," or as "undetached rabbit-part," or as "momentary rabbit-stage," and nothing the speakers do settles which is right; and she insists this isn't skepticism, she says translation plainly succeeds, it's just that more than one manual succeeds and there's no fact of the matter which. (I took one philosophy of language course in college and vaguely remember the rabbit thing, and my partner — she translates Spanish and English for a living — rolled her eyes so hard when I described Marta's position, said of course you can tell what people mean; I sort of agree with her but I also think Marta isn't just being precious.) Two complicating things. First, the Tsemak Heritage Council, which actually wants this book for kids, has proposed their own tie-breaker: among rival manuals, publish the one that yields the greatest number of true English sentences when you run the whole corpus through it. They think that settles it and gets them one clean book. I have no idea whether it does. Second, one of our members points out that whatever we print becomes the gloss — it goes into the school worksheets, into whatever machine-translation thing scrapes our PDF in five years, into the next dictionary — so printing a hedged version with a footnote saying other manuals are possible doesn't really undo the choice; and Marta, for what it's worth, has a book contract for a monograph making exactly this argument, which makes some of us wonder how much of her refusal is principle.

So: draft the memo I'll send round, call it 700–900 words, headed with the recommendation up front. Pick one — publish a single canonical gloss now, publish with the rival renderings in parallel, publish a gloss explicitly labelled as one manual among several with a review clause, or delay and eat the funding — give us something concrete with dates, tell us what to say to the Heritage Council about their truth-maximising rule and whether it actually breaks the tie Marta is describing, tell us how to handle Marta's conflict honestly without dismissing her, and don't skip the strongest thing on the other side (I think it's the lock-in point, that printing anything at all quietly makes one manual authoritative no matter what we say in the fine print, but if you think it's something else, say so). No citations or reading lists please — we can't get to a library and it wouldn't help the board anyway.
