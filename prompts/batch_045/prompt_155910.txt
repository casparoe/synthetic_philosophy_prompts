Hey — hoping someone here knows experimental philosophy better than I do. I got into this via an argument about whether a hospital triage algorithm "disadvantaged" a group of patients on purpose, and I cant tell whether the thing I keep noticing is one phenomenon with a name, or three that look alike.

You probably know the basic case: a company starts a program to make money, the exec is told it will also wreck a river, he says he cares about the river not at all, the program runs, the river gets wrecked, and if you then ask people whether he wrecked the river on purpose most say yes. Swap "wreck" for "clean up" and most say no. I gather that's the Knobe effect, or the side-effect effect, or both. Fine. Its the stuff around the edges I can't place. Three things I've noticed when I try versions of this on friends and family:

1. Neutral control. I made a third version where the side effect is completely inert — the program will change the colour of the water, helps nobody, hurts nobody. I expected the "yes" rate to land between the harm and help versions. It dosn't; it lands right on top of the help version. So it looks like nothing about the good case is doing any work and the whole thing is the bad case lifting off a baseline. If that's right, a story about moral valence moving a judgment in two directions predicts something different from a story where blame does all the work. Also, when I make the "bad" side effect bad only by a rule nobody believes in — the program will violate a dress code or something — the gap mostly vanishes, even though "he broke the rule on purpose" seems plainly true.

2. Question swap. Keep the story identical and change what you ask. "Did he know it would happen" — no intention word anywhere, just foresight — gives roughly the same harm/help gap. "Did the program cause it" gives a smaller one. "How much profit did the company make" gives none. So whatever this is, it spreads to some dependent measures and not others, and I have no idea what the dividing line is supposed to be.

3. Agent swap. Replace the chairman with something nobody thinks has beliefs or desires: a model update that tanks accuracy for one group of users and improves it for another, with the engineers told in advance. Now "on purpose" gets you nothing — people balk — but "the update was responsible for" and "the update caused" still track harm vs help, and "somebody should fix it" tracks it harder than either. So there seem to be two things here, one that needs a mind behind the action and one that doesn't, and I don't know whether the literature treats those as one effect with several measures or as separate effects that happen to travel together.

So: what am I actually looking at? Is any of this already named, is the named thing broader or narrower than what I've described, and where exactly are the boundaries — which dependent measures does it show up in, and which kinds of "bad" does it need (moral badness, any norm violation at all, or a norm the person answering actually endorses)? Please don't just hand me a label; I want to know what's in the box and what isn't, with the edge cases marked. And if one of the three has no name, give it one and define it so it can be told apart from its neighbours. If point 1 is just me having asked badly, say that instead and say why. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning — no preamble, and don't restate my question back to me.
