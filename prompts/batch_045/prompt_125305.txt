Write an essay of roughly 1,200 words on the following question: can a thought experiment give us knowledge about the natural world that we did not already have, given that running one in the head supplies no new data? Reach a verdict and defend it; do not end by saying that both sides have a point.

Let two rival accounts structure the essay, stated in your own terms and attributed to no one in particular. On the deflationary account, a thought experiment is an argument in disguise: its premises are empirical or theoretical commitments we antecedently hold, its conclusion follows from them by ordinary reasoning, and the imaginative staging is psychologically useful but epistemically idle — everything the thought experiment teaches could in principle have been wrung out of those premises already. On the inflationary account, at least some thought experiments do work that no argument from already-held premises could do: by bringing us into rational contact with the modal structure of nature, they let us see that something must be so. Say where each account goes right and where it goes wrong.

Then test your verdict in detail on the following case, which you should treat as constructed for this prompt rather than as something to be looked up.

A vessel of gas is held at one uniform temperature and divided into two chambers by a rigid membrane pierced with a dense array of tiny pores. Every pore is a narrow truncated cone, wide at the left face of the membrane and narrow at the right. A molecule arriving from the left enters the wide mouth, ricochets a few times, and is very likely to come out on the right; a molecule arriving from the right meets the narrow mouth and is very likely to be turned back. Nothing in the membrane moves. There is no demon, no measurement, no record, no erasure. If such a membrane could exist, molecules would drift left to right until a pressure difference built up, and that difference could be used to lift a weight — a cyclic engine drawing work from a single heat bath.

Notice that the two standard replies to this family of cases do not engage it. There is no information processing here, so the reply that observation can be made reversible while erasure cannot — at a cost of at least kT ln 2 per bit — has nothing to bite on. And there is no light moving part, so the reply that any pawl or latch fine enough to let a wheel turn one way will itself be jostled by thermal agitation and will sometimes let it slip back has nothing to bite on either.

Ask, then: does working through this case teach us that no such membrane is possible? If you say yes, say exactly what we learn — a modal claim about what nature permits, a claim about what our best theories entail, or merely a claim about how the situation must be redescribed — and say where the warrant comes from. Is the operative reason that the underlying dynamics are time-reversal invariant, so that for every trajectory threading a pore from left to right there is an equally weighted time-reverse threading it from right to left, so that net flux must vanish at equilibrium? And if that is the reason, has the deflationary account simply won this case, the thought experiment having done no more than unpack a commitment to reversibility that we brought to it?

Now take the case somewhere the simple argument cannot follow. Grant, for the sake of the argument, that in a sufficiently strong magnetic field electrons confined to a thin layer travel along the boundary of that layer in one direction only: the field breaks time-reversal symmetry from outside, and the transport is genuinely one-way. Imagine the sieve built out of such electrons instead of gas molecules. The time-reversal argument is then unavailable, and it is unavailable in precisely the sort of case where one-way transport is actual. Does this refute the conclusion the thought experiment seemed to yield, or does it show that the conclusion was right while resting on the wrong premise? If the latter, what does it rest on instead, and does the corrected reasoning still deliver knowledge — or does it deliver only what the second law, or whatever else is doing the real work, had already told us?

Close by addressing reliability. Thought experiments that felt just as compelling have turned out to establish nothing: consider the familiar argument that a stone dropped from a tower on a rotating Earth would be left far behind to the west. If you hold that the sieve yields knowledge, state the criterion that separates it from failures of that kind and test your criterion against one of them. If you deny that it yields knowledge, say what we should take ourselves to be doing when we run cases like this one, and how that practice has managed to be so fruitful in physics.

Write for a scientifically literate reader who is not a specialist in this material. Argue from the physics as stated above and from general reasoning; the essay should not depend on any citation, bibliography, or reading list, and none should be offered. A qualified verdict is welcome; a non-committal one is not.
