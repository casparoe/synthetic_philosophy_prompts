In his 1615 Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina, Galileo writes:

"I hold the sun to be situated motionless in the center of the revolution of the celestial orbs while the earth rotates on its axis and revolves about the sun. They know also that I support this position not only by refuting the arguments of Ptolemy and Aristotle ... especially some pertaining to physical effects whose causes perhaps cannot be determined in any other way, and other astronomical discoveries; these discoveries clearly confute the Ptolemaic system, and they agree admirably with this other position and confirm it."

I am working through the logic of this passage and would like help understanding one specific move in it. My interest is in how Galileo’s appeal to astronomical discoveries is supposed to interact with his views about the interpretation of scripture. To sharpen the question, I have been using the following distinction of my own: call a scriptural claim about the heavens “phenomenally adequate” if it is true as a report of the observable appearances available to a pre-telescopic observer, even if the underlying physical explanation is false; call it “kinematically committal” if it asserts something about real celestial motion that would be false if the earth both rotates on its axis and revolves about the sun. Galileo’s broader thesis seems to require that problematic scriptural passages be phenomenally adequate rather than kinematically committal.

Please help me understand how the argument in the quoted passage supports that requirement. In particular: (1) reconstruct the inferential role of the astronomical discoveries Galileo mentions—are they meant to directly refute scriptural literalism, or only to refute Ptolemaic astronomy and thereby remove a rival interpretation of the appearances? (2) Apply this to the scriptural episode, discussed elsewhere in the letter, in which a military leader asks that the sun stop in the sky so that daylight will last longer and a battle can be finished. Would Galileo’s argument, as stated in the passage, entail that this episode is phenomenally adequate rather than kinematically committal? Explain which step in the passage does the most work in reaching that conclusion, and note whether that step is explicit in the quoted sentences or has to be supplied from Galileo’s other arguments. (3) Finally, tell me whether my distinction between being phenomenally adequate and being kinematically committal helps or hinders an understanding of Galileo’s actual view, given that he also holds that nature does not accommodate while scripture does.
