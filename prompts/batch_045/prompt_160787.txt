I teach a 4th-year metaphysics seminar — modality, identity, persistence — and I'm putting together the final. I want one essay question on the essentiality of origin for organisms that a student can't answer by reciting the Kripke-style line about Elizabeth II not being able to have been born of different parents, plus the standard modal argument that's supposed to back it (the one where if you could have had a different origin, then there's a world in which both origins get realized and, by the necessity of distinctness plus transitivity, you'd be two people). They've all read that stuff; regurgitating it should get a C+ at best.

Here's the material I want it built around. I've been using a graded series of cases in class, and I want the exam to force them to locate the line rather than just assert it:

(i) Ordinary swap: same parents, roughly the same act of conception, but a different sperm out of the millions gets there first. (ii) Replacement: as above, except that the winning sperm is qualitatively identical to the actual one — same complete genome, same crossovers, same mutations, stipulate the idealization as needed. (iii) Delay: the actual sperm and the actual egg, but fusion happens about forty minutes later than it in fact did. (iv) Cytoplast: the actual sperm and the actual egg nucleus, but the egg cytoplasm is replaced with donor cytoplasm before fertilization (the mitochondrial-donation setup). (v) Split: the actual zygote splits at day five and two infants are born. (vi) Clone: a somatic cell taken from you at thirty is used to generate an embryo that grows into an adult.

The question has to make them do something specific with that modal argument: run it on (i), (ii), (iii) and (vi); say where, if anywhere, it stops working; name the substantive premise about cross-world identity it needs in order to do its work; and say whether that premise is independent of the conclusion or is just the thesis in different clothes. Then, wherever they decide the line falls, they have to say what makes that resolution the right one rather than a convenient place to stop, and they have to deal with (v): if the split is possible, what does the view say about which of the two you would have been, and what happens to the view if the honest answer is neither?

Feel free to insert a case of your own into the series if it sharpens the crux.

Deliverables, please: the question as I'd actually put it on the paper (closed-book, one hour, 800–1000 words — assume students have seen all six cases, so don't burn words describing them); a model answer of about 900 words that takes a real position on where the line goes instead of surveying the options; and a marking scheme out of 20 with five or six must-hit moves, the marks each is worth, and one line on how people typically blow each one. The scheme has to seperate an A from a B by naming the move only the A answer makes. My own guess is that the crux is either whether the cross-world-identity premise is independent of the thesis, or whether coarse-graining the origin event (so that the forty-minute delay stops counting as a different origin) saves the view or empties it — but use your judgment if you think the real crux is somewhere else. Also, one thing I always forget: please write the whole thing in American English.
