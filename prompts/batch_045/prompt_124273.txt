"Death in Damascus is basically Newcomb's problem with cities swapped in for boxes. Death, who predicts what you'll do and is never wrong, tells you he's coming for you tomorrow; you can either stay in Damascus or ride to Aleppo. Ride, and that's excellent evidence Death foresaw the ride and is waiting in Aleppo; stay, and the same reasoning puts him in Damascus. Evidential decision theory therefore says fleeing is pointless — it rates acts by how good the news would be, both acts are equally grim news, so you may as well stay and save yourself the journey. Causal decision theory gets it right instead: riding to Aleppo can't change where Death already is, so there's a genuine fifty-fifty shot that he's sitting in Damascus while you slip away, and even a small chance of surviving beats the certainty of not surviving, at the price of a donkey. The moral is that evidential decision theory is secretly committed to backwards causation — it has to think your decision reaches back and fixes what Death wrote in his book. Which is why essentially nobody defends it any more, and why the same lesson carries straight over to the medical Newcomb cases, where a gene gives you both the craving for a cigarette and the tumour, so that smoking is harmless but terrible news: there too the right move is the causal one, and then hope you drew the lucky genotype."
