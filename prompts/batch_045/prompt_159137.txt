I am defending a dissertation chapter in a forty-minute job talk with twenty-five minutes of questions afterward, and I want a Q&A prep sheet written against a specific room rather than against the debate as it exists in the abstract. My thesis: the pessimistic meta-induction — the argument that because most past theories which enjoyed empirical success were later judged false, our own successful theories are probably false too — derives its force from a badly constructed reference class, and once that class is repaired the induction no longer supports its conclusion. The class of once-successful theories is assembled backwards: one starts from theories we now reject and then asks whether they were successful, which all but guarantees a high falsehood rate among the sample, since a theory we still accept cannot appear in it at all. The class I propose instead is what I call forced novel predictive success, FNPS for short. A theoretical posit earns FNPS when it generates a prediction that (i) was not among the phenomena the theory was constructed to accommodate, and (ii) could not have been accommodated by retuning the theory's adjustable parameters without forfeiting successes the theory had already banked. My illustrations will be the prediction of a previously unobserved planet from residual perturbations in the orbit of a known one, the prediction of the properties of an element occupying a gap in a periodic classification, and the prediction of a specific new particle from a symmetry scheme. My two historical claims are, first, a base-rate claim: among posits that earned FNPS, the falsehood rate is far lower than the rate the pessimistic induction advertises, and second, an asymmetry claim: posits that merely accommodated known data, or that earned success only through adjustable parameters, are the ones that get discarded at theory change, while FNPS posits are retained in limiting or structural form by the theories that replace them. Call the resulting position discriminating realism. It does not say that our best theories are true or even approximately true as wholes; it says that the posits which earn FNPS carry a warrant that survives theory change, and that the pessimistic induction supplies no reason to deny this. Because the warrant is local and earned case by case rather than a global reliability claim about science as such, I will argue it is not vulnerable to the obvious retort that it is just another induction over the history of science and hence hoist by its own petard.

The room, in the order in which they are likely to speak:

2. A structural realist who thinks my position is structural realism with extra steps and will press the point that retention in a limiting relation is not reference to an entity.
3. An entity realist of the no-miracles persuasion who thinks I have conceded away the whole abductive case and bought nothing with the concession.
4. A constructive empiricist who holds that empirical adequacy is all we should want and will ask what FNPS warrant buys that acceptance of a theory does not already give us.
5. A formal epistemologist who works on Bayesian confirmation and reference class problems, and who will want likelihoods, priors over a space of theories, and an answer to whether FNPS is a property I can identify in advance or only in hindsight.
6. A particle physicist from the department down the hall who is impatient with metalevel debate and will ask darkly what FNPS is supposed to say about dark matter, about inflation, and about interpretations of quantum mechanics.
7. Two generalists in moral philosophy and ancient philosophy who will ask, politely, why any of this matters outside the philosophy of science.
