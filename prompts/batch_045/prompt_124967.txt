I'm writing the design document for an artificial-agent architecture and want a principled way to allocate credit among the four grades of creature in Dennett's taxonomy of minds: Darwinian, shaped by selection over generations; Skinnerian, shaped by reinforcement over its own lifetime of overt trial and error; Popperian, shaped by internal selection among candidate actions before any of them is tried; Gregorian, shaped by mind-tools and behavioural recipes imported from others.

Fix on the following test environment, chosen so that the four mechanisms do not collapse into one another:

- The payoff-relevant structure of the world is redrawn at random every twenty or so generations, so that little of what was learned in one cycle is reliably relevant in the next.
- A badly chosen overt action is fatal on the first offence, while nothing short of that is even mildly costly; agents can, however, run candidate actions through an accurate internal simulator beforehand at negligible cost.
- Agents can cheaply observe which actions conspecifics attempted and how those turned out, but the social channel transmits only the observable recipe, never the reason it worked.

Give me four numbers summing to 1: the share of the total information about the environment that shows up in behaviour by the end of a cycle which you would attribute to each of the four mechanisms, with a sentence of justification apiece. Then name the single change to this environment that would move the most credit from one mechanism to another, say in which direction it moves, and say why.
