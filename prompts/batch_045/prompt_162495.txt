I am deciding whether to assign a chapter from a recent trade-press book on population ethics in my upper-level seminar, and before I commit to it I would like an independent read of one paragraph. The book is aimed at general readers and is, by the standards of the genre, responsibly written. The chapter sets out the procreation asymmetry — the argument associated with David Benatar — and then draws several further conclusions from it. The passage reads as follows:

"The asymmetry is simple once you see it. Pleasure is good and pain is bad when they are actually experienced by somebody. But when nobody exists to experience anything, the two come apart: the absence of pain is good — a world without sufferers is better for that — while the absence of pleasure is not bad, since there is nobody there to be deprived of it. That is why we think we owe it to a possible child not to bring it into existence if its life would be miserable, but owe it to no one to bring a child into existence if its life would be happy. And it is why the goods in a life cannot be set against its evils when we are deciding whether to create the person who will live it: only the evils count, because a person who never exists is not missing out on the goods. Every human life contains some pain, so bringing anybody into existence is a harm to the person brought into existence, and procreation is therefore wrong. The wrongness does not vary with how good the life turns out to be: a single bad afternoon makes creating a person as serious a matter as creating one who will be tortured from birth to death, since the alternative was never a loss to anyone. The logic is perfectly general, and it settles more than the question of children. It also tells us that we should not bring into existence even a creature guaranteed a life of pure pleasure and no suffering, and it tells us that death is not bad for the person who dies, since the pleasures he is no longer there to enjoy are not bad for anyone."

My own suspicion is that the first half of the paragraph is close to the argument as it appears in the philosophical literature, and that the trouble, if there is any, lies in the inferences drawn from the fourth sentence onward — in particular the claim about invariance and the claim about death. But I am not confident, and this argument has a way of being slippery precisely where it looks obvious. I would be grateful for a claim-by-claim assessment, roughly along these lines:

1. Does the four-part statement of the asymmetry — pleasure and pain when present, pain when absent, pleasure when absent — get the argument right, including the qualifiers in the third and fourth limbs? Is there anything in the formulation, or in the gloss "a world without sufferers is better for that," that a defender of the asymmetry would want stated differently?

2. The author claims that in deciding whether to create a person only the evils in that person's life can count, because a person who never exists is not missing out on the goods. Is that what the asymmetry says, or is it an overstatement of it? Does the second limb of the asymmetry, on which the presence of pleasure is good, do any work that this sentence quietly cancels?

3. Is the step from "a harm to the person created" to "procreation is therefore wrong" one that the asymmetry itself licenses, or does it require a further premise? If the latter, what would that premise be, and is it contested?

4. Does the view really commit its holder to the invariance claim — that creating a person who will have one bad afternoon is as wrong as creating a person who will be tortured throughout? Please distinguish, where it matters, the comparative claim that existence is worse for a person than never existing, claims about how much worse, and claims about the stringency of the moral reason against creating.

5. What does the asymmetry yield, if anything, for the case of a creature guaranteed a life of pleasure with no pain at all? Is the verdict the author reports the one the argument supports?

6. I take the application to death to be a straightforward misapplication of the fourth limb, since a person who exists and then dies, unlike a merely possible person, is someone for whom the absence of future pleasures is a deprivation. Is that reply as decisive as it seems to me? I can imagine the author's defender pressing the thought that a harm requires a subject who exists at the time of the harm, and I want to know how much force that has here.

7. Finally, the author treats the alternative to creating this person as that person's never existing, but in many actual decisions the alternative is that a different person exists instead. Does the asymmetry, or the argument built on it, have resources for such substitution cases, or does the framing quietly presuppose that it need not address them?

What I need in the end is a verdict I can act on: which sentences I would have to flag for my students if I assigned the chapter, and which of the chapter's conclusions survive once the misleading ones are corrected.
