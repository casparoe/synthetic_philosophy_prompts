Hey — I'm putting together a discussion handout for my grad seminar on understanding, and I want something livelier than the standard back-and-forth, so could you write me a dialogue? Two characters, call them Mara and Dev, something I could read out loud in about ten minutes (so ballpark 1000–1200 words). They should be colleagues who have obviously had this argument before — not a student being corrected by a sage, and not two people talking past each other. I want each of them to be genuinely good at this.

The question: does heavy reliance on AI systems erode understanding?

Here's the case I want them fighting over, and please treat these as the facts. A regional outbreak-modeling team — six epidemiologists who used to build their own compartmental models from scratch in code. Two years ago their funders gave them an assistant system: you describe the situation in plain language, it hands back a fitted model, a written rationale, and a sensitivity report. The team now sanity-checks the outputs against known benchmarks, and their published forecasts are more accurate than they used to be. But none of them can re-derive the models by hand anymore. And here's the bit I keep chewing on: the questions they use to audit an output — does this handle household structure, does it break if reporting lags, is the prior doing work here — are themselves suggested by the system, because it knows the failure modes better than they do. Once a year the license lapses for a week and they run a drill, hand-building a model from scratch; the drills have been getting slower and the write-ups vaguer.

Roughly: Mara holds that understanding is constitutively a matter of the agent's own grip on why the result follows from the inputs, and that what the team has left is calibration rather than comprehension — they can tell that a model is acceptable without being able to see why, and the fact that accuracy went up doesn't answer that. Dev holds that understanding is more like navigation: being able to move around the space of answers, anticipate what changes if the inputs change, locate where an output is fragile, know which questions to put. On that view the team may have lost a skill and gained a capacity, and Dev should press the analogy with delegation we don't count as erosion — calculators, statistical packages, a junior RA who runs the regressions.

Two things I specifically want handled, because this is where these conversations usually cheat. Mara has to actually answer the delegation analogy: she needs a principled difference between outsourcing to a stats package and outsourcing to this thing, not just an appeal to how it feels. Dev has to actually answer the second-order point: if the team's auditing capacity is itself on loan from the system, then "we can tell when it's wrong" may be an illusion that survives only as long as the system is wrong in familiar ways — so what exactly is left when it fails in a new one?

Beyond that, some shape notes. Let each of them concede at least one real thing, the kind they'd defend to a third colleague afterwards. Let them get to the crux rather than circling it — I want it to be clear by the end whether they're disagreeing about what understanding is, or about an empirical question of what the team can still do when no one is watching, or about something third I haven't thought of. Let them use the annual drill as a test case, since that's the one place the disagreement cashes out in something observable. Keep it in spoken register, no lectures and no name-dropping of papers. And don't let it end in tidy agreement — but don't let it end in shrugging either. I want a reader to come away knowing exactly what each of them would have to be wrong about. (I honestly go back and forth on which one I am, so don't stack it toward either side — I'd rather find out that my own arguement has a hole in it.)
