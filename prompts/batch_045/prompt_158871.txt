The passage is the section on the teletransporter in a recent popular book about mind uploading and the self. It reads, in relevant part, as follows:

'In his 1984 book Reasons and Persons, the philosopher Derek Parfit asks us to imagine a teletransporter: a scanner records the complete state of every cell in your brain and body, destroys the body, and radios the record to Mars, where a receiver assembles an exact replica out of local matter. The person who wakes up on Mars remembers your life, has your character, and takes himself to be you. Parfit's claim, the chapter says, is that whether he really is you is a further fact — something fixed independently of all the physical and psychological facts and hidden from us — and that we therefore cannot say. Since this hidden fact cannot be settled, Parfit concludes that identity is not what matters in survival. What matters instead is psychological continuity: the persistence of memory, intention and character. Parfit calls this Relation R, and he insists that it counts only when it is produced in the normal way, by the continued operation of your own brain, and not by any artificial copying process, which merely imitates continuity. Because the replica on Mars has all of your psychology, travelling by teletransporter is, on this view, just as good as ordinary survival. The chapter then draws Parfit's moral: ordinary self-concern is an illusion, and if all that matters is that someone has your psychology, then providing for your own old age has no better rational claim on you than providing for a stranger. The two-replica case is presented as the same argument run twice: with the scanner switched on a second time, neither of the two people is really you, and because identity cannot hold twice over, this refutes the view that we are separately existing entities whose identity is a deep fact fixed by nature. The chapter then extends the argument on its own initiative. Suppose the scanner records only the coarse-grained state of your brain — enough to rebuild someone who, on every test we could devise, shares your beliefs, your episodic memories, your habits of speech and your dispositions, but not the fine detail of every synapse. Call this the low-resolution teletransporter. Since what matters is psychological continuity, the chapter argues, Parfit is committed to saying that this too is just as good as ordinary survival; and if the coarse record is used to run off a hundred replicas, you survive a hundred times over, each one as completely as you would have survived a night's sleep.'
