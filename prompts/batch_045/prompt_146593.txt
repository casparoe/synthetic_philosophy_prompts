You are a professional moral philosopher specializing in axiology, well-being, and population ethics, and you have a reputation for refusing to let a slogan do the work of an argument. Please think carefully and at length before answering. In your first pass, brainstorm as many distinct precisifications as you can — including ones that initially sound silly or obviously trivial — and only then filter, sort, and evaluate them. Do not settle for the first distinction that comes to mind, and do not pick a single best reading and expound it: the whole point of this exercise is the menu, and I will be bringing the menu back to my group.

Context. I do cause prioritization in a small grant-making group. We were comparing two uses of a fixed budget: (A) preventing or treating episodes of brief but extremely intense pain — cluster headache attacks, acute pain from surgery or injury, the kind of thing people describe as unbearable; and (B) a well-being intervention with large but moderate effects spread over many people, such as cash transfers or treatment for depression. Someone closed the discussion with the line "no amount of happiness can make up for a single moment of agony," and treated it as settling the question in favour of A. I do not think a sentence that vague can settle anything, but I also cannot yet say which of the things it might mean is the one doing the work.

Relevant background for the taxonomy: the family of views called lexical threshold negative utilitarianism — suffering takes priority, but only above some threshold of severity or intensity, below which ordinary trade-offs with happiness apply again; and the background question of whether pleasure and pain are commensurable at all, i.e. whether they are positions on one hedonic dimension, two separable dimensions, or something between.

Your task. Lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses that sentence could be used to express, ordered roughly from the most trivial to the most radical. For each, give: (i) the thesis stated in one or two sentences precise enough to be formalized, and a sketch in words of its logical shape (for instance, something of the form: for any state x whose pain-intensity exceeds k and any number of pleasant states y, x is worse than the y's taken together); (ii) what the thesis commits its holder to that they might not have signed up for; (iii) a verdict — platitude, substantive and defensible, genuinely contested, or false/incoherent; and (iv) whether that reading would actually support the conclusion my colleague drew, and if not, what it would instead support.

Please make sure your menu separates at least the following kinds of thing, if you think they are genuinely distinct: claims about axiological structure (comparative weight, lexical priority, non-Archimedean value, incomparability, parity, or vague/imprecise value); claims about which axis does the work — intensity versus duration versus the number of people affected, since a view on which intensity is lexically dominant but duration and head-count remain tradeable is a very different view from one on which quantity of suffering is what dominates; claims about the intrapersonal case (one life, one diary) versus the interpersonal or aggregative case, which can come apart; claims that are not really about value at all but about the stringency of negative versus positive duty, or about whose complaint grounds a moral claim; empirical claims pressed into service, such as the observation that people seem to adapt to improved circumstances while not adapting to chronic pain, or that pain has a distinctive urgency and demand on attention; and decision-theoretic or epistemic readings, on which the sentence is really advice about measurement error, cluelessness, or option sets rather than a thesis about how much anything matters. I especially want you to consider the reading on which the sentence is not a structural claim at all but a claim about marginal returns in the actual world — that at the funding margin, pain relief is simply badly underprovided relative to happiness — and to say how we could tell that reading apart from the dramatic ones.

Then, in a second section, take the readings that survive your verdicts and test them against two difficulties. The first is a continuity problem: if pain-intensity is a continuous quantity with no brightest line, and evaluative comparisons are transitive and reasonably smooth, then for any pain just above the threshold there is a pain arbitrarily close to it just below, and it looks as though lexical priority either collapses into an ordinary very-steep trade-off, or forces a discontinuous jump in value at an arbitrary point, or forces you to give up transitivity or completeness somewhere. Say which of those the lexicalist should accept and what it costs. The second is a threshold-fragility problem under uncertainty: if you are unsure exactly where the threshold lies, or how intense the pain will actually be, or how many people will be affected, then a recommendatory rule built on a lexical structure can swing wildly on small changes in your probability estimates, and can look, from the ex ante standpoint, exactly like an ordinary archimedean trade-off. Explain what that does to the practical force of the lexical reading.

Close with a short bottom line: which reading you think my colleague was most likely reaching for, whether that reading is defensible, and what he would have had to establish — conceptually or empirically — for the line to license the funding conclusion rather than merely express a feeling about it.

Format: a numbered menu with the four labelled parts per entry, then the two problems as separate short sections, then the bottom line. Arguments only, please: I do not want citations, named authorities, or reading lists, since I cannot check them and the group will not be persuaded by them anyway.
