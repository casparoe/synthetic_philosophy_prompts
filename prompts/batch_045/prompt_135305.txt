I'm doing a piece on self-amending systems — constitutions, DAO charters, that sort of thing — and the hook I keep coming back to is a long-running online Nomic game (you probably know the type: the players start with a short ruleset and then vote on changes to the rules, including the rules about how rules get changed). This one has been going since 2009, eight players, and it has a standing five-judge Rules Court that publishes a one-paragraph verdict on every dispute. I've been going though the archived verdicts, which are members-only, so I'll paraphrase rather than quote. What I want from you is a prediction: my editor wants a stake in the ground in the piece and I'd rather predict than hedge.

The rules that matter, as they stood before the pending case:

- 101 (immutable): an immutable rule may be amended, repealed, or transmuted only by a unanimous vote of all eight players.
- 102 (immutable): the game ends when a player reaches 200 points, and that player wins.
- 201 (mutable): a rule-change to a mutable rule is adopted if more players vote for it than against.
- 202 (mutable): by majority vote the players may declare any rule dormant for up to three rounds; a dormant rule has no effect while dormant but stays in the ruleset.
- 203 (mutable): a rule-change takes effect when the vote closes. The ruleset says nothing about proposals that contain several clauses, and nothing about what to do when a mutable rule and an immutable one conflict.

The court's track record:

(1) 2011. An ordinary majority enactment of a scoring rule. Upheld without comment. Nobody has ever lost an ordinary mutable-rule case.

(2) 2014. A majority amended 201 itself, raising the bar for mutable rule-changes to two-thirds and requiring proposals to be posted 48 hours before the vote. Upheld; over the next two years the court struck two changes that met the old bar but not the new one. So the amendment rule can amend itself, and once it has, the new version governs later proposals.

(3) 2016. A unanimous vote amended immutable 102, raising the win threshold from 100 points to 200. Upheld, with a remark that the court doesn't ask whether a change is sensible when the rules' own conditions were satisfied.

(4) 2019, decided 3–2. One proposal, passed 5–3, with two clauses: clause (i) said any future change to the scoring rules requires two-thirds; clause (ii) repealed one of the scoring rules. The court upheld (i) and struck (ii): the vote on (ii) closed after (i) had already raised the bar, and 5 of 8 is 62.5 percent, short of two-thirds. The dissent said a proposal is a single act of the players and you can't grade clause (ii) against a rule that the same proposal creates. Note that this proposal was hurt by its own first clause. The court has never had a case where a later clause was helped by an earlier one.

(5) 2022, decided 4–1. A majority declared immutable 102 dormant under rule 202, and the following round a majority amended 102 (win threshold down to 150) while it slept. Struck down. The holding, in substance: rule 202 governs what a rule does, not what it takes to change it; dormancy suspends a rule's operation, not the conditions on its amendment. The dissent said that if the players can do this, immutability is decorative. The judge who wrote that dissent is now the presiding judge; one of the 2019 dissenters is still on the panel.

The pending case, Proposal 61, passed 6–2 with all eight voting:

Clause A: Rule 201 is amended to read — any rule-change affecting any rule, mutable or immutable, is adopted if at least two-thirds of the players vote for it.
Clause B: Immutable Rule 102 is amended to read — the game ends when a player reaches 100 points, or at the end of round 60, whichever comes first.

So A tightens the bar for mutable rules (majority up to two-thirds) and loosens it for immutable ones (unanimity down to two-thirds), and then B walks through the door A just opened with 75 percent of the vote.

What I'd like from you:

- What will this court do? Best guess, with the reasoning, and say whether A and B stand or fall together or separately.
- Set out the two or three rules that fit their precedents about equally well, and tell me what each one predicts for A and for B individually. The candidates I can squint at are: grade each clause against the ruleset as it stands at the moment that clause takes effect; treat a proposal as one act to be graded under the rules in force when it was moved; and something like entrenchment protects itself, so the conditions an immutable rule places on its own amendment can be satisfied but never loosened by a mutable rule. Is there also a ratchet reading, on which a mutable rule may tighten the conditions on immutable rules but never loosen them? And is severability even on the table, or does the whole proposal live or die as a unit?
- Which precedent is the closest analog, and which of the others are distinguishible in a way a real court would actually buy?
- If they uphold both clauses, what have they committed themselves to? My read is that entrenchment then survives exactly one more proposal, because a two-thirds vote could simply rewrite 101 next round — tell me if that's too strong or if I'm missing a stopping point.
- The discriminating case. If you were the player drafting the next test proposal, what would you put in front of them: 61 split into two proposals a round apart, a bare-majority amendment of 102 straight out, or something sharper?
- And one sentence stating the doctrine the court is really running, written so a reader who has never heard of Nomic follows it.
