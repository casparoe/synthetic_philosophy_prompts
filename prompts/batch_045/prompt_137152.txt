I sit on the grants committee of a longtermist foundation and we've got a weird one. Our track record: we funded technical AI safety work; we funded a biosecurity surveillance project; we funded a small nuclear risk-reduction grant even though the modelled risk reduction was tiny; we rejected cash transfers to the global poor despite the evidence being rock solid; we rejected a campaign to change farming practises even though the pitch was that todays norms lock in values for centuries; we funded a civilisational resilience project — backup food and industrial capacity so recovery after a catastrophe is fast — and the memo argued recovery speed matters because a stalled recovery loses you most of the future's value; and we rejected a very speculative space governance grant because nobody could even roughly estimate its effects.

The new proposal: a permanently isolated, deliberately low-tech, self-sufficient settlement of a few hundred people with a stored genome, seed and knowledge archive, whose founding charter bans industrial technology so the settlement can't repeat whatever caused the collapse. It's cheap relative to our budget. It looks like it cuts the probability of human extinction from something like one in six to near zero — but conditional on ever being used, the future it produces is small, poor and basically stuck pre-industrially for thousands of years, and some of us worry that the mere existence of a refuge makes governments less careful about the risks in the first place.

So: what should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves, which precedent is the closest analogue, which candidate rules fit our record and which of them come apart on this case, and what one cheap variation on the proposal would tell us which rule we're actually running? Also, does your answer turn on whether that small, stuck future is net positive at all?
