You are a philosopher of science who works on cultural evolution. You know the Darwinian literature on memes from the inside — the replicator/interactor distinction, the meme's-eye view, the parasitic-meme reading on which the beneficiary of the spread is the item itself rather than the people carrying it — and you also know the main critics: the epidemiology-of-representations alternative, the transmission-bias models in the Boyd–Richerson style, and the charge that cultural items are reconstructed rather than copied, so that nothing here has the stability and the independently measurable fitness that a Darwinian explanation requires.

What I want is a case analysis, and I want it run in both directions: theory applied to case, case brought back to bear on theory.

The case is the ice bucket challenge of the northern summer of 2014. I am not interested in the priority dispute about who began it — bracket origins entirely, and treat the campaign as it existed at its peak. Take the following as given; nothing turns on the exact numbers, and you may assume them whether or not you remember the details exactly as I put them.

- The routine: you film yourself having a bucket of ice water poured over your head, post the video to a social platform, and nominate roughly three other people, who have 24 hours to do the same or to donate to an ALS charity instead.
- It spread mainly through Facebook, Instagram and Twitter, and for a few weeks it was inescapable: celebrities, athletes and heads of state took part, and it generated enormous amounts of commentary and press.
- It produced a large, well-documented spike in donations to ALS organizations, far above their normal intake for a comparable period.
- It drew immediate criticism on three fronts: that it was slacktivism, that it wasted water during a drought, and that participation was really self-advertising dressed as altruism.
- Participation collapsed in the summers that followed, and the routine essentially vanished, though the organizations' name recognition and funding base were left higher than before.
- Variants circulated: some substituted another substance for the water or dropped the water altogether, and some redirected the money to a different cause.

Please do the following, in this order.

First, before the analysis proper, lay out your candidate mappings explicitly and pick one. What is the replicator here — the filmed ritual, the nomination protocol, the recorded performance, the commitment to a cause, some bundle of these? What is the inheritance mechanism? Where does variation come from, and what is the selective environment? I want to see the alternatives you considered and why you rejected them, not just the finished choice.

Second, test a hypothesis of mine. I claim that the cause-content was not a costly passenger that the meme would do better to shed, but a fitness-enhancing covering: theALS framing is what made an otherwise transparent act of self-display socially licensed, so the content raised transmission rather than lowering it, and the reason later variants that swapped the cause survived is that any adequate prosocial cover will do. Run this against the case. What does it predict that the competing story does not, and does the case support it?

Third, consider a variant that drops the nomination step and keeps everything else, and consider the cause-swapping variants. Say what meme theory predicts about each, and then say whether that prediction is even testable against what actually happened, or whether it is only available after the fact.

Fourth, and most important: be disciplined about the limits. Tell me what a meme's-eye story cannot see in this episode — the orchestration by advocacy organizations, platform ranking and recommendation, press amplification, the fact that this is a single unrepeated event with no baseline and no comparison class — and say plainly where the theory is silent rather than stretching it to speak. In particular, address the circularity worry: if fitness is measured by spread, what independent content is left in the claim that the meme was well adapted to its environment?

Fifth, give me the verdict in both directions. Does the episode confirm the Darwinian account, is it an anomaly, or does it fall outside the theory's proper domain? And tell me what would have had to happen for this episode to have counted as a refutation of a meme's-eye explanation — if the honest answer is that nothing would have, say so, because that is the single most useful thing you can tell me about the theory's status here.

If you have room, add one paragraph of contrast with Movember, the annual campaign in which men grow a moustache through November to raise money for men's health: a much slower, much cheaper, non-nominating, recurring charity routine that has now lasted for two decades. Which of the two is the better case for a Darwinian treatment of culture, and why?

Assume I know the episode and that I have read the popular treatments of memes: do not summarize either. No citations, no bibliography, no reading suggestions — I cannot check them and I do not want them.

Finally, a confidence note. For each of the substantive claims you make, mark whether it is something the literature genuinely supports, something this case supports, or your own reconstruction; then single out the one claim you are least confident in and say what would change your mind about it.
