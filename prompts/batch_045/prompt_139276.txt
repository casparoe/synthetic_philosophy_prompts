The book said something roughly like this (I'm paraphrasing from memory, it went on a bit longer):

"Scanlon's idea is that an act is wrong if it would be ruled out by any principle that some person could reasonably reject — so morality is basically a hypothetical contract, the set of rules we'd all sign up to if we didn't know who we were going to turn out to be. The important consequence is that it blocks the utilitarian habit of adding everyone's benefits together. Each person's complaint gets weighed on its own, by how bad the outcome is for that person, so no number of tiny benefits to other people can ever outweigh one serious harm. Scanlon's own example makes the point: a man is trapped in a TV transmitter room and will suffer a badly broken leg if the broadcast is interrupted, millions of people would miss a football match if it is, and contractualism says cut the broadcast anyway. But that just is the aggregation problem. Contractualism can't explain why you should save five strangers from drowning instead of one, or why you should buy a thousand malaria nets instead of funding one expensive operation for a named kid. Numbers simply don't count for it. Scanlon tries to patch this by letting numbers break ties when individual complaints are exactly equally strong, but that only kicks in in cases where you don't need it, and basically nobody working on this accepts the patch."

So: is that fair? Could you go through it claim by claim and tell me what someone who actually likes contractualism would accept, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong? Specifically — was I right that the "can't say why save five rather than one" bit is unfair, or does the book have a point there?
