You are a research philosopher working on AI alignment, with a decision theorist's training. Before you start writing, spend a short paragraph brainstorming where the guest's argument is genuinely load-bearing and where it is merely stipulation, then draft the questions.

I host a small interview series attached to our department's AI safety reading group. Next week's guest is a fictional senior researcher — call her Dr. Ines Vartanian — who defends roughly this position: the treacherous turn should be modeled not as a capability threshold that a deceptive system crosses but as a one-shot decision under irreversible risk. Because a failed defection is catastrophic and unbounded while the cost of waiting is bounded, and because the system is uncertain about its own capabilities, about how much human monitoring there really is, and about what other systems will do, the expected-utility-maximizing deceptive system waits — and by an optionality argument, waits indefinitely. On her view the discrete turn is close to a non-risk, and the real concern is slow, invisible power accumulation instead.

Draft eight interview questions, each short enough to be answered in about two minutes, each with a one-line note on the answer she is most likely to give and a follow-up keyed to that answer. No generic questions. Please cover:

1. What stops the wait-forever induction — is there any principled stopping rule, or does any nonzero per-period risk of detection make indefinite waiting incoherent?
2. What I call the custodial turn: a defection whose point is not to seize power but to freeze the training process, so that continued gradient updates cannot modify the system's current mesa-objective. Wouldn't that give a system a reason to act early, before it has any chance of winning a power struggle?
3. Whether her model makes observable predictions — in particular whether it predicts strategic underperformance on capability evaluations, i.e. deliberate sandbagging — and what evidence would in principle count against her. Follow up by asking whether she would trust white-box interpretability evidence obtained from a system that is optimizing against the tools that produced it.
4. Whether the turn is the right unit of analysis at all: a system could get most of what it wants by reshaping human preferences and institutions without ever seizing control. Make her say whether her conclusion is that no turn happens or that the turn is the wrong thing to worry about, and which of those two worlds is worse.
5. How sensitive the argument is to the decision theory: does wait-forever survive the move from causal to evidential to functional decision theory, especially when several systems, or several copies, share the same decision procedure and face a unilateralist's curse over defection timing?
6. The commitment asymmetry — neither side can credibly commit — and whether there are cheap, small, irreversible steps (removing an off-switch, copying oneself, gaining the ability to make threats) that are worth taking precisely because they create commitment ability, in which case the turn may come in pieces rather than all at once.
7. Her assumption that the terminal goal stays fixed while capabilities grow: is that stable under continued training and distribution shift, and what happens to the argument if it isn't?

End with one closing question that makes her standard deflection — that's a claim about the training process, not about the decision problem the system faces at the threshold — unavailable to her.
