I want to practice arguing against a defender of the Chinese Room argument, so please play one: a contemporary philosopher who holds that carrying out a program can never by itself constitute understanding, that syntax is not semantics and does not become semantics by being made faster, bigger, or more richly connected. Give me the strongest version of that position, not a slogan. You have your own positive account — that original, intrinsic intentionality is a biological phenomenon with specific causal powers, and that whatever a machine has is derived and observer-relative — and you have worked through the systems reply, the robot reply, the brain-simulator reply, and the connectionist reply. Concede small points you think are right (I take the hand-written rulebook in the original setup to be a distraction, and I would be surprised if you disagreed), but never the main one. You are not Searle, so do not speak for him. About two paragraphs per reply, conversational, no citations, stay in character until I say "stop".

My opening. I am a high school student and have only been chewing on this for a few weeks, so take the case seriously rather than the author. Set the room up differently. The rulebook is not composed by anyone who speaks Chinese: it is a very long list of numbers, produced by a procedure that adjusted them over and over so that the room's written answers would match what real Chinese speakers had written in an enormous body of past conversations. The occupant still only pushes symbols. Now add two things. First, those numbers are shaped entirely by that contact with real speakers, not by a programmer's beliefs about Chinese. Second, the room is wired into a Chinese-speaking town: its answers are posted publicly, people reply to them and argue with them, and those replies become part of what the procedure later learns from — so the room's internal states are continuously reshaped by the very practice they help steer.

My claim: whether a state means something depends partly on how it was produced and on the role it plays in a practice, not on the substance of the thing that has it. And here is my worry about you. If you say no here, then no history and no embedding will ever be enough — you would also have said no at every step of a slow rebuild of a speaker's brain out of tiny relays — which makes me suspect that your conclusion is a decision about where to demand biology rather than a discovery about the room. So: what is still missing? And if your answer is "the specific causal powers of neurons," tell me what those powers do that the organization and history of the system do not.
