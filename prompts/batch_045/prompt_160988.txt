So I'm stuck on something and I keep going back and forth, so I thought I'd just ask. I'm on the incident-review team at work — when something breaks we have to write up a root cause, and the rule my teammates keep repeating is one we jokingly call the mirror test: if your explanation would also have explained the opposite outcome, it isn't an explanation. "The deploy was rushed" fails, because a rushed deploy would equally have gone fine. I always treated that as just engineering hygiene, nothing philosophical.

Then at thanksgiving my brother-in-law spent two hours on a theory about why the water testing in our town keeps coming back clean — the lab's contract, who sits on the council, the reporter who dropped the story. Every single thing that would normally count against the theory had an explanation for why it *looks* that way. My sister thinks he's lost it, I told him a theory that can absorb every counterexample isn't explaining anything, it's just storytelling, and then I realised that's exactly the mirror test and it's also kind of cheap, because good explanations absorb counterexamples constantly. Half of my job is deciding that a spike on the dashboard was a logging artifact; scientists throw out readings as instrument failure all the time. My roommate (also a data scientist, we argue about this constantly) says none of this is special, it's all just Bayes with a prior that likes simple hypotheses, and the "absorption" thing is just what happens when a hypothesis predicts a low likelihood for some data and you were right to trust the hypothesis. I half believe her, but the thing people say about inference to the best explanation — that it picks the loveliest explanation rather than the most probable one, and that loveliness is supposed to be a guide to truth — seems to be exactly what breaks here. Loveliness is precisely what a conspiracy theory has loads of. I also vaguely remember someone (van Fraassen? I might have the name wrong, and I don't want a literature review or any citations, just the argument) saying you can only ever infer the best of a bad lot, since you never know the true hypothesis is on your menu at all — and the mirror test feels like a different, sharper version of that worry, one that applies even when the true hypothesis *is* on the menu.

So: write me an essay, under 900 words, on whether inference to the best explanation can survive the fact that a hypothesis can rack up explanatory points by explaining away the evidence against it. Put your verdict in the first sentence, literally the first one, and then defend it for the rest — weigh the strongest version of my roommate's Bayes-with-a-simplicity-prior response and the strongest version of the worry, tell me whether the mirror test (or something like it) should just be folded into what we mean by "best", and don't end on a shrug about how both sides have a point. If you think the good kind of absorption and my brother-in-law's kind come apart, say exactly where the line is.
