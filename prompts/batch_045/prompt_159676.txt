Write a piece of sustained satire in the form of a referee's report — Reviewer 2's report on a manuscript submitted to a philosophy journal — running to 700 words or fewer, counting the header and the signature. Use British English throughout.

The manuscript under review is "The Night Watchman's Burden", by Dr Miriam Ffoulkes (invented author, invented department), submitted to the Journal of Thought-Experiment Studies. In it she proposes what she calls a "new and decisive" intuition pump against the principle that no agent is morally responsible for an outcome to which her conduct made no difference. The pump runs as follows: Alma is the only nurse on a night ward; unknown to her, a hospital administrator who wishes to discredit the ward's director has disabled the monitoring system; a patient's deterioration therefore goes unobserved and the patient dies; Alma was at her station all night and no conceivable degree of attentiveness on her part could have made any difference. Ffoulkes reports that readers "find it irresistible" that Alma nonetheless bears some responsibility, and concludes that the principle is refuted.

The target of the satire is the practice of constructing and auditing intuition pumps: specifically, the standing demand that a pump be purified of every narrative detail not entailed by the principle under test, when it is exactly those details that make the intuition fire. Give Reviewer 2 two invented methodological norms to enforce — say, the Parity Requirement (any detail not licensed by the principle must be shown to be intuition-neutral) and the Detail-Swap Test (re-run the case with each vivid detail replaced by a drab counterpart and report whether the intuition survives). Let him apply them relentlessly: the hospital becomes a warehouse, the dying patient a crate of glassware, the scheming administrator a scheduling bug, the nurse a night-shift inventory clerk — and the intuition duly evaporates. He should then treat that evaporation as simultaneously proving that the pump was cheating and as grounds for complaining that the paper no longer contains an argument at all, before escalating to the demand that the author submit "the bare schema, with no example whatsoever".

The jokes must come from getting the mechanics right: that a pump substitutes narrative salience for argument, that its vividness is both its power and its liability, that intuition reports are not data, and that an author's own intuition is not a finding. Put the real referee-report idiom to work — requests for effect sizes, for a pre-registration of the predicted intuition, for the sample size and provenance of the intuitions reported ("the author's? a graduate seminar? the author's partner?"). End with a footnote in which the reviewer proposes, in complete innocence, a replacement pump of his own that is loaded in precisely the manner he has spent the report forbidding — a gratuitously named villain, an emotive verb, a detail that does the argument's work.

Stay deadpan: no winking, no signalling that this is a joke. A reader should be able to tell what is being ironised from the content alone, and a philosopher who works on the method of cases should recognise the target as fairly described rather than invented. It should be funny to a specialist and legible to a non-specialist.

Format the piece as a genuine referee report: a header with manuscript number, title, and a one-line recommendation, then numbered comments, then the sign-off "Reviewer 2". Lead with the recommendation. Deliver the report only — no covering note, no explanation afterwards, no note on the jokes.
