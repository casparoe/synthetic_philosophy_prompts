I spent forty years in experimental physics, the last decade of it largely on statistical methods for collider data, and since retiring I have been reading my way into the philosophy of probability. I have found that I hold fairly firm verdicts on a scattering of cases, and I would like to know whether any single account of what probability *is* delivers them, and if not, which of them I ought to surrender. I also have a candidate principle of my own, which I set out at the end; I would rather you tested and repaired it than redescribed it to me.

**The cases.**

*(i) A machine-tossed coin.* The dynamics are deterministic; the outcome varies so finely with the launch parameters that any region of parameter space large enough for a machine to hit contains heads-regions and tails-regions in very nearly equal measure. My verdict: one-half is the correct probability, it is a fact about the set-up rather than about anyone's beliefs or about the actual long run of tosses, and I want to say this *without* appealing to indeterminism in the fundamental physics. So I do not want chance tied to indeterminism.

*(ii) A single uranium-238 nucleus.* Given its half-life, I reckon the probability that it decays within the next hour at something of order ten to the minus fourteen. My verdict: this is a real, single-case probability, and it is a fact about that nucleus — not about me, and not, I think, a frequency. There is no long run of *this* nucleus. The long run of nuclei is my evidence for the single-case figure, not what the figure means.

*(iii) A die with a delayed bias.* Fair for its first million rolls; thereafter an internal mechanism makes six come up very nearly one time in three; it will be rolled a billion times in all, so over its whole career six appears very nearly one time in three. My verdict: on the first roll, the probability of six is one-sixth, and the career-long frequency does not move me. But note what makes me uneasy: had I been told only the career-long frequency and nothing about the mechanism, one-third would have been the reasonable thing to say. My verdict therefore seems to depend on something other than frequencies, and I would like to know what.

*(iv) The googol-th digit of 0.123456789101112…*, whose digits are known to be normal in base ten, so that each digit occurs with limiting frequency one-tenth. My verdict: there is no *chance* about that digit; it is what it is. I would nevertheless take a bet at odds slightly better than nine to one against its being a seven, and I take that to be the uniquely sensible attitude for someone in my epistemic position — though someone who had actually done the computation would be right to refuse the bet. Note also that if a position is chosen at random from the first *N* places, the frequency story does yield a genuine probability, approaching one-tenth as *N* grows; but that is a probability about the random selection, not about the digit. So here is a case with frequency present, chance absent, and a single-case number I am prepared to act on that I take to be neither a chance nor a matter of taste. I do not know what it is.

*(v) A five-sigma excess.* When a signal of roughly that significance was reported a dozen years ago, the press office converted it into a chance of a few parts in ten million that the result was a background fluctuation. My verdict: I am entirely comfortable with the probability-of-the-data-given-no-signal statement, and I treat it as at least model-relative fact. I am not comfortable with "the probability that the particle exists is one minus that." But I am equally uncomfortable being told that the latter is merely my personal degree of belief: I think the confidence the community arrived at was rationally required of anyone with that evidence, and I do not see how to make sense of that without a prior I cannot defend as *the* right one. This is a probability attaching to a hypothesis — to a lawlike claim rather than to an event — and I do not know what to do with it.

*(vi) The unrepeatable case.* The probability that the West Antarctic ice sheet commits to irreversible collapse before 2200 under a stated emissions path; or that a particular fault segment ruptures in the next thirty years. My verdict: I need such numbers, some are better than others, 0.02 and 0.9 cannot both be right, and yet I do not think there is a *chance* of the ice sheet's collapsing — there is no set-up of which this century is one realization, no ensemble of Antarctic ice sheets. At the same time I refuse to say the number is simply what I happen to feel, because then I cannot see what the modellers are arguing about. This case and case (ii) are the pair I most want addressed: both are contingent, future, single-case; in one I hear a real probability in the world, in the other I do not, and I cannot say what the difference consists in.

*(vii) The settled past.* The probability that it rained in Cambridge on a particular day in 1740, judged from barometric and other records: I would say something like a third. Obviously there is no chance now. Yet I catch myself saying "it probably rained" as though something in 1740 were still open. And a frequency account will offer me the frequency among relevantly similar days — where my answer swings with which features I hold fixed (the season? the barometer reading? the whole synoptic situation?), and that swing troubles me in a way the corresponding swing in my confidence about the coin does not.

**The case I go back and forth on, which I would like your account to decide rather than me.** A perturbed-parameter ensemble of climate models: under a given scenario, seven-tenths of the runs exceed two degrees of warming by 2100. Here there *is* a repeatable set-up with stable frequencies, but what is repeated is the model, not the century. Is 0.7 a chance? It does not look like one — there is only one earth. But the number is not up for grabs either: if someone reweights the ensemble to taste, I want to say they have got it wrong. My own principle, below, does not clearly settle this, and I do not know whether that is a defect in the principle or in the question.

**My tentative principle.** A probability claim is a *chance* claim just in case it is anchored: there is a physically specifiable set-up, characterizable without reference to any particular token, whose repeated realizations would be to good approximation independent and identically distributed with a stable limiting frequency — where the stability may come either from indeterministic dynamics or from the outcome's being sensitively but evenly distributed under small perturbations of initial conditions, so that determinism is no obstacle. Where a claim is unanchored, it reports a degree of belief that is nonetheless objectively constrained: it is correct only if it is the number a calibration-minded agent with my total evidence would assign, and numbers outside a certain range are simply wrong, not merely inconvenient. As I understand it, this is roughly a propensity story for the first tier and an objective-Bayesian story for the second.

A friend in my reading group objects that I have stapled two theories together and inherited both diseases: either my anchoring condition is propensity-talk in disguise, leaving me with the same unanswered question about what fixes the *right* set-up — the reference-class problem reappearing one level up, as in case (iii) — or my "objectively constrained credence" is either empty, because there is no such unique number, or a chance in disguise, which would make case (vi) a case of self-deception rather than of honest ignorance.

**What I want.** One account, or one precisely stated hybrid, stated tightly enough that I can test it, with the clause of it that each of my cases motivates, and its verdict on the ensemble case and on one further case of your choosing that would separate it from its nearest rivals. Then tell me plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and which you would overturn and why — particularly between (ii) and (vi), where I suspect I am holding something inconsistent. Please do not survey the literature for me; I can read that myself, and I would rather have your position than a map of other people's. Commit to one: say what you think probability is, where you think it runs out, and which claim in your account you are least sure of and what would make you abandon it.
