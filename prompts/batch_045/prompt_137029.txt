"Epiphenomenalism is the view that consciousness is real but does nothing: brain activity throws off experiences the way a running engine throws off exhaust, and those experiences then have no influence on anything at all. It was a respectable position once, but it was finished off by something called the paradox of phenomenal judgment. The paradox is simple. If my feeling of pain has no effects, then it doesn't cause me to say 'that hurts'. So my reports about my experiences aren't caused by my experiences, they don't track them, and they aren't really about them either — they're just noises my brain makes. It follows that if someone switched our experiences off one by one, we'd go right on producing exactly the same reports, and those reports would be just as true as they are now. So the epiphenomenalist has to hold that nothing anyone says about their own conscious experience amounts to knowledge of it, including the epiphenomenalist's own reasons for holding the view. That is straightforwardly self-refuting, and the view is dead. And the moral is a large one: the only way out of the paradox is to let consciousness cause our reports, so a science of consciousness has to treat what subjects say about their experience as its basic evidence — first-person methods, not brain scans, are where the real data is."

My instinct is that most of this is recognisable but that he's sliding around somewhere between "not caused by", "doesn't track" and "isn't about", and that "just as true as they are now" can't be right. But I also don't want to overcorrect and defend a view I don't hold.
