So: I've realised I have a pile of gut reactions about whether anything in a virtual world is real, and I want to know what principle they add up to, or which of them I have to drop. I'll describe the cases in a bit of invented vocabulary so we're not importing anyone's theory. Call an object hard-loss if, once it's gone in that world, it's gone — no rollback, no backup, no support ticket. Call it grind-unique if it's technically replaceable but only by something like four hundred more hours of play, and fiat-unique if the operator will only ever let one exist. Call an object witness-dependent if the whole point of it is that other players can see it (a trophy hall on a busy server, a rare mount), and use-dependent if the point is what it does for the owner's own experience (a cabin on a private server nobody else visits). Every world I have in mind publishes a loss schedule up front: who is allowed to destroy what, and by what mechanic.

Here are my verdicts. A griefer destroys another player's grind-unique sword in open PvP that the schedule permits: real loss, and the griefer did something wrong. The operator deletes the same kind of sword in a balance patch, as the schedule allows: real loss to the owner, but nobody did anything wrong — this feels genuinely different from the first case even though the loss is identical. Infinite-copy cosmetic item everyone has, deleted: no real loss at all. Someone phishes an account and transfers out ten million gold: real, and straightforwardly theft, not merely rule-breaking. An exploit that duplicates gold without taking any from anyone: wrong, but I feel it's less bad than the phishing, even though it wrecks the economy for more people in total. A player's cabin, built over two years on a private server they host for themselves alone, corrupted by a bug: real loss to them, but nobody wronged them, and I don't think anyone owes them anything. Corpse-camping a low-level player for an hour so they can't play: a real but small harm, and the camper is being a jerk but I wouldn't call it theft or destruction. Unwanted sexualised contact between avatars, where the player behind the target afterwards cries and can't sleep: real harm, and it's a different kind of thing from any of the property cases — it's a harm to the person's actual body and life, not to an object. Someone's in-game spouse dumps them and it genuinely hurts: not a real harm, just a hurt, and I'm fairly confident about that. An NPC's shop burning down in a scripted event: nothing real, obviously.
