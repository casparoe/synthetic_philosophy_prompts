I work in machine learning on uncertainty quantification — calibrated classifiers, deep ensembles, conformal prediction, out-of-distribution detection, and the aleatoric/epistemic split — and I have no formal training in philosophy. I would like Keynes's position on probability restated in that vocabulary. Since I cannot check whether you have his 1921 book right, I will state the position myself, and I want you to translate what I state rather than what you remember. It has four parts:

(i) Probability is a logical relation between a body of evidence and a conclusion — a degree of partial implication fixed by the evidence, not by how confident anyone happens to feel.
(ii) Probabilities are neither necessarily numerical nor necessarily comparable: of two propositions on the same evidence it can be the case that neither is more probable, nor less probable, nor equally probable, so the ordering is only partial.
(iii) The weight of an argument is a quantity distinct from its probability: it tracks the balance between relevant knowledge and relevant ignorance, it is itself comparable across arguments, and new evidence always raises it even when that evidence lowers the probability.
(iv) For many propositions that matter in practice — the yield of an investment decades out, the outbreak of a war — no serviceable numerical probability exists, and the obstacle lies in the evidence rather than in our patience or computing budget.

Translate each of the four in turn, as faithfully as you can, using: scalar model outputs and calibration; proper scoring rules; the aleatoric/epistemic decomposition and second-order probabilities; credal sets and sets of predictive distributions; model misspecification, distribution shift and OOD detection; and sample size, information, and the sensitivity of an output to perturbing or deleting part of the training set.
