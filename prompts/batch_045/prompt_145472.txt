so I'm two thirds into a novel and I've written myself into a corner that turns out to be a philosophy problem, so I need the spec before I write the last third.

Two threads. (1) The republic in the book — which I've been makeing up as I go — ratified a Founding Charter about forty years before the action starts. The Charter contains what everybody in the book calls the Durability Clause: the founding generation, who had just come out of a long civil war and didn't trust themselves, wrote in that certain provisions can't be repealed except by a two-thirds vote followed by a nine-year wait, during which the repeal has to be re-passed twice. Now a clear majority wants out, they've done the first votes, and they're five years into the wait. (2) A character, Mira, has a recurrent bipolar illness; years earlier, between episodes, she signed a standing instruction with her clinic saying that if she ever refuses her medication she's to be treated anyway, and that her refusal at the time isn't to count as a refusal. She's refusing now, she says she's never felt better, the clinicians think she's in an episode, and the clinic is petitioning to treat her against her will.

What I want isn't the answer to either case. It's the standard. Before I let my characters argue, I want the requirements list: what any account of when a precommitment legitimately binds the person — or the polity — that made it has to get right, with the case behind each requirement, including the cases I already feel sure about and the ones I can't decide. The ones I know I need handled: Ulysses and the ropes versus Ulysses and the contract (is tying yourself up different from authorizing someone else to tie you?); the person who changes her mind because she learned something, or because she converted, or because someone paid her; the founder's dead hand; the people born later who never voted for the thing; the precommitment that was itself made under pressure; the case where the later self has strictly better information than the earlier one had.

Distinctions I need it to respect rather than collapse: binding your options versus binding your will versus binding what counts as a reason for you; waiving a right versus handing a power to someone else; "I wasn't competent then" versus "I'm not competent now"; and a precommitment that fails because it was never really mine versus one that fails because it was never really about me.

Three devices my characters keep proposing, and I need to know whether they're real answers or just ways of making the problem prettier — say which, and why: (a) the ratchet, where a clause can be amended only in the direction of making itself harder to amend, so each generation binds the next one tighter than it was bound; (b) the two-key release, where the precommitment can be lifted only by the present self together with someone who knew the earlier self and is charged with speaking for her; (c) the reversion clause, where the whole thing lapses unless it's reaffirmed every few years — and tell me whether something that has to be re-signed is a precommitment at all or just a habit.

Then: which requirements are non-negotiable and which are only what one camp happens to want; which pair I should stop hoping to satisfy at the same time; and which single requirement knocks out the most of the usual moves — the "the later self is just a different self with different preferences" move, the "true self / second-order endorsement" move, and the "a constitution isn't a contract, it's a framework that has to stay put for there to be anyone to repeal it" move. A line each on those three is plenty, the criteria are the part I actually need.

The standard I'm after is one good enough to tell me whether Mira is being kept faith with or being governed by a stranger who happens to share her name, and whether the majority in my republic is being asked to wait or being told to obey its grandparents.

Also — say how confident you are in what you're claiming, and single out the one thing you're least sure of. I'd rather know where the argument is thin before I put it in somebody's mouth.
