I'm a high school student who has been reading about AI safety in my spare time, and I want to check a summary before I repeat it in a talk I'm giving to my school's computer science club.

A post on an alignment blog — I don't remember which, and I'd rather not name it — explains the debate proposal roughly like this:

"Safety via debate works like a courtroom. Two copies of a very capable AI are each assigned one answer to a hard question, and they argue back and forth in front of a human judge who could not solve the question themselves. Because the game is zero-sum and each debater is rewarded only for winning, the strongest strategy is to argue for the true answer: a false claim can always be attacked by asking about its weakest step, and the honest side can always point to that step, so with enough cross-examination the judge ends up trusting the truth. It has been shown that as long as the judge reasons correctly, debate lets a weak judge supervise a much stronger AI, and it scales to any question the judge could in principle check one step at a time. Skeptics talk about 'obfuscated arguments' — arguments so long or so tangled that no judge could check them in the time available — but that is only a complaint about the judge being slow. Make the argument shorter, or give the judge more time, and the problem goes away, so it does not threaten the method itself."

My instinct is that this is too clean, but I can't locate where. A friend who reads more of this material than I do says the skeptical worry is not about length at all: it is that some wrong arguments have no single step you can isolate and attack, because every individual inference checks out and the error only appears when the pieces are assembled, so cross-examination has nothing to grab. Is that a genuinely different objection from the one the post waves away, or the same one stated more carefully?

Finally, does the post's conclusion survive once the summary is corrected?
