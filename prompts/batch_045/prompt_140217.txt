i'm a novelist, and at a residency last week I got into one of those dinner-table arguments with a mathematician who was there for some reason. I said something like: my charcters aren't real and everybody is fine with that. Nobody asks how I manage to *know* things about them, or what sort of causal contact I have with Persephne Vasquez, and yet inside the book the facts are totally objective — I can't decide on page 200 that she grew up in Lisbon if page 40 says Leeds, and if I do, that's a mistake, not a creative choice. She grinned and said: yes, exactly, and that's why Benacerraf's dilemma isn't a real problem. Mathematical objects are like characters in a novel. The axioms are what gets stipulated, the theory is the text, numbers are the cast, and "there are infinitely many primes" is true in the way "Persephone grew up in Leeds" is true — true in the story, not true of some invisible realm of abstracta that we'd need a sixth sense to bump into. You keep all the objectivity, you drop the access problem.

I want to put a version of this in the book I'm working on — the mathematician character lays it out at a dinner and somebody destroys it — but I can't write the destroying until I actually know how far the thing holds, and I don't want her to be a strawman.

So: can you do me an audit? Give me the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the author, the text, the canon / sequel, the character, the mistake, the retcon. Then where it breaks. The bits I keep tripping over: (1) my novel is finite and finished and math isn't — the story never stops being written, and you can't in general prove the story is even consistent. (2) There are a million novels and no "the" novel, but mathematicians talk as if there's a single right answer to Goldbach, so which story is the one, and why that one. (3) Characters do what I say, except when they don't — anyone who writes knows the feeling that a character "wouldn't do that," and I can't tell whether that's discovery or just me not knowing my own intentions, and whether that helps the analogy or just relocates the mystery inside my skull. (4) The one that bothers me most: my story doesn't hold anything up. Nothing about Persephone makes a bridge stand. But the mathematical story is load-bearing in physics, so "true in the story" has to cash out into real predictions about a world outside the story, which is work it never does for fiction.

Then the actual question: does the conclusion she wanted — the dilemma is dissolved, not solved — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or does it just turn into a choice between "why this story?" and "why does this story fit the world?" And if the novel is a bad analogy for what mathematicians are doing, what's a better one — a game like chess, where the rules are stipulated but whether there's a forced mate is discovered and not up to you? A codebase? A legal system? Tell me what each of those would smuggle in instead, and what my mathematician should say to win the scene without being obviously wrong.
