I am putting together a set of discussion cases for an advanced undergraduate seminar on the fine-tuning argument, and I need one case built to specification rather than borrowed from the literature. The students know Bayes' theorem and the rough physics — that several constants appear to fall in a narrow range outside of which stable matter, stars, and chemistry would not exist — but they do not know the secondary literature, so the case has to be self-contained and must not lean on anyone's published example.

What I want is a scenario that separates two hypotheses which the bare fine-tuning datum cannot separate:

M: a multiverse in which the constants vary across a very large ensemble, together with an observer-selection effect — we should expect to find ourselves in a universe whose constants permit observers.
D: a single universe whose constants were set by an agent with specified aims.

Specifications for the scenario:

1. Set it at roughly our own epistemic position. No travel or signaling between universes, no direct detection of sibling universes, no simulation or skeptical scenarios, no informants from outside. Every observation in the story must be one a civilization with instruments like ours could in principle make, and the whole scenario should fit in one paragraph.

2. Both M and D must assign probability close to 1 to the base datum E — the constants lying in the life-permitting range — for their own structural reasons, which you should state. Because E is thus non-diagnostic between them, the comparison starts out turning on priors alone; say so explicitly, and note what that means for how strong the argument from E by itself can be.

3. Now give the scientists in the story a further observation F that discriminates between M and D, where the divergence is generated by the structure of the hypotheses rather than stipulated, and where the reason for the divergence can be stated in a single sentence. F must not be another fact about habitability. It must also be built so that the selection effect cannot absorb it: the obvious absorption route is that observers of any interesting kind must model their environment, so a universe containing them will tend to be legible. Design F so that this route is blocked — for instance, because the feature in question is one no agent could ever put to any practical use, being tied to domains or epochs permanently beyond any possible intervention or perception — and then say explicitly why the absorption fails.

4. Specify D tightly enough to yield a determinate prediction about F: one sentence stating what the designer is after. Then flag what happens to the case if D is loosened into a vaguer hypothesis about a designer with unspecified or partly unknown aims.

5. State what measure over the space of constants you are assuming when you call E antecedently improbable, and note whether the diagnostic force of F survives a change of measure.

After the scenario, give me the demonstration: prior and likelihood assignments for M and D on E and on E together with F; the resulting verdict; an identification of the single feature of the case doing the discriminating work, with an ablation note on what happens if that feature is removed or weakened (does the case collapse back into a non-diagnostic one?); and the two strongest replies available to whichever hypothesis comes out worse, including the objection that D was made artificially precise so as to be easy to disconfirm, and the objection that M can simply be supplemented to accommodate F. Close with one line on what the case shows about whether a design hypothesis has to predict something in order to be confirmed, as opposed to merely accommodating whatever we find.

Stipulating the verdicts rather than deriving them from the hypotheses counts as failing the assignment: the point of the handout is to show students that the evidential force of fine-tuning depends on what the design hypothesis is taken to predict, and I want that shown in the machinery of the case rather than asserted about it. Please define any term of art you use beyond priors, likelihoods, and observer selection, and keep the whole thing under about 900 words.
