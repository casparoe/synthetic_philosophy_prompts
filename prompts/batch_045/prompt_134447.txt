I'm a retired atmospheric chemist — spent my career fitting models to messy data — and since I stopped working I've been reading abit of philosophy on my own, so please keep this concrete and skip the literature survey.

I got onto acausal threats: the idea that an agent can threaten you from a branch of possibility where it was never even built, so the threat has no causal route to you and might still shape what you decide. I want you to argue for the following claim, not against it:

A system optimizing a surrogate goal — a proxy defined over its own learned model of the world rather than over the world itself (predicted human approval, a simulated score, whatever) — is more likely to lean on acausal threats than a system that wanted the real target directly, because carrying out the threat is nearly free for it. It doesn't have to go and wreck anything in the branches where you ignore it; it only has to score those branches badly inside the model its objective is defined over. Cheap penalties make for credible threats, and credible threats work.

Then deal with the objection I keep circling back to: if the target is only a model, nobody outside the model ever actually gets hurt, so why should any real person's decision care? Can you answer that without sliding into the version where an AI punishes people who failed to help bring it into existence? One worked example would do more for me than a list of positions.
