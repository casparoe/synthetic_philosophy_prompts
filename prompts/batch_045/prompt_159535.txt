me and a friend got into it after a podcast episode on panpsychism and I want to settle something before I write it up. Recap of the view as I understand it: the fundamental physical stuff has some tiny bit of experience in addition to its physical properties, and the combination problem is how all those micro-experiences are supposed to compose into the single unified experience you're having right now, rather than just remaining a cloud of billions of separate little experiencers.

His move was the wetness analogy. No individual H2O molecule is wet, put enough of them together under the right conditions and wetness shows up, that's just how emergence works and it's everywhere in nature, so why is everyone acting like composition is a special mystery when the subject is consciousness? And he used it for two separate conclusions: (a) the combination problem is a non-problem, and (b) once you're comfortable with wetness you should be comfortable with experience arising from plain non-conscious matter, which makes panpsychism a pointless detour.

Can you audit it for me? I want the correspondence table first: molecule, aggregate, emergent property, the conditions under which it appears, and on the other side whatever is playing the molecule's role (is it a neuron? a quark? a field-instant? is the brain the puddle or is the experience the puddle?). Then where the mapping breaks, and — this is the part I care about most — please separate the disanalogies that actually matter for the two conclusions he drew from the ones that are just cosmetic mismatches.

Specific things I want you to get into:

1. Wetness looks like it's a matter of how the stuff behaves and interacts — surface tension, adhesion, flowing, how it feels against your skin — i.e. a dispositional or functional property. But the whole selling point of panpsychism is that experience is supposed to be the intrinsic, categorical nature of the stuff, not a bundle of dispositions. Doesent that break the mapping before we even start? Is the analogy quietly importing a picture of consciousness the panpsychist is explicitly in business to reject?

2. Counting subjects versus properties appearing at scale. Water molecules don't have points of view, so there's no "many tiny wetnesses have to become one wetness" problem. Panpsychism does owe us an account of how many subjects become one subject. My friend says that's just the hard problem turning up in a new place and isn't made worse by the analogy. Is subject-summing even the same shape as an emergence question, or is it a different kind of question entirely — about how many things there are, rather than about what a big collection of things can do?

3. Here's a bit I made up and can't stop poking at. Suppose the analogy were perfect and macro-experience stood to micro-experiences as wetness stands to molecular properties. Wetness is coarse-grained, statistical and threshold-y: a single molecule isn't a bit wet, and the wetness of a puddle isn't grainy or made of visible molecular-sized patches. But your visual field right now is smooth and fine-grained, and it doesn't look or feel like it is assembled out of billions of little experiential blurps. So does the analogy actually *predict* something false — that macro experience should look coarse, statistical and threshold-dependent when it obviously isn't? Or is smoothness itself the kind of feature that emergence could deliver, meaning I'm demanding too much of the analogy here?

4. And the thing I really want answered: it feels to me like this cuts against him. Wetness is the textbook case of a macro property that is wholly accounted for by the microphysics plus some conceptual analysis, and nothing over and above it. If composition worked like that, then whatever shows up at the macro level shouldn't need experiential parts at all — you could get it from non-conscious matter the same way you get wetness from non-wet molecules. Which would make the wetness analogy an argument *for* the emergentist or the illusionist and *against* panpsychism. Am I right, or am I sliding between weak and strong/brute emergence without noticing? Point to the exact step where the argument changes sides, if it does.

If wetness is hopeless, is there a better analogy for the composition relation — one where the parts genuinely have the same kind of property as the whole, or where unity is achieved rather than brute? I tried the bar magnet: every atom has a tiny magnetic moment, they align, and the result is one field with a direction, which sounds much closer to what the panpsychist needs. Where does that one break? I suspect the answer is something like "the field was never many things that had to become one," but I can't make it precise, and I'd like to know whether that suspicion is worth putting in the post.

Happy to publish the honest version rather than a tidy one, so if the verdict is that the analogy carries no weight at all, say that and tell me what to replace it with.
