I'm a journalist working on a long piece about how we carve up the past — roughly, whether the Middle Ages, the Renaissance and the Scientific Revolution are things that happened or filing conventions we inherited. Before I file, I'd like the position I've ended up with stress-tested. I suspect two or three of these commitments are doing incompatible work, and I can't locate the fault.

Here is the set, roughly in the order I came to it:

1. Period terms are retrospective: nobody in 1340 thought they were living in the Middle Ages. I don't find that damning. I'm a realist about periods in something like the way I'm a realist about mountains — a real thing can have contested edges, and "vague, therefore invented" strikes me as a bad argument.

2. I nonetheless want to say that the Scientific Revolution is a different case, and that the deflationary thing is the true thing: there wasn't one. The continuities with what preceded it, and the number of separate threads that got bundled together well after the fact, make it a packaging job rather than a period. I want to say this while keeping (1).

3. I'm happy to keep "the Renaissance," although I know the term arrives loaded and was defined against a dark age that never existed as described.

4. My test for any periodization is explanatory: if you can't state something that holds across the period and fails outside it, the period is decoration. I apply this to every period term, including the ones I like.

5. I regularly write sentences of the form "in the Middle Ages, people thought X," knowing that the claim has exceptions, varies by region, and varies enormously across a millennium. I mean them as rough statistical generalizations, not as universals.

6. I think round numbers are a trap — nothing happened at 1500 — and yet I use centuries and half-centuries as units constantly.

7. I don't want the conclusion to be "so stop using the words." I want to keep all three terms and put the burden on how they are used.

8. I'm attracted to the thought that the genuinely real item isn't the interval but the rate of change inside it, so that periodizing is really curve-fitting over accelerations and decelerations.

What I'd like back: which of these are in genuine tension, ranked by severity rather than listed uniformly; for each, what would have to give and what that revision costs me in the copy; which pairs only look tense and dissolve under the right distinction; and whether there is a single revision — perhaps to (1) or (8) — that cleans up more than one problem at once. Please also tell me where my own practice in (5) and (6) quietly contradicts the test in (4), in a way I'd be embarrassed by if a historian read the piece and quoted me back at myself. I'd rather not be told that periodization is simply conventional; I know that move and I don't think it settles anything.
