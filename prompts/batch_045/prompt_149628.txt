Hi — hoping someone here can help. I've been going down a rabbit hole with those decision theory puzzles and I keep tripping over something I don't have a word for, so I thought I'd just describe it and see if it rings a bell.

The two setups I keep coming back to are the one where a super-forecaster flips a coin and, if it comes up the bad way, asks you for money you'll never see again, having already handed you a big pile in the other branch based on what you would have done — and the one where you get a letter saying pay up or something embarrassing gets published, where the letter only goes out to people for whom exactly one of two things is true: they'd pay, or they have the rare condition. I've read the usual reactions to both. What I can't pin down is what exactly is doing the work in them.

Here's what got me stuck. I tried rewriting the letter one so that nothing is tracking me. Version A: a hospital, years ago, drew up a mailing rule by committee — you get the invite if and only if (your old intake form said you're the sort of person who shows up when called) XOR (you actually have the condition). Nobody modelled me, nobody is watching me, the flag on my form was set ages ago from a checkbox I ticked once. In version A the letter is just evidence. Taking it seriously is completely unproblematic, I go and get screened, there's no paradox and no weird feeling that I'd rather have been a different sort of person.

Version B: same letter, same rule, but the would-you-show-up half is recomputed fresh every morning by a system with a very good model of how I actually decide things right now. Now the old feeling comes back. If I'm the sort who'd show up, letters stop meaning anything. If I'm the sort who wouldn't, the letter means I probably have it. And I can't seem to settle on being either sort, because whichever I settle on changes what the letter was evidence of. I can get the loop in version B and I can't get it in version A, and the only difference I can point to is whether the thing filling in the would-you half is sensitive to how I actually decide at the moment of deciding, or is a stale record set independently of how I turned out.

So: does that difference have a name? I don't mean the general causation-versus-correlation thing, and I don't mean a good predictor versus a bad one — the checkbox in version A predicts me decently well too, it's just frozen. I mean the difference between a prediction that would have come out differently if I had been a different kind of decider, and a prediction that's fixed however I turn out, even when both are equally accurate about what I'll do. Does that distinction have an established name in this literature, or is it usually just gestured at? And if it's unnamed, I'd love a coined term, a definition, and a test for which side of the line a case falls on.

I'd also want the edges sorted out rather than just a label. What about a predictor that models a group I belong to and is retrained weekly on data from people like me, so that my own decision nudges the model only through the aggregate — A or B? What about a perfect simulation of me that was run last year and whose output is sitting in a sealed envelope now, so it did track how I decide but can no longer change? What about a case where the tracking is real but changing which sort of person I am costs something, like a public vow I'd have to make? And does the distinction actually explain why the coin-flip one bites at all, given that in that one the money is already paid or already not, and it still seems to matter what I would have done?

Please don't just hand me a name and a one line gloss, I've had enough of that. I want to know what the phenomenon really is, what it isn't, which nearby ideas it could be confused with and why those aren't it, and where it stops applying. Write it out in continuous prose please, no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, just an email-length reply like you'd send a friend.
