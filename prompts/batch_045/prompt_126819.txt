My thesis, in short. First: the 'alignment tax' is a misnomer, and the phrase is doing political work. What people actually measure — a dip on some evals, higher refusal rates, a slower release cadence — is mostly what I've started calling second-order friction: costs that land on users, in the form of a more nagging product, and on the lab, in the form of deferred launches. That's not the same thing as a first-order loss of capability, and conflating the two lets a lab present a product decision as a technical necessity. Second: the competitiveness constraint is real, but everyone tells it one-directionally — we'd like to be careful, but they won't be. There's a reciprocal version that gets much less airtime: firms are also competitively constrained away from being visibly reckless, because of insurers, enterprise procurement, and the prospect of liability further out. So the race story is doing less work than its fans think, the tax story is doing more, and together they make a set of business choices look like physics.
