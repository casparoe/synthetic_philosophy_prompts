I've gotten weirdly into Schrödinger's late, Vedanta-soaked essays — Mind and Matter and My View of the World — where he argues something like: consciousness is numerically one, the many minds are an appearance, and the reason the scientific world picture contains no thinking ego is that the ego isn't an object inside the picture but the single thing the picture appears to. Plus the dream analogy, one dreamer playing all the characters, each convinced it's a seperate subject, and the Upanishadic formula about you being that. I don't think I believe it but I can't currently say what would make me stop half-believing it, and that bugs me.

So: what would count against the unity-of-consciousness view? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal down to merely awkward, and I want them split into the ones a committed defender would actually concede as counting against them versus the ones they'd swat away as resting on a misreading of what the claim is. Please say which is which, that distinction is the thing I'm most after.

Things I keep circling on, tell me if I've got any of them wrong:

1. The access asymmetry. Whatever else is true, I have direct access to this stream and none to yours. The view says that asymmetry is itself an appearance inside the one consciousness, not evidence of two. But does it owe an explanation of why the one consciousness is partitioned the way it is — why the seams track brains, why they split the way they do after callosal surgery, why anaesthesia dims this stream and not others? If the answer is just "no explanation is owed, the partition is part of the appearance", haven't we arrived at the solipsism problem, where every candidate defeater gets redescribed as more appearance?

2. Empirical stuff. Two I can imagine: (a) split-brain and hemispherectomy work where each hemisphere looks like it runs its own integrated, self-monitoring, report-capable stream; (b) a slow-bridge case, suppose we could gradually wire two brains together so that over months every test we have — report, metacognition, volition, working memory — flips from "two subjects" to "one". Does either touch the claim? My guess is the defender says the counting belongs to the empirical apparatus and the view was never about that level, but then I need an honest verdict on whether it's been qualified into something empirically inert, and whether that's a bug or just what a claim of this kind is supposed to be, if it's meant as a transcendental point about the conditions of there being a world picture at all.

3. Moral arithmetic. If there's one subject, either there is one experiencer of everything and the world's total suffering gets borne by a single subject, which sounds catastrophic, or the number of persons drops out and ordinary aggregative ethics stops making sense. Which direction does the view actually push, and is that consequence one defenders should welcome or be embarrassed by?

4. The fission/fusion asymmetry. Fission gives the pluralist two subjects where there was one; fusion seems to give the monist nothing, because on their story it was always one. Does that favour the monist, by sparing them any vague number-of-subjects problem, or does it show they're simply declining to answer the how-many question?

5. Quantum, only if it isn't a confusion: there were Bell-type experiments a few years ago where each wing had a stand-in observer recording a definite outcome inside a sealed box, and the results were reported as incompatible with everyone's outcomes all being absolute facts at once. Does the loss of observer-independent facts lend this view any support, or does observer-relativity multiply observers and cut the other way?

What would move me most is anything that forces the view to either explain the partition or admit it has nothing to say about it, so weight that area highest. And if the honest verdict is that the view is very well insulated and that the insulation is legitimate for this kind of claim, say that plainly and tell me what would have to change in philosophy of mind for it to stop being legitimate.
