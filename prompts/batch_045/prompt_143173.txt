Consider co-source epiphenomenalism: phenomenal states are caused by neural states but cause nothing, and our phenomenal judgements—the occurrent and standing beliefs we voice when we say that things now seem thus-and-so to us—are produced by a separate mechanism that is likewise a downstream effect of those same neural states, so that the content of the judgement counterfactually covaries with the character of the experience (had the character been different, the judgement would have been different) without any causal link running from the experience to the judgement. I want your credence that the paradox of phenomenal judgement nonetheless remains a decisive objection to epiphenomenalism: that no version of the view, co-source or otherwise, can account for either the reliability or the aboutness of our phenomenal judgements without conceding that phenomenal states do causal work. Mine sits near 0.6; a friend who defends co-source epiphenomenalism puts it near 0.15.

Decompose it. Give a number and one line of justification for each of: whether knowledge of, or justified belief about, a state requires causal contact with it, or whether mere counterfactual covariance plus the right explanatory story can substitute; whether the co-source mechanism really delivers the covariance that substitution would need, given that it seems compatible with systematic dissociation of the sort illustrated by a case in which phenomenal character varies while the outputs of the judgement mechanism stay fixed; whether the live problem is reliability at all, or rather content-fixation, i.e. whether a judgement never caused by an experience can so much as be about that experience; and how much weight to give the self-referential worry that if epiphenomenalism were true my very belief in it would be unexplained, which is itself a reason for doubt. Add any further term you think the credence rests on, show how the numbers combine, and give the resulting figure. Then say which single term the final number is most sensitive to, what argument or finding would move it most, and which sub-question most likely separates me from my friend at 0.15. I don't want a tour of the literature; the derivation is the point.
