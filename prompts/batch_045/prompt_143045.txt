hey — hoping you can help me settle an argument that started out dumb and has now eaten my whole week. I do layout and the occasional bit of signage for a small community newspaper, and we've been kicking around the idea of commissioning a cheap custom typeface instead of just licensing something off the shelf. One of my friends says that's a waste of money because, quote, "every a is already an a, you'd just be redrawing Helvetica with extra steps." Someone else pointed me at that old Letter Spirit thing where researchers tried to get a program to invent lowercase letters that all looked like they belonged to the same font, and said the real question is what makes every 'a' an 'a' in the first place. Now I can't stop seeing it everywhere: type designers apparently draw a handful of "key" letters and then derive the rest by rotating and recombining parts (the 'n' basically hands you the 'h' and the 'u'), foundries ship a single file with a continuous weight axis where the 'a' at 200 and the 'a' at 900 are generated by interpolating between two master drawings and everyone still calls them the same 'a' in the same typeface, and yet the very same people will lose their minds if a competitor's 'a' has the same bowl shape and terminal angles, while tracing a 16th-century punchcutter's 'a' counts as homage. (Also, apparently in the US the outlines themselves are barely protected while the font file is — which seems to me to presuppose something fairly specific about what the design even is, if true.)

So here's what I actually want: reconstruct what this whole cluster of practices presupposes about what a letterform is. Is an 'a' supposed to be a fixed schema or essence that survives deformation, a family-resemblance cluster with better and worse members, a list of features with some marked as optional, a point in a continuous space of possible shapes, or just whatever a competent reader reads as an 'a'? Be careful about the difference between what the practices strictly require and what they merely sit comfortably with — I suspect some of the outrage about copying only looks like it needs a strong theory of letterform identity and is really about money and reputation, and I'd like you to tell me where the practice is genuinely underdetermined and several theories would license it equally well.

Two test cases I keep coming back to, please use them. First: imagine a font where no individual letter is drawn first at all — a system that only encodes relations among letters (the 'o' fixes how round round is, the 'n' fixes the stem-and-shoulder rhythm, the 'p' and 'd' are just rotated consequences) and the whole alphabet falls out at once. In that setup, is there still a fact of the matter about which shape is "the" 'a', or is 'a' just a node in a network? Second: take an 'a' and deform it into an 'o' one small step at a time — thicker bowl, shorter stem, stem gone — and tell me where it stops being an 'a'. Whatever your theory says there, check it against the practice: does it explain why some alterations get called theft and others style, and why interpolation across a weight axis is treated as one typeface but interpolation toward another foundry's design isn't? And finally, if we went all-in on the functional view — anything a reader reliably reads as an 'a' is an 'a', nothing more — what in type design, criticism, and selling would actually have to change, and what, if anything, would break? I don't want book recommendations or a reading list, I want the argument.
