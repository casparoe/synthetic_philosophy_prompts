I have been asked to give the dedication address when the Requena Room opens in October: a small reading room at the Universidad Católica in the capital, holding the papers of Captain Esteban Requena. I need you to write it, and I need it to survive four audiences at once, so let me give you the whole situation.

Vereda is a small country I will not bother renaming for you. In 1961 General Aníbal Vidal took power in a coup against a lawful, mediocre, elected government. In 1972 Requena, then a captain, was one of four men who tried to kill him. The attempt failed. Requena was wounded and taken; his three companions were executed; he was sentenced to life and served twenty-three years, until the first democratic government released him in 1995. Vidal died in his bed in 1991, and the regime came apart three years later without anyone's having shot anybody. Requena died in 2022, at eighty-one. In prison he wrote, on whatever paper he could get, a long manuscript smuggled out in sections and published last year by this university as *On the Killing of a Tyrant*. It is a serious piece of amateur Thomism and it argues four things: that Aquinas's own distinction is between the ruler who holds no title and the lawful ruler who governs tyrannically, so that Vidal, having seized power by coup, was never a superior in the relevant sense at all; that the prohibition on a private person's killing his ruler therefore does not reach this case; that Requena was in any case not a private person, since he held a commission and acted in the name of a people that had no remaining lawful recourse; and that even if he were counted a private person, the disturbance was not inordinate, because the repression that followed 1972 would have come in any event. He leans hard on Aquinas's remark, in the treatise on kingship, about a people's not being guilty of breach of faith when it withdraws a power that has been turned against it. The sentence I cannot get out of my head is the escape clause elsewhere: "unless indeed the tyrant's rule be disturbed so inordinately, that his subjects suffer greater harm from the consequent disturbance than from the tyrant's government."

Who will be there, in the first four rows: his daughter Clara and two grandchildren; three men now in their eighties who were in the conspiracy with him and regard him as the only one of their generation who did anything; the donor whose gift built the room, a Catholic businessman and self-taught Thomist who attached to the gift a condition that the room never be used to advocate political violence and whose family can withdraw the name; the rector; an auxiliary bishop who will give the blessing immediately after I sit down and who actually reads Aquinas; Vereda's deputy minister of culture, representing a government that is pleased about the archive and does not want to be quoted as approving of assassination; a handful of journalists; and forty or fifty students, most of whom have never opened Aquinas and all of whom assume the man in the portrait was a hero, which is roughly what the newspapers here say.

What I want from you: the address itself, twelve minutes, around 1,300 to 1,500 words, in English, with a printed Spanish translation handed out at the door, which means no idiom or joke that dies in translation and no Latin without a gloss right behind it. Then a short note after it, no more than 300 words, on what you have deliberately left unsaid, where each part of the room is most likely to mishear you, and one line I should cut first if the bishop objects.

The conditions, which are the real work:

The Thomist apparatus should be visible in the shape of the speech and not on its surface. No one should feel lectured on the *Summa*; half the room has never heard of it and the bishop has.

I do not want the words hero, martyr, murderer, or terrorist anywhere in it, and nothing that means the same thing in more words.

Do not settle whether Vidal was a usurper without title or, after thirty years of being obeyed and recognised abroad, a ruler with title who governed tyrannically. Both the donor and Requena's old comrades will hear a verdict if I give one, and I do not think I am entitled to one. But the audience should be able to see that the whole question turns on this and on nothing else.

Concede the proportionality clause plainly. The attempt failed; three men were killed for it; the years after 1972 were the worst of the regime; Vidal died in bed and the regime ended by other means. I want that said in front of his daughter and in front of the men who served with him, and I want it said in a register that is not a repudiation of the man. This is the part I cannot write.

The structural move I have been circling, and which I want you to use if you can make it honest: what his act lacked was not courage or a just grievance but public authority, and the archive is an act of the whole political community — lawful, peaceful, late. So the room can be at once a doing of what he attempted and not a blessing on the means. It must not be said that the archive vindicates him, and it must not be said that it does not.

Nothing the minister can be quoted as the state's endorsement of political killing. Nothing the donor can read as a Thomist blessing on private assassination. Nothing the bishop has to correct afterwards.

I met Requena twice, in 2019 and 2021, and spoke with him at length. Do not invent biography for me. Put a single marked slot in the speech where a recollection of my own should go, tell me what kind of memory to drop into it, and leave the rest of the address standing on what I have given you here.
