So my friend and I spent way too long arguing about Ubuntu last night — the cluster of ideas that a person is a person through other persons, that you grow into personhood (and can partly fail at it) by how you relate to others, and that all of this is supposed to tell us something about ethics and not just about what a person is. We never settled whether it's a metaphysical claim about what constitutes a person, a claim about a moral status you earn by caring for others, or really just a roundabout way of saying we should promote harmony and shared identity — and I realised I don't know which one I'd actually be using if I tried to do anything with it.

Heres the practical bit. I run the clinical ethics consultation service at a big hospital — me, a senior nurse and a social worker, none of us philosophers. Three or four times a month we get asked to weigh an individual patient's stated wish against what their family or community wants, we have maybe 48 hours, and the output has to be a one-page note the attending and risk management will both sign off on.

Please turn relational personhood into a procedure my team can actually run: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the decision back to a person because the issue is one of values rather than facts. Be explicit which of those three readings of Ubuntu your procedure is leaning on at each step, because they don't seem to give the same answers. And flag where the procedure is likely to misfire — assuming there is a single harmonious community, letting whoever shows up speak for it, using it totalk anyone into going along.

Then run it on three cases: a competent patient who asks that her HIV diagnosis not be shared with her husband, who is also her main caregiver; an elderly patient with moderate dementia whose nephew insists on speaking for her even though she still answers for herself; and a patient who competently refuses a recommended surgery while his family begs us to go around his refusal — I think his refusal should be the end of it, so if your procedure says otherwise I want to see it say so plainly.
