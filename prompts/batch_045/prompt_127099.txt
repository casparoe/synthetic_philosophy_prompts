You are a professional philosopher who works on population ethics and on decision-making under deep uncertainty. Take your time with this: brainstorm several candidate explanations before committing to one, and tell me which you find most plausible and why.

I'm a risk economist, and about six months ago I joined a foundation that funds work on very-long-term risks. In meetings I keep hitting the same asymmetry. My colleagues will cheerfully multiply a small, barely estimable probability by an astronomically large number of possible future lives when the sign is positive: a one-in-a-hundred chance of losing a civilisation's worth of future value is treated as obviously warranting very large present-day expenditure. Run the same multiplication with a negative sign — a one-in-a-hundred chance of a future containing vastly more suffering than happiness, the sort of thing people here call s-risks — and the response flips to "that's science fiction", "you can't put a number on that", "we should be humble", with the burden of proof treated as effectively unmeetable. Same people, same orders of magnitude, same epistemic position, same readiness to spend money now.

I've run the obvious explanations and they seem to point the wrong way, and I'd like you to say why they fail as well as what does fit. Loss aversion, negativity bias, dread, and the affect heuristic should all make a bad tail more urgent, not less; so should an implicit maximin. It isn't act versus omission or status-quo bias, since both responses are active spending. It isn't scope neglect as usually described, because the same people take numbers of that size perfectly seriously when the sign flips. And it survives among colleagues who endorse a fully symmetric axiology, counting a unit of suffering and a unit of happiness as equal and opposite.

So what is this? Is there an established concept or literature for it, or is it several different things that I am conflating? Is it best read as an implicit axiological asymmetry, as an epistemic asymmetry that might actually be defensible — a real difference in the kind of evidence available, or in who bears the burden of proof — or as something motivational? Where are its edges: does the same reversal show up if you substitute pandemic risk for extinction risk, or is there something specific about suffering? If nothing established fits, coin a term, define it, and describe a case that would test whether it is present. A bare label will not answer my question.
