Background they both accepted for the sake of argument: the famous four-part asymmetry — pain is bad, pleasure is good, the absence of pain is good even when there's nobody existing who's spared it, but the absence of pleasure isn't bad unless there's someone for whom it's a deprivation.

The case they were arguing over: a couple has one frozen embryo. Implant now and the child gets an unstable, pretty rough childhood but a life clearly worth living. Implant in seven years, once things have settled, and it's the same child — same embryo, so they stipulated the same individual — with a much better life of roughly the same length. Waiting carries about a one-in-three risk that the embryo is never implanted and there's no child at all.

1. Marcus: the asymmetry only ever compares a world containing someone against a world that doesn't contain them. In the branches where the child exists here, it's the same child either way, so the asymmetry is idle. What's left is ordinary beneficence to someone you can name, plus a risk to price in. Nothing about existence versus non-existence does any work.

2. Nadia: it does work, and you've got the direction wrong. If the asymmetry is true, the branch where the embryo is never implanted isn't bad for anyone — no subject, no deprivation. So the one-in-three risk isn't a cost you're allowed to weigh against the better life. Delay doesn't narrowly win, it wins trivially: you trade a guaranteed decent life for a chance of a better one plus a chance of an outcome that's bad for nobody.

3. Marcus: that's my reductio, not my conclusion. Zero out the failure branch and you'd have to say the same about a 99% chance of never implanting, or 99.9%. Any chance of a better life then beats a certain decent one. That isn't a finding about embryos, it's the asymmetry eating the decision procedure.
