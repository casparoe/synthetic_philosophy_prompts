I'm a journalist — I write a column on science and ideas for one of the UK papers — and I'm finally doing the piece I've been threatening for years: why Gödel's incompleteness theorems keep getting wheeled into arguments they have no business being in (whether an AI could ever be conscious, whether physics could ever be finished, whether all worldviews are equally ungrounded), and where, if anywhere, they genuinely bite. I've got maybe 1,800 words and readers who are bright but haven't touched formal logic since school, so I need the conceptual shape right before I start turning it into sentences; if the whole thing is going to hinge on some technicality I'd need three paragraphs to explain, I'd rather find that out now and cut it.

The bit I keep tieing myself in knots over is this. When people say "Gödel proved there are truths no system can prove", I think there are two quite different claims hiding in there, and I've started calling them axiom-relativity and axiom-inexhaustibility. Axiom-relativity is the boring one: any set of assumptions rests on things it can't itself justify, so there's unprovability all the way down — but that's basically Euclid, it doesn't need Gödel, and I suspect it's the version most of the sloppier pop uses are actually leaning on. Axiom-inexhaustibility is the interesting one: fix your axioms how you like, there will still be sentences your system can't settle, and you can't patch it by just adding the undecided sentence as a new axiom, because the patched system has its own Gödel sentence that you can then add, and so on forever — the subject matter outruns any single formalisation of it. So my first question is whether that's the right way to carve it up, and which of the famous applications is really leaning on which.

Then there's the objection my friend made — she's a condensed-matter physicist, we were arguing about this at a wedding, which tells you everything you need to know about my social life — which is: fine, but the Gödel sentence is just "I am not provable" dressed up in arithmetic, so the theorem is a fact about the logic of self-reference, and nothing we care about in physics or economics is self-referential, so why should anyone outside logic care at all? My half-formed reply was that there are statements which are true, unprovable in Peano arithmetic, and don't look remotely self-referential — Goodstein's theorem about those integer sequences that always eventually hit zero, the Paris–Harrington thing about finite Ramsey numbers — plus the Chaitin-style result that a formal system can't establish complexity bounds much above its own complexity. Something in me thinks that changes the picture, but I can't say exactly what or how much, and I don't trust myself on the details.

So: walk me through what the two theorems do and don't license, intuitively but not hand-wavy — I can follow things like "Π1 sentence" and the difference between soundness and consistency if you remind me quickly. Specifically: sharpen or fix my axiom-relativity / axiom-inexhaustibility split; take a view on whether the "it's only self-reference" objection survives the natural independent statements and the Chaitin-style results, or whether those just relocate the problem rather than answering it; say what the Lucas–Penrose "minds can't be machines" argument would actually need in order to work and where people think it breaks (I gather it turns on whether a reasoner can somehow know that its own formalisation is consistent, but I may have that garbled, so correct me); and run the "there can never be a theory of everything" and "all belief systems are equally ungrounded" claims through the same apparatus, telling me honestly whether there's a defensible core to either. No links, citations or a reading list please — I'll get an actual logician to check the details before it runs, I just need the structure right first. As you go, flag how confident you are in each thing you're claiming, and single out the one point you're least sure about: I'd much rather print a single honest "this bit is contested" than ten smooth confident sentences that fall apart under fact-checking.
