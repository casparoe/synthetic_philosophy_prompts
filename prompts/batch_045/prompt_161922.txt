Hey — I'm a PhD student in philosophy and I'm trying to get clear on when extra-legal social sanction (cancellation, deplatforming, that whole family) is actually warranted, and I keep going case by case and geting a pattern I can't state. My gut verdicts:

- A tenured prof reads a passage from a 19th-century text aloud in seminar, slur included, with a warning first; a decontextualised clip circulates and he loses a book contract. Not OK.
- A PR person posts something vicious about a murdered journalist's family the day of the murder, refuses to apologise, doubles down on a podcast; she's fired and unemployable in the field for about two years. Fine, roughly proportionate.
- A 15-year-old's private edgelord DMs resurface when she wins a scholarship; the scholarship is pulled. Not fine.
- A photographer's peers quietly stop referring him work and tell each other why; he loses most of his income. Fine, or at least none of my business.
- Two hundred strangers email his venue clients until he's dropped — same income loss, same underlying reason. Not fine.
- Someone's old blog posts from age 19 resurface via a documentary when he's 35; he apologised at 20 and his work since has been fine; he's dropped from a keynote. Not fine. Same posts, same documentary, no apology and still defending them: fine.
- Students disinvite a speaker because of the claims he makes about a group: fine. The same campaign then pressures a journal to retract his unrelated co-authored paper: not fine.
- A podcaster hosts the disinvited speaker, and listeners go after the podcaster's advertisers: really not fine.

My tentative rule: withdrawing your own association is basically always permitted, while enlisting third parties as instruments (employers, platforms, advertisers, venues) is where this becomes punishment proper and so has to be proportionate, aimed at the thing that's actually wrong, and somehow revisable by the target. But that rule overturns my verdict on the prof — the publisher dropping his book is the publisher's own withdrawal, so on the rule it's permitted, and I don't think it is. It also says nothing about two things that bother me: in most of these cases nobody is in charge, so the severity is set by whoever happens to amplify it and nobody can proportion it to anything; and a lot of the people piling on would ignore the identical thing done by someone on their side. I also can't tell whether the it's-just-consequences move is a defence of all this or a description of what's distinctive and worrying about it.

Can you give me one principle, or two or three candidates, that gets as many of these verdicts as possible — saying for each clause which case motivates it, being honest about which of my verdicts it forces me to drop, and telling me what it predicts for two cases I haven't listed: (a) my department stripping a donor's name off a building for things he did that were legal and publicly known at the time, and (b) a case where the only available lever is off-topic — someone independently wealthy and self-publishing, where the only traction anyone has is the handful of events that still book her.
