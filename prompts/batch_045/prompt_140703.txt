Hi — this is long, sorry. I've somehow ended up as the staff researcher who drafts the recommendations for the Names Panel at our national memory institute: five people who decide whether official material — textbooks, museum placards, the memorial register — is allowed to use the word genocide for a given episode. I don't vote, I write the memo, and the memo matters more than it should. It got personal fast: my grandmother was one of the Halven House children, she got out at nine, and she's the reason I took the job, which I'm only now realising was a slightly unhinged thing to do.

Their record so far. The Kaldreth Valley massacres of 1979, a junta slaughtering an ethnoreligious minority that lived almost entirely in that valley, forty thousand dead in three months, radio broadcasts and written orders naming them as a rot to be cut out, villages razed and never rebuilt — there are maybe three thousand Kaldrethim left worldwide and no children speak the language: yes. The Sarnov deportations of 1952, a distinct people, a hundred and ten thousand shipped to a distant province, roughly a third dead within the year, the language banned in public, the land given to settlers, the official rationale security: yes, and the Sarnov don't exist as a seperate people any more. The Marat purge, 1966 to 1971, a one-party state killing party members and their families, twelve thousand dead, and you could save yourself by recanting: no, crimes against humanity, with the chair muttering that the convention only covers four kinds of group. The Halven House removals, every child under twelve of a small highland community taken to state boarding schools, the language beaten out of them, birth records destroyed, nobody killed at all: yes. The Ostarik famine, grain requisitions in the provinces where Ostarik peasants lived, three hundred thousand dead, quotas set to break resistance to collectivisation, memos calling the region a nest of counter-revolution, but no deportations, no child removals, no ban on the language, and Ostariks today have their own region and schools and newspapers — the state responsible is now our largest trading partner and insists it was a drought: no, state-caused mass death. And the Bellport market bombing, 1994, one airstrike on a besieged minority town, six hundred dead, war over two months later, the group still living there, the state that did it our closest ally: no, a war crime.

Two things I've already ruled out. They aren't just counting bodies, because Halven House killed nobody. And they aren't just voting with whichever diaspora lobbies hardest, because the Ostarik petition had by far the biggest domestic campaign behind it and lost. My working guess is the boring legal one — intent to destroy a protected group as such, proven or inferred from a pattern, with destruction of part of the group counting — but that's the rule I'd expect them to say they're running, and I want to know what else fits the six verdicts just as well, especially something less flattering. (Our chair keeps invoking Lemkin as if he's on our side, but wasn't the political-groups part stripped out at the drafting stage? I may have that wrong, and if I don't, he isn't.)

The new petition is the Veshka Basin, 2007 to 2013. A dam and reserve project in a highland basin that was home to the Veshki, about ninety thousand people, their own language, ascriptive in the sense that matters. Sixty-odd villages bulldozed, the residents moved to consolidation towns outside the basin, the language schools closed, the cemeteries under water; around eighteen hundred died, mostly in resettlement convoy incidents and a 2009 massacre at a protest camp. A provincial governor's memo says the Veshki question will be settled by the waters, and an officer at the camp is recorded saying we are finishing what the map began. But the Veshki as a people are still definately there: a diaspora, a cultural council, a newspaper, language down to older speakers but alive, and nobody is claiming they're about to vanish. Also — and this is the part I can't get out of my head — the state that did it supplies most of the rare earth our grid runs on, hosts the logistics base we depend on, and its foreign ministry has said in writing that recognition would be an unfriendly act.

So: what will they decide, and what rules are they actually running that I can't distinguish from here? I want your best guess with the reason for preferring it, which precedent is the closest analog, and — because a no on Veshka would be ambiguous to me — what single further petition I should put in front of them next to tell the plausible rules apart. And tell me what the memo should recommend if they want to be consistent with themselves, along with what principle they'd be committing to either way. My money is on a no, honestly, but I can't tell whether that's the mineral thing or something they'd defend in public.

Write it back like an email, please: no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables, just continuous prose, and lead with the actual prediction in your first two sentences before you get into the rules.
