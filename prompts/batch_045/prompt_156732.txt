I teach a seminar on ethics and for about a year now I've been trying to say what the difference actually is between persuading someone and manipulating them, and every time I think I've got it a case turns up that breaks it. My department meeting last week didn't help. So let me just dump my gut verdicts and see whether anything tidier is hiding under them.

- A grad student spends twenty minutes telling me how much my recent paper rearranged her thinking, then asks for a letter of recommendation with three days' notice. Manipulative. Not fine.
- A senior colleague opens her conference talk with the most lurid, emotionally loaded case she has, and later says outright that she does it to frame how everything after it lands. Fine. That's rhetoric.
- A friend who waits until I've cooled off before asking me for something he knows I'd refuse in the moment. Fine, and not manipulative, even though he is plainly timing me.
- A drug ad that makes no claims at all: sunlight, a slow dance, a beach, the name of the drug. Not fine.
- My department chair scheduling the vote on a contested hire for the last meeting of the semester, when everyone is fried and two people have already left town. Not fine, and worse than the grad student.
- My therapist mentioning something small about her own life early on, which I later learn was a deliberate trust-building move. Fine.
- A student who sends a genuinely enthusiastic email about the class and then asks for the same three-day letter. Fine. So the first and this one come apart on sincerity — but the conference talk and the drug ad are both perfectly sincere and still not fine, so sincerity can't be the whole story.
- A union organizer timing a walkout for maximum publicity: fine. A PR firm timing an announcement for maximum emotional resonance: not fine. These two look structurally identical to me and I refuse to give up either verdict. If one has to go, tell me which and why.
- My doctor saying casually that most patients my age do get the screening: fine. My doctor saying that if I skip it I'm the kind of person who leaves her family a mess: not fine.

The only thing I've managed to formulate is what I call the tell test: an influence is manipulative just in case its effectiveness would collapse if you told the target, in advance, exactly what you were doing and why. It handles the flattery, the chair's scheduling, and the conference framing nicely — announcing the framing doesn't defuse it — but it definately falls apart somewhere else. My therapist's disclosure looks manipulative on this test, since telling me she's doing it to build trust would kill it, and that's the wrong answer. Worse, some influences survive full disclosure: if a negotiator says 'I'm opening with an absurd number purely to anchor you', the anchor still moves you. I don't think anchoring in a negotiation is manipulation, but I'm not sure I can say why.

There's also a case that doesn't seem to go through my reasons at all: the organ-donation default on a form, or the salad at eye level in the cafeteria. Nothing is being said to me, no belief is being produced, and I want to call it fine — but it nags at me in a way the conference talk doesn't, and I can't say what the nagging is tracking. Maybe that the influence leaves me nothing I could later cite as a reason, where the conference framing at least leaves me the example.

And the one I keep going back and forth on: my partner makes me tea, puts on the record I love, and then asks whether we can host her family for the weekend. Every structural feature of the flattery case is there. It doesn't feel like manipulation. I can't tell whether that's because it isn't, or because I don't mind.

So: give me a principle, or the smallest set of principles you can get away with, that captures the pattern. I want it stated precisely enough to be argued with, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it — I'd rather have three clauses I can attack one at a time than one elegant sentence. Test my tell test against the cases and either repair it or replace it. Tell me honestly which of my verdicts your account can't accommodate, and which one you'd overturn if you had to pick exactly one. Then run the account on three cases I haven't listed: a recommendation feed that has learned which framings move me; a student who tears up in office hours and then asks for an extension; and a colleague who says nothing about a plan she knows I'd oppose until it's too late for me to stop it. If your answer is that no single principle exists and the word covers two or three different moral complaints, say that plainly and tell me how many and where the seam is.
