I am assembling a short collection of technically honest jokes about results in formal epistemology and decision theory, and I want one on Aumann's agreement theorem.

Write a short comic scene — two or three speakers, dialogue with only minimal stage directions, 250 to 400 words — that works as a joke for an audience of people who actually know the result rather than people who have heard of it.

The constraints:

1. The laugh has to come from the machinery of the theorem. No wordplay on priors, Bayesians, or posteriors; no generic nerd humor that would survive having all the terminology swapped out. Someone who knows the proof should be able to point at a particular step and say: that is the joke.

2. The joke must rest on the real result and not on the garbled version that circulates. The common prior is doing essential work, and the conclusion concerns posteriors being common knowledge, not merely agents having told each other their numbers. If the joke collapses under a heckler who points out that agents with different priors can perfectly well agree to disagree, it is the wrong joke.

3. The technical seam I want exploited: saying your number out loud is not the same as your number's being common knowledge. After you speak, I know your posterior, but you do not yet know that I know it, nor that I know that you know that I know it, and so on. That gap is not part of the theorem itself; it is closed only by a further argument about repeated reliable announcements among agents whose whole information structure is already common knowledge, and it reopens the moment any message can fail to arrive. I want your characters stuck inside some version of that regress, or just outside it, and I want the joke to be about what the regress does to them.

4. To keep this from being a retread, bring in one further ingredient of your own choosing from the neighborhood of the result and make it load-bearing in the punchline rather than decorative. Plausible candidates: the fragility of common knowledge when each confirmation is lost with some small probability; an agent who learns only that another agent has updated, without learning what the update was; an agent whose information is not given by a partition of the states, so that she is unsure what she knows; the difference between agreeing on the probability of an event and agreeing on the expectation of a quantity. An ingredient I have not listed is welcome.

5. Set it somewhere with concrete stakes — a jury room, a couple choosing a restaurant, a group chat organizing something, two pilots, a hiring committee. Not a seminar room, not a philosophy department, no three-people-walk-into-a-bar opener, and no character should explain the theorem or name Aumann.

Below the scene, in under 100 words, add a gloss: which feature of the result the punchline is riding on, and where, if anywhere, you deliberately simplified the mathematics for the sake of the joke. If your joke quietly depends on a misreading of the theorem, say so there rather than dressing it up.
