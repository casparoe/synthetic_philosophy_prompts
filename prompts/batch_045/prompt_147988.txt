Hey — I'm a PhD student working on blame and standing, and I've got an argument I built for a reading group last week that I'm now pretty sure proves way too much, but I can't put my finger on the move. Would love a diagnosis.

Start with the case that got us going, which I'll call *structural hypocrisy*. A blames B for participating in some scheme — an industry, a supply chain, an institution, a professional incentive structure. A doesn't do what B does. But A's life is woven into that same scheme in a different role: A benefits from the fact that people like B do it, A would be put out if it stopped, and A hasn't made any move to exit. Some-one who flies constantly blaming an oil company's PR person for greenwashing. A meat-eater blaming a slaughterhouse worker. A journal editor blaming a grad student for CV-padding. Intuitively, A's standing looks compromised — not as badly as in plain hypocrisy, but compromised.

Here's the argument I built off that intuition.

(1) When A blames B for φ-ing, A implicitly asserts that B had adequate reason not to φ, and asserts it second-personally, as a demand A is entitled to make of B.
(2) A is entitled to make such a demand only if A is not, with respect to the norm grounding the demand, in a worse or equal position than B. (Call it the clean-hands condition. The plain hypocrisy datum is supposed to fall out of this.)
(3) What defeats standing in plain hypocrisy can't be identity of act-type: the smoking parent blaming the vaping kid hasn't vaped, and the father who cheated on his taxes can't blame his son for cheating on a test. What does the work must be something looser — that A is *implicated* in the norm A invokes: benefits from it, is disposed to keep accepting the pattern of behaviour A is calling out.
(4) So standing is defeated by implication in a scheme, not only by having performed the same act.
(5) Implication in this sense is nearly universal. Almost anyone who blames anyone for anything is a net beneficiary of some scheme that produces or enables wrongs of the relevant kind, and is disposed to go on benefiting.
(6) Standing is a status of the blamer *with respect to the norm*, not with respect to the particular act blamed, so if it's defeated at all it's defeated across everything that norm covers.
(7) So almost nobody has standing to blame almost anyone for almost anything.
(8) But some blame is apt and some isn't, and the whole point of the category is to mark that difference. A view on which standing is basically never present doesn't distinguish apt from inapt blame, it just deletes the distinction we set out to draw.

And the bit that makes me most suspicious: the argument seems to get things backwards. The worse and more systemic the wrong, the more scheme-like it is, so the more likely it is that any given blamer is implicated — which means the argument strips standing most aggressively exactly where we'd most want to insist that the blame is fine.

I'd like to keep (3) and (8) if I possibly can — the move off act-type identity feels right to me and I don't want to go back to it. My friend says the flaw is (6) and that standing is local to the norm rather than global. But if I just localize it I don't see how I still get the smoking parent, whose relation to the norm looks local in exactly the way my friend says is fine.

Which step do you reject? Why did it look okay, and what does rejecting it cost me — do I lose the structural-hypocrisy intuition, do I have to say something weird about the parent case, or is there a version of the argument that survives? Also: could the fix be that standing is restored by *acknowledging* implication, i.e. that self-blame buys it back? If so I'd want to know whether that makes standing suspiciously cheap, or whether it's actually the right story and my argument just shows that.

Sorry if this is undercooked, I've been staring at it too long.
