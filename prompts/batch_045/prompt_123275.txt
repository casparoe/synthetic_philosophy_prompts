I direct the translation and measurement team for a multi-country health and well-being survey that runs in a little over forty languages. Every week my analysts review a few dozen items where the same question, asked in two languages, produces response distributions that we do not think mean the same thing. Each of these items has to receive one of four verdicts: pool as-is; adapt the wording and pool with a documented caveat; do not pool for this language; or send the item back to the design team as unusable across the whole survey. At the moment the calls are made on instinct, different analysts decide differently, and we have been burned in both directions — pooling items that a reviewer later showed were not comparable, and dropping whole countries from a headline comparison for what turned out to be a bad translation.

What I want is a decision procedure my team can actually run, grounded in what the linguistic relativity literature genuinely supports rather than in what a first-year textbook says it says. Two constraints on the theory side. First, I keep being told that the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis was refuted decades ago, and I need the procedure built out of only what survives that refutation: I do not want any step that assumes language determines what a person can represent or think, and I do want it said explicitly which familiar-sounding steps are of that kind and must be left out. Second, my current working rule — flag an item only when the cross-language difference sits in obligatory grammar, and ignore differences that are merely lexical — has started to look wrong to me. Please test it against the cases below and either repair it or replace it, naming the cases that break it and saying what the repaired rule costs us in false alarms.

On the practice side: the procedure has to be executable in about twenty minutes by an analyst whose only training is a single undergraduate linguistics course, and it has to terminate in one of my four verdicts. For each step, say what would count as a sufficient answer, and whether the answer can be found in materials we already hold — translation notes, back-translations, cognitive interview transcripts, split-sample data — or requires a question to the country team, whose time I can spend at roughly two hours per flagged item and no more. Where a question is settled by fact, the procedure should settle it. Where it is settled by what we want the survey to claim — whether a construct is intended to travel or is allowed to be culture-specific — the procedure should stop, hand the decision to me, and say in plain language what I am choosing between. I also need a one-page version, free of theory vocabulary, for the sign-off meeting with country coordinators, and I want it marked honestly which steps are real tests and which are judgment calls with a checklist wrapped around them.

Then run the procedure on three items from current work. For each, give me the verdict, the step at which the procedure reached it or the point at which it stopped and handed over, and the short plain-language paragraph I would take into sign-off.

1. In Language A, the verb obligatorily marks the source of the speaker's information: whether they witnessed the thing, inferred it, or were told it. Our confidence item — How confident are you that the health information you get from your clinic is accurate? — could only be rendered in the reported form, and mean confidence came out well below the pooled sample. Our cognitive interviews suggest that respondents given the inferred form would score differently again. Is this a translation and encoding problem to be fixed by rewording, a difference in how speakers of A are led to construe confidence, or a substantive difference in trust in clinics?

2. In Language B, every clause describing an event must mark its cause as either an agent acting intentionally or an impersonal process. Our self-efficacy item — How much control do you feel you have over your own health? — came back from back-translation in the impersonal-cause form; the translator said the agentive form reads as boastful and unnatural. Scores are lower than the pooled sample. Note two things: the item was designed to measure individual agency over health, a local advisory group has asked us to drop it as culturally inappropriate, and our funders have the self-efficacy index written into the deliverables.

3. This is the one I expect not to like. Our headline single item — Overall, how satisfied are you with your life these days? — has no general evaluative term in Language C. The nearest word is closer to contentment with the lot one has been given; it carries no implication that a life is the sort of thing one assesses, or could improve. That item is what the cross-national ranking and all the press coverage rest on. If the procedure says Language C cannot be pooled, I want that said plainly, together with whatever the procedure then implies about the other thirty-nine languages — because I suspect the same argument tells against our English item as well, and I need to know whether I have to go to the funders and say so.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do. I especially want to know how it handles, or fails to handle, the standing confound that in our sample every linguistic difference is paired with a non-linguistic one — different health systems, different schooling, different interview settings, different norms about giving scalar answers — and what it should say when an item is answered by people responding in a second language rather than their first.
