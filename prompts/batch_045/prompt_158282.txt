Write a piece of sustained satire: an internal memorandum of roughly 600–700 words from a fictional federal Office of Moral Status Accreditation to regional caseworkers, announcing the rollout of the Cranial Equivalence Protocol — a scheme that assigns every citizen a graded moral-status score indexed to intracranial volume and then administers legal standing, tort damages, and voting weight on a sliding scale accordingly.

Ground rules. The memo's reasoning should be a straight-faced extension of a real philosophical move: the thought that moral status plausibly tracks some measurable neural property (neuron count, cortical surface area, phenomenal or welfare capacity), that this property varies continuously, and that we should therefore stop treating personhood as a binary credential and start administering it as a continuous quantity. Invent concrete administrative machinery — a volumetric audit at intake, provisional status pending measurement, an appeals process, an indexing formula that corrects raw brain mass for body size and neuronal packing density, a rule for aggregating fractional status across a population — and let the discomfort come entirely from following that one idea into paperwork. Somewhere the memo should note, without registering it as a problem, that whales and elephants score above the human median, and that congenital conditions together with ordinary natural variation place real citizens below the threshold for full standing.

Voice and constraints: deadpan civil-service prose throughout, no winking, no editorializing, no named living person as a target; the target is the credentialing move itself and the style of discourse that makes it sound administrable. The humor should land for a reader who knows the moral-status literature, the argument from marginal cases, and the proxy problem of what exactly brain size is supposed to be a proxy for — such a reader should laugh because the memo gets the structure right, not because of invented absurdity. Close with the Office's plan for retroactive review of settled cases. Write it in American English.
