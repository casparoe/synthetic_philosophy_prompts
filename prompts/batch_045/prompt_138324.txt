I've been going for a few months to an informal reading group on the history of economic thought — mostly economics graduate students, plus one political theorist and one computer scientist — and we've now spent three sessions on the socialist calculation debate without getting anywhere, in a way that I find more interesting than the usual shrug that Mises and Hayek won, Lange lost, and the topic is closed. Here is roughly where the argument sits with us. Mises's original objection, as I understand it, is that a socialist commonwealth could not perform rational economic calculation: with public ownership of the means of production there is no market in factors of production, therefore no money prices for them, therefore no common denominator in which to compare heterogeneous inputs, and therefore no way for a planner to tell whether a given plan is wasting resources. Lange's reply concedes the machinery but denies the conclusion: a central planning board can announce a vector of accounting prices, instruct enterprise managers to minimize costs and to expand output until price equals marginal cost, watch for surpluses and shortages, mark prices up when there is excess demand and down when there is excess supply, and iterate — which, he argued, reproduces the outcome of a competitive market by a different route, and shows that the formal theory of equilibrium is in principle neutral between capitalism and socialism. Hayek's answer to that is the thing my group keeps circling: he seems to grant that Lange's board could in principle compute an equilibrium given the data, and to insist that this was never the interesting problem, because the knowledge relevant to economic coordination is dispersed among particular people, local to particular times and places, often not the sort of thing anyone can write down in a report to a board, and changing faster than any reporting cycle could track; and in his later work he pushes this further by describing competition as a discovery procedure rather than a mechanism for reaching an optimum that is already, in principle, known.

My hypothesis — which I want attacked or refined rather than confirmed — is that the debate stalled because the participants were never answering one question, and the locus of the disagreement moved without anyone quite announcing that it had. Mises raises a question about what prices are for; Lange answers a question about whether an equilibrium can in principle be computed and approached by iteration; Hayek then raises a third question, about discovery and the utilization of knowledge, which is not the sort of question that a planner's data set could even in principle settle. If that is right, the century-long impasse is not a standoff between two complete arguments on a shared question; it is a series of near-misses, and the appearance of a deadlock is an artifact of our calling all three questions by the single name of the calculation problem. Two things complicate this for me. First, a friend of mine in operations research says the dispute is now obsolete, on two grounds: that we can solve planning and equilibrium problems with millions of variables on a laptop, so whatever Mises meant, brute arithmetical difficulty cannot be the issue, and Lange was basically right that this is an engineering problem; and second, what she calls the Amazon argument — that the largest planned economies in the world are now firms, which allocate internally by directive rather than by internal prices, at a scale larger than most states, and seem to do fine, so that planning versus markets must be an empirical question about organizational scale rather than a conceptual one. I find both arguments suspicious but cannot say precisely why, and my guess is that each begs the question against a different one of the three questions I distinguished.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it verbal — a fight about what to call socialism, or about whether a board handing down accounting prices counts as a market? Is it a disagreement about which cases count as data, with Lange reasoning from a stylized board with a handful of commodities and Hayek reasoning from the shipper who hears about a change in freight rates before anyone publishes it? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing the real work — how hard the computation is, how much of the relevant knowledge is articulable, how fast it changes, how costly it is to elicit truthfully from subordinates — that nobody bothered to test because the debate ended for external political reasons in 1989? Or does it bottom out in something like a difference about what a satisfactory explanation in economics would even look like, so that no amount of evidence could adjudicate it? Please diagnose rather than survey: argue from what each side actually claims, tell me which of my three questions (if any) is the real one, and then say honestly, given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a formal result, a piece of empirical or computational work, a conceptual distinction, a change of question — or whether we should expect no progress, and why. I can get a verdict on socialism anywhere; what I want is the structure of the disagreement.
