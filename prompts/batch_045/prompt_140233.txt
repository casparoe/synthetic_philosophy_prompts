I want to understand what a familiar but oddly under-described administrative pattern presupposes about desert, reciprocity, and the obligations of membership: the selective use of conditionality in means-tested welfare. Grant me the following stylized description, even where the details vary by jurisdiction.

A person claiming out-of-work support must register, must document a specified number of job-search steps per week, must accept any offer of work the agency classes as suitable, and must attend appointments, on pain of losing part or all of the payment for a fixed period; in some programmes the claimant must perform unpaid work in order to keep receiving support. Entitlement is assessed at the level of the household, so a partner's income counts against it, cohabitation counts as a reportable change of circumstances, and the burden of disclosing and re-disclosing lies on the claimant, continuously and under threat of retroactive recovery. None of this attaches to the transfers that flow to people who are not poor: contributory pensions, tax allowances and reliefs, and publicly subsidised insurance are paid without any inquiry into the recipient's conduct, and no one proposes that a recipient of a tax allowance document that they searched for a cheaper way to secure the same advantage. The same system also treats error asymmetrically. If an error produces an overpayment to a claimant, the sum is recoverable and sometimes penalised, even where the agency itself caused it; if the agency's error produces an underpayment, arrears are paid on discovery, without penalty and typically without interest. And the vocabulary of fraud is applied to claimant error, while comparable non-compliance by better-off recipients of public money is settled civilly and described as a compliance matter.

Here is the inconsistency I want explained. Conditionality is imposed exactly where recipients are poor, monitored exactly where they are administratively visible, and enforced with criminal-fraud rhetoric exactly where the sums are small; it is absent where recipients are solvent, and it is abandoned where it would be costly to enforce against people with exit options and lawyers. That pattern is compatible with two very different stories. Call one the reciprocity reading: the community supports the able-bodied only on terms, support is a relation of mutual obligation rather than a right of membership, and the conditions express what a citizen owes in return for being maintained by others. Call the other the captive-beneficiary reading: nothing about desert is presupposed at all, and conditionality is simply what a principal can impose when the other party cannot walk away, has no private alternative, and is cheap to surveil.

So: what must someone believe about desert, reciprocity, or citizenship for this bundle of practices to make sense? Is the reciprocity reading strictly presupposed, or is the whole pattern equally available to a pure deterrence or moral-hazard rationale that happens to be applied only where it is enforceable? Does the practice require a voluntarist account of unemployment, such that continued joblessness is the claimant's act rather than a structural outcome, and does the household means test require a view of the family as a single unit of responsibility that the surrounding tax system does not hold? Does the error asymmetry follow from any of these commitments, or is it a free-floating artifact that no reconstruction of the theory would license?

I would also like the practice tested rather than only described. Apply what I will call the symmetry test: a condition is legitimate only if we would accept it when attached to a benefit whose recipients could exit the scheme — say, requiring recipients of publicly subsidised flood insurance to prove they searched for cheaper cover, or requiring pensioners to accept any suitable offer of paid work. Apply as well an inversion test: suppose a statute mirrored every claimant duty onto the agency, so that a sanction is void unless a suitable offer was actually made within a fixed period, and underpayments caused by the agency are recovered on the same terms, and with the same interest, as overpayments caused by the claimant. Which parts of the existing practice survive these tests, and which collapse into machinery that only ever pointed one way?

Finally, tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined. Would a sufficientarian, who cares only that people reach a threshold, have reason to retain any conduct condition other than the income test itself? Would a relational egalitarian treat conditionality as a remedy for status inequality or as a producer of the very undeservingness it claims to detect? And if the reciprocity reading were replaced by the view that support is an unconditional incident of membership, what would actually have to change: the sanctions, the means test, the fraud rhetoric, the household assessment, or something deeper about who is thought to have standing to make a claim on others? Please reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say explicitly where several theories would license the same institutions. I am not asking for citations or for a survey of the literature, and I do not want the answer to depend on the details of any one country's statute book.
