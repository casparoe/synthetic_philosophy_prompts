I'm a science journalist, and I'm working on a feature about how physicists decide which parts of a theory's mathematics actually describe something — my philosopher-of-physics friend keeps calling it "ontology identification" and I keep zoning out when he explains it, so I'm trying to approach it from the case-by-case end instead. Here's where I'm stuck: I have fairly firm gut verdicts about a bunch of cases where a theory carries more structure than anyone could ever check, and I can't find a single rule that hands them all back to me.

The cases, with my verdicts: absolute position in Newtonian mechanics — not real, drop it. The rest frame of the ether in Lorentz's version of electrodynamics, which the contraction hypothesis makes permanently undetectable — not real, and the fact that the theory still works afterward is part of why. The vector potential in the Aharonov–Bohm setup, where an electron's behavior shifts even though it only ever travels through regions where the fields vanish — I want to say something is real there, but I don't think it's the potential as such, since I can change the potential by a gauge transformation without changing anything; it feels like the thing that's real is the loop integral, the holonomy, not the field I wrote down. (Whereas the overall phase of a quantum state: obviously nothing.) Now two cases I made up while stuck on a train, and I'd like them treated as seriously as the real ones. "Ghost molecules": a theory of a gas that, alongside the ordinary molecules, postulates an exact duplicate set of shadow molecules that exert no forces, are never detected, and are included in the state description for no reason — not real, obviously, and I'd say the theory is better off rewritten without them. "The decay clock": a theory of radioactive decay in which each atom carries an extra variable, a clock set when the atom is created, whose reading determines when it decays; the reading can't be inspected before the decay happens, and the theory's predictions are exactly the same as the standard exponential decay law — I want to say this one is real, or at least that I have no urge to call it fake, because it explains why two atoms in identical states decay at different times. And then: regions of the universe beyond our cosmological horizon, which no signal from them can ever reach us — real, I'm confident. Other people's toothaches, inaccessible to me in a pretty strong sense — obviously real. Atoms around 1890, when plenty of good physicists thought them unobservable in principle — real, and the people who denied it were wrong, not just unlucky.

The rule I've been playing with is something like: a piece of formalism earns ontological rights if some possible observation could in principle pin it down, or if deleting it would cost the theory some explanatory work — otherwise it's decoration. But that seems to give me the wrong answer on the decay clock (deleting it doesn't change a single prediction, yet I still want it), on the horizon regions, and on toothaches, so I suspect the rule is broken rather than that my verdicts are. Can you do better? Give me the principle you'd actually defend, clause by clause, with the case that forces each clause, stated tightly enough that I could quote it in print. Tell me what it says about two cases I haven't listed — the other branches in an Everettian quantum mechanics, and the interior of a black hole as described by someone who stays outside — plus one case of your own choosing that you think tests it hard. Then be straight with me about which of my verdicts your principle can't accommodate and which one you'd overturn, and whether the ghost molecules and the decay clock really are different in a way that matters or whether I'm just being inconsistent.
