You are a philosopher of mind and action who also advises research institutions on the epistemology of collective and distributed reasoning; you have written on how far recursive decomposition can serve as an idealization of careful deliberation, and on where that idealization breaks. Work carefully and in stages. Before you commit to anything, write a private brainstorming section in which you list at least four candidate principles that the decision-making body described below could be running, test each of them against every precedent, and note where each one strains. Only then give your ruling. Do not resolve the tension by declaring the whole exercise confused; the body's track record is imperfect but not arbitrary, and part of your job is to say which part of it is doing real work.

Context. A large foundation answers hard questions using a recursive decomposition protocol of the kind discussed in the machine-intelligence literature under names like factored cognition or HCH. (If you do not know that literature, nothing below depends on it: everything you need is stated here.) A judge splits a question into sub-questions. Consultants answer each sub-question seeing only that sub-question and, where relevant, the answers to its children; no consultant sees the top-level question, the identity of the asker, or the sibling sub-questions, and this concealment is deliberate, on the theory that knowing the destination would contaminate the parts. The judge merges the sub-answers into an answer to the parent, and that answer becomes an input one level up.

A standing review board decides, for each question the protocol is asked to answer, whether the question may be settled by decomposition at all or must instead be answered undistanced, in a single act by a designated person or by the board sitting together. The board also distinguishes two different denials: this question is not decomposable, versus this tree is not a decomposition of that question. And it has a third disposition short of approval: shadow mode, in which the tree is run anyway, its output is compared with an undistanced answer, and any divergence triggers review rather than being treated as the answer.

The board's precedents, condensed from its minutes:

1. Should the city approve the revised levee design? Approved without conditions: hydrology, geotechnical risk, cost, and environmental impact were split out and recombined by the judge.

2. What was the median household income in the district last year? Approved with a note that this is the sort of thing the protocol exists for.

3. Should the journal publish the dual-use paper? Approved on the second attempt, with two conditions: the consultants assessing expected benefit and misuse potential were not told which recommendation the editors were thought to favour, and the judge had to write a synthesis memo stating the weights used.

4. Which of the two finalists should be hired? Split disposition: the tree was approved for scoring each candidate on stated dimensions, but the final choice was reserved to the committee, which had to read the dimension scores and then decide itself. The reasoning was that the choice had to be the committee's own.

5. Was the defendant's confession voluntary? Denied. The board held that pressure, duration, and vulnerability cannot be assessed separately without changing what each of them is, and that the judgment is about the interrogation as a whole.

6. Did Ana act courageously? Denied, on the ground that the verdict is not built additively out of independently answerable parts.

7. Is this recording of the late quartet beautiful? Denied, with the observation that even a well-run tree would only tell you what such a tree says about the recording.

8. Should I forgive my brother? Denied on a different ground: the answer is not a report on a state of affairs but the stance itself, and a stance cannot be delegated to parts that do not know they are contributing to it.

9. Should the lab promote Ravi? The board refused to rule the question undecidable, but rejected the proposed tree: its sub-questions were framed so as to invite a list of Ravi's shortcomings, with no paired questions about the other candidate. It invited a re-specified tree.

10. Is the contractor's statistical analysis sound? Approved, even though no single consultant read the analysis as a whole and no consultant was told what conclusion the analysis was used to support.

The new case. The Halvorsen Institute was founded in 1961 to study human population biology. Between 1968 and 1979 one of its research programmes produced data sets and a classificatory scheme that were later cited in another country to justify a programme of compulsory sterilisation. The Institute never performed or advocated the sterilisation, and whether its work was a cause of it, a pretext for it, or merely available to it is genuinely contested. The Institute's current director has asked the protocol a two-part question: should the Institute issue a public acknowledgment, and if so, what should the acknowledgment say?

The tree the protocol office has proposed has four top-level sub-questions, to be answered by consultants who do not know they are working on this question: (a) what did the Institute's programme actually do, and what was known in the field at the time; (b) how strong is the causal chain from the data sets and the classification to the sterilisation programme, compared with available counterfactual accounts; (c) what do comparable institutions do when their historical materials have been used this way; (d) would an acknowledgment help or damage the Institute's standing with its funders and its scientific partners. A judge would combine the four into a yes or no and, if yes, into a draft statement.

What I want from you, in about 900 to 1,100 words, under headings:

First, the candidate principles. State at least four rules the board could be running that fit all ten precedents, name each one, and say where each of them explains a precedent less well than it appears to.

Second, what each rule predicts about the new case, treating the three parts separately where they come apart: the historical and causal findings, the yes-or-no decision whether to acknowledge, and the drafting of the wording.

Third, your recommended disposition for the board, if the board wants to be consistent with itself. Say which precedent is the closest analog and why, say whether the ruling should be approval, denial, a split disposition, or shadow mode, and say plainly what principle the board would be adopting with each of the options it rejects. If its precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, say so and say what the board would have to concede about itself either way.

Fourth, an assessment of the proposed tree: is any defect you find in it a defect of the tree or of the question, and does the board's own distinction in precedent 9 settle which?

Finally, name one or two further cases the board could put to itself that would best separate the two rules you take to be the live contenders, and say what each rule predicts about them.
