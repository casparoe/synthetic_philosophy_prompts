I'm taking an upper-level seminar on consequentialism and its critics, and this week we're on Bernard Williams. Our professor handed out his own one-page summary of the critique (he said explicitly that it isn't for quotation or citation), and I'd like to know how much of it to rely on before I write my response paper. It reads as follows:

"Williams's objection to utilitarianism is an objection from integrity. Utilitarianism holds that my projects, my relationships and my loyalties are worth anything only insofar as they show up in an impartial sum of welfare, so at every point I must be prepared to drop them, or to act against them, if doing so produces even a small net gain overall. In demanding this, utilitarianism alienates a person from the ground projects that give his life its point, and so attacks his integrity. His most famous example makes exactly the same complaint in miniature: a man whose wife is drowning jumps in and saves her rather than a stranger. If he has to be able to tell himself that this is permitted because it maximizes welfare — if that thought must be available to him as his justification — then he has had one thought too many; he should simply be reaching for his wife. Utilitarianism makes that thought unavoidable, because on utilitarianism the fact that she is his wife counts for nothing in its own right. Any acceptable morality therefore has to permit partiality directly, rather than deriving it from an impartial calculus, and that is why Williams thinks he has refuted utilitarianism: a theory that cannot let a man save his own wife without a philosophical footnote is not a morality anyone could live by."

Two things make me suspicious, though I'm not confident about either and I'd guess at least one of them is wrong. First, the paragraph seems to run together two arguments that I take Williams to keep apart: the complaint about integrity and ground projects, which comes with the example of the traveller who is invited to shoot one of twenty captives himself or watch all twenty be killed, and the very different complaint about the man in the river. The handout treats the river case as the same point "in miniature," but it isn't obvious to me that they are the same point. Second, I've heard that Williams's conclusion is not exactly that utilitarianism has been refuted, but rather that if utilitarianism were true it would be a doctrine best confined to an enlightened minority — that it could function only as an esoteric morality rather than as the public morality of a community — and that he treats that consequence as itself a reason to reject it. The handout says nothing of the sort.

Could you go through the passage clause by clause and tell me which parts Williams would accept, which are defensible compression, and which are misrepresentations a careful reader should reject? I'm especially interested in three things: whether the complaint about the redundant thought is genuinely an objection to utilitarianism as opposed to any view that can say saving one's wife is permissible; whether the example has any force at all unless utilitarianism agrees that he may save her (if it told him to save the stranger instead, wouldn't we just have a conflict of verdicts rather than this distinctive complaint?); and whether it is fair to say utilitarianism "makes the thought unavoidable," given that a utilitarian can distinguish the criterion of rightness from the decision procedure an agent should use. Finally: once the passage is corrected, does its last sentence survive? And what, in your view, is the strongest form of the objection I should be arguing against in the paper?
