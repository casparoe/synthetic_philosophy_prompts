My study group talked me into something I really don't want to believe, and I can't find the bad step:

(1) I can hallucinate a red apple: nothing red is in front of me, but it visually seems to me just as it does when there is one. So how things seem to me can come apart from how things are — and that gap is supposed to force you to posit qualia.
(2) The exact same gap shows up in a machine. Take a fruit-sorting robot whose red and green sensor wires got swapped at the factory, or a self-driving car that brakes for a stop sign that isn't there because someone stuck a sticker over the real one. Its sensors register something the world doesn't contain, and nothing in its behavior, its functional role, or its full physical description settles which way the swap went — it can be miswired from birth and still sort fruit perfectly.
(3) The only difference anyone has managed to point at between my case and the robot's is that mine is conscious. But "conscious" here can't mean anything other than "has qualia", and that's precisely what's up for grabs — so leaning on it just begs the question.
(4) So either the sorting robot has qualia, or the appearance–reality gap doesn't get you qualia in my case either.
(5) Sorting robots don't have qualia. So the gap argument establishes nothing and the posit of qualia is idle.

I want to keep both halves of what I'd need to deny: that there's something it's like to be me, and that the box on my kitchen counter isn't living a private life. So which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me? If it's (3), I want to know what work "conscious" is doing there if it isn't allowed to mean "has qualia". If it's (2), I want to know exactly what the robot is missing: the gap itself, or just someone for the gap to show up to? And one thing I can't sort out: does an argument like this show that qualia are an incoherent posit, or only that this particular route to them fails?
