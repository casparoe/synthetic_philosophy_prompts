Hey — I'm trying to get clear on something conceptual about amnesty in transitional justice, and every time I try to lay it out for myself I end up going in circles, so I'd rather have someone else lay it out than read another overview of the field.

The thing I can't pin down is what amnesty actually *does*. It looks like three different things get bundled together and people slide between them without noticing:

1. The state declines to impose a punishment it was otherwise entitled to impose (a waiver of enforcement).
2. The state declines to officially condemn — no blame, no official record of "you did wrong" (a waiver of the expressive/condemnatory bit).
3. The state somehow treats the act as no longer generating a claim at all — the slate is wiped in a stronger sense than just "we're not punishing you" (a waiver of the normative standing of the claim).

Which of these is doing the work in the standard defenses of transitional amnesty? My suspicion is that the arguments only really go through for (1), but that the rhetoric around reconciliation reaches for (3), and that a lot of the confusion I have comes from that slide. I'd like that made precise rather than gestured at.

Two wrinkles I keep chewing on and can't resolve:

(a) Borrowed mercy. The wrongs being amnestied were primarily done *to* particular victims, but the amnesty is granted by a legislature or hammered out in an elite bargain. So whose forgiveness is this, exactly? One answer is that this is just the ordinary fact that states prosecute on victims' behalf and can equally decline to, so there's nothing special here. But it feels like waiving is different from prosecuting in a way that matters. A move I've seen is to say these are *public* wrongs — wrongs to the political order itself, not just an aggregation of private wrongs — so the political community has something of its own to release. I want to know what "public wrong" would have to mean for that to work, and whether the move proves too much: if any state can declare a wrong public and then release its own agents, haven't we just re-described self-amnesty (the outgoing regime pardoning itself on the way out), which is supposed to be the paradigm of an illegitimate amnesty? Is there a principled line between the transitional case and the self-amnesty case, or is the difference purely that one side won?

(b) The extortion shape. Amnesty is often defended as necessary: the old security forces or the army will wreck the transition otherwise, so the real choice isn't amnesty versus trials, it's amnesty versus no transition. But the threat is issued by the very people who'd benefit from the concession, and they're the ones who control whether it materializes. Is that structurally any different from "pay up or the hostage dies"? If it is different, what exactly is the difference? If it isn't, does that sink the necessity defense, or does it just mean we should stop describing transitions as moral achievements and start describing them honestly as bargains?

And one more, since it seems to cut across both: does the conditional model — where amnesty is granted individually, only in exchange for a full account of what you did, as in South Africa — dissolve either (a) or (b)? Or does it introduce its own problem, namely that eligibility now depends on the applicant's own account of their motive, so applicants have a reason to shape their story toward whatever counts as 'political,' and the historical record the commission produces is partly an artifact of the incentive rather than a discovery? If that's right, is a partly-artifactual official record still worth having, and why?

Please flag how confident you are in each of the moving parts, and single out the specific claim you're least sure about.
