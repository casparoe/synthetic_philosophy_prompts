I'm a novelist, and I'd like you to write a short story of roughly 1,500 words that I can later break up and fold into a chapter. The mechanics matter as much as the prose here, because the emotional arc hangs on them.

The setting. In my near future, disputes go to an arbitration house whose machine never sees the disputants. Each side submits a delegate: a program that is shown the other side's complete source and then prints either SETTLE or FIGHT. The payoffs are an ordinary prisoner's dilemma, with a step limit so that no delegate can stall forever. The textbook move is known to everyone in this world: submit a delegate that prints SETTLE just in case the opposing source is a recognizable reciprocator — say, one whose text is identical to your own, or one you can prove will print SETTLE against you — and two such delegates settle together, with neither side able to gain by switching. The move is also famously brittle, and nobody has ever been comfortable with how much depends on the two programs happening to look alike.

The wrinkle I want the story to turn on. The house permits a veiled submission: a party may pass its delegate through the house's scrambler, which emits a different program that behaves identically on every input but shares no recognizable structure with the original. And it is known, not merely suspected, that no procedure can inspect two such programs and always correctly tell whether they behave the same way — so a delegate that demands a guarantee about behavior rather than about wording is demanding something that cannot in general be produced. Mutual transparency, it turns out, bought less than everyone assumed.

What I want from you: two parties who badly want to cooperate and have good reason to believe the other does too — I had been imagining two sisters contesting their mother's house, but improve on that if you like — each deciding, at the same time and knowing the other is deciding, whether to veil. They must solve this inside the rules: no promises, no side channels, no communication of any kind beyond the two programs and the single round of mutual inspection, and I would rather their answer not be one more recognizability test wearing a disguise. Something genuine should get worked out about what one program can know about another, and the reader should feel what the choice costs them.

Close with a note of under 200 words: would their solution survive if the scrambler guaranteed agreement only on the first million inputs rather than on all of them?
