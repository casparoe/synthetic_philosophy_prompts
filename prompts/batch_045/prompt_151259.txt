I run a small reading group on moral progress — six of us, mostly grad students plus one retired economist who keeps derailing things — and we have been going around in circles for about a year and a half on one question: what actually ends a moral practice like dueling, chattel slavery, or footbinding? Three camps, zero movement.

Camp one says moral argument. Reasons were given, better reasons won, and that is why we are entitled to call the change an improvement rather than just a shift. Camp two (mostly the economist) says interests: the practice stopped paying, or the coalition defending it lost power, and the moral arguments are decoration people produce afterwards to feel like agents. Camp three, which has been winning lately, says honor, in the Appiah vein: what retargets a practice isn't argument or profit but respectability — slave trading became disreputable while it was still profitable and while the West Indian planters still had real political weight; dueling didn't get abolished so much as it became slightly ridiculous; footbinding collapsed inside roughly a generation because bound feet stopped being a credential and started being a liability in the marriage market.

My hypothesis is that this isn't really three competing causal claims, and I want you to tell me whether that's right or whether I'm just bored of the argument. The reason I think that: the argument camp keeps answering a vindicatory question — were we right to stop, can we say we know better now, does the story leave us entitled to our conviction — while the interest camp is answering a distal-cause question and the honor camp a proximate-mechanism question. They only look like rivals because all three get phrased as "what really ended it." Underneath, though, I keep hitting three things that make me suspect there's more going on than a simple equivocation.

First, the missing apologia. Successful revolutions don't merely defeat the old arguments; they seem to make them unavailable. Nobody in 1900 could produce the case for dueling as a live option — not because it had been refuted but because the vocabulary it needed had evaporated. That looks like a fact about the phenomenology of change that none of the three accounts was built to explain, and I can't tell whether it's evidence for one of them or a separate phenomenon entirely. Second, selection: we only study practices that ended. Nobody calls a failed campaign a "moral revolution," they call it a movement, so the sample is chosen on the outcome and none of the three theories ever gets tested against the near-misses. Third, the shape of the change. Some practices erode over a century, others fall off a cliff in fifteen years, and erosion-versus-collapse looks like the one genuinely empirical thing that could discriminate between mechanisms — if I knew which mechanism produced which shape, I'd know where to look.

So: what kind of disagreement is this? Verbal, different questions under one name, a hidden empirical question about timing and social structure doing all the work, or bedrock disagreement about what would even count as an explanation of a norm change? And there's a fork I particularly want you to take seriously: does the honor story help the vindicatory project or quietly gut it? Williams's worry was that absent moral objectivism we can't say the abolitionists knew, only that we have come round to their view — and if the actual mechanism was status competition, that seems like a further step away from reason, though Appiah himself seems oddly cheerful about it. Maybe the real dispute is just between people who want the causal story to underwrite the normative one and people who don't.

Give me the diagnosis with reasons, not a tour of the positions, and then tell me honestly what would count as progress given it: a distinction, a taxonomy of cases with predicted signatures, a different question entirely, or nothing at all. I'd also take an argument that the whole thing is ill posed and I should stop.
