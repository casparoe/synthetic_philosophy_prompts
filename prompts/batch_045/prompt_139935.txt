I'm giving a paper at a political theory seminar next week, and I want to be prepared for the question period rather than for the comments I'd get online.

My thesis is that compromise between holders of deeply different value systems can produce genuine gains from trade, and — the part I expect trouble over — that these gains are typically larger, not smaller, when the two value systems are incommensurable rather than merely different weightings of a common currency. Where a common currency exists, compromise degenerates into splitting a difference: every feasible settlement lies on a short segment between the two positions, and the surplus to be divided is small. Where there is no common currency, each side can still rank the contested provisions by what I call moral urgency — how much is at stake, for that side, in having its way on this item — and I argue that whenever the two urgency orderings are inversely ordered on some pair of provisions (one side's stake in A exceeds its stake in B, the other's is the reverse), there exists a settlement that each side prefers, by its own lights, to any point on the line between its own position and the other's. Often this is an off-diagonal arrangement that neither side would have proposed on its own and that neither value system can represent as a mixture of the two original positions. Incommensurability, on this picture, is not an obstacle to gains from trade but the mechanism that creates them.

The room is about twenty people: mostly analytic political philosophers working on public reason, legitimacy, and the ethics of compromise; two social-choice theorists; and one person who writes on integrity and dirty hands. They are receptive to the claim that compromise can be morally required and hostile to its being modeled as a bargain. Nobody there is a friend of modus vivendi.

Give me the objections I will actually get from this room, roughly in the order they will come and phrased in the vocabulary of whichever subgroup is raising them — six or seven, not an exhaustive list. For each, give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, a note on where that reply is soft, and a judgment about whether I should concede the point up front instead. Flag the single objection I should try to defuse in the opening two minutes of the talk, and end with the question I am hoping nobody asks.
