so heres the situation. A bunch of us from the monthly laptop-music night are putting out a little cassette where each of us takes the same six-second sample -- a guy humming four notes while making coffee, recorded on a phone -- and does a variation of it, and I volunteered to write something short for the insert, a poem rather than liner notes. I got into this because I'd been chewing on the idea (I think I got it from a chunky book by some cog-sci guy whose name I always forget, or maybe from a friend of a friend) that making something new isn't about jumping arbitrarily far but about turning knobs on something you already have: a style is a point in a big space, the knobs are the dimensions, and most of what we call creativity is moving around inside that space rather than teleporting. The part I keep getting stuck on, and the part I want the poem to actually do something with rather than just mention, is that distance in the space doesn't seem to be what makes a variation feel alive. Moving one deep knob a long way -- the shape of the phrase, where the silence falls, whether the thing is asked or stated -- strikes me as much more of an act than nudging six surface knobs a little, even though you could draw it as the same length of arrow in the space. I've started calling that "cousin distance": a good variation is far enough away to be a relative rather than a photocopy, but the distance has to be taken along the dimensions that carry the identity of the thing. Which raises the bit I can't settle, because if you keep twiddling a deep knob there's a point where it stops being a variation of this theme and becomes something else, and I can't find the line, and it feels like there shouldn't be a sharp one -- so being the "same theme" has to be a matter of degree, which means the knob story owes me an account of what makes some dimensions identity-carrying in the first place. And separately: a friend said the whole picture has a hole in it, because you can only twiddle knobs that already exist, and sometimes people don't move around in the space, they change the space -- they add a dimension nobody had -- and that's supposed to be a different kind of thing entirely, not just a bigger twiddle. I half think she's right and half think the difference is one of degree too.

So: I want a sestina. Six stanzas of six lines plus the three-line envoi, unrhymed, the normal end-word rotation, nothing fancy about the line lengths but it should be readable out loud in one go at a listening night. The six end-words should be six knobs on that coffee-humming sample -- tempo/swing, grain, decay, register, the room (reverb), and density or whatever you'd rather use instead, your call -- and each stanza should be the same small scene rendered with those knobs at different settings, so the machinery of the form is doing the argument: the words are identical every time, only their order and their settings change, and yet each stanza is supposed to be a genuinely different piece and not the same piece six times. (That's the test, I think -- if the rotations feel like a trick rather than six variations, the form has failed at the thing it was supposed to demonstrate.) Please make it accurate rather than name-droppy: someone who actually thinks about this stuff should read it and think the cousin-distance point and the vague-horizon problem are right, not just see the word "creativity" near the word "variation." Someone who knows nothing about any of it should just get a decent poem about a man humming in a kitchen and all the other songs hiding in it. And then the envoi, the last three lines: I want it to bring in a seventh word that isn't one of the six -- a knob that wasn't in the original set, a dimension the space didn't have -- and I want that to land as either the honest answer to my friend's objection or as a cheat, whichever you actually think it is, but don't resolve the whole thing into "creativity is just recombining" or "creativity is magic." Leave the tension standing; that's where the night ended up too.

After the poem, give me the apparatus: first a table with the columns stanza (1-6, then envoi), knob (which end-word closes the stanza, or all six for the envoi plus the seventh), setting (one clause on what that knob is doing to the sample in this stanza), distance (near / cousin / far / past the horizon, your verdict), and what_the_rotation_does (one clause on why putting that word at the end of that particular stanza changes the reading). Then a JSON object with exactly these fields: identity_carrying_dimensions (which of the six knobs you treated as deep, and what makes them deep here), cousin_distance (a one-sentence definition as the poem actually used it), horizon (whether the poem found a line where it stops being the same theme, and where), new_knob (its name, what dimension it adds, and whether you think it was reachable by twiddling the original six), verdict_on_the_objection (whether changing the space is a different kind of thing from moving in it, as far as this poem and this example go), and unresolved (the one bit the poem deliberately left open). Just the poem, the table, and the JSON, please -- no essay around it.
