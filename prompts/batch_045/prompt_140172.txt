In the historiography seminar I attend, a senior colleague keeps closing off arguments with the old couplet — in the version everyone repeats: for want of a nail the shoe was lost, for want of a shoe the horse, for want of the horse the rider, for want of the rider the battle, for want of the battle the kingdom, and all for the want of a horseshoe nail. He puts it to two uses: first, to establish that large historical outcomes are sensitive to arbitrarily small antecedent details, and second, to conclude from that that questions of the form "Was the assassination at Sarajevo genuinely decisive, or was it the mobilization timetables, or the alliance structure, or the railway gauge?" have no stable answer, since any event can be shown after the fact to have hinged on some quotidian, replaceable particular.

I think he is getting more out of those lines than they contain, but I cannot cleanly separate what they might be claiming. I am not interested in who first wrote them down. I want the distinct precise theses the proverb could be expressing, laid out as a menu, with verdicts, and with an explicit note for each about whether it would actually do the work he needs it to do.

The rough distinctions I have started sketching, which you should correct, split, or collapse as you see fit: a trivial-necessity reading (every event has innumerable causally necessary conditions, so "for want of X, the kingdom" is true of arbitrarily many X and therefore supports no conclusion about ranking causes); a series-reliability or maintenance reading (the substance is that a chain of stages fails if any stage fails, which is a logistics and redundancy-engineering maxim rather than a metaphysical one, and which argues for checking nails without arguing that history is unpredictable); a chaotic-amplification reading (sensitivity to arbitrarily fine-grained difference, so the mapping from antecedents to outcomes is unstable across most of the space and not merely at a few tipping points); a threshold or cliff reading (sensitivity is real but localized, so a nail can be decisive only if the situation was already near a cliff, which leaves most micro-details idle and would underwrite rather than undermine the ranking of causes); a substitutability reading (if nails are fungible and spares exist, the missing nail is not a knife-edge at all, and the explanatory weight moves upstream to whatever removed the spare, which makes the proverb a claim about enabling versus triggering conditions and about contrast classes in causal selection); an epistemic reading (a claim about our inability to tell in advance which details will matter, compatible with determinism and with macro-level robustness); and a practical reading (attend to the small controllable conditions because the large ones are not controllable even when they matter more — a claim about where to intervene, not about what matters most).

Two things I would particularly like addressed. First, the proverb seems to pull in opposite directions: read one way it says the kingdom nearly fell, so the outcome was fragile; read another it says the grand factors did not settle the outcome, so the outcome was robust with respect to the grand factors and settled by noise. Second, even the strong sensitivity reading does not by itself license treating every counterfactual as equally respectable, since counterfactual inquiry is constrained by minimal-rewrite requirements and by holding the background fixed; I want that gap made explicit.

For each reading, please give the precise thesis, whether it is a platitude, substantive and defensible, substantive and contested, or false, what kind of case or formal result would discriminate it from its neighbors, and whether it supports either of my colleague's two moves. Say which readings are mutually compatible and which conflict, and which one he is most likely invoking. What would persuade me most is a reading that keeps whatever is true about enabling conditions and chained failure while still licensing the ranking of causes by how robust an outcome is to variation in them — plus an honest statement of what we would have to know about real historical systems to tell whether they are closer to the amplification reading or the cliff reading. Please lay it out as a table or list I can bring to the next session rather than adopting a single reading and defending it.
