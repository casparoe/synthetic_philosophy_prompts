I've been in a small reading group working through radical probabilism and the rules for updating on evidence you do not receive with certainty — Jeffrey conditioning, the rigidity condition it relies on, and the contrast with plain conditioning on whatever the evidence report says — and I keep coming back to the 1983 Soviet nuclear false alarm as the case that should either vindicate the machinery or expose it. The episode, as I have it: on 26 September 1983, three weeks after Soviet fighters shot down a South Korean airliner and during an unusually tense stretch of the Cold War, Lieutenant Colonel Stanislav Petrov was the duty officer at the command centre for the Oko early-warning system when the newly deployed satellite network reported a single intercontinental missile launch from the United States, then up to four more; ground radar picked up nothing even after several minutes; standing doctrine said an American first strike would be all-out, so five missiles looked wrong as an opening move, and the report had come up through many layers of verification suspiciously fast; Petrov judged it a malfunction and passed it up the chain as a false alarm rather than as an attack; nothing was launched, and the episode was later traced to sunlight reflecting off high-altitude clouds above North Dakota into the satellites' Molniya orbits. He said afterwards that he had never been certain at the time, and his role was to monitor and report, not to launch. This has become oddly personal to me because I do on-call triage for a large server fleet, where the overwhelming majority of alerts are false positives, and the question of how much an alarm should move me as opposed to how much it would move me if I simply treated it as a fact is more or less my job description.

What I want is a disciplined mapping rather than a retelling. Lay out the hypotheses and the evidence partition explicitly, and tell me which numbers actually carry the result: the prior on a real launch that night, the probability of a report given an attack, and above all the probability of a report given no attack, which is where I suspect everything turns. I have been playing with something like one in a thousand for the prior and a very high hit rate for the system, but I cannot tell whether the answer is driven by those or by the false-alarm rate. Then work through the candidate updates and compare them: plain conditioning on the report as though the report itself were certain; a Jeffrey shift on the report partition with the conditional probabilities held rigid; and the variant one member of my group prefers, which instead holds fixed the likelihood ratio of the report given attack versus no attack and lets the probability of the report itself move. Do these genuinely diverge on numbers in this range, and if so, what is the argument for picking one?

Then press on the assumptions, because that is where I think the interesting part is. Part of what moved Petrov was doubt about the reporting channel: the system was new and, in his judgement, not yet trustworthy. But if what shifts him is a discovery about the channel itself, is rigidity even available here, or does holding the conditional probabilities fixed while moving the probability of the report quietly assume away the very thing he learned? His signals also arrived in a stream — the satellite report, then radar silence, then the implausibly small number of missiles — and I cannot settle whether the order in which those are absorbed should matter for where he should end up, or whether composing these updates is order-independent the way ordinary conditioning is. Finally, run it back the other way: is this a case where the theory earns its keep by forbidding the naive update, or an anomaly that exposes something it misdescribes — that its output is a posterior when what the agent needs is a decision under a deadline, that it says nothing about the option of buying more evidence by waiting, or that it is a norm for a single agent when this update was in fact distributed across a chain of people with different priors and different stakes? Please be explicit about where the theory is silent instead of forcing it to speak, and tell me honestly whether some other apparatus — decision theory with wildly asymmetric costs, signal-detection theory, or something else — is doing the work that Jeffrey conditioning cannot.
