I have no academic background in either philosophy or astronomy — I work in logistics and read this sort of thing on my own at night — and for a while now I've been using the Pluto affair as a test case for something I read about Wittgenstein. I think I'm running the test badly, and I'd like you to run it properly.

The episode, as I have it: Pluto was found in 1930 and went into the books as the ninth planet. From the 1990s onward astronomers began finding a great many other icy bodies out beyond Neptune, some of them close to Pluto in size. In 2006 the International Astronomical Union put a definition of 'planet' to a vote — roughly, that a planet orbits the Sun, is pulled into a round shape by its own gravity, and has cleared other bodies out of the neighborhood of its orbit — Pluto failed the last clause, and became a 'dwarf planet'. There was genuine public upset, and textbooks and museum displays had to be rewritten. As I understand it the definition only covers bodies orbiting our Sun, so the word still has no official sense for the planets found around other stars. If any of that is wrong, correct it in passing, but please don't let the analysis hang on it.

The idea I'm testing it against, in my own words: that a great many words are not held together by one feature that everything they cover has in common, but by overlapping likenesses running across a family — his example being games — so that a word can be in perfectly good working order without a hard edge, and that the hunt for the real definition is the thing that gets us into trouble.

Two claims I keep sliding between, which I suspect are actually two and not one: that there is no single feature shared by all the things we call planets, and that there is no fact of the matter about the borderline ones. It seems to me the first could be true while the second is false — that a word can lack an essence and still deliver a definite verdict in a particular hard case. Does that distinction hold up, and which of the two does the 2006 dispute really turn on?

What I want, in this order:

First, set up the mapping for me: who was using the word and for what — working astronomers, the people who write schoolbooks and run planetariums, the rest of us — what each of them needed 'planet' to do, and what would have counted as evidence for each of them (a telescope image, a model of orbital dynamics, the contents of a third-grade textbook).

Second, say what the family-likeness view predicts about the situation before 2006 — should we have expected trouble once the similar objects started turning up? — and what it says about the vote itself. Is a vote the right sort of thing to settle a question like this, and is the fact that it took a vote evidence for Wittgenstein, or just an unconnected fact about how astronomy is organized?

Third, tell me where the view goes quiet. Does it have anything at all to say about whether the 2006 decision was a good one? If the honest answer is that it only describes how the word was working and has nothing to say about what should have been done, say that rather than squeezing a recommendation out of it.

Fourth, run it the other way: is the episode a point for Wittgenstein, a point against him, or neither? My worry is that a lot of the words people offer as examples of this — in law, in medicine, in astronomy — are exactly the ones that somebody eventually has to sharpen into a yes-or-no test, and that a view which tells us the soft edges are fine has no account of the moment an institution has to produce a verdict. If Wittgenstein has an answer to that, tell me what it is and whether it works here.

Fifth, give me the case I can't work out for myself: Ceres. It was called a planet for some decades after 1801 and then quietly stopped being one, as far as I know without anyone voting on it and without anybody much caring. On your account, what happened to the word there — the same kind of event as 2006, or a different one?

Please answer as exactly five numbered items, one following the other in that order, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no summing-up at the end. Somewhere in the list, in one sentence, tell me which parts of what you've said are settled among people who actually work on this and which are live disputes, and mark a claim as contested rather than handing me a contested reading as the consensus. American English, please.
