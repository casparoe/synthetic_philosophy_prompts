I am assembling a set of worked examples for a graduate seminar on diachronic rationality, and I need one case that will fit on a single slide: a case in which an agent's present credence in a proposition, conditional on the supposition that her credence in that same proposition at some specified later time will be x, comes out as something other than x — the sort of case standardly offered against van Fraassen's Reflection principle — but engineered so that the usual diagnoses are unavailable. The point I want the slide to make is that Reflection is not a purely synchronic coherence requirement: it presupposes something substantive about how an agent's evidence at one time relates to her evidence at another. A case that works by making the later agent defective in some way, or by quietly having the two credences attach to different propositions, would miss that point even if it met the letter of the specification.

What I want from you is the scenario plus a demonstration that it does what I have asked of it.

The scenario: one paragraph, an ordinary setting — a hospital, a workplace, a court, a long journey, nothing that could not appear in a novel. No science fiction, no duplication or cloning, no Sleeping Beauty-style experimental protocols, no predictors better than a shrewd human being. Supply explicit numbers: name the proposition H, the two times, the agent's credence in H at the earlier time, her credence in H at the later time, and her earlier credence in H conditional on the later credence being x, with a line or two of arithmetic. I need the divergence in this direction: the earlier conditional credence should be lower than x, so that she ought to be less confident in H than her future self will be. The verdicts must fall out of the credences you specify rather than be stipulated; show why each number is what it is, given what she knows at each time.

Constraints on the mechanism, each of which I should like you to discharge explicitly in a sentence or two:

(a) the later self must be defective in none of the familiar ways — no anticipated irrationality, no known bias or wishful thinking, no intoxication, exhaustion or impairment, no drugs, hypnosis, brainwashing or manipulation by others;
(b) no memory loss, and no loss of any item of evidence that could be stated without indexicals;
(c) no misleading evidence, and no evidence whose general character she cannot already anticipate at the earlier time;
(d) the later credence must not itself make H more or less likely to be true — no self-fulfilling or self-defeating predictions, no commitment devices, nothing that turns the later credence into a decision rather than a prediction;
(e) if the case turns on the content of H shifting between the two times — indexicality or self-location, context-sensitivity, vagueness, conceptual change — say so plainly, and argue that this is a genuine instance of the phenomenon rather than a pair of credences directed at different propositions;
(f) tell me which of these constraints came closest to being violated, and why it is not.

Then the analysis. Set out the sequence of bets in the diachronic Dutch book argument usually given in support of Reflection, and tell me whether it actually lands on your agent: is the loss genuinely a sure loss, does she accept each bet at her own prices, and if the book does go through, what should she say in reply?

Finish with three short items: the single feature of the case that is doing the work; what becomes of the case if that feature is removed; and the strongest reply available to someone who wants to keep Reflection in full generality, including the restriction or reformulation they would have to adopt and what that would cost them.
