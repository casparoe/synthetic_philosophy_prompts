I'm in a fortnightly reading group on consciousness that meets in a philosophy grad student's kitchen — mostly philosophy people plus one neuroscience postdoc — and last night two of them, Jonas and Priya, got stuck on epiphenomenalism and I honestly couldn't tell who was winning. I'll reconstruct it as best I can (they both accused me afterwards of favouring the other one, so I'll keep my own view out of it). (1) Priya opened with the paradox: if phenomenal properties do no causal work, then my saying or judging that I'm in intense pain is caused entirely by non-phenomenal neural goings-on, so the best causal explanation of that judgment never mentions the pain itself; and since people usually arrive at epiphenomenalism by reflecting on what their own experience is like, the view seems to saw off the branch it's sitting on. (2) Jonas said that's too fast: the judgment is caused by the neural state N, and N also gives rise to the quale, so N is a common cause and the judgment reliably co-varies with the experience; knowledge needs reliable tracking, not causal contact with the thing known. (3) Priya said the common-cause move only relocates the problem, because the co-variation is then a brute, contingent, empirical fact that nothing about the experience itself secures — she asked us to imagine a subject whose introspective mechanism is "cross-wired," so that the neural state that normally produces pain instead drives a confident judgment of not being in pain, with the experiences unchanged; epiphenomenalism, she said, can't rule this out in principle, and no amount of checking from the inside could reveal it, since every check lands on the judgment side of the divide, not the experiential side. (4) Jonas replied that what makes the judgment *about* pain isn't causal contact at all but a non-causal, constitutive relation — he called it "content anchoring by nomic co-instantiation," the idea being that a judgment state gets its phenomenal content from whichever phenomenal property is nomically co-instantiated with its neural cause — and he added that Priya was conflating two different complaints: a complaint about whether the belief is *true*, and a complaint about whether I have *a reason* for holding it, where only the second is touched by the paradox. (5) Priya said the anchoring move forces a dilemma: either the neural state N just is (or constitutes) the pain, in which case the experiential is causally relevant after all and we've quietly slid into ordinary physicalism, or there's a real gap between N and the pain, in which case the paradox stands, so epiphenomenalism either collapses into its opponent or stays self-stultifying. (6) Jonas took the second horn deliberately: yes, we lose introspective authority, our "I'm in pain" judgments are more like a thermometer's readout than like testimony about an object we're acquainted with, and the view should be accepted as an inference to the best explanation from the physical side rather than from the armchair — giving up authority, he said, is cheaper than giving up the causal closure of the physical or than swallowing interactionist dualism. (7) Priya's last move was that the cost isn't confined to armchair philosophy: basically all of the empirical science of consciousness runs on subjects' reports about what they experience, so if reports are caused wholly by non-phenomenal states whose correlation with experience is a brute contingency, then epiphenomenalism can't underwrite the very data set it would be inferred from — the thermometer reading is doing all the evidential work while being, on the view, evidentially unanchored.

Can you go through this move by move — rate each of the seven from clearly mistaken to clearly right, say what each person is implicitly relying on at that point, and whether the reply actually answers the objection it's answering (I suspect a couple of them talk past each other)? Also: is Priya's dilemma in (5) real, is the cross-wiring case in (3) doing the work she thinks it is, and is there something both of them are missing — some distinction, or some version of the view, that dissolves the argument rather than awarding it to either side? I'd like the verdict on the arguments as they were actually made, not a general primer on epiphenomenalism.
