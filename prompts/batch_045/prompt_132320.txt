I'm a novelist, and somewhere around my third book I picked up a view I now can't seem to get rid of: that the Moorean certainties Wittgenstein fiddles with in the late notes published as On Certainty — I have two hands, the world didn't begin five minutes ago, that woman is my mother — aren't beliefs at all. Not true, not false, not even things I hold while being ready to be wrong. They're closer to the rules of the practice inside which believing, doubting and asking for evidence do any work, so demanding my grounds for them is a category mistake rather than a skeptical challenge I happen to lose. I've bolted on two embellishments of my own: that hinges are installed by training rather than acquired from evidence, and that the best everyday model for them is the opening page of a novel, where my narrator says flatly that her mother died in March and the reader neither believes that nor disbelieves it nor suspends judgement about it — they take it as fixed and get on with reading. I use that analogy in every interview and I've started to suspect its doing me damage.

Play it straight with me: what would count against this? I don't want a list of standard objections; I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to mark which ones a committed hinge theorist would concede as really counting against the view rather than as a misreading, because from outside the literature I can't tell the difference. Cover the ones I'm already half-aware of: that some things that look like hinges have in fact been given up for reasons (whether anyone has been to the moon, how old the earth is), which may mean my "rules" are just very stubborn beliefs and the whole position quietly collapses into ordinary holism; that a purely negative characterisation — not belief, not doubt, not knowledge — might not be a theory of anything; and that I may have insulated it so thoroughly that nothing could touch it, plus whether for a view of this kind that's a defect or the whole point. Also tell me what it commits me to that a rival doesn't, and what would have to be true about how we learn or how we revise for me to have to drop it.

I'm most likely to be moved by a real case of a hinge being rationally revised, and least by being told I'm doing amateur psychology. I'm at a residency with no library access, so don't send me to papers or tell me what someone argues in a footnote — argue it in your own words. Say how confident you are in each of your main claims and single out the one you're least sure of, including on the fiction analogy, where I suspect you'll be tempted to tell me it's charming.
