A mate sent me a long-read from one of those weekend magazine supplements on Camus and capital punishment (I won't name the piece, and I can't get hold of the original essay — my French is basically nil, so please don't just tell me to go and read Réflexions sur la peine capitale and find out for myself). I've no idea how much of it to trust. The bit that stuck with me went roughly like this:

Basically Camus thought the death penalty should be abolished, and his case was that it doesn't work and it's barbaric. He went through the crime statistics and argued that no one has ever shown capital punishment deters anybody — murder rates didn't move in countries that kept hanging — and, more importantly, that no court is reliable enough to be handing out a sentence you can't take back. Once you admit the possibility of a miscarriage of justice, and that nobody is innocent enough to be entitled to condemn another person to death, the whole thing collapses. Underneath the statistics, he thought the guillotine was 'absurd', in the sense he'd already made famous: the universe doesn't care, there's no meaning written into things, so the state's solemn ceremony of revenge is just theatre pretending to be justice. He also thought you couldn't stop half way — if premeditated killing by the state is wrong because it treats a human being as something to be disposed of, the same logic tells against locking people up for decades, and a consistent Camusian should be suspicious of punishment as such. What actually horrified him, though, was the machine itself: the blade falls, and the severed head is still alive afterwards for a while, and that, the piece said, is what the whole argument really comes down to.

I've got two suspicions and I don't trust either. First, the 'nothing means anything, so the state's moral theatre is a joke' bit sounds like the sort of thing people say about Camus but not like something he'd actually argue — but I can't say why. Second, my instinct is that the generalising move is right: if the argument works, it works against a whole style of thinking about punishment, not just the guillotine. But my mate reckons that turns Camus into a sort of abolitionist anarchist, and he definately wasn't that.
