I work in applied causal inference — potential outcomes, DAGs, instrumental variables, natural experiments, exogenous shocks, treatment-effect heterogeneity — and I read a fair amount of quantitative history. A historian colleague insists that germs, climate, and volcanoes should be treated as historical actors rather than as background conditions, and that this is not mere metaphor. Please translate the strongest version of that claim into my vocabulary as faithfully as you can, and then tell me what the translation loses.

Use my provisional gloss of the distinction rather than replacing it: something counts as an actor only if it has a response function — that is, only if its behaviour counterfactually depends on the state of the system it perturbs — whereas a mere condition exerts causal influence from outside that system. On this gloss volcanoes look like pure exogenous shocks, pathogens look like co-evolving systems whose behaviour shifts with human density, sanitation, and migration, and climate now looks partly endogenous, since human activity feeds back into it.

Two things I want beyond the translation. First, the residue report: which claims about non-human agency map cleanly onto causal structure, which survive only with distortion — I suspect the notion that a cause can figure in someone's reasons, or matter to a narrative, has nowhere to go in my framework — and which cannot be expressed at all. Second, a verdict on the worry about environmental determinism: in causal terms, is it best read as a complaint about inflated effect sizes, or as a complaint about which counterfactual contrasts historians are licensed to hold fixed when they say that a volcano or a pathogen brought something about? Where the residue sits, say whether it indicts my vocabulary or indicates that the historians' claim was less clear than it looks.
