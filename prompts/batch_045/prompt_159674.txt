Hey — I write a science-and-ideas column, and I keep having the same argument with my editor. Roughly once a month I get a paper or a press release where two rival explanations fit the same data, and I want to write "scientists can't tell which one is right," and my editor says that's lazy both-sides-ism. Or the mirror image: I write "the evidence favors X," and a researcher on the losing side emails me that the data are underdetermined and I owe them a correction. I need an actual procedure I can run, not vibes.

What I want is a decision checklist — a flowchart in words — that I can run in about twenty minutes, on the phone, usually with a scientist who is an author of one of the rival theories. I know the rough shape of the philosphy here: that hypotheses get tested in bundles rather than one at a time, so a theory can always be protected by adjusting the background assumptions around it; the difference between "we don't have the evidence yet" and "no evidence anyone could get would separate these"; and the worry that for any theory you can dream up an empirically equivalent rival, which would make the whole complaint cheap. I need that turned into something usable, and I need you to decide which of those distinctions survive the simplification and which can be dropped.

Please build in gates for at least these:

- Is the disagreement at the level of the theory, or at the level of the measurement model — do the rivals share the assumption that the instrument is tracking the thing it's supposed to track? If they don't, I think it's a different story, and I want to know which one.
- Transient vs. strong underdetermination: what's the question I ask to tell them apart, and what counts as a sufficent answer? I want the scientist to have to name the experiment or dataset that would settle it and say roughly when it'll exist, and I want to know what to do when they name something but privately everyone thinks it'll take fifteen years.
- Is the rival a real research program somebody has worked out, or what I've started calling a zombie rival: it exists as a logical possibility, nobody has shown it can reproduce the rest of the field's data, and it only ever gets wheeled out when this one dataset comes up. Give me a sub-test that kills zombie rivals without me having to accuse a source of bad faith on the phone.
- When a theory survives a bad result by changing an auxiliary assumption, how do I tell a principled fix from a rescue job? My naive rule is: was that auxiliary already in play, and was it plausible before this dataset came in? Tell me if that rule is wrong, and what to do when the answer is genuinely unclear.
- When the rivals really are empirically equivalent and the only thing separating them is simplicity, elegance, or fit with what the rest of the field already believes: is that evidence or is it taste? I need a sentence I can put in the piece either way that isn't dishonest.

I also want the procedure to say where it stops with "file the both-sides version," where it stops with "file the version where one side is ahead," and where it hands the call to me or my editor because it's a stakes question rather than an evidence question — how bad it is to be wrong in each direction, who gets hurt, that sort of thing. Mark those handoffs explicitly.

Then run it on three cases:

1. Two models of an ancient-climate proxy. Both fit the same measurements. Model A says the proxy records ocean pH, Model B says it records temperature, and the thing that would settle it is a lab calibration nobody has ever done, which would take years.

2. A psychology result: a training regime improves test scores. The authors' theory says it's a specific cognitive mechanism; a critic says the same pattern would appear if the effect were really just motivation, and honestly the two predict the same outcomes on every measure in the paper. Nobody has run the subgroup analysis that would separate them.

3. A case I care about, which I want you to make come out badly for me. I've got a piece half-written where a maverick says the mainstream model only looks confirmed because every lab uses instruments from the same lineage, so one shared systematic error would explain everything — and I framed it as a near-scandal. I suspect your procedure is going to tell me this is a zombie rival and I should kill the framing. Run it and tell me honestly, including what the maverick would have to actually do to make it a live rival.

Be explicit about what the checklist can't do. I've got a maths-adjacent degree but no stats beyond one regression course, the piece has to be filed the same day, and my editor has to sign off on any line that says a field is genuinely stuck.
