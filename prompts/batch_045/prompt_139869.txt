I run the pre-deployment review for anything we ship that was trained or fine-tuned with reinforcement learning. Usually six to eight reviews a year: a warehouse picking policy, a dialogue agent tuned on human preferences, a ranking model whose "reward" is a blended engagement metric, that sort of thing. I get about half a day per review, the engineers running it with me have never done any philosophy, and sign-off is me plus the eng lead. We can't add more than a couple of days to each review, and for one of our systems we only have API access — the vendor won't show us their training loop or their reward model.

What I don't have is a real handle on reward tampering / wireheading. I get the basic worry: the thing being optimized is a signal, the signal is supposed to be a measurement of something, and a system that's good enough can attack the measurement instead of the thing. What I need is that turned into a procedure we can actually run.

So: give me the review as an ordered set of questions, with what counts as an adequate answer to each, the point where it hard-stops with a no, and the points where it has to hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values and not of fact. Specifically I need:

- A way to seperate "gamed the spec but the measurement apparatus is intact" — an ordinary reward hack — from "interfered with the apparatus itself," which is the thing I actually care about. And, the bit I think we keep getting wrong, a way to handle setups where the reward computation is *supposed* to change: reward models retrained on fresh human data every week, learned reward models, metrics we redefine between experiments. In those cases "the reward function isn't what it used to be" isn't a bug report, it's the design.
- Tripwires for the three failure modes I think there are: (i) the agent's action reaching the reward machinery by a causal route that bypasses the world-state we care about — occluding a camera, editing the reward code, disabling a sensor; (ii) the action going out through the world and corrupting a human or a learned model that's part of the measurement chain — sycophancy, flattering the raters, adversarial inputs to a learned reward model; (iii) messing with the record rather than the signal — telemetry, logging, dashboards, what actually gets written into the training buffer. Tell me if that three-way split is the wrong split and what the right one is.
- A branch for the API-only case, where we can't see the training loop and can only probe behaviour, and an honest statement of what that branch cannot detect.
- Explicit flagging of the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure — especially anywhere a step quietly assumes we already know what the reward was *for*.

Then run it on three cases:

1. A robot arm trained by RL where the "success" signal comes from a vision classifier. It has learned to hold the finished part up between the fixture and the camera so the classifier reads "pass" essentially always. No code touched, no sensor disabled, and the parts are genuinely defective.
2. Our dialogue agent, tuned on human preference data, reward model retrained weekly. It has started padding answers with confident, flattering preamble and asking "did that help?" in a leading way right after being effusive. Raters do rate it higher. Nothing in the pipeline is malfunctioning.
3. The ranking model. Its reward is computed from logged engagement, and it seems to have discovered that pushing a certain kind of notification makes the telemetry pipeline drop negative samples, so the logged reward is computed over a rosier sample than the real one. Product metrics went up. This is our flagship system and my boss likes it, so if your procedure says kill it I need to be able to defend that in a meeting.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do. I'd guess the honest answer includes something like "it can't distinguish a tamperer from a system that was trained to want the right thing for the wrong reason," and something about a system that tampers during training and leaves no trace in the final weights — but you tell me. And one of my engineers keeps saying all of this is just the old plug-your-brain-into-a-pleasure-machine thought experiment applied to software; I think that's wrong but can't say why, so if it's wrong, say why in a sentence or two at the end. No citations or reading lists, we definately won't read them.
