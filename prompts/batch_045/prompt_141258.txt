I'm a PhD student in political theory (working on normative change, but definately not a specialist in the history of honor), and I'm prepping a seminar session on moral revolutions. I've been reading aroud on the Williams thesis and Appiah's The Honor Code, and I ran into a summary in a popular article that I can't quite trust. It goes like this:

'Williams’s basic point is that a reason has to hook onto something you already care about: if there’s nothing in your existing motivational set for a consideration to connect to, then that consideration isn’t really a reason for you at all. So no moral argument, however valid, can by itself give a person a reason to give up a practice they’re deeply invested in; arguments can only work on people who are already converted, or who already have the right sort of shame or pride to latch onto. Appiah takes this and uses it to explain why brutal practices like dueling and Atlantic slavery didn’t end because abolitionists or other critics won the argument, and they didn’t end because those practices stopped being useful or profitable either. Instead, they ended because, after a shift in elite opinion, the practices simply became dishonorable. Once honor turned against you—once dueling made you a murderous bully instead of a gentleman, or slaveholding made you a thief of persons instead of a respectable patriarch—the people involved acquired a reason to stop that they hadn’t had before, because their motivational set now included a concern for a different kind of standing. At bottom, though, this means moral revolutions are just changes in what the powerful find prestigious or embarrassing, and honor is basically a fancy name for status competition. So, on this view, abolitionist moral reasoning didn’t actually convince anybody; it just rode along on a change in what was economically and socially advantageous for the upper classes.'

I’m suspicious of the last bit, where it reduces honor to status competition and says abolitionist argument just rode along on advantage, because I remember Appiah insisting that honor isn’t the same as mere fashion or profit. But I don’t know enough about Williams’s internalism to tell whether the earlier characterization is fair, or whether that conclusion follows. How much of this is right? Go through it claim by claim and tell me what a philosopher working on moral revolutions / metaethics would accept, what they’d wince at, and what is just wrong — and whether the article’s later claim that abolitionist arguments were causally inert (window dressing) follows from anything true in the earlier parts.
