I'm a science-and-ideas journalist and I've somehow let myself be talked into running a 90-minute session at a summer school for foreign correspondents in September. About 16 people: mostly reporters, a few foreign-desk editors, mixed nationalities, nobody's a lawyer and nobody's a specialist in this. What I want them to walk out having *felt* is that picking a word for a mass atrocity isn't reading a verdict off the facts — it's a move, with consequences attached: legal duties that switch on, diplomatic blowback, what survivors get to say happened to them, whether your stringer keeps her visa, whether the desk can defend the sentence in print a week later. And that the legal use of the word and the ordinary moral one pull apart in ways you can't paper over. I do not want to stand up and explain this. I want the room to run into it.

So design me a runnable thing: a staged editorial conference or role-play where they have to actually decide what to print. Invent the case — fictional country, fictional group, obviously made up, because if it smells like a real one half the room will spend the hour arguing about that instead. Build it so the knotty parts are structural, not decorative:

- The targeted group is defined ambiguously. The perpetrators classify them one way (religious, say, or by a category the census doesn't use), the victims describe themselves another. Ideally it straddles one of the four group-types the 1948 convention covers and something the treaty leaves out. So the group question is genuinely not settled by the facts.
- The intent evidence — an intercepted order, a speech with one slippery phrase — only lands after they've already filed a first formulation. Make them commit before they know.
- The death-toll estimate gets revised upward after publication. (There's a reason this should be able to move them, given how the treaty's "in whole or in part" gets read.)
- Survivor voices in the room disagree with each other about whether they want the word. Not a monologue.
- Every word they could print carries a real cost, drawn blind: access revoked, inquiry opened, a neighbouring state suddenly implicated, an exile community denouncing you, your lawyers, your audience tuning out.
- The actual treaty text is available, but only if they pay for it — lose the front-page slot or go several hours later. Accuracy vs. speed, concretely.

Give me roles, printed materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, and whatever rules keep them inside the fiction. They should end by producing one sentence for tomorrow's paper and being able to defend both the word and the hedge.

Then the debrief: map specific moments onto specific features of the problem, tell me what will predictably go wrong (someone will say just describe what happened and let readers decide; someone will insist the exercise is rigged because I withheld facts — I think that's a feature, tell me how to use it), and be honest about what the game can't reproduce and I'll have to say myself — I'm thinking the real stakes, the decades-long process by which a label sticks or doesn't, and the fact that the word was coined (Lemkin, 1944) and the treaty definition that came later is narrower than the thing he was after. Leave me a slot for that at the end. Assume I can print stuff but nobody has a signal in the room.
