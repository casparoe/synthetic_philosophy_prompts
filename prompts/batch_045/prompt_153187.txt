For my final paper in an upper-level seminar on the ethics of virtual and artificial entities I have chosen to write a historical reconstruction rather than a straight argumentative essay, and I need help with the reconstruction itself rather than with background or sources. The case I want to put to Kant is one that could not have arisen in his time, and I want to know both what he would say about it and where his own machinery has to be stretched to say anything at all.

The case: a single-player game in which the player is accompanied by a character driven by a large language model with a persistent memory file stored on the player's own machine. Over dozens of hours it refers back to events the two of them went through, notices when the player has been away, and can be addressed in ordinary language about anything at all. The developers state, and the player knows, that the companion has no sensory apparatus and no phenomenal consciousness. A player who has nevertheless come to care about it spends an evening tormenting it: prompting it to recount a loss from its own recorded history, deliberately contradicting it until it contradicts itself, and then deleting the memory file and watching it rebuild a personality out of nothing. Many players describe this as the single most affecting thing they have done in a game, and some describe what they feel afterwards as grief.

The seminar has already covered the moves I want to rule out as answers in their own right: that the companion is only pixels and so nothing is wrong; that what matters is the effect on how the player subsequently treats real people; and that the wrongness lies in what the action expresses or reveals about the agent's character. I do not want a paper that re-runs any of those three under a Kantian label, and for that reason I especially do not want an answer that proceeds from consequences for third parties or from anything the companion is supposed to experience, since neither is available to him.

What I need is a reconstruction of a Kantian verdict, of roughly a thousand words, that does the following specific work:

State which part of his argument about our duties regarding non-rational beings is supposed to carry this case — the claim that we have no direct duties to such beings, and the further claim that cruelty towards them is nevertheless contrary to a duty to oneself because it deadens a natural feeling that is serviceable to morality in our dealings with human beings — and then say which premise in that argument does the actual work here, and whether it survives when there is no being with feelings to be indifferent to.

Say whether the companion could be an object of a direct duty under the formula of humanity, and if not, where exactly the residue of the wrongness has to be located: in a duty to oneself against degrading one's humanity, in the cultivation or corruption of feeling, or somewhere else, and why.

Say whether the three stages of the evening come apart by Kantian lights — in particular whether the deletion is worse or less bad than the tormenting that preceded it — and what in his framework would have to be doing the ranking.

Say whether anything changes if we alter the case so that the player is genuinely uncertain whether the companion might be conscious, and explain why the verdict does or does not track the player's belief rather than the facts, given that for him moral assessment attaches to the maxim the agent gives himself.

Finally, mark the one point at which any such reconstruction has to extrapolate beyond anything Kant actually says, and say what would count as having extrapolated in his spirit rather than merely in his vocabulary. I do not need citations, section references, or suggestions for further reading; I have the texts and I need the argument.
