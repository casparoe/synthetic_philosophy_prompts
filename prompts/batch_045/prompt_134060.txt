I would be grateful for your help with something I have been trying, without much success, to organise for my reading group.

I have no academic background. I spent close to thirty years in occupational health and safety, and for the last six years or so I have been reading philosophy on my own and running a small group at my local library. We recently gave two evenings to the question of whether whether someone knows something can depend on what is at stake for them, and to the related question of whether one speaker can truly say that Hanne knows while another speaker, in a different situation, truly says that she does not. Both evenings were a muddle in an instructive way: everyone agreed about what they would say in the cases, everyone disagreed about what it was they had thereby committed themselves to, and nobody could work out which of the named positions they actually held. Labels seemed to be doing no work at all. What I want from you is a decision tree that will do that work instead.

To be precise about the deliverable: I want a branching instrument, not a prose comparison and not a ranking. It should start from a commitment that every member of the group already shares and end at a named position, with the branch questions doing all the sorting.

The starting node should be something like this. Hanne checked the ferry timetable on her phone this morning; it is now mid-afternoon; she tells a friend that the last boat leaves at 18:40; if she is wrong the friend takes a taxi and arrives twenty minutes late. I take it that everyone in the room will say that Hanne knows. Begin there.

Constraints on the branch nodes:

1. Ten to twelve nodes, each a single question with two or three answer options.
2. No node may ask whether a label applies, and no node may use a term of art. Every node must be a concrete case, or a forced choice about a concrete case, that someone who has never heard of contextualism, invariantism, pragmatic encroachment or any of the rest could answer straight off. If a node needs to target a theoretical distinction, find the case in which that distinction shows up in what people are inclined to say.
3. Every answer option should be one a thoughtful person could actually give. No strawmen.
4. Please avoid the cases that have been done to death in the introductory material: no bank open on Saturday, no lottery ticket, no unfaithful spouse. My group has met all of them and has learned to answer them by rote, which defeats the purpose. Invent your own, or use the ones I set out below.
5. Between them, the nodes should vary the following, one or two per node: whether the high stakes are the subject's or a third party's; whether the subject knows the stakes are high; whether the stakes are raised by someone else's arbitrary bet or threat rather than by any fact about the world; whether what has changed is the size of the stakes or merely how noticeable some possibility of error has become; whether the stakes are prudential, financial, moral, or nothing more than curiosity; whether the subject will ever act on the belief at all or is merely being described; whether the person with the high stakes is the one making the attribution rather than the one whose knowledge is in question; and at least one case in which the evidence is very strong and the stakes are very high at the same time.

Four cases I would like built into the tree if you can make them work:

- Maya and Rob read the same departures board at the same moment and have exactly the same evidence about the 18:40 ferry. Maya is going to a birthday dinner on the island; if she misses it, the next boat is at 21:40 and she is merely late and mildly annoyed. Rob is a marine mechanic; the 18:40 is the last crossing tonight; if he misses it he cannot reach a vessel whose bilge alarm has been sounding since morning, and someone could be injured. Does Rob know that the ferry leaves at 18:40, and does Maya, and do they differ?
- Priya runs the calibration check on a hospital machine at 09:00, writes the result in the log, and has excellent evidence that it was calibrated. At 16:00, without her knowing, the machine's output is used to set a radiation dose for a patient. Did Priya know at 09:00 that the machine was calibrated?
- A stranger, as a joke, tells you that they will donate a large sum to a cause you find abhorrent if you are wrong about whether the museum is closed on Mondays. Nothing about your evidence has changed; only this stranger's whim is now riding on it. Do you know that the museum is closed on Mondays?
- At dinner, a guest mentions that the ferry company migrated its website last night and that caches have been known to serve out-of-date timetables. Nobody is relying on the timetable for anything. Has this remark, by itself, changed whether Hanne knows?

Leaves: eight to ten. Name each one. I would like at least the following to appear: classical or insensitive invariantism; contextualism of the attributor kind; subject-sensitive invariantism; interest-relative or pragmatic encroachment views; contrastivism; and relativism about knowledge attributions. If there is room, I would also like the tree to have somewhere for the position that ends in scepticism, and somewhere for the position that says there is no fact of the matter here beyond what we are doing when we attribute knowledge. Each leaf should carry one sentence of characterisation and one sentence on the most awkward commitment a person who lands there is stuck with.

Then, after the tree, an appendix that builds it from the logical space rather than from the list of names. Set out the distinctions that generate the space: whose practical situation is doing the work, the subject's or the attributor's or both or neither; whether it is the size of the stakes, the salience of a possibility of error, or the evidence itself that shifts things; whether what varies is whether the subject knows or only the standard under which the word applies in a given conversation; whether the link to practical factors runs through action, through assertion, or through belief; and whether first-person and third-person attributions answer to the same standard. Show which combinations of answers are available, place the named positions in the cells, and mark the cells nobody occupies, saying briefly in each case whether that is because the combination is incoherent, because it is merely unattractive, or because nobody has bothered to explore it.

Finally, please flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, and say whether some further case would separate them or whether, as far as you can tell, they differ only in theoretical taste. And flag two or three answer patterns that do not land cleanly on any leaf, with a note on what you would do with a person who produced one.

On practicalities: the group is eight people, including two retired engineers, a nurse, a schoolteacher and a former bank manager. Most of them have read some popular philosophy; none of them has studied it. Keep the questions in plain language and put any unavoidable jargon in a short glossary at the end. Please do not give me reading suggestions, bibliographies or an account of who argued for what and when: I can find that sort of thing myself, and I would rather the tree rested entirely on the cases. Four printed pages or thereabouts would be ideal.
