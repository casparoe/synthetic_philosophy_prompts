In my metaphysics reading group we went over concrete modal realism — Lewis's view, roughly, that every possible world is a real concrete universe, spatiotemporally cut off from ours, ours just one among them. Someone raised the obvious complaint: surely our world is the one that's real and the merely possible ones aren't, so the view is dead on arrival. And this guy just says "actual is an indexical, like here or now," as if that ends it. Nobody in the room could say what that was supposed to establish.

Can you lay out the distinct precise claims that one-liner could be making — a claim about the word itself, a claim that there's no absolute property of being actual, a claim about what you can or can't tell from inside a world, even a claim about whether our world's evils should matter to us more than another world's? Say which are platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, which are just false, and above all which reading would actually do the work he needed, i.e. answer the "but surely our world is the one that's real" complaint.

Two other things got thrown in that I couldnt sort out. (1) Someone said the indexical move is totally neutral: an actualist who thinks there's just one world and possible worlds are abstract representations of it can also say "actual" picks out the speaker's world, so the slogan does no work for modal realism at all. (2) Someone else said it's the same move as treating "now" and "I" as indexical, and stands or falls with the B-theory of time and perdurantism about persons. Is that analogy even a good one? It feels off to me for a reason I can't quite articulate: there's one space containing every place "here" could pick out, but on this view there's nothing containing all the worlds, so there's no space you could be lost in. I'd like a menu with verdicts, not just one reading, that I can take to next week's session.
