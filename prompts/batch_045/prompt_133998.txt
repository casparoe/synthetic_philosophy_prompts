I am in my final year of secondary school, and I am putting together a workshop for our economics-and-society club on one question: why do large groups of people end up making the same choice when the choice looks bad, and are all such cases really the same phenomenon? Everything I can find on the topic calls every instance of people copying each other "herding," which makes the word useless for my purposes. So I do not need an essay; I need a classification instrument.

Please build me a decision tree in plain nested text that starts from a single neutral observation and ends at a named mechanism. The observation to start from: a sequence of people each make a one-off choice between two options; each person can see what the people before them chose; each person also has some private hint about which option is better; and the group ends up nearly unanimous on the option that I privately suspect is worse. From there, every branch node should be a question that a club member who has never heard any of these terms could still answer about a concrete case — about what the choosers could see, about whether their reward depended on being seen as sensible by others, about whether they would say the same thing on an anonymous slip of paper, about whether they all faced the same piece of news at the same time — and never about what the mechanism is called or which theory is true.

Constraints on the instrument:

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first the questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then the actual mechanisms located in that space, with the unoccupied combinations noted and briefly diagnosed (is nobody there because the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored?). Where two leaves cannot be separated by your tree, say so explicitly instead of forcing a distinction, and tell me what extra observation would separate them (anonymous ballots, randomising the order of choosers, revealing people's reasons as well as their actions, and so on).

Size: eight to ten branch questions and eight to ten leaves.

Leaves that must appear, under whatever name you think is correct — I have invented labels where I do not know the standard one, so please rename them:

- observational learning from actions alone, where the queue locks in and private hints stop mattering (the thing usually called an information cascade);
- apparent uniformity with no social influence at all, because everyone was reacting to the same news or drawing from the same source (a common cause);
- genuine convergence, because people could see each other's reasons and not just their choices;
- copying driven by the fact that the action is simply better for me when more people do it as well;
- copying driven by wanting to look competent or to avoid standing out;
- public agreement that nobody privately believes, where people misjudge what everyone else really thinks;
- uniformity that is an artefact of who is still in the room (the dissenters left, stayed silent, or never showed up);
- herding about whether to herd, i.e. people deferring to their sense of what everyone else is deferring to.

Branch questions I think belong somewhere, which you should improve, reorder, or replace: could each person see the others' reasons or only their choices; does my payoff go up simply because others chose the same thing; would the fiftieth person choose differently if told the previous forty-nine choices were made at random; what would people say on an anonymous ballot; does one loud dissenter with a strong private hint break the run; would shuffling the order of the choosers change the outcome; do the choosers have different tastes from one another, or identical ones; can a private hint be arbitrarily strong or is it a coarse signal like a coin flip; did everyone move at once or in a fixed sequence; is there any reward for being first or for being pivotal, which would produce early contrarianism followed by late conformity. Please include the fragile-versus-robust distinction somewhere: I have been told that a cascade of the first kind should be easy to break with a small amount of public information, and that it should not really lock in at all when private hints can be arbitrarily strong. Put those in as branch questions if they are correct, and tell me plainly if either is wrong.

Please use school-level cases at the branch nodes rather than financial-market examples: two new options at the cafeteria and the queue that forms; signing up for one of two electives on a public sheet where everyone sees the running tally; an online poll whose results are hidden until you have voted; a sign-up sheet for a competitive training programme where being seen to try and fail is embarrassing.

Finally, apply the finished tree to this case, which is the one I actually care about, since our school has just adopted a new policy and I suspect some specific mechanism is at work: at a meeting of about thirty teachers, the head announces a new homework policy; three teachers are known to dislike it, but each of them can see that nobody else is objecting, and each also believes privately that the policy will fail for reasons they could each articulate; no one speaks; afterwards, in private, most of the staff say they thought it was a bad idea. Run this through your tree, say which leaf it lands on and which branch question decided it, name the one observation I could make at the next meeting that would most cleanly confirm or refute that classification, and note which other leaf your tree cannot rule out here.

Two housekeeping requests. First, do not give me a bibliography, reading list, or any citations: I have no library access over the holidays, so a list of papers and authors would be useless to me. If a position has a canonical label, give me the label only. Second, end your reply with one line in exactly this form, with nothing after it:

\answer{...}

where the braces contain nothing but the label of the leaf you assigned to the staff-meeting case, using your own label if you renamed mine.
