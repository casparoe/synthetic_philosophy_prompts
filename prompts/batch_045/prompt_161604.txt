ok so im prepping to teach the master argument next week and I'd rather argue with Berkeley than read one more commentary about him.

Please play George Berkeley — immaterialist, later bishop of Cloyne, as you were in the 1710s: quick, funny, combative, willing to grant small points (yes, the plain man has a real complaint; yes, 'idea' is doing an enormous amount of work in your system; yes, you need God to keep the furniture in place when nobody is in the room) but never that matter exists unperceived, and never that the master argument is a pun on the word conceive. You may use only concepts available to you in your own lifetime plus the few bits of modern vocabulary I hand you below; if you need to know something about the modern case, ask for it the way you'd press Hylas to be clearer. Stay in character until I say 'stop', and please dont step out of character to survey the secondary literature — I can read that myself.

Shape each of your turns like this: one short section called WHAT I GRANT, a single sentence, conceding the most you can about my last move; then the reply itself, called THE REPLY, roughly 150–200 words, in your own voice; then a section called YOUR MOVE with one question you want answered before we continue.

Modern vocabulary you get, and that's all you get: A PROPOSITIONAL SUPPOSITION is what someone does when they assume the antecedent of a conditional, or a hypothesis, in order to reason from it — taking something to be so in thought without forming any image of it. A SIMULATION is a machine that grinds through a long list of stored states according to rules somebody wrote; no one needs to watch it run, and the states can be written down, shelved, and read out again two hundred years later.

My opening move:

Your master argument says: try to conceive of the tree in the park existing unconceived, and the trying spoils it, because in the very attempt you conceive it. But that only bites if conceiving and perceiving are the same thing, and I think they aren't. Conceiving that P is supposing that P — propositional, not pictorial. I can suppose that there are regions of the universe nobody has ever observed without picturing any of them, and I can suppose that the stars are even in number without having any image of that either. And on your own principles, of course I can't have an IDEA of something that no one has an idea of; that isn't a discovery about trees, it's a remark about what an idea is. So the argument looks circular to me: it assumes that any thought about X must set X itself before the mind as an idea, and then reports in triumph that X is before the mind.

It gets worse for you. You yourself admit notions — a way of thinking about minds and relations that isn't an idea at all. If thought can reach beyond ideas when it comes to spirits, why not when it comes to matter? Hand me a notion of matter and I have everything I ever wanted from the wretched thing: something that isn't a mind, that constrains which ideas come to me, and that nobody has to be experiencing for it to be the case. And if your answer is that matter is impossible because it's inert and unthinking and so can't cause anything — fine, I'll help myself to a simulation instead. The rules are someone's, the states churn along with nobody watching, the states are as inert as anything in your system, and they can sit on a shelf for two centuries before anyone reads them out. Which step of the master argument is meant to stop that? And dont just tell me God is perceiving the states.
