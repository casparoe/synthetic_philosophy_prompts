Hey — I'm in a political theory reading group (mostly philosophy grad students plus one econ guy who keeps derailing us) and we've gotten stuck on the Condorcet jury theorem, and it's definately changed how I read the whole epistemic-democracy literature. Here's where I ended up, and I'd like someone to just tell me which of us is wrong.

My understanding is that the theorem says: if every voter is individually more than 50% likely to get the right answer and the votes are independent, the majority's chance of being right goes to 1 as you add voters. My friend reads that as "always make the group as big as possible." But when I ran the numbers on a toy case I got something weird: three reviewers who are each 90% reliable get the right answer 97.2% of the time by majority; add two more reviewers who are each only 51% reliable and the five-person majority drops to about 92%. So "bigger is better" looks false to me, even though everyone added is still above chance.

The bigger disagreement is about what "independent" is even doing in there. I built a little model I've been calling the briefing-memo setup: before voting, everyone reads the same one-page memo, which points at the correct answer 80% of the time; each person votes with the memo unless their own private hunch (right 60% of the time, independent across people) says otherwise. Each person then ends up 60% reliable overall — still above chance — and since the hunches are independent, I figured the noise washes out and the majority still converges on the truth, just more slowly. My friend says any shared input like the memo breaks the theorem entirely and the majority gets stuck below certainty no matter how many people you pile on. That seems too strong: surely a memo that is itself right 80% of the time can only help? Relatedly, if independence is really required without qualification, doesn't the theorem end up implying that juries shouldn't deliberate at all, which is nuts? I've spent alot of time on this and can't see who's making the mistake.

So: who's right, does the memo case converge to 1 or plateau, and if it plateaus, at what number? And does my 97.2 → 92 calculation actually contradict the theorem, or have I misread what the theorem promises? Please lead with the bottom line and keep the whole answer under 300 words, in American English.
