my reading group has me and another guy who've been arguing about revealed preference for like two years, and we finally agreed to stop and write a joint statement, so draft it. He's the hardline behaviorist side: preferences just are stable choice dispositions, utility is only a bookkeeping index that lets you summarize choice data, and asking for anything more is asking for something empty. I'm on the mentalist/welfarist side: utility is supposed to name something real about how strongly someone wants a thing or how good an outcome is for them, and observed choice is evidence of that, but noisy and defeasible evidence.

Two things to use, both made up but specified so you can actually reason about them:

- The intensity probe. Instead of making someone choose, you ask them to compare outcomes directly: how many units of one outcome would feel exactly as good to them as one unit of another, and does A feel more than twice as good as B. Stipulate that within a person these answers are stable across sessions, transitive, don't shift with what else is on offer, and predict that person's later choices better than their earlier choices predict each other.
- Stable cycling under full feedback. A person, over hundreds of trials with full feedback, no time pressure and real stakes, reliably takes A over B, B over C, and C over A. When you show them the cycle they don't correct it, and they say that's genuinely how each comparison feels to them in the moment.

Also here's the policy case we keep coming back to: a city decides whether to cut a bus route using consumer surplus computed from revealed preference, and the intensity probe says the riders' losses are about three times bigger than that number.
