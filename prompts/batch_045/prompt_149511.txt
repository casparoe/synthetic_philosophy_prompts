Write a short story — 1,200 to 1,800 words, close third person, past tense, in plain and slightly dry prose — set in a country where acts that go beyond what anyone was required to do are handled by a public body called the Registry of Unclaimed Deeds.

The Registry exists to certify one thing: that a deed was *unclaimed*. Nobody owed it. Certification has two legal effects. First, a certified deed is statutorily non-precedential: it may never be cited in any later determination of what a reasonable person, or the holder of a given job, was required to do. This rule was written to stop the ratchet by which a single rescue quietly converts an optional act into an expected one for everyone afterwards. Second, the state indemnifies the certified person's losses — medical costs, lost wages, the ruined boat. In the thirty years the Registry has operated, the second effect has quietly undone the first: because indemnity claims must be assessed, assessors have had to ask whether the claimant acted with the competence of someone who had taken the rescue on, and that competence standard has hardened in practice into something like a professional one. The doctrine says the deed cannot bind anyone; the paperwork says otherwise.

Your protagonist is a senior assessor in her early fifties. At twenty-three she waded into a flooded road underpass and got two people out; a third drowned. Her fiancé at the time stood on the railing above with a coiled rope and did not go in. She has not spoken to him since, and she has never filed her own deed. Filing it now would formally record that he owed nothing that night.

The case on her desk concerns a nineteen-year-old deckhand who went back into a burning engine room on a harbour ferry to close a fuel line, and who says in his statement that he never decided anything: he knew the procedure and nobody else aboard did, so there was nothing to decide. The ferry operator's insurer argues the act fell within his role and is therefore not certifiable. His union argues it was unclaimed, because certification brings the larger state indemnity and the medal. Both sides are arguing about money.

Dramatize, without ever resolving, three things. Whether a role — a deckhand, a firefighter, a nurse — can have duties that swallow what would otherwise be beyond duty, or whether there is always something left over that the role does not require. Whether an act can be unclaimed if the person doing it sincerely believed, at the time, that it was simply what was being asked of him. And whether an act can survive being officially recognised as optional, given that recognition brings compensation, a standard, and eventually an expectation.

The story should end on the decision your protagonist makes about her own thirty-year-old file, and it should not tell the reader whether she was right. No character should use the vocabulary of moral philosophy; the institution's own words — unclaimed, certified, owed, the standard — should carry all of it.
