I write a short essay series that takes one recent film as the way into a position in metaphysics, and I want to use *Everything Everywhere All at Once* as the way into modal realism. You can assume I know the Lewisian machinery — concrete worlds, causal and spatiotemporal isolation, the indexicality of actuality, counterpart theory in place of transworld identity, the principle of recombination — and that I know the standard objections to it. I don't want a piece that is a paragraph of modal realism 101 with clips stapled to it; I want a reading that begins where that leaves off. Three things, in order of how much I care about them.

First, a classification problem. On Lewis's own criterion a world is a maximal spatiotemporally and causally interrelated whole, and worlds are isolated from one another. But verse-jumping is interaction across universes: people travel between them, carry information, are injured and killed in them. If that interaction is causal, then the film's universes are not isolated, and so they are not a plurality of worlds at all but regions of one large, gerrymandered world — the verdict that the many worlds of cosmological and quantum speculation are usually said to receive. Is that the right verdict about the film, and can it be resisted? I can think of two escapes: treat the jumps as relations that are not causal (brute trans-world relations of the sort that modal realism arguably already needs for similarity among worlds), or treat verse-jumping as not travel but possession, so that what crosses over is a mind and not a body. Say which, if either, is defensible, what it costs, and — this matters most for the piece — what the film has left to say about modality if its multiverse turns out to be a single scattered world.

Second, a term I want to try out, and I want you to tell me whether it earns its keep. Call it *de se leakage*: a character treats the doings of a counterpart as belonging to her own first-personal deliberative situation — envying, regretting, taking credit for, or taking consolation in what someone very like her does elsewhere — when counterpart theory says that that person is simply not her. Plenty of leakage is available in the film: the alphaverse Evelyn's relation to the Evelyn who became a singer rather than a laundromat owner, Jobu Tupaki's conviction that because everything happens somewhere nothing she does here can matter, and, on a kinder reading, Waymond's speech in the laundromat about choosing kindness and his remark that in another life he would have wanted nothing more than laundry and taxes with her. My question is whether the film's resolution requires de se leakage. There is a familiar Lewisian reply to the complaint that if every possibility is somewhere realized then deliberation collapses: that deliberation is about what *I* do, not about what my counterparts do, and that the fact that other people elsewhere do other things does not answer my question. If that reply holds, Waymond's scene dramatizes it and the film vindicates Lewis; if the scene only lands because the audience has quietly slipped back into transworld identity, then the film secretly rejects counterpart theory, and the piece has to say so. Which is it, and which scenes would you point to as the evidence?

Third, two worries I want the essay to be honest about rather than dodge. (a) Is Lewis the best fit at all? Given how much of the film depends on other Evelyns being reachable, inspectable, and emotionally continuous with ours, some other position — an ersatz or abundant variety of modal realism, or a branching, actualist picture — may track its actual commitments better. Say which theory the film's commitments really favour, even if it is the less famous one. (b) Where does the reading break? The everything bagel is a joke aboutnihilism that is mostly not a modal thesis; verse-jumping is genre machinery; and the story is, in the end, about an audit, a laundromat, some divorce papers, and a mother and daughter. I don't want to over-intellectualize it, but I also don't want the usual escape hatch of saying the philosophy is only decoration. Tell me which scenes genuinely bear metaphysical weight and which I should leave alone, and why.

What I want at the end is a verdict on what the film adds to the debate that Lewis's own prose does not: something, I suspect, about the lived phenomenology of modal abundance — what it is like, from the inside, to occupy a world in which every live option of yours is somebody's actuality — that the argumentative literature can only gesture at.
