ok so I've been trying to write down where I've actually landed on epistemic injustice — I sit on a clinical ethics committee and we keep hitting versions of this in real intake and triage decisions, so it's not idle for me — and I want someone to tell me whether the package holds together. Here it is, roughly in the order I believe it rather than in order of how sure I am:

1. Testimonial injustice is a wrong to the speaker specifically in her capacity as a knower/informer. It isn't just unfairness or disrespect that happens to have epistemic side-effects; the 'epistemic' is doing real work, and I don't want it reduced to a distributive complaint about who gets believed how much.
2. The wrong is intrinsic to the exchange. It's fully present even if the speaker never finds out and nothing bad downstream follows — no setback, no lost opportunity, nothing.
3. Nobody ever owes it to anyone to believe them; belief isn't the kind of thing you can owe. So what a hearer owes is: don't let identity prejudice do the work in forming your credibility judgement.
4. Credibility excess is as much a failure as credibility deficit and is the same basic phenomenon — the smooth, over-credited guy in the meeting is part of this topic, not a different one.
5. It can be done by a hearer that isn't an agent at all: an intake or triage model that weights self-reported symptoms by demographic priors seems to me to be a perfectly good candidate for committing it. Nothing in the account should require a person with character traits on the receiving end.
6. A case verdict I'm attached to and don't want to give up: a clinician discounted a Black patient's pain report, and I want to say testimonial injustice, and that the clinician is blameworthy. But when we went through the case, the lowered credence was fully accounted for by a statistical generalisation that was accurate-in-the-circumstances about how pain gets recorded in that unit. If you 'subtract' the prejudice, the credence would have landed in exactly the same place. I still want to say injustice, and I don't think "well the generalisation is itself downstream of injustice" is enough to settle it.
7. Hermeneutical injustice is the same genus: a gap in the shared concepts available for making sense of your own experience is an injustice even when there's no individual who could have done otherwise.
8. In practice every case I've seen also involves an ordinary moral wrong — disrespect, discrimination, just plain bad treatment — and I don't think that's a coincidence. They travel together.

What I want is an audit, not a tour of the literature. Go through these and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against each other, which only look like they do, and for each real tension what the cheapest fix is and what that fix would cost me elsewhere in the set. Rank the tensions by severity — I'd much rather hear "this one is fatal, that one's cosmetic" than get eight equal-sized worries — and then tell me whether there's a single revision that cleans up the most. Where a tension turns on an ambiguity, say so explicitly instead of quietly picking a side; the one I keep circling is whether the wrong lives in the level of the credence or in its causal history. Also: put a rough confidence on each tension you flag, and single out the one claim in your answer you're least sure about. I'd rather know where you're guessing.
