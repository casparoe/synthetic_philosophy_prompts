"Winner's classic claim is that the things we build are never neutral. His central example is Robert Moses, who had the overpasses on the parkways out to Jones Beach built too low for buses to pass under, which kept poor and Black New Yorkers — who didn't own cars — off the beach. The politics here is not in anyone's intentions; it is in the concrete, and it operates whether or not Moses meant it to. Winner calls artifacts like this inherently political technologies, and what he means is that the exclusion is a property of the object itself, so no change in law, funding, or personnel can undo it — you would have to tear the bridges down. His second example, the mechanical tomato harvester developed at UC Davis in the 1940s, works the same way: breeding a firmer tomato settled in hardware a fight between growers and farmworkers that the growers had been losing in politics. So Winner is a technological determinist, though a sophisticated one: he is updating Marx's remark about hand mills giving you feudal lords, and the real claim is that whoever controls the machinery controls the society. The point generalizes, and it is now standard in the second-wave literature as the unwinding asymmetry: it is far easier to build a political arrangement into infrastructure than to build it out again, because once an arrangement is in steel or in code it stops looking like a decision anyone made. This is why the low overpass affords exclusion the way a chair affords sitting, and why the later social-constructivist literature, which holds that artifacts are neutral until users give them meaning, more or less settled the debate: artifacts have no intentions, only designers and users do, and the same bridge would be politically inert under a different city government."
