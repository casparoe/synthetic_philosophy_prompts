Analyze the cold fusion episode of 1989 as a case study in inference to the best explanation, and be explicit about where the framework does real work and where it falls silent.

Take the following as the stipulated facts, and do not rest anything on details beyond them. In March 1989 two electrochemists, Martin Fleischmann and Stanley Pons, announced at the University of Utah that their bench-top electrolysis cells, with a palladium cathode in heavy water, produced more heat than any chemical process they could account for, together with small amounts of what they took to be nuclear byproducts. Within weeks laboratories around the world attempted replication; most failed to find the heat, a minority reported confirming signals including occasional nuclear ones. A federal advisory panel convened that year judged the evidence unpersuasive. The quantitative crux, as it was understood at the time: if the heat was nuclear in origin at the rate implied, it should have been accompanied by neutron, tritium and gamma production far above what anyone measured, at levels that would have exposed the experimenters themselves; the nuclear products actually reported fell short of the claimed heat by a wide margin. A small research community nevertheless continued under names such as low-energy nuclear reactions and condensed matter nuclear science.

What I want, first, is a disciplined mapping. Write out the candidate explanations actually on the table in 1989 — conventional fusion in the metal lattice, some previously unrecognised process that produces heat without the expected radiation, an unmodelled chemical or materials effect, calorimetric or instrumental error, contamination, error or fabrication — and say, for each, which part of the evidence it explained and which part it left dangling. Frame the question contrastively, as explanationists recommend, and keep the distinction between the loveliest explanation and the likeliest one in view, since the two came apart here. Then say what each candidate's treatment of the missing radiation is: an anomaly to be explained away, or a falsifier.

Second, I want the reverse direction, and here are the distinctions I care about. Distinguish two jobs that background theory can do in an inference to the best explanation: filtering which candidates are even admitted to the competition, and weighting candidates once admitted. Ask which job the expectations of nuclear physics actually performed in this episode, and whether that role was legitimate or merely conservative. Then adjudicate among three verdicts the episode might support: that inference to the best explanation performed exactly as it should, since dismissing an extraordinary claim is what "best" means once priors are taken seriously; that inference to the best explanation was not the operative epistemic engine at all, because replication, calibration and institutional incentives did the real work and the explanationist vocabulary merely rationalised a verdict reached otherwise; or that the bad-lot objection bites here, because the true accounts of the positive reports — including accounts nobody proposed in 1989 — were never in the candidate set, so that "best of a bad lot" carried little information. Say which of the three the episode supports, and what each would imply for whether a modern funder should entertain work in low-energy nuclear reactions at all. Along the way, handle one asymmetry honestly: inference to the best explanation is normally stated as inference from positive evidence, yet what settled this episode was largely other people's failures to reproduce it, so tell me whether the framework has any disciplined way to aggregate non-replications across laboratories or whether it is simply silent there. Finally, name the observation that would have made an explanationist account of the episode and a sociological one diverge, and say whether it ever occurred — and, if the framework cannot supply an answer to any part of this, say so rather than stretching it.
