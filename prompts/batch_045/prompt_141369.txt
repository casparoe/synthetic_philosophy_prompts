hey — a question about a practice rather than a text, and I want the philosophy that's smuggled in by the practice, not a verdict on whether the practice is nice.

Most rich-world governments run a public no-concession policy on hostage-taking: they announce in advance that they will never pay ransoms or release prisoners for hostages, several of them have made paying up a criminal offence rather than merely a dumb idea, and every time somebody gets taken they repeat the formula out loud before anyone has even made a demand. And yet the same governments quietly arrange payments through intermediaries, do prisoner swaps, and carve out exceptions for rescues and humanitarian pauses — the stated rule and the actual behaviour come apart in ways that everybody, including the takers, can see. Don't spend your answer on the gossip; I want the implicit theory behind the *public* rule.

Two things I can't get straight.

First, the self-binding. The rule is a present agent trying to handcuff its own future self, and it seems to need both that this is possible and that it's sometimes rational — that a commitment is worth *more* the harder it is to back out of, which is a strange thing for a theory of rationality to say, since it makes it rational to arrange to be unable to do what you'll later have most reason to do. Reconstruct the theory of action and practical reason doing the work. Which bits does the practice strictly presuppose, and which does it merely sit well with? And is there a thinner story that licenses the same public rule without genuine self-binding at all — something about signalling, or about a state being a different kind of agent from a person?

Second, the race. I'll coin a term: call it a **commitment race** — not a race in speed but in *hardness*, where each side gains by being the one whose position is more costly to reverse, so both keep ratcheting up how irreversible they are. Here the takers escalate because a soft commitment invites a harder one, and the state escalates by writing the rule into law, saying it on the record, criminalising payment; the two of them can end up somewhere nobody on the outside would have picked. Does the practice as it actually exists presuppose that this ratchet has a stopping point, or that being the harder one always wins? And if the implicit theory says the ratchet runs away, does that refute the rule or just make it tragic — is the policy definately committed to something false about itself?

Last bit: what would a policy built on the opposite view look like? If you thought a government genuinely can't bind its future self, or that a commitment only counts when you could still break it, what would you actually do on the morning somebody is taken?

Please write in American English, keep it to about a page, be explicit about how confident you are in each main claim, and single out the one point you're least sure about and say why. And Dont just tell me "it's deterrence" — I can get that off a bumper sticker.
