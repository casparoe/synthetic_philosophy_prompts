Hi — I help run a small forecasting group (about twenty of us, mostly ML-adjacent, definitely not philosophers) and we've got a recurring argument about how fast AI takeoff will be. Every time we try to resolve it, it turns out that when people say 'fast takeoff' they mean about four different things, and the argument is really four arguments talking past each other. So I want to build a decision tree we can stick on the group wiki that sorts people by what they actually think will happen, rather than by which blog post they happened to read first.

What I'd like from you:

A taxonomy / decision tree with exactly eight diagnostic questions and eight to ten leaves. Every branch node should be labelled with the claim that does the separating, not with the name of a view — I want someone who has never read anything about AI to be able to walk the tree by answering the questions.

The questions should be concrete dated scenarios, not abstract slogans. Some I definitely want in there, in some form:

- It's 2032. One lab's systems now do essentially all of the ML research that lab does internally, but the outside world looks basically unchanged, GDP growth is 2.4%, and nobody has heard of the lab. Is that an intelligence explosion already underway, or not yet?
- A system is roughly ten times better than the best human at writing and reviewing research code, and at human level or below at basically everything else, including long-horizon planning and persuasion.
- Three labs are within about three months of each other on the same capability milestones, and none of them can verify what the others have.
- Capabilities on your favourite benchmark double every four months for two years, but almost none of it gets deployed, because of liability rules and because nobody can tell whether the outputs are right.

The distinctions I care most about, and which I want the branches to actually track (add ones I've missed, but these should be load-bearing):

- capability vs power: is what's accelerating the thing systems can do, or what their operators get to convert it into?
- what the acceleration is in: the amount of cognitive labour available, versus the returns to a fixed unit of it — and separately, whether what changes is the *rate* of improvement or the *level* jumped to once.
- what the feedback loop runs through: the system automating the research that improves it, versus money → compute → data → slightly better systems, versus something market-like or evolution-like where no single object is doing the improving.
- local vs global: one organisation, or an economy-wide pattern.
- calendar time vs doublings: a takeoff that takes nine months and one that takes six years might be the same number of doublings of effective cognitive labour, and I want the tree to be able to say whether that difference matters for the leaf someone lands on.
- legibility: whether the people living through it could tell, from macro data or from their own jobs, that it was happening.
- a slightly odd one I thought of: does the process require the improving system to have any model of its own improvement loop, or can it be completely blind to it? I suspect this separates more views than people realise.

Leaves must include, named or described: the recursive-self-improvement/'hard takeoff' cluster; the fast-but-continuous ramp cluster; the slow, economically-diffused, institutionally-dragged cluster; the 'there is no single object, capabilities arrive as a pile of services and there's no sharp moment' cluster; a plateau / capabilities-saturate leaf; a multipolar many-projects-at-once leaf; and a 'narrow path' leaf where power shifts fast without any general capability explosion.

Two more things. First, please build the space before you place the views: lay out the questions, say how many combinations of answers they allow, then locate the actual positions in that space, and mark the unoccupied regions — with a one-line diagnosis each for why nobody lives there (incoherent? just unattractive? merely unexplored?). Second, flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree can't really separate, rather than quietly forcing a question that pretends to split them.

Each leaf should also carry one sentence on its most awkward commitment — the thing a holder of that view has to swallow.

Please don't give me a literature review: no 'as X argued in Y', no citations, no timelines of who said what first. I don't care about priority, and half our group has no idea who said any of it anyway. Just the instrument.
