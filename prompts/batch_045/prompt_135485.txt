Sorry if this is a basic question — I read history for fun, not philosophy. My mate and I keep having the same argument about Malthus and neither of us is shifting.

His line is that Malthus was basicly "right but early": population pressing against subsistence is what most of human history actually looked like, and the fact that Britain and then everyone else scraped out of it doesn't make him wrong. My line is that this is pure get-out-of-jail. If any failed doomsday forecast can just be relabelled "early", then no long-run prediction is ever falsified, and the whole exercise is pseudoscience. Popper and all that.

So I've tried to build a fair test instead of us just trading slogans. I call it the off-switch test: a long-run prediction counts as rescued rather than refuted only if the thing that stopped it coming true was a factor the theory itself named in advance. Malthus fails it flat — what actually broke the trap was sustained growth in yields, and then fertility falling as people got richer, and he named neither. He just didn't see it coming, and "wait longer" isn't a prediction. My mate says that's definately unfair: the escape was precisely the kind of thing that couldn't have been named in advance, so failing to name it can't count against him.

Who's making the mistake here, and is there anything worth saving in my test? I doubt I'm the first person to want a rule for telling "right but early" apart from "wrong and wriggling", I just don't know the vocabulary for it.
