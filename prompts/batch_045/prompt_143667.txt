I got into an argument with a friend a couple weeks ago about something from an old Plato dialogue — the bit where someone asks why knowledge is worth more than mere true belief, using the example of two people heading to the same town, one of whom actually knows the road and one of whom just has a correct hunch about it. I should say up front that I've read basically no philosophy since school. I plan delivery routes for a regional parcel company, so I sort of live inside that example all day, and to me the answer seemed obvious: knowledge is just true belief that came from a reliable process, and a reliable process gets the van there more often. So knowledge is better in exactly the way good routing data is better than a lucky guess, and the size of the difference should be something you can actually measure. Like, if one driver's route came from our software and another driver's came from a coworker's hunch that happened to be right, then across a hundred runs the software driver arrives more often, and there's some exchange rate — extra expected minutes saved per run, or whatever — that tells you how much more a reliably produced true belief is worth. I even started trying to work out that number for our fleet, to settle whether paying for a data upgrade is worth it.

But my friend keeps saying I've changed the subject: he says the question is why knowledge is better than *true belief*, not better than guessing, and that once the hunch is true you arrive either way, so reliability can't be doing the work I think it's doing. That just sounds like giving up on the example to me. Then somebody in the comments on a forum said my whole approach gets "swamped," which I definately didn't follow — surely more reliability is always worth something? So: is the exchange-rate idea the right way to think about this, and is there a formula I could actually use, or is my friend right that I'm answering a different question? And if he is right, what question am I supposed to be answering instead?
