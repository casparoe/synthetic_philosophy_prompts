Hey, semi-random question that's been eating my hobby time. I'm hand-drawing a lowercase typeface in my spare time — third attempt, and I keep restarting at 'a' — and it's made me realize I have no idea what I'm even trying to hold fixed when I redraw it. Every 'a' is an 'a', obvously, but the 'a' in a geometric sans, in a humanist serif, in my own handwriting, in a Blackletter, and the single-story 'ɑ' share almost nothing you can point at, while my 'a' and my 'd' (same bowl, taller stem) or my 'a' and my 'o' share a great deal. I went looking for help and bumped into the old Letter Spirit project — Hofstadter's group, I think, trying to model how the 26 lowercase letters get rendered in many internally coherent styles, using deliberately simplified schematic letterforms — and the way I'd now put the puzzle is that a glyph varies along two more or less independent axes: which letter it is, and which style the whole font is in. Sameness of letter is then invariance along the style axis. But what decides which features sit on which axis? Nothing intrinsic to the marks seems to: terminal angle, stroke contrast, bowl size are pure style in one comparison and identity-constitutive in another, and somewhere out there is a font whose 'a' looks like another font's 'o', and fonts where stem height is doing the letter-differentiating work between 'a' and 'd'.

So here's the case I invented to make it actually hurt, and it's what I want most of your answer spent on. Call it the uniform-grid script: an invented writing system whose 26 glyphs are produced by sweeping one or two continuous parameters — stroke curvature, bowl closure, stem height, pick your own — across a very fine grid, so evenly that the visual difference between glyph #13 and glyph #14 is exactly as small as the difference between two fonts' versions of the same letter. Cross-letter distance and cross-style distance are now the same size and there is no clustering left to appeal to. So: does the glyph halfway between #13 and #14 have a determinate letter-identity at all, or is that simply a place where the question lapses? If it lapses, does that show 'a'-ness was never more than the name of a cluster in a similarity space, so that flattening the space destroys the category rather than just making it hard to apply? Or is there something else — use by a community that can be surprised or corrected, historical descent from earlier letterforms, the functional role a glyph plays in a system of contrasts — that would still pin identity down in the uniform-grid script, and pin it down differently depending on which of those you pick?

Please don't give me the family-resemblance one-liner or an "it's just convention", and don't just tell me the axes are orthogonal. Work the concrete stuff: (i) my 'a' vs 'd' vs 'o' vs 'ɑ', and which of those differences are letter-differences vs style-differences given that some real font makes the opposite call; (ii) the glyph halfway between 'c' and 'e', i.e. one added crossbar — sloppy 'c', broken 'e', or genuinely neither, and what would settle it; (iii) whether anything changes if nobody reads the script and it's just marks in a drawer, versus there being users who can be pulled up short by a misshapen glyph; (iv) if 'a'-ness really is a cluster, what exactly is the metric and who or what is doing the measuring. I guess the underlying thing I want stated plainly: what would have to be true of a pile of marks for "every 'a' is an 'a'" to say something, instead of being true just because we've decided to call all of them 'a'?
