Next week I'm taking over one 75-minute session of a friend's philosophy of biology seminar — about a dozen second-years, whiteboard only, no slides, and they haven't read anything for it (I'd rather not put author names on the board and attach them at the end, so the plan has to work with names withheld). Roughly half are bio majors who've had natural selection but seem to think of it as survival of the fittest in a vaguely optimizing way; the other half are philosophy majors who hear "function" as "purpose" and have done a bit on intentionality. I want them to leave holding the three-way contrast between the selected-effect story, the causal-role story (what the item contributes to some containing system's capacity), and the forward-looking story (what it would do for fitness down the line). That's my working gloss — tell me if the gloss itself is what's going to mislead them.

Don't explain the debate to me; plan how I teach it. Concretely, I need the dependency structure: what has to be in place before each piece can even be stated, and why. I was going to open with a stenosed heart valve, on the grounds that "this heart isn't doing what it's supposed to do" is the datum every account has to earn — but does opening there quietly tilt the room toward the selected-effect view before the other two are even on the table? Then: which single case should carry the hour? My candidate is a plastic-chewing enzyme produced by directed evolution in a lab and then sprayed on a landfill — it has a real selection history, but no organism, no fitness, no ancestors, and its unclear what the containing system is supposed to be. Does that discriminate the three accounts cleanly, or does it drag in artifacts and experimenters' intentions way too early? Either way, tell me what the carrying case has to have and what it has to lack.

Misconceptions I'm braced for: that "selected for" just means "useful right now"; that the selected-effect view smuggles in a designer; that the causal-role view makes any old effect a function (heart sounds, the thump a valve makes); that vestigial organs refute the selected-effect view; and the big one, that the whole dispute is merely verbal and you can mean whatever you like by "function". Which of these will actually form first and which am I inventing, where in the sequence should I head each off, and is the "its just verbal" one something to defuse or to partly concede at minute 60? The bio majors will also ask, obv, what any of this has to do with that junk-DNA fight about how much of the genome is really functional — where does that fit? And I want one question for the last five minutes that they can't answer with "it depends what you mean by function". Finally, what should I openly refuse to explain at this level — units of selection, the disease-as-dysfunction literature, the link to mental content — and say out loud that I'm refusing?
