I'm a retired experimental plasma physicist — spent most of my career on a tokamak, of all things — and since I packed it in I've been going to a philosophy of history reading group at the local university, which I'm enjoying way more then I expected. The thing that set me off is this business about contrastive explanation. As I've understood it from the reading: a why-question in history isn't really "why P?" but "why P rather than Q?", where Q is the contrast class; the answer is supposed to be a difference-maker, something that actually differs between the actual case and the contrast case; and the answer is definately supposed to be sensitive to how you fix Q, so that "why Britain and not China?" and "why Britain and not the Netherlands?" can come out with different, non-mergeable lists of causes. I've also started to suspect — this bit is my own, I haven't seen it in the reading, so tell me if it's daft — that contrastive questions quietly rely on what I'd call the shared-aim assumption: they only make sense if the contrast case was also in the running for P, or at least plausibly trying for it. If it wasn't, then the question may have no answer of the sort people are fishing for, which is my standing worry about the whole Needham-question industry.

So: analyze the moon race through all this. Take the following as given, I'm not asking you to check my history: the US landed men on the Moon in July 1969; the Soviets had Sputnik and Gagarin first and were generally reckoned to be ahead until about 1962; Kennedy committed the US to a landing before the decade was out in 1961; the Soviet side only formally approved a manned landing program in the mid-1960s, years later, and then split the work between rival design bureaus; Korolev died in 1966; the N1 failed on all four of its test launches between 1969 and 1972; the USSR was running a substantially smaller economy than the US and Apollo ate a few percent of the federal budget at its peak (don't hold me to the numbers); and the Soviets ran a very successful robotic lunar program on the side, including automated sample return by 1970, and denied for years afterwards that they had ever been in a race at all.

What I want is a disciplined mapping, not a list of causes. Set Q = "the USSR" and give me the candidate difference-makers, with an honest note about which ones you'd actually stake money on and how confident you'd be in each. Then switch the contrast: Q = "the US, but in 1979 or 1981 instead of 1969" (why then and not a decade later), and Q = "nobody, ever" (why a landing at all rather than none). Which of the earlier difference-makers survive those switches, which turn out to be artifacts of the USA-versus-USSR framing, and can the three answers be added together into one story about "why America in 1969", or does the aggregation fail in an interesting way? Then the bit I care most about: where does the contrastive machinery break here? My hunch is that the shared-aim assumption is doing a lot of quiet work — a big chunk of "why not the USSR" threatens to collapse into "they never really decided to" — and if that's right the question starts to look like it was never well posed, in the same way "why Britain and not China?" wouldn't be if China wasn't trying to do the thing Britain did. Tell me whether that hunch is right, and if you think the parallel to the great divergence debate is bogus, say exactly where it breaks. And in the other direction: does this case leave you trusting contrastive explanation more or less as a tool for historians, and where is it silent rather than merely hard to apply?
