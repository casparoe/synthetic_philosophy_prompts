Hi — I need help drafting a memo and I'm kind of drowning. Context: I'm a second-year cog sci PhD student and I somehow ended up on the steering committee of a small open evaluation suite for visual reasoning (six members, a Slack that's 90% meme threads, and a release date in six weeks). We have to decide on one task submission and I'm the one stuck writing the recommendation memo.

The submission is a Bongard-style few-shot concept-induction task: you get twelve figures — six on the left that share a rule, six on the right that violate it — and then you have to say whether a new figure follows the rule. The intended rule here is about containment: every left-hand figure has one shape sitting entirely inside another (triangle in a circle, square in a pentagon, circle in a circle, two nested rectangles), while every right-hand figure has shapes that are either fully disjoint or only partially overlapping, with nothing contained in anything. We all liked it. Then one reviewer ran a three-line baseline that just measures average ink density per image and got 98%: the generator apparently has two rendering branches, and the positive branch also draws with a thinner stroke, and because of how the layout code samples sizes the positives also wierdly tend to have a slightly smaller bounding box. So a system can ace the task without representing containment at all.

Now the committee is split and I have to recommend something. Our ML person says: fine, just regenerate the item pool with the correlates flipped or randomized and score on held-out items, so anything relying on stroke weight fails. Our vision chair says that fixes nothing: the general worry is that no finite set of examples pins down a rule — there are always grue-style alternatives that fit all the data and diverge on the next case — and "randomize the confounds we happened to notice" only works because we already decided which features are projectible, i.e. we smuggled in the answer, and by that logic the whole few-shot format is broken rather than just this task. Someone else floated a different scoring rule: don't score the model's label on the held-out image at all; score whether the model can state a rule in words that a naive human annotator can then apply to fresh figures and get right. I have a nagging suspicion that this just tests verbalization, which current systems are very good at, rather than whether the system has got the concept, but I can't get the worry into focus. Complicating everything: our rubric says accepted tasks must be "solvable by an untrained human in under two minutes," and it occurs to me that a human staring at these twelve figures faces exactly the same underdetermination and just picks the rule that feels natural — so that criterion might already presuppose whatever we were trying to test. Our existing rubric language ("requires abstract reasoning") is useless and I'd like the memo to actually say what our criterion should be.

So: please write the memo I'll circulate, around 800 words, addressed to the committee, recommending one of: accept as submitted, reject, or accept with specified conditions. It should be something we could actually vote on, and it should include a short criteria section saying what we are testing for when we claim a task measures induction of a concept from a few examples rather than surface pattern-matching. Please treat the underdetermination objection seriously and give the strongest version of whichever position you end up rejecting — I don't want the losing side to feel strawmanned, especially not the vision chair, who will absolutely push back in the meeting. No bibliography or citations please, we can't check them and none of us will read them anyway. End the document with exactly one final line: \answer{accept}, \answer{accept with conditions}, or \answer{reject}.
