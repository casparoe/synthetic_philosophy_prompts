I work on model safety at a mid-sized lab, and on Thursday I have twenty minutes in front of our Frontier Model Release Board to argue for reallocating next quarter's safety budget. My background is in reliability engineering — years spent on failure detection in industrial control systems before I moved into ML — and that shapes how I want to put the case; a colleague of mine calls the view I'm defending 'alignment as pharmacovigilance'. Right now roughly a third of our effort goes into expanding red-team prompt suites and a third into interpretability tooling for release review, and I want to argue that both are being asked to do a job they cannot do. The money should go instead into what I've been calling drift-and-blast accounting: for each deployment class, estimate how long a harmful behavioural drift would run before anyone noticed, and how much damage accrues in that window, and then judge every alignment technique we use by how far it shortens the first number or shrinks the second. My underlying claim is that for systems of roughly today's generation — shaped by reinforcement learning from human feedback, tool-using, with no good reason to posit a unified long-run goal — the failure we should be budgeting against is not a deceptive agent with a plan but brittle compliance: behaviour that satisfies evaluators by tracking shallow correlates of approval rather than the reasons that approval was grounded in, and which therefore degrades smoothly, and degrades first, in exactly the situations where our oversight is thinnest, because those are also the situations where the training signal was thinnest. On this picture the value of preference training isn't that it instils values, it's that it renders failure legible and steerable once it appears, and that legibility is what we should actually be measuring.

The room matters as much as the argument. The chair is a pragmatic ML researcher who thinks current methods are broadly fine and wants a decision he can minute; the head of product wants the launch date held and will say the existing evaluations already cover this; one research engineer thinks I am badly underrating deliberate deception in frontier systems and will press me on why my story isn't simply exploited by a model that is strategising; our interpretability lead will hear 'cut interpretability' even if I never say it, and will defend her roadmap on the grounds that without mechanism-level access we cannot tell brittle compliance from the genuine article; the external ethics member on the board is likely to object that reframing alignment as detection latency quietly changes the subject from what the system ought to do to whether we will get caught; and our compliance lead cares only about whether any of this could be shown to a regulator as due diligence.

What I need is a Q&A prep sheet for that room: the objections I will actually get, from whom, in their vocabulary, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each with the reply I should give in about two minutes and a note on where that reply is soft or what I should concede up front instead, including the one question I am quietly hoping nobody asks. Please don't flatter the framework: if the drift-and-blast framing rests on a confused premise — if detection latency turns out not to be estimable without already assuming the thing it was meant to establish, or if brittle compliance and deliberate deception are not separable even in principle at the level of behaviour — say so plainly rather than helping me polish the pitch, and tell me where you think I am most overconfident and which of my replies you would expect to fail in the room. Give me exactly eight numbered entries and nothing else, no preamble and no closing advice: entries one to seven are the objections in order, entry eight is the one I hope nobody asks, and each entry runs to three or four sentences covering the objection as that person would phrase it, my reply, and the weak spot.
