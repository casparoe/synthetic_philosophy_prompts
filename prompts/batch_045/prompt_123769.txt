I'm writing a long-ish piece for a science-and-ideas magazine on why my phone can OCR a photographed paragraph in forty fonts and then completely falls over on a post-it from my aunt, and I've hit the same wall in every interview: the type designers tell me an 'a' is a shape plus a style, the vision people tell me there's no shape there at all and it's just a learned category, and the ML people tell me it's solved. Before I write the section where I try to say what would actually count as an answer, I want the spec from someone who'll think it through properly.

So: write me the list of requirements that a theory has to meet to count as explaining what makes every 'a' an 'a'. Not a survey, not a history of ideas — the criteria. For each one, give me the concrete case or consideration that makes it a requirement (that's the part I'll be quoting). Then tell me which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just hobby-horses of one camp, flag any pair you think can't be jointly satisfied, and name the single requirement that knocks out the most candidate theories.

Fix the purpose as this: the theory is meant to underwrite a system that has to do both directions — read a letter in a hand or a display face it has never seen, and generate the rest of an alphabet from a couple of samples of somebody's style. Adequacy relative to that, not relative to everything anyone might want.

Here's my own scratch list of cases. Cut the ones that are bogus or that just smuggle in one camp's assumptions, and add the ones I'm missing:

- A ransom note: letters clipped from six different fonts. You read it fine and you also instantly see that it's assembled from different sources. So identity has to be invariant to style while style is still perceived — the theory can't just throw style away.
- An ornate script wedding invitation: I can read the whole line and cannot name a single letter in isolation.
- Cyrillic 'а' and Latin 'a' can be pixel-identical and still be different letters (this is why phishing domains work). Shape seems to underdetermine identity entirely.
- Greek alpha and Latin 'a' are near-identical in shape and share an ancestor, and are different letters.
- Double-storey 'a' versus single-storey 'ɑ' are wildly different shapes and the same letter.
- 'l', '1' and 'I' are the same strokes in a lot of faces, disambiguated only by neighbours.
- Degradation: an 'a' with its bowl rubbed off, a 'c' with a chunk missing. The letter survives the loss of parts, so no single stroke can be essential.
- Segmentation: cursive has no gaps at all. The theory has to say how the units get found before it says what they are.
- One-shot extrapolation: I see one 'g' in a strange display face and can sketch a plausible 'q'. Where does that come from?
- Kids and some patients swap 'b' and 'd'. Mirror images are systematically confusable, which seems to tell you something about how shape is being coded.
- I can tell a typeface is corporate or friendly without being able to point to the letter that carries it.
- Historical continuity: is the 'a' in a twelfth-century manuscript the same letter as the 'a' in this sentence, or just a causal descendant of it?
- Gradedness: some 'a's are better 'a's than others. And the taxonomy questions — is 'á' an 'a'? is 'ª' an 'a'? is the IPA symbol 'ɑ' an 'a'? is turned 'ǝ' an 'e'?
- Logotypes and display faces where a near-letter object stands in for a letter, and readers accept it without being able to say what licenses the substitution.
- Cross-script transfer: knowing Greek makes Cyrillic easier; you can copy Korean shapes accurately with no idea what you're writing.

I also want the distinctions spelled out: at minimum token (this mark on this page), glyph/allograph variant, letter as a type, and the writing system it belongs to — and a view on which of those levels the "essence" is supposed to live at. My suspicion is that most theories cheat by sliding between them.

Two last things. Any desideratum that's really a prejudice of one approach (must be learned from one example, must be symbolic, must be a neural net, must explain our aesthetic response, must fit the clinical dissociations I keep being told about, must generalize to scripts it wasn't built for) — say so and demote it. And if some subset of the real desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, that's the finding I most want to print, so make it explicit rather than quietly dropping one.

One line each on how templates, structural or stroke-based descriptions, stroke-generative models in the Letter Spirit vein, and modern convnets do against the list — I don't need a literature review. And no citations or reading lists, I'll source those myself and my fact-checker will kill me if I get them wrong. A bulleted list is fine; I'll be lifting bits of it.
