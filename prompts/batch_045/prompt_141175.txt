I've spent the better part of a decade working in market design — two-sided matching, kidney exchange, combinatorial clock auctions for spectrum, and one long-running headache involving the allocation of school places — so my native vocabulary is bidders, preference orderings over allocations, transfers, individual rationality, strategy-proofness, budget balance, thickness and congestion, and repugnant transactions. None of that needs explaining to me. A friend who works on global health funding has been trying to recruit me to an idea she calls moral trade, and I'd like you to restate it for me in those terms. As she explains it: two parties who disagree about what is worth doing can still trade to mutual advantage, because each can buy cheaply an action the other regards as worthless. If I believe a marginal pound does most good buying insecticide-treated bednets while you believe it does most good restoring a parish roof, I give to your roof on condition that you give to my bednets, and each of us, by our own lights, has secured a great deal of value for a small outlay; the same structure is meant to work for votes, careers and abstentions, not just money.

What I want is a systematic translation, not an explanation pitched at a beginner. Take the case for moral trade and the main cases against it — the commitment problem, given that nothing is ever delivered to the payer and each side would prefer the other to move first; the charge that paying someone to do what they themselves take to be worthless is a kind of corruption (she bristles at the word simony); the worry that the practice is only moral offsetting under a new name; the dependence of the supposed gains on contested empirical and moral claims — and render each in market-design language as precisely as you can, one by one, without quietly crediting my framework with resources it does not have. If a step of the argument needs interpersonal comparisons of value, a common cardinal scale, or something playing the role of a prior over moral theories, name the need rather than assume it satisfied. A few short sections are fine; I'd rather have precision than completeness.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about: what translated cleanly, what went through only after you had stretched a term, and what cannot be said in my vocabulary at all — whether, for instance, Pareto improvement still means anything when the two parties apply different measures of value, or whether a market in which the buyer never consumes the good and the seller attaches no value to producing it is a market in anything I would recognise. My own suspicion is that this is just Coasean bargaining over an externality with no agreed property rights, where an unexamined status quo allocation and an unearned transferable-utility assumption do all the work, and that once said out loud the idea is either trivial or incoherent; test that suspicion rather than indulge it. Two further wrinkles I cannot fit into my framework at all, and which you should have a go at: trades made conditional on a contingency that neither party can verify, with a mutually trusted arbiter; and trades between parties who never meet and cannot observe each other's performance, resting on expected reciprocity alone. If the residue points to something my framework is missing, say what; if it points to something confused in the moral-trade literature, say that instead.
