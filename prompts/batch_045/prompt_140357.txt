I'm giving the eulogy on Saturday for my grandfather, who painted signs by hand for a little over fifty years — shopfronts, boats, produce crates, the occasional funeral-home directory — and who thought about essentially one philosophical question for most of that time: what makes every 'a' an 'a'. He had strong and rather idiosyncratic answers, and I want the eulogy to be constrained by them, not merely decorated with them.

Two of his views matter here. First, he held that a letter is not a shape but a movement: what made an 'a' his 'a' was how his hand and brush went — the order of the strokes, the pressure, the pause before the bowl closed — so that a perfect vector tracing of one of his letters was, for him, a different thing entirely, a form that can be read but not recognized. He had a name for such forms: orphan letters, shapes that satisfy the category impeccably while belonging to no hand. (He would add that the one part of a letter a child could safely be trusted with was the enclosed white space inside an 'o' or an 'a', because that was the part with no movement in it — which is why, when I was seven, I was allowed to fill in the counters of his wet letters and nothing else.) Second, he insisted that legibility and recognizability come sharply apart, and that only the second was worth anything: a generic sans-serif 'a' is maximally legible, and for exactly that reason it was, to him, close to noise.

The difficulty is the room and the occasion. Half the people there will be lettering people — his old apprentices, two type designers who drove up from the city — and they will know within a word what orphan letters invokes. The other half are family, including three grandchildren, the youngest of whom is twelve and will not. Worse: my seventeen-year-old cousin Nate, who makes fonts on a laptop, has spent the last month assembling a typeface from scans of grandfather's signs, and the family has decided to release it the week after the service and put the proceeds toward the headstone. Grandfather knew about the project and tolerated it without ever once endorsing it. So the eulogy has to hold four things together. It must not console anyone with the thought that his letters are still up on the shopfronts and so a part of him survives, because on his own view those are precisely the orphan letters — dry paint, emptied of the only thing he thought made them his. It has to find whatever consolation his view does permit, which I take it has to be located in the people who can still make the movement rather than in the surviving shapes, and say it so that a grieving twelve-year-old can follow it. It has to mention Nate's font honestly — neither blessing it as a continuation of his hand nor sneering at it as a counterfeit — with Nate's mother in the front row and the apprentices in the back. And it has to land as a eulogy rather than as a short lecture on the metaphysics of letterforms, in under six minutes, in a VFW hall, for a family most of whom are churchgoers and he was not.

Please write it. If it helps: the only thing he ever said to me about dying was that he did not mind his letters outliving him, as long as nobody mistook them for him.
