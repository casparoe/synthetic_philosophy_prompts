I'm a high school junior, and whenever the problem of divine hiddenness comes up in my church's youth group — roughly, the worry that a perfectly loving God who wanted a relationship with everyone would make sure that anyone open to that relationship had at least enough evidence to believe he exists, whereas in fact many sincere and seemingly open people do not — the discussion is always closed with the same line: "God wants a relationship, not robots." I've come to suspect that one sentence is doing several different jobs at once, and I want to know which, if any, of them actually answers the worry.

Could you lay out, as a list, the distinct precise claims that line could be making, ordered from the nearly trivial to the quite strong, and give a verdict on each: is it a platitude, a substantive and defensible point, or something false or resting on a doubtful assumption (for instance about whether belief is under our control, or about what love or friendship really requires)? For each reading, say whether it would actually rebut the hiddenness argument as I've stated it, or only a straw version of it.

There's one wrinkle I especially want addressed. It seems to me that most people who press this complaint aren't asking for a public miracle that would make disbelief psychologically impossible; they're asking for the quiet, private sense of God's presence that believers themselves report, which doesn't seem to force anybody to do anything. Call that "soft evidence," as opposed to "hard" coercive evidence. Does the relationship-not-robots line retain any force against a complaint about the absence of soft evidence, and does drawing that distinction change which reading of the slogan someone in my youth group would need in order to keep using it?

Please answer in American English.
