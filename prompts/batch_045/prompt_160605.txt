Hey — I need a runnable activity for a single session of a second-year undergrad module on collective decision making. 18 students, 90 minutes, one room with movable tables and a projector I'd rather not use until the very end. Roughly a third are fine with basic probability, the rest glaze over the second a formula appears. They have not met the Condorcet jury theorem and I don't want them hearing the name or seeing any statement of it until after the debrief, maybe not even then.

The thing I want them to feel in their gut: that "more voters is better, and a big enough majority is basically infallible" isn't carried by the number of heads. It's carried almost entirely by two assumptions — everybody being a bit better than a coin toss, and the signals being independent. I want them to be in a room of 15 people who are each individually right about 60% of the time, confidently feeling like the group is crushing it, and actually getting it wrong a lot, and then having to work out why. Nothing said up front. The rules and the cards have to do it.

Constraints: no real money (chips, sweets, or a silly league table between two halves of the room), materials from a stationery cupboard, and I've got maybe an hour of prep. So I need the signal-generation recipe spelled out concretely — how to precompute the cards (dice method, or the exact spreadsheet formula) so that per-voter competence really is the number I want and the correlation really is the structure I want, including how to avoid accidentally generating a trial where everyone gets the same card.

Here's the shape I have in mind; please fix it, reorder it, or tell me a round is a bad idea.

1. Baseline: 15 voters, each signal right with prob 0.6, simple majority, chips for a correct verdict. My back-of-envelope says the group lands around three-quarters correct, which is already lower than students expect — check that, and tell me how many trials we need before the difference between 60% and ~75% is something they can actually see rather than noise. Also: do I tell them the truth after every trial, or batch the reveals? I'm worried that immediate feedback lets them reverse-engineer their own competence and start second-guessing their cards.
2. The flip: same set-up but competence around 0.45, and they're split into threes and fifteens so they can watch the big group do worse than the small one.
3. The cascade: before the vote, one designated "expert" announces their signal out loud (expert is right 70% of the time). Everyone then votes.
4. The one I care most about — hidden correlation. I want a round where every individual card is generated with exactly the same marginal competence as round 1 (0.6) and the only difference is that the cards secretly share a common source. My construction is: draw one source signal that's right 60% of the time; each voter's card copies the source with prob 0.9 and otherwise is an independent draw that's right 60% of the time. Marginal competence then stays 0.6 exactly, but the group is basically just the source, so accuracy collapses to roughly 60% instead of ~75%. Sanity-check that arithmetic, tune the parameters if there's a version with a bigger visible gap or one that's easier to generate by hand, and tell me how many trials to run and how to keep them from comparing cards and spotting the trick. Also how to stage the reveal so they generate the "wait, our signals must have been correlated" hypothesis themselves.
5. Unequal weight: three people get much better cards, or one person's vote counts five times, and we see accuracy jump. I want them to feel both the epistemic gain and the "that's not a democracy anymore" squirm, without me editorialising.
