I'm taking an upper-level seminar this term on ethics under empirical uncertainty, and I've gotten stuck on a point about the scale argument for taking insect suffering seriously, so I'd like your help with it.

The version of the argument we were given runs roughly like this: suppose our credence that a given insect is sentient is only about 0.1. There are, by the standard rough estimates, on the order of a quintillion insects alive at any one moment. Multiply the small probability by the enormous number and the expected quantity of insect suffering comes out astronomical, so insect-inclusive causes should dominate our priorities.

My worry is that this multiplication seems to spread the uncertainty independently across individuals, as though each insect drew its own ticket and about a tenth of a quintillion insects turned out to be sentient. But sentience is not a per-individual lottery. What we are actually uncertain about, it seems to me, is a single hypothesis — say, that insects share one deeply conserved mechanism doing the relevant work, in which case either every insect is sentient or none is. On that reading there is no distribution of sentience across the population at all: there is one coin flip, and the quintillion is not a quintillion separate chances but merely the size of the stake hanging on a single chance. And then the appeal to vast numbers looks like pure rhetoric, doing no argumentative work that the phrase "a very large stake" would not do, and the whole case for prioritizing insects collapses into a single improbable gamble.

A classmate replied that on this reading the case for funding research into insect sentience becomes far stronger than the case for funding interventions to help insects, since one decisive study would settle the value of every insect-inclusive intervention at once. I cannot tell whether that is a reductio of the position or just a consequence we should accept.

So my question is this: given that the uncertainty is global rather than distributed, and that the resulting bet is a one-off, all-or-nothing affair with a wildly bimodal outcome — either an immense amount of suffering averted or nothing at all — isn't expected-value reasoning the wrong decision rule here, and shouldn't we instead act on the more likely hypothesis, treat insects as non-sentient, and direct our limited resources to interventions whose effects are known and measurable?

Please reply as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion, in American English.
