My brother-in-law is a field linguist, works on a language with maybe 300 speakers left, and at a family dinner last month we got into an argument about whether he can ever really know what a word in that language means. He was annoyingly smug about it. I'm a programmer, I read a bit of philosophy on the side, and I realised about halfway through that we were doing the gavagai thing — except neither of us could say what exactly we were disagreeing about, and I now think the reason is that we kept sliding between three different claims without noticing. I want to hand him something next weekend that forces the issue.

So here's the set-up. The word he was being smug about (I'll call it "dhamba") — assume he has all of the following evidence and nothing else that matters:

- Speakers say it when a river is in view, and also when only a stretch of the water is visible between the trees.
- They don't say it for a lake, an irrigation canal, or a puddle.
- When the river got diverted for a small hydro scheme a few years back, older speakers still said "dhamba" walking past the dry bed; younger ones didn't, and they'd correct each other.
- If two rivers are in view at once they'll say it twice, and they get irritated if you say it once.
- There's a separate word for water that they'll happily use for a glass of it, and a word for the whole valley the river runs through.

What I want from you is a decision tree of the positions on whether there's a fact of the matter about what "dhamba" picks out — and, if there is, whether anyone in his position could ever find it out. Roughly seven or eight branch nodes, each phrased as a yes/no or three-way question I could put to him over dessert without him being able to tell where it leads. So no jargon and no labels in the questions: "if the two of you disagreed about some new case forever and neither of you budged, would one of you be wrong?" is fine; "do you accept the inscrutability of reference?" is not. Every node should turn on something my five facts actually bear on, or on a hypothetical extension of them. And please don't just re-run the textbook rabbit case — build the questions out of "dhamba" and the evidence above.

Leaves: seven to nine, named. I want at least these to show up somewhere: (a) full-bore no-fact-of-the-matter, two manuals of translation can fit every scrap of possible evidence and neither is the right one; (b) there is a fact of the matter and enough fieldwork converges on it, end of story; (c) the move where some candidate meanings are just more natural than others and that's what breaks the tie; (d) this is only ordinary underdetermination of theory by evidence, nothing special about meaning at all; (e) there's a fact of the matter but it's in principle unreachable from the outside, so the appearance of indeterminacy is an epistemic shadow not a semantic one; (f) something deflationary, where the whole question turns out to be somehow confused or merely practical. Use descriptive labels if you want — I'd much rather have a label I can look up later than a name you're guessing at, so please don't attribute any position to an author unless you're actually sure, and no reading lists or citations, I just want the tree.

Two more things, and these are the bits I actually care about.

First: I think there are three theses that get hopelessly run together in this debate — that you can systematically swap what all the words pick out (rabbits, undetached rabbit parts, whatever) and nothing observable changes; that two whole manuals for translating sentences can fit all possible evidence with no fact of which is correct; and plain old underdetermination of theory by data, the kind you get in physics too. For each leaf, say which of those three it accepts and which it rejects. I want to see which combinations are actually occupied, and for the empty ones I want one sentence on whether they're empty because incoherent, because unattractive, or just because nobody's bothered to defend them.

Second: flag any pair of leaves the tree can't genuinely separate. My hunch is that (b) and (e) collapse into each other at the level of anything anybody could ever observe, and if that's right, say so plainly instead of inventing a question that pretends to pull them apart.

And if you can: tell me which of my five bits of evidence are doing the real work — which one, if you deleted it, would leave the argument genuinely unresolvable. My suspicion is that the counting thing (saying it twice) is the load-bearing one and the generational split is a red herring that just muddies it, but I want to see whether the tree agrees.
