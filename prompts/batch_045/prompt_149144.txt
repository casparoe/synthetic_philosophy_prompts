I teach a first-year course on democratic theory, and I spent most of last night arguing with a colleague across the corridor about lottocracy, so I want to test the argument on a case rather than in the abstract. Her position is that citizens' assemblies only look successful because they are advisory: what actually does the work in the famous cases is that an elected legislature could take the parts of the report it liked and quietly drop the rest. On her view a randomly selected panel is a focus group with a better facilitator, and the interesting question is not whether sortition is democratic but why anyone bothers to convene one when a referendum or a select committee is cheaper. My position is that advisory status cuts the other way, and the case that made me think so is the Irish Citizens' Assembly on the Eighth Amendment, roughly 2016–17: as I remember it, about a hundred randomly selected residents plus a chair met over a series of weekends, heard mainly from medical and legal experts and from advocacy groups on both sides, and ended up recommending a considerably more liberal abortion regime than the one then in force; the recommendation went to a committee of the Oireachtas, then to a referendum in 2018 that passed comfortably, and the earlier Irish Constitutional Convention had done something structurally similar on same-sex marriage a few years before. If I have any of those details wrong, correct me at the top rather than building on them — but please don't let the case turn on a number I may have misremembered.

What I want is that episode analyzed through lottocratic theory, with the analysis running in both directions: theory illuminating case, and case testing theory. Be disciplined about the mapping, in a way most commentary on this case isn't. Name the players — the panel, the experts and advocacy groups who briefed it, the Oireachtas committee, the party system, the electorate in the subsequent referendum — and say what each player's actual options were (recommend, qualify, dilute, ignore, refuse to legislate, send it to a vote) and what was supposed to count as evidence as opposed to mere pressure. Then say which features of the episode lottocracy explains, which it misdescribes, and about which it is simply silent: in particular, whether a theory that grounds legitimacy partly in descriptive representation can account for a panel whose members visibly changed their minds under expert briefing, and whether the fact that the outcome then had to be ratified by a popular vote makes the assembly redundant, a filtering device, a legitimacy lender, or something the theory has no vocabulary for.

Two points I'd like taken seriously rather than waved at, because they are exactly where the argument stalled. First, what I have started calling the mirroring–updating tradeoff: a sortition body has one claim to authority because it resembles the population and another because it has been informed and has deliberated, and the more the second is true the less the first is — the panel that updates is no longer a mirror. Does the Irish case show the tradeoff biting, and can any design escape it? I floated the idea of always selecting two statistically identical panels, briefing and deliberating with only one, and publishing both sets of answers so the drift is measured rather than asserted; my colleague says the control panel's answers would be irrelevant, and I am not sure she is wrong. Second, the selective-uptake objection: if assemblies tend to be convened on issues where ordinary party competition is already blocked, and their reports are implemented only in part, then the track record may be a survivorship effect, and lottocracy may be less a model of democracy than a device for letting elected actors take credit for decisions they were unable to make on their own. Give me a plain verdict on whether the episode is evidence for lottocracy, an anomaly for it, or a case it never anticipated; tell me which of my two points survives and which collapses; and say where you think the framework goes silent instead of forcing it to speak. No citations needed, and please don't dissolve the question into a survey of who has written what — a short paragraph won't do it; I want something I could adapt into a lecture.
