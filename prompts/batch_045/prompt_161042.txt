I'm convening a reading group on social kinds and I've volunteered to do the session on generations — Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z and the rest — and I've realized the discussion keeps stalling because everyone arrives with a different implicit test for whether a cohort is a real thing or just a calendar convention that happened to stick. So before we look at any particular account, I want the specification: what would a theory of generational cohorts have to deliver to count as adequate for a purpose I want to fix in advance — not good enough to sort a marketing deck, and not good enough for dinner-table sociology, but good enough to license cohort explanations in the social sciences, i.e. to make claims of the form "she votes the way she does partly because of the cohort she came of age in" genuinely explanatory rather than merely suggestive.

Please write the desiderata list: which phenomena such a theory has to explain, which cases it has to get right, which distinctions it must respect and which it may collapse, and which results from demography or social psychology it cannot contradict — with the case or consideration behind each requirement. I'd like each requirement attached to a concrete test. Here are five I've sketched; keep them, sharpen them, or throw them out, and say which: the undisclosed-boundary test (a competent member of the population should be able to sort actual people into the cohort without being told the birth-year cutoffs — is that a genuine requirement or a prejudice of the cohort-as-folk-category camp?); the re-enlistment test (does membership still do explanatory work decades after the putative formative window, or has it quietly become a period effect?); the counterfactual birth-year test (if someone born in the late 1980s had instead been born twenty years earlier, would the theory predict differences in the respects it cares about, and does it trace those differences to a shared formative cause rather than to being young or old at a given time?); the portability test (cohorts defined by one country's shock — a war, a one-child policy, a currency reform — don't line up with another country's; may a generation be real in one population and not in another?); and the half-generation problem (what to say about the micro-generations people posit at the seams, like the early-to-mid-1980s band alleged to be not quite one generation and not quite the next: real kinds, edge effects, or artifacts of an arbitrary boundary?). The list should also say what a theory owes the standard demographic result that age, period, and cohort effects are not separately identifiable from one another without extra identifying assumptions, and it should handle the asymmetry between labels that caught on and near-identical labels that didn't, since it seems wrong for a cohort's reality to depend on whether its name succeeded.

Two things matter more than completeness. First: mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp, and flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — if the list turns out to be unsatisfiable as a whole, say so plainly rather than smoothing it over, because that would itself be the interesting result. Second: identify the single requirement that excludes the most otherwise-popular accounts, and explain why it does the work. Commit to definite verdicts throughout and give your reasons; where you want to drop a requirement or demote it to a camp preference, say so instead of hedging, and close by naming the one item on your list you are least confident belongs there, together with what would change your mind. A short scorecard for three contenders — the convention view that cohorts are stipulated reporting categories, the shared-formative-event view, and the deflationary view that they are institutionalized labels with looping effects on the people classified — is welcome, but keep it to a few lines each; the criteria and their motivating cases are the part I actually need.
