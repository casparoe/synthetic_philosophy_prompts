I am seventeen, the senior captain of my school's Ethics Bowl team, and I have been asked to give the closing speech at our end-of-season banquet next week: about six minutes, in a hotel banquet room, to roughly sixty people. About a third of the room is my teammates, who have spent the year arguing cases and will catch every shortcut I take; another third is parents, younger siblings, and a few teachers who have never heard of a trolley problem and mostly believe we debate current events; the rest is our coach, who is retiring after eleven years and who is the person who first taught us the term 'intuition pump,' and the coach of the school we beat in the state final. The thing I want to talk about is an uncomfortable detail from that semifinal. We won it partly on a hypothetical we invented ourselves: a single named senior, described in two sentences of concrete detail — first in her family to apply to college, works the closing shift at a pharmacy, one scholarship standing between her and the state school — set against thirty unnamed students who would each receive a small tutoring grant if the scholarship money were divided. During practice we noticed that almost all of the force of the case came from those two sentences, and that when we stripped them out and argued the bare structure, nobody found it nearly as obvious. We kept the detail in, and it worked. My teammate Priya said afterward that the frightening part is not that we used it but that we would probably have lost without it, which means we won a round with something that was never really an argument. I do not know how to say that out loud in a room where half the people are proud of us and the other half are paying for the chicken.

So I would like you to write the speech. The idea has to do real structural work in it, not decorate it: the speech is about how vivid hypotheticals can bypass judgment while appearing to inform it, and therefore the speech may not itself get away with doing that. Concretely, please hold to the following. Every example the speech uses should be given twice — once in its vivid, concrete form and once stripped down to the bare structural description — with the speech naming out loud what happened to the intuition in between; and at least one example should be a case where stripping the detail does *not* change people's verdict, so that the speech does not collapse into the claim that all thought experiments are sophistry. Include a live demonstration of the test our team actually uses, which I would like the speech to name and explain for the parents: we call it the one-detail flip — change a single feature everyone agrees is irrelevant, such as whether the bystander uses their hands or a remote, whether the student has a name or a case number, or whether the story is told in the first person or the third — and if the verdict flips, the case is doing rhetorical work rather than evidential work, whereas if it survives, it is at least a candidate for evidence. Please also solve a problem I keep running into when I draft this myself: any sentence warning a room against persuasive imagery tends to be a piece of persuasive imagery, so if the speech must use a loaded metaphor, have it name the metaphor as it uses it and say what work the metaphor is being asked to do.

Two further constraints. The tone has to survive the occasion: warm, a little funny, genuinely grateful to a retiring coach, and not a lecture, and not the captain confessing a sin at a celebration — the parents should leave thinking the activity is worthwhile and that we are worth being proud of, without being told the trophy was won by a trick. It must also hand something usable to the incoming captain, a sophomore who will run next season and who will inherit the same temptation. And please do not put any quotation into the mouth of a real philosopher or attribute any specific line or title to anyone, since I will be reading this aloud and cannot check it: if a line is needed, paraphrase it and attribute it vaguely, or leave it unattributed.
