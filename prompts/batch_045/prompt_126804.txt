I've been following the epistocracy vs. democracy argument for a couple of years now — the competence side says most voters know almost nothing and you wouldn't let someone who's never studied aviation fly a plane, so why let them decide policy; the proceduralist side says any competence test would be unjustifiable to the people it disenfranchises and would hand power to already-advantaged groups, and the competence side replies that's a practical worry dressed up as a principled one — and literally nobody has moved an inch. My own hunch is that it isnt really an empirical disagreement at all: both camps seem to grant that voters are badly informed and that a better-informed electorate would do somewhat better. What they actually fight about is what a decision procedure owes the people it coerces — whether it has to be answerable to each of them, or only has to produce good outcomes. But maybe the hidden question is empirical after all: whether political knowledge aggregates into better collective judgement, or whether voters are just rationalizing their tribal membership with slightly fancier stories.

Here's the wrinkle I've started using to probe it, and I'd like you to take it seriously. Imagine two designs. (1) A "blind" epistocracy: everyone sits the competence test, but whether you may vote is then assigned by lottery, so that the enfranchised set correlates with test scores only statistically and nobody is ever told they failed. (2) A "stratified" epistocracy: you enfranchise the top scorers *within* each racial, class and education group separately, so the voting population ends up demographically identical to the whole population. My thought is that these separate the two standard objections: if someone still objects to both, their objection cant be about the insult of being excluded or about demographic skew, so it has to be about the bare principle of weighting by competence; if they accept both but still reject the plain version, then the objections really were about the contingent bad effects.

So what kind of stalemate is this? A verbal one, a clash of bedrock intuitions about equal status that neither side can argue for, a buried empirical question, or two different questions — "which procedure is legitimate" and "which procedure gets things right" — sharing a name? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress, or should I expect none? Please don't just survey the positions.
