I'd be grateful for comments on a draft essay. The subject has become personal rather than academic for me, which is both why I am writing it and why I don't trust my own jud平 judgement about it — I'd like an outsider's read on the argument, not on my motives.

The background: last spring, at forty-one, I learned from a consumer DNA test that the man who raised me is not my biological father. The clinic's records, which I eventually obtained, are more precise than I was prepared for: my mother underwent IVF with donor sperm, two straws from the same donor were thawed that morning, one was used, and the embryologist selected a single sperm cell under the microscope from among several graded as equally suitable. So I now have a sentence I cannot settle: had a different straw been taken from the Dewar, or a different sperm picked from the drop, the child born nine months later would have been my never-born sibling, and I would never have existed at all.

Here is the draft.

**Vial 4471**

The necessity of origin is usually stated over gametes: no human being could have originated from a different sperm and a different egg. The cluster of views that grew out of Kripke's *Naming and Necessity* is standardly presented this way, and the presentation invites a picture in which my existence is pinned to two particular objects. My claim in what follows is that gametes are the wrong kind of thing to be essential to a person, and that once you see why, the doctrine has to be restated — with consequences that are not merely verbal.

Consider four candidate theses, in ascending strength.

*Progenitor essentialism*: necessarily, I have the two biological progenitors I actually have. Too weak. My full sibling — same mother, same donor, a different straw — satisfies it, and is not me.

*Genome essentialism*: necessarily, my nuclear genome is the sequence it actually is. Also too weak, and for two reasons rather than one. A monozygotic twin shares that sequence and is not me. Nor would a synthetic embryo, assembled from a sequenced and synthesised copy of my genome in some future laboratory, be me; it would be my twin, born late. So the genome underdetermines me, even though it feels like the thing that makes me the animal I am.

*Gamete essentialism*: necessarily, I developed from the very sperm cell and the very egg cell that I actually developed from. This is the received formulation, and I think it is too strong.

*Provenance essentialism* (my proposal): necessarily, I developed from a zygote whose two haploid nuclear genomes were produced by the two meiotic divisions that actually produced them — these crossovers, this segregation, these two draws from two lotteries.

Three cases are meant to drive the wedge between the third and the fourth.

*Meiotic coincidence.* Meiosis shuffles and segregates; nothing in the mechanics rules out two distinct meioses in the same testis yielding sperm whose DNA is base-pair identical. Had the second of them fertilised my mother's egg, the zygote's nuclear genome would have been indistinguishable from mine. That child would not have been me — but, I want to say, not in virtue of the sperm's being a different object. What would have differed is which draw produced the genome, and that is a fact about provenance, not about vehicles.

*The stripped carrier.* Under ICSI a single sperm is selected and injected; its tail is immobilised, its membranes and most of its cytoplasm broken down and not passed on. What the sperm delivers into the egg is, for our purposes, a haploid genome together with a centriole and a small cargo of RNAs. Sperm cells are vehicles. If the same haploid genome had arrived in a different vehicle whose non-nuclear parts were likewise discarded, the embryo's nuclear starting state would have been the same, and I can find no difference that could bear the weight of my non-existence.

*The corrected lesion.* Suppose the sperm that in fact fertilised the egg carried a point mutation which maternal repair machinery corrected shortly after delivery, so that the sequence actually read is the sequence an unmutated sperm would have delivered. Two different sperm cells then converge on one post-repair genome. My intuition is that which one it was makes no difference to me. But I am unsure of the intuition, because to describe the correction as happening *to me* already assumes that I was present at the moment of repair — which quietly settles when I began, and I would rather settle that separately.

Provenance essentialism survives all three. It is finer-grained than genome essentialism, since two draws can coincide in output while differing in provenance, and coarser than gamete essentialism, since it is indifferent to the identity of the vehicle.

Two further cases test whether it generalises. In mitochondrial donation, the nuclear genome comes from one egg and the cytoplasm and mitochondria from another; provenance essentialism treats the cytoplasm donor the way it treats a gestational carrier — as part of the circumstances of my development rather than of my origin. In tetragametic chimerism, two zygotes fuse and one person results, with two nuclear genomes rather than one. Provenance essentialism must here become cumulative or disjunctive: essential to me is that I descend from a specified *lineage*, not from a specified pair of cells. That, I think, is the general form of the doctrine — origins are essential as lineages, not as objects.

A worry I cannot place. If which sperm reaches the oocyte first is physically indeterministic, then the nearest world in which a different sperm wins is not a world that differs from mine by some earlier hair's-breadth; it is a world that diverges from mine with no difference that explains the divergence. Does the essentialist's claim need there to be such a difference? I do not know, and I suspect the answer distinguishes the metaphysical claim from the causal intuitions that support it.

What is at stake is not only metaphysical. Parfit's non-identity problem gets its grip from exactly this: if a different straw had been taken, the resulting child would have been someone else, and so nobody would have been harmed by the choice — a conclusion I find harder to accept the more concretely I can picture the straw. What the doctrine gave me, in its gamete form, was the thought that my existence was pinned to a particular object. What I actually have is the thought that it was pinned to a particular draw. And yet the difference between the two straws does not present itself to me as the difference between me and my brother; it presents itself as noise. Whether that feeling is evidence about modality or merely the residue of wanting to have been inevitable is the question the essay leaves open.
