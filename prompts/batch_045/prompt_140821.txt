I sit on the ethics committee of a mid-sized hospital, and we are drafting a short guidance note on when a patient's earlier written instruction — a transfusion refusal, a psychiatric advance directive, a request not to be allowed to discharge herself during a crisis — should still be treated as binding when the same patient, later and apparently lucidly, asks for the opposite. Most of our disagreement turned out not to be about autonomy or best interests but about something prior: what a person has to be doing at the moment she signs for her signing to count as adopting a plan, as opposed to making a prediction about her future self or expressing a wish about what she hopes that self will do. A reading group I run separately has been stuck on Kavka's toxin puzzle — the stranger who will pay you a large sum at midnight if by then you have formed the intention to drink, the next morning, a toxin that will make you miserable for a day and do nothing else, with no further payment for actually drinking it — because it isolates exactly this: a present reason to acquire a future-directed attitude you know you will have no reason to act on. So I want a specification rather than a survey: what would a theory of intention have to get right in order to be usable for my committee's question? Give me the requirements — the cases it must handle, the distinctions it must not collapse (intending versus predicting, intending versus wanting or hoping, intending versus trying, conditional versus unconditional intention, occurrent versus standing intention, planning for oneself versus committing to another), and the ordinary facts about planning it must not render mysterious — each with the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and each marked as non-negotiable or as the preference of a particular camp. Then tell me which subset of the requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate theories.

Please build the list around these cases, several of which my group invented: the plain toxin case; a counter-bribe variant in which you know tonight that tomorrow at eleven a second stranger will credibly offer you more money not to drink, and you know you will take it — does that make intending tonight easier, harder, or no different, and whatever the answer, what does it show the original puzzle was about?; an iterated variant in which the same offer recurs every night for a month, payment each time only for the intention, with full knowledge each night that you will never drink — does repetition help, and if so does that tell against a theory of what intending is?; a dyadic variant in which you and a partner are paid only if you jointly intend and each of you knows the other will not drink; and a variant in which the payoff is keyed to your confident belief that you will drink rather than to your intention, so that the question becomes whether the puzzle is really about prediction all along. Add the non-exotic cases any such theory must leave intact: the smoker who locks money into a deposit contract knowing she will want to smoke, the traveller who books a non-refundable room in a city she will later not want to visit, the standing intention never acted on, the intention to do something you expect to fail at.

Two constraints. First, I have no library access and no interest in who said what: describe positions by the claims that constitute them, skip citations and reading suggestions entirely, and if you include a scorecard of the main contenders at all, give it one line each and do not let it displace the criteria — I would rather have twelve requirements with their motivating cases than twelve views with their labels. Second, and the part I most want: mark explicitly which of the things people demand here are genuine desiderata and which are commitments of one camp that the rest of us never agreed to — that intentions must track reasons the agent expects to have at the time of action, that intending requires believing you will do it, that deliberation and self-prediction are in tension, that a rational intention must be revisable only for a reason — say which of those are negotiable, and flag any requirement that quietly asks the theory to do normative work it may not be able to do, namely telling us what to do later, rather than telling us what it is to have a plan. About 700 to 1000 words; a table is fine if it helps.
