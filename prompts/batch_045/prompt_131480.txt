hey — I'm an economist, I work mostly on market design (matching, auctions, a bit of contract theory), so my reflex is to turn everything into agents with types, an allocation rule, incentive constraints and some equilibrium notion.

I keep runing into this idea from the effective-altruism side called moral trade. The pitch, as I've had it explained to me: two people who disagree about what's actually morally valuable can both win by each doing the act the other's theory cares about — you perform the action that's cheap for you under your theory but that my theory counts as really important, I do the same for you, no money changes hands, and the claimed gains are supposed to be enormous. There's a stronger version where you don't communicate or bargain at all, you just act on the expectation that sufficiently many people with the other theory are doing their part, and this is still called trade.

Can you restate that argument as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary, and then give me the residue report? I want three specific things.

1) Where's the numeraire? The exchange rate between your acts and mine is doing the work of a price, but two moral theories don't share a unit of value, so I don't see how you clear anything without comparing value across theories — which is exactly the interpersonal-utility-comparison problem my field spends its life avoiding.
2) These acts look non-rival and non-excludable, i.e. a public good, not a private good. So why is the thing framed as barter and gains from trade at all, and how much of the argument survives once free riding is on the table?
3) The one I can't get out of my head: nobody can verify anyone elses moral theory, so your type is private information and you have every incentive to misreport it — announce that your theory cares deeply about X, get others to perform the expensive X-acts, and do the cheap ones yourself. There's no principal with a pot of money to run VCG with. So can any nontrivial moral-trade mechanism be incentive compatible, or is the whole scheme only stable inside small communities with reputation?

Be honest about the translation: flag every place where you had to quietly import an economics assumption to make it go through, and tell me what my vocabulary literally cannot say — the part where each side thinks the other is morally mistaken, and the no-causal-contact version. Then give me your verdict on whether that residue shows my framework is too thin, or shows a hole in the moral trade argument that its fans haven't noticed.
