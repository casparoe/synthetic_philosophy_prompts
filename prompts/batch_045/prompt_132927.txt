I host a podcast on the history and philosophy of science, and next week's guest is a historian of early modern astronomy who has just published a book arguing that Kepler's astrology is not the embarrassing leftover it is usually taken to be but one continuous research programme with his harmonics: a soul-like sensitive faculty in the Earth responds to those angular separations between planets that answer to regular figures inscribable in the ecliptic circle, and to no others. She drops the signs, houses, dignities and lots as Kepler did, keeps only what she calls the constructibility constraint, and maintains that what remains should be tested on weather and collective mood rather than on individual nativities.

Draft me seven interview questions, each pressing a place where this reconstruction is most likely to buckle rather than letting her restate it. I want at least one on: (i) the famous remark in which astrology appears as astronomy's foolish daughter who nonetheless has to feed her mother, and whether that is irony, an epistemic ranking, or just a comment about who paid for the tables; (ii) whether the constructibility constraint is principled or fitted to the data, given that it seems to licence many separations of the circle that play no part in Kepler's practice; (iii) whether a programme that has discarded signs, houses, dignities, lots and event-prediction is still recognisably astrology or has been reformed into emptiness; (iv) how a harmonic configuration is supposed to act on a sensitive faculty at all, given that Kepler's own celestial physics is what helped make efficient causation in the heavens thinkable without souls; (v) what could have counted as a failed prediction for someone who restricted himself to tendencies, characters and weather, in light of his forecasts of unrest in 1618. For at least three of them, add a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give. Close with one question about what evidence available now could count against the programme.
