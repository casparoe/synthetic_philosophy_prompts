I'm a junior double-majoring in art history and philosophy, and I'm the undergraduate member of our university's Public Art and Legacy Committee. I'm also taking a seminar this semester on aesthetics and the ethics of art -- we spent a couple of weeks on whether a work's moral defects, or its maker's, can count against it *as art* -- and it's made me uncomfortably aware of how much our committee improvises. I've been asked to draft the decision memo for our November meeting, and I'd like your help with it, because I keep going in circles.

The case: in 1961 a local painter named Alden Rees, who taught in our studio art department for nearly thirty years and is something of a civic icon here, painted an eleven-panel mural directly onto the curved plaster wall of the east corridor of the student union -- a harvest scene from life, with about forty identifiable faces of members of a nearby farming cooperative, including several of Rees's own neighbors. It's the best thing in our collection and arguably the most important surviving example of that regional mural style in the state. Last year, when a state archive opened its surveillance files, it emerged that Rees had been a paid informant from 1959 to 1965, and that at least two of the men whose faces are on that wall served prison terms in the late sixties on cases built partly on his reports. So the entanglement here is worse than the usual one: the qualities people cite when they call the mural great -- the closeness of observation, the fact that these are not generic types but people he knew and was trusted by, the tenderness of the faces -- are the very same qualities that made the betrayal possible, and once you know the archival material you can't see the tenderness without also seeing a kind of theft. Meanwhile the descendant families are split: one granddaughter has petitioned for the wall to be destroyed, and the grandson of another of the informant's targets has asked in writing that it stay, because it's the only image of his grandfather that exists and he thinks his grandfather would have wanted to be seen. Three further complications the memo has to live inside: the work is painted on curved plaster and cannot be moved without destroying most of its surface, so "relocate it" is not a real option -- the live options are leave it as is, leave it with new interpretive text, cover it with a retractable scrim, or destroy or paint it out; an anonymous donor has offered a large gift toward what the letter calls "restoring the corridor to bare brick," but the offer lapses at the end of the semester and is explicitly contingent on a vote to remove; and Rees's niece, who controls the estate, has said she will sue if the work is altered or destroyed, in a suit we'd probably win but which would tie the corridor up in litigation for years.

What I need is a memo I could actually put my name on and circulate: a proper header block, a one-sentence bottom line, a recommendation of one of the four options with a concrete sequence and timeframe (what the facilities office does before finals, what happens over the summer, when the decision gets revisited and by whom), and the reasoning behind it. Two things matter to me beyond the bottom line. First, I want the memo to take seriously both of the arguments I keep oscillating between -- that here, unusually, the moral defect is internal to the aesthetic achievement rather than merely adjacent to it, and that the faces arguably now belong to the people depicted and their families, so that erasing the work would injure them a second time -- and then to give the option it *doesn't* recommend the strongest version of itself, the version a colleague who favors it would read and say "yes, that's my argument." Second, our student government has formally asked us to produce a scoring rubric weighting "artistic significance" against "harm to campus climate" so that future cases can be settled uniformly; I want the memo to tell them, with reasons, whether we should hand over such a rubric or decline, and if we decline, what we should give them instead. Please close with three or four sentences of standing guidance for the three other works in our collection with comparable problems, one of which was donated by a living alumna whose name is on the building. Keep it in the register of a committee document rather than a term paper -- name the philosophical issue only where doing so helps someone act -- and don't hedge into "the committee may wish to consider"; I need a recommendation I'll have to defend out loud.
