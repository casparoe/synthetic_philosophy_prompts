Hi. I'm 71 and retired — I spent my career in electrophysiology, mostly single-channel recording, and about a year ago I joined a philosophy reading group at the local library. Take the role of a professional philosopher who has also spent time around real experiments, ideally someone who has worked with the people who test how ordinary subjects actually reason about cases. Before you answer, brainstorm: jot down six to ten candidate concepts from philosophy, psychology, law or rhetoric that might cover what I describe below, then cross out the ones that don't fit and say why each fails. Take your time with it.

Here's the pattern I keep running into. A philosophical case is set up to isolate one difference — five people die versus one, or psychological continuity with versus without material continuity. The author tells you explicitly which features are supposed to be doing the work and which are decoration. But then you change only the decoration and the verdict moves. Three examples from what we've read:

One. A vehicle is out of control on a track and will kill five people; you can throw a lever that sends it onto a side track where it kills one. Nearly everyone in the room says throw it. Then: same vehicle, same five, but the only way to stop it is to shove a heavyset stranger off a footbridge into its path, where his body stops it and he dies. Most of the room says no. Our group leader said the difference between those two is where the interesting work is. What I noticed: when I read the footbridge version first and the lever version second, two people who had said no to the footbridge said yes — and when I rewrote the footbridge case in flat language ("you stop the vehicle by increasing the mass on the track by means of a human body, which kills that person") instead of the hands-on, shoving-a-man-to-his-death wording, support for it went up as well.

Two. A machine records the exact state of your body, your body is then destroyed, and a perfect replica is built on Mars out of new matter; did you survive? About half of us said no. Then the same process described as: you go to sleep, and you wake up on Mars in a body that is not the same matter but is psychologically continuous with you — and several of the same people said yes. Nothing changed except words like "destroyed", "replica" and "new matter", and the person who wrote it insists material continuity is not what matters.

Three. A case about sending money to save a child: when the child has a name, a photo and a village, people answer differently than when it is "one of ten thousand children will die". Our leader said psychologists already have a name for that one, something like the identifiable victim effect — which makes me suspect cases one and two are just that same effect and there's nothing specially philosophical going on. But I'm not convinced, because in one and two the detail doing the work is a detail the argument itself instructs you to disregard.

So: is there an established concept for this — a case whose persuasive force is supplied by a feature that the case's own framing declares irrelevant? A friend gave me the phrase "intuition pump"; if that's the answer, I want to know whether my phenomenon is the whole of that word or one specific failure mode of it. If nothing established fits, coin a term and define it properly, and give me the edges: one clear instance and one near miss that looks like it but isn't. I've provisionally been calling it a sealed confound, a confound the case tells you to hold fixed. I recieved a couple of nudges toward rhetorical notions like loaded questions and persuasive definitions, so tell me if those cover it or if they're something else entirely.

Two more things. First, I want a test, not just a label: some procedure like a control condition or a blind that I could run on a case in a paper to find out whether its verdict really does depend on the avowed-irrelevant detail — bearing in mind my subject pool is me and about six retirees, and I can't randomize or blind anything properly. And tell me honestly whether my lab analogy is any good: no controls, no blinding, something like experimenter expectancy, the instrument's zero drifting with what the operator hopes to see. Where does it break? In a real experiment you can separate the person running it from the person being measured; here they're the same person.

Second, and this is the part I care about: commit. Give me a definite position on which of my three examples genuinely has the artifact and which don't — if you think I've bundled two or three different things together, say which is which and why, and pick the one that best fits the concept you're offering. Tell me how confident you are, in plain terms, and say what would change your mind. Don't give me a balanced menu of options with the choice left to me, and don't point me at a reading list — our library has no journal access, so anything I can't reconstruct from the argument itself is useless to me. Oh, and if you think one of my three examples is definately fine and I'm reading an artifact into it that isn't there, say so directly.
