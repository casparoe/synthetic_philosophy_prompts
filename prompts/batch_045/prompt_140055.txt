Write a ghazal of six couplets on the difference between indicative and counterfactual conditionals: twelve lines, autonomous couplets as the form requires, and one fixed refrain, the same phrase in every couplet: "it happened anyway." The refrain should sound identical in both moods while doing entirely different work. Closed by an indicative antecedent it costs almost nothing to assert, since once you know how things came out you may attach that outcome to any supposition you have not ruled out. Closed by a counterfactual antecedent it costs everything, since it claims that the actual past, rerun with that one thing forced, re-converges on what in fact happened. Alternate the moods — couplets one, three and five indicative, two, four and six counterfactual — and do not let the counterfactual couplets use "would" or "have": the missing auxiliary just is the difference, and the reader should feel its absence rather than be told about it.

Build the poem from this invented case rather than from the textbook examples. A search committee never opens the third file, a drawer that sticks; D is not shortlisted; the job goes to R in June. Had the file been read, D would have been shortlisted; had D been shortlisted, the committee would have split; had it split, the dean would have intervened and reopened the search, and there R is not hired. Every link holds and the chain from "the file was read" to "R was not hired" does not, because at each step the imagined divergence is reabsorbed into the facts you were never asked to give up. Let the closing couplet do what the maqta does — the speaker signing — and let the signature be a confession: she is the one who never pulled the drawer.

No epigraph, notes, or afterword; the poem itself has to carry the distinction. Someone who knows this literature should be able to tell which couplets are which without being told, and someone who does not should still read a poem about a woman who cannot decide whether she is being consoled.
