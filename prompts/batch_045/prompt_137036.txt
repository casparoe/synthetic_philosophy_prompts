I've been reading about satisficing and bounded rationality on my own for about a year — no philosophy or economics background, I got into it because flat-hunting in London broke my brain a bit, and then I noticed the same thing happening with job applications and, honestly, with picking something to watch. The basic move — set a bar in advance, take the first option that clears it, stop looking — seems obviously right to me in practice and I've more or less started living by it. But when I push on it I keep hitting questions I can't tell are deep or just me being confused. And I've got limited resources here: a couple of evenings a week, no maths past GCSE, no library access, and I mainly want to end up with a decision procedure I'd actually defend out loud to my partner rather than something I could publish.

So: if you were me, what would you spend those evenings on? Which open questions about satisficing and bounded rationality would you prioritise, and which would you drop as a bad use of someone in my position? I don't want a survey of the area and I don't want suggestions for what to read. I want an ordering with the reasoning showing — what makes a question worth the time, how many other questions sit downstream of it, whether it's the kind of thing someone with no formal tools can make progress on just by thinking carefully, or whether it needs machinery I don't have or has been stuck for decades for a reason. Some of the things I keep circling, in case they help (tell me if any of these already have a name, or are just muddled):

- Threshold drift. My bar definately slides during a long search — stuff I'd have rejected in week one looks fine by week six. If the threshold moves, isn't "satisficing" just whatever I happened to end up with, and does that matter?
- The backtrack thing. The story I read assumes you can go back to an option you passed over. Sometimes you can (the flat is still on the market), often you can't (the candidate took another job). Does the whole advice change depending on which, or is that a detail?
- Reshaping instead of choosing. Satisficing treats the options as fixed and the only question as which one to take. But most real options get modified after you take them — you rewrite the job, you repaint the flat. If that's a big part of what's actually going on, maybe search isn't the right frame at all and I've been optimising the wrong thing.
- Cheap search. On apps, searching is nearly free — swipe costs nothing. By the logic of bounded rationality that should make everything easy, and yet people seem to end up worse off and more exhausted. Is that a real problem for the idea or just a separate story about feeds?
- Joint satisficing. Two people with different bars and veto power. My guess is this is where the interesting version of the problem lives and also where it stops being solvable by just being sensible.

Please rank them, or tell me the ones I haven't thought of matter more, and use British English. And be straight about the epistemic side: where are you most likely to be wrong or just guessing, which of these are you least sure are even real questions, and what would change your mind about the ordering — I'd rather have the cruxes than a confident list.
