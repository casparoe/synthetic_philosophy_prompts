I work on the scheduler and admission-control layer of a multi-tenant distributed database, so the vocabulary I actually think in is that of consensus protocols and capacity planning: nodes and quorums, unanimity versus majority, per-tenant SLOs and p99 latency, safety versus liveness, a global-throughput objective versus a floor guarantee for every tenant, tail risk, starvation, and the difference between a property of a single scheduling decision and a property of a whole run. A colleague and I were arguing about whether we should keep a hard per-tenant latency floor, which visibly costs us aggregate throughput when the cluster is hot, or switch to maximizing the sum of tenant utility and accept that the same few tenants eat the queueing delay. She said this is just the aggregation problem for Scanlon's contractualism in a different notation: the per-tenant floor is the contractualist option, because contractualism tests a principle by whether anyone could reasonably reject it and refuses to let many small gains to many people outweigh one person's serious burden, whereas the global objective is utilitarian and adds everything up. That struck me as both right and too glib — renaming "no one can reasonably reject" as "every node has a veto" doesn't tell me whether the two are the same thing.

So I'd like the translation done properly rather than as a slogan. Restate contractualism into my vocabulary as faithfully as you can: the fact that it evaluates principles or rules rather than individual acts or individual outcomes (which I take to be like evaluating a protocol rather than a particular execution of it); the standpoint of each individual person; reasonable rejectability as a test a principle has to pass for each person separately; the individualist restriction on the reasons that can be appealed to; the way a single person's larger burden is supposed to defeat a much larger number of much smaller benefits, and the way numbers enter, if they enter at all, only by breaking ties between otherwise comparable complaints; and the point at which contractualism gets into trouble with large numbers of small losses. Then give me the residue report: which of these translate cleanly onto quorums, vetoes, per-tenant guarantees, and tail risk; which translate only if I quietly smuggle in something my vocabulary doesn't have; and which have no expression at all in how I talk about scheduling — I suspect "justifiable to each person, as opposed to merely good or bad for each person" is where the machinery runs out. Say for each piece of residue whether it shows that my vocabulary is missing a distinction or that contractualism is less clear than it looks, and whether the aggregation problem is a real fault line or just a clash of intuitions about numbers that a protocol designer would settle by fiat.

Two wrinkles I want addressed inside that report, because they're the ones my job actually turns on. First: our scheduler re-decides every couple of seconds, and in any given round the losing tenant's complaint is small enough that overruling it looks reasonable, but over an hour the same tenant is always the loser. Does contractualism have any way of registering a cumulative complaint across a sequence of decisions, or does it evaluate the schedule as a whole (in which case the complaint does register, and my per-round framing was the distortion)? Second: in consensus protocols there is a magic number — a quorum, a tolerated number of faulty nodes — and once you cross it the protocol's behavior changes qualitatively. Is there any analogue of that threshold for the number of complainers in contractualism, or is the count of people literally irrelevant except in tie-breaking, and what does that say about how different it really is from my world?

Answer as a numbered list of exactly seven items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion: the mapping, what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion and what the distortion hides, what has no expression in my vocabulary at all, your verdict on the residue, the sequential-scheduling wrinkle, and the threshold wrinkle. Please write in American English.
