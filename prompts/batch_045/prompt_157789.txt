I've been chewing on the Friedman thing for about a year, by which I mean that move where unrealistic assumptions in a model are supposed to be not just forgivable but something like a selling point: the more unrealistic the assumptions, the more general and wide-ranging the theory, the more situations it covers. (I think it comes from an essay sometime in the 50s; I'm not fussy about the details.) The reason I can't let go of it is a case from my own work:

At our depot there's a model that predicts how many trucks will be sitting at the loading docks each hour. It assumes every driver takes the same time to unload, that drivers don't slow down over a shift, and that a dock never goes down. All three are flatly false. It predicted queue lengths at our main site really well for two years. Then two things happened: we let it talk us into removing a buffer bay, and we opened a second site with the same layout but a different shift pattern. At site two it was off by roughly a factor of two, and without the buffer bay the whole thing gridlocked.

I read that as: false assumptions, great performance inside the regime it was tuned in, and failure exactly when the model was used to pick an intervention rather than just to predict, and when it was moved to a new regime.

Here are my own pet distinctions, which I'd like you to work into the document — use them where they survive, and say plainly where they collapse:

- I dont think "it works" is one thing. I'd seperate (1) predicting held-out data from the same regime, (2) surviving an intervention the model itself recommended, and (3) transferring to a new site or regime. I suspect the two camps weight these differently and that most of the argument is really about which one counts as working.
- The swap test: take one known-false assumption, replace it with a truer version, and see whether the predictions move. If they don't, was the assumption decorative? Both camps should have a view on what the swap test does and doesn't prove — and each should predict what it would show about the equal-unload-time assumption at site two.
- Load-bearing versus decorative false assumptions, plus the case where two false assumptions are wrong in opposite directions and the errors cancel. Can a model be right for the wrong reasons twice over, and does that matter as long as it keeps predicting well?
- The audit problem: can you certify a false assumption harmless without already knowing the real mechanism? If you can't, doesn't the as-if camp secretly need the mechanism camp's work?
- And my own worry about how many model variants get tried before one fits. If you quietly tried forty and kept the best, does the fit still count as evidence — and does the generality claim depend on nobody keeping score?
