I teach a three-session module on the foundations of probability to graduate students in statistics and machine learning. My students use probability daily and are comfortable with measure theory, but few of them have ever been asked what a probability statement means, and when I ask them directly which interpretation they accept, I mostly get back whatever word happened to be used in their first textbook. So I want an instrument that sorts them by what they are willing to say about cases, not by which label they recognize.

Please write me a decision tree that a student can walk alone, in about ten minutes, starting from a sentence every one of them would happily assert and ending at a named view about the nature of probability. The starting sentence should be an unrepeatable single-case claim of the kind my students actually make: this particular classifier is wrong on this particular image, with probability about 0.7 — an event that will happen once, in a set-up with no symmetry to appeal to, produced by a procedure whose calibration can nonetheless be measured over many other images.

Constraints on the instrument:

Entry and internal nodes. Ten to twelve branch questions, each phrased as a question about a concrete case, never as a question about a theory, and worded so that a student who has never heard the names can answer it. Each node should have two or three answer options, and where an answer does not in fact force a subsequent path, or where two nodes cross-cut rather than nest, say so explicitly instead of hiding it. Among the nodes I want the following cases used, placed wherever they genuinely do diagnostic work rather than distributed for show:

- a coin that is flipped exactly once and then destroyed, with no repetitions before or after, in a world that ends shortly afterwards;
- the same flip described by a perfectly deterministic classical model, where the outcome is fixed by the exact initial conditions;
- a one-off event whose only available statistics come from a contested reference class — a patient who belongs to several overlapping classes that give different rates, and no class that is more natural than the others;
- a claim about how probable a theory in the student's own field is, before it has been tested at all, where the theory is the sort of thing of which there is at most one instance;
- two agents with exactly the same total evidence who assign different numbers to the same event, and neither can be shown to be incoherent;
- a directional case: there is a well-defined tendency for an emitted particle to produce a click at a detector, and I then ask for the probability, given the click, that the particle was emitted — that is, for the inverse of the tendency;
- a forecaster who assigns numbers to hundreds of one-off events and is later scored by how often the events occurred, where I ask what exactly the score is checking;
- a long run of trials after which the world stops for good, so that no actual limiting frequency will ever exist, only the frequency in the finite run;
- two possible worlds that agree in every particular matter of fact — every event, every property instantiated, every pattern in the total history — and I ask whether the chance of heads could still differ between them.

Leaves. Ten to twelve leaves, each with the name of the view, a one-sentence statement of what it says, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. These must appear as leaves wherever the questions put them: strict finite or actual frequentism; hypothetical or limiting frequentism; long-run propensity; single-case propensity; a Humean best-system account, on which the chances are whatever the best balance of simplicity and informativeness over the actual occurrent facts says they are; a non-Humean primitivism that takes chance as an irreducible feature of the world; logical or evidential probability, on which probabilistic support is a matter of relations between propositions; an objective Bayesianism that constrains degrees of belief by symmetry or maximum-entropy-style considerations; a subjectivism that constrains them only by coherence; and an error-theoretic or fictionalist leaf on which objective chance talk is systematically untrue but useful. If the questions generate a legitimate region that no named view occupies, keep it as an unnamed leaf and mark it as such.

Build the space before filling it. I would rather see the tree constructed from the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, with the named views then located inside that space, than see a list of views with questions reverse-engineered to reach them. For each combination of answers that is empty, give one sentence on why: whether it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unoccupied by anyone's published view.

Separations I expect to fail, and want flagged. I suspect my tree will not really be able to separate the logical-probability leaf from the objective-Bayesian leaf, nor the long-run-propensity leaf from the hypothetical-frequentist leaf. If you think different pairs collapse, or that the ones I have named can in fact be separated by a case I have not thought of, say which and why, and flag every leaf that no question distinguishes from a sibling.

Format. An indented outline or an ASCII flowchart that fits on a single side of a handout: internal nodes as questions, edge labels as answers, leaves as the named views. No citations, reading lists, or historical attributions are needed; I want the argumentative structure, not the bibliography.

Finally, one case I want you to deal with rather than route around. The starting sentence — a calibrated probability for a genuinely unrepeatable event, used to make a decision — does not sit comfortably in any of the leaves I listed: it is a single case with no symmetry, it is not a frequency in any run, it is not plausibly a physical disposition of the classifier, and yet it is the output of a procedure whose calibration is empirically checkable, and my students will reasonably want to know what makes it true or false. Tell me which leaf it lands on, or, if none of them can hold it, say so and either add a leaf for it or explain what has to be added to an existing leaf to accommodate it. Do not let the tree reach a leaf in this case by quietly reinterpreting the sentence as a claim about something else.
