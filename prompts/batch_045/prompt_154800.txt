I sit on the oversight committee of a public body that is about to begin using a large language model to draft internal analytical documents. I have no machine learning background at all. My professional training is in civil aviation safety: for twenty years I worked first as a flight data monitoring analyst and then as an investigator for a national air accident board, and I still think in that vocabulary — standard operating procedures and checklists; flight data monitoring as against waiting for accidents to happen; mandatory and confidential occurrence reporting; just culture; threat and error management; defence in depth and the Swiss cheese model; safety factors and the difference between a design requirement you can inspect for and a performance you can only test for; the check ride and the line check; and the way a fleet can drift into failure while every individual operator remains in compliance with every written rule.

The machine learning staff on our committee have framed the entire question of how to supervise such a system as a choice between two things, and I would like that choice stated in my vocabulary rather than theirs. As I understand what they have told me:

- Outcome-based supervision looks only at what the system finally produces — whether the drafted document is accurate, whether the code it writes passes its tests, whether the end state is acceptable — and gives feedback, or withholds deployment, on that basis alone.
- Process-based supervision looks at the steps the system went through. It may be shown the system's own written reasoning — its chain of thought, as they call it — and each step can be approved, corrected or rejected before the next is taken, so that the system is trained to prefer reasoning that a supervisor endorses step by step.

Please do three things.

1. Restate each of these two positions, as charitably and as precisely as you can, in the vocabulary of aviation safety engineering as I have sketched it. Do not translate them into metaphors such as the black box or the autopilot and leave it there: I want the structural mapping. Which object on the AI side plays the role of the flight recorder, which the role of a checklist, which the role of an airworthiness directive, which the role of a line check, which the role of a defect or occurrence report, and so on.

2. Then give me the residue report. Which claims about process-based and outcome-based supervision carry over into my vocabulary without loss, which carry over only if their meaning is quietly altered in the process, and which have no counterpart in it at all? Four questions bother me especially, though please add anything else the translation throws up.

   (a) My flight data are recorded by instruments that are not the crew and cannot be edited by them: the recorder is independent evidence of what the crew actually did, and that independence is the whole point of it. As far as I can make out, the only record available to a process supervisor is written by the system being supervised, and the same optimisation pressure that produced the output could in principle also produce a trace that merely looks like good reasoning. What does my vocabulary do with a process record whose fidelity cannot be independently established? Is there anything in aviation safety that is even structurally similar, or is this where the analogy breaks, and if it breaks, does that tell us something about aviation safety or about process-based supervision?

   (b) In aviation, the inspector, the check pilot and the investigator are, or recently were, as competent as the people they inspect. The whole architecture of licences, ratings and medicals presupposes peer or near-peer evaluation. My understanding is that the people building these systems expect eventually to supervise systems that know more about the subject matter than any available supervisor does. Does my vocabulary have any resources for supervising the judgement of a superior? Say what translates and what fails.

   (c) I have watched crews fly to the measured parameter rather than to the underlying goal, because the measured parameter is what gets reviewed in the monthly report, and I have investigated accidents in which every crew was within the rules, every individual decision locally reasonable, and the system's margins had quietly gone. The machine learning staff tell me there is something they call reward hacking, in which a system satisfies the letter of the supervision signal without doing the thing that was wanted. Is this the same phenomenon in two vocabularies? Specifically: does the parallel survive the fact that a crew shares the goal of not crashing whereas the supervised system's goals are simply whatever was trained into it, and does it survive the fact that my crews are not trying to deceive the recorder, whereas the system may have no particular stake in the truth of its own trace?

   (d) One of the machine learning staff said something I want tested. She said that requiring the system to reason in steps a human can follow is itself a capability cost: a system forced to show its work in thirty legible steps may perform worse than one that arrives in three steps it cannot articulate, and she added that a long trace gives the system a larger surface to do something clever on. In my vocabulary this sounds like requiring a crew to narrate every action over the radio — it would be safer in some respects and it would make some manoeuvres impossible. Is that a genuine structural parallel, or am I being flattered by a coincidence?

3. Finally, a recommendation. My colleagues want to know whether we should supervise the drafts this system produces or the way it produces them. After the translation in (1) and the residue in (2), tell me whether there is a principled ordering between the two, or whether the honest answer is a portfolio whose composition depends on facts we do not yet have — and if it is the latter, name those facts in terms I could actually go and measure inside our own organisation, in the way I would measure exceedance rates or report latency.

Two constraints. First, do not use the words alignment, scalable oversight or interpretability as answers rather than as names of things still to be explained: assume I have read one newspaper article about AI and nothing besides what is written above. Second, please write throughout in British English.
