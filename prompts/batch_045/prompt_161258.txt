I'm a journalist who mostly covers science and ideas, but I'm doing a long reported piece on the afterlife of the Fischer controversy — Fritz Fischer's 1961 book arguing that the Imperial German government more or less seized on the July Crisis as a window for a bid at world power, versus Christopher Clark's *Sleepwalkers* half a century later spreading the thing back out into a continental stumble — and the bit I keep tripping over isn't the archival fight, its that nobody in these debates ever says what *Germany* names in the sentence *Germany was responsible*. My editor wants a normative spine for the piece, so I sat down and wrote out my own gut verdicts on a pile of cases, and they definately don't add up.

Mine, in no order: Germany in 1914 — yes, blame, and I mean something about the state and also, hazily, something about the German public that cheered in August. Austria-Hungary over the ultimatum to Serbia — blame for starting a Balkan war but not, I feel, for the world war, even though the escalation was plainly forseeable from Vienna, which looks inconsistent of me. The Serbian government and the Black Hand — almost no blame on the state: it tolerated the networks without authorising the assassination, and I want *tolerating* to be a lot weaker than *authorising*. Russia's general mobilisation — blame, but of a different flavour, closer to *you turned a Balkan war into a European one* than to *you started it*. Britain — not to blame for going in, but I do blame Grey for never making the commitment to France legible beforehand, which is blame for failing to prevent rather than for doing. The cases that actually embarrass me: I'll say *Germany started it* about 1914 and then balk at *Germans started it*, while with Iraq 2003 I say both *America started it* and *Americans started it* without flinching, since they reelected the government mid-war. And I blame the American public, diffusely, for Iraq, but not for the post-election covert targeted-killing programme, which was also reported on and also continued after a vote — and I can't tell you what the difference is meant to be. I also think the Versailles war-guilt clause was corrupt moral bookkeeping even granting Fischer was substantively right: blame and liability feel like different currencies and I don't know why.

What I've been scribbling as a candidate is something I call the audit test: a political community is blameworthy for a war only if it had some functioning route by which opposition could have registered and actually changed the decision — a press that would print it, a parliament whose vote mattered, an electorate that could plausibly have turned the leaders out — with blame scaled by how well that route in fact worked, so the subjects of a police state come out near zero and the citizens of a functioning democracy come out meaningfully above it. Test that against my verdicts, repair it or kill it, tell me which clause each case forces and which of my verdicts I have to give up. Then two cases I haven't decided and want your principle's verdict on. One: a state whose leader starts a war after winning a referendum in which 58% explicitly authorised it — my instinct is the public eats most of the blame and the leader less, but the audit test as I've written it seems to push the other way. Two, and this is the one I actually care about: a war that begins because automated early-warning systems on both sides misread a routine test launch as an incoming strike and the humans have maybe four minutes, so every decision is locally reasonable and nobody authors anything. Does your account say *no one is to blame*, does it relocate the blame onto whoever built and fielded the systems, or does it just break? And if it says no one, is that the right answer or a reductio?
