I'm writing a short paper for a graduate seminar on material objects, and I have managed to paint myself into a corner. I want to defend a moderate answer to the special composition question — the organicist line, on which some things compose a further thing just when they are caught up in the activity of a single life. My supervisor remarked, rather casually, that no moderate answer can survive the argument from vagueness, and then sketched a version of it that I have since written out in full. Every step looked innocent while I was writing it, but the conclusion is one I cannot accept, and I am fairly sure some single quiet move is doing all the work. A friend added that the template generalises — run it on neighbouring constitutive questions and it seems to rule out moderate answers there too — which is why I now think the argument is a machine for producing absurdity rather than a refutation of my view. I just cannot locate the faulty part.

Here is the argument, with the numbering I use in my draft. (1) The special composition question asks for a condition C such that, necessarily, for any xs, the xs compose something if and only if C(xs); and a genuine answer must be fully general — statable without naming particular objects, places, times or kinds — and must specify a mind-independent matter of fact, not something that varies with how the xs are described or with anyone's interests or conventions. (2) C may not be sortal-relative: it cannot say that the xs compose something if and only if they compose a cat, or a table, or an organism, since whether there are things of those kinds is exactly what an answer to the composition question was supposed to settle. (3) Nor may C be a bare disjunction of unrelated conditions, nor may it be brute: it must state what it is about the xs in virtue of which they compose — so "either there is just one of the xs, or the activity of the xs constitutes a life" is no answer. (4) So C has to be given in non-sortal, unified, explanatory terms: causal integration, functional organisation, cohesive bonding, metabolic unity, joint participation in a single life, and the like. (5) Each of these answers to a scalar dimension — cohesion, integration, metabolic coupling all come in degrees — and they come in degrees continuously: the biology of colonial organisms (siphonophores, fungal networks, slime moulds) shows that between a single polyp and a diffuse colony there is no natural break. (6) A condition that answers to a continuous scalar dimension has borderline cases: for some precisely specified xs — and I have been careful to specify the xs precisely, so the slack is not in which things I am talking about — it is indeterminate whether C(xs). The only escape is to impose a sharp threshold somewhere along the dimension, and any such threshold would be arbitrary; an arbitrary threshold cannot be the necessarily correct answer to a metaphysical question. (7) Given the necessary biconditional in (1), indeterminacy transfers: if it is indeterminate whether C(xs), then it is indeterminate whether the xs compose something — that is, indeterminate whether there is a y that the xs compose. (8) But existence is never indeterminate: "there is a y that the xs compose" is determinately true or determinately false, which is what the familiar case against vague identity is supposed to secure, and which I am happy to take for granted. (9) Hence no condition meeting (1) to (3) exists, and the only coherent answers left are "always" and "never" — unrestricted composition or nihilism. And those two say nothing about what it is in virtue of which the xs compose, so they fall foul of (3) as well: the special composition question has no answer at all. There is obviously something wrong here, since the question plainly has an answer and I think it is mine.
