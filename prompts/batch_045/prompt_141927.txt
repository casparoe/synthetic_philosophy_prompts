ok so i'm a junior and i'm on this student-faculty committee at my school that's been trying to rewrite the phone policy for like a year and a half, and we're completely stuck in the same loop every single meeting. There's basically two groups. One side (mostly teachers plus a couple of kids) says the problem with the old policy wasn't the rules, it was that nobody enforced them the same way, so whether you got in trouble depended on which hallway monitor happened to see you, and that's just unfair, and the fix is clear rules applied identically to everyone every time. The other side (mostly students plus one teacher) says any rule with no way of being adjusted to the actual situation is going to be wrong for somebody, and if your only protection is hoping you get a nice enforcer, then you've made it a lottery anyway, so flexibility has to be built into the rule itself. Then someone says "so you want no rules," someone else says "no, i want better rules," and we start over. I looked into it a bit because it felt bigger than our school, and there's this whole research literature on "tight" vs "loose" cultures — societies where norms are strong and deviance gets punished hard vs ones where norms are weaker and there's more tolerance — and the big version of our question is just how much rule-following a society should actually demand. Reading about that didn't unstick us at all though.

I think I might know why, but I'm not confident. My guess is that "tight vs loose" is secretly three different dials that happen to move together in the real world, which is why everyone can feel right at the same time: (1) how many areas of life have a rule at all, (2) how harsh the sanction is when you break one, and (3) how easy and how humiliating it is to argue "this rule shouldn't really apply to me here" — like, is there an accepted, cheap, non-embarrassing way to get an exception after the fact, or is even asking a social risk. I bet the scale people actually use mostly tracks (1) and (2), and that (3) usually moves opposite to them in real societies, so nobody notices it's a separate thing. And my actual hypothesis about our committee is that neither side is really fighting about (1) or (2): the "consistency" people would happily accept fewer rules if they were enforced identically every time, and the "flexibility" people would accept plenty of rules if there were a decent built-in way to be excused. So the real fight is about (3) and it's been invisible because we don't have a word for it. There's also probably a straight-up verbal bit where "fair" means "the same for everybody" to one side and "responsive to who you actually are" to the other.
