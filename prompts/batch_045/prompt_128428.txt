I work as a statistician at a small environmental consultancy, and until about three months ago I had never had any professional reason to think about Bertrand's paradox. I now have to sign a report that turns on it.

The setting: a client holds a licence for works on the edge of a wetland reserve that is, to within the tolerances that matter, a disc. The licence conditions require an estimate of a quantity defined by sampling "straight-line transects selected uniformly at random from the set of all straight lines meeting the reserve", and the regulation says nothing more than that. Two of my analysts produced two estimates that differ by enough to move the predicted figure across the threshold the licence decision turns on; neither made an error. One sampled by taking two independent points uniformly on the boundary and joining them, the other by taking a radius uniformly and a point uniformly along it and drawing the perpendicular. As you will recognise, this is Bertrand's chord problem in field dress: those two schemes answer the corresponding threshold question as 1/3 and 1/2, and a third available scheme (the midpoint uniform over the area of the disc) answers it as 1/4.

My principal statistician's response is that we are overthinking this, and he leans on an analogy that everyone in the office has since started repeating: it is, he says, exactly like a fair die or a properly shuffled deck. A real randomiser is a physical object with a fixed, finite, already-individuated set of cases, and the symmetries of the set-up force the chances onto them; if you get a paradox, you have simply failed to say what the randomiser was. He uses it to license three conclusions, and I need to know how much of this survives: (i) that in any real case like ours there is a single fact of the matter about what "uniformly at random" requires; (ii) that the paradox is nothing but an incompletely specified question, so that specifying the chance set-up — the die, the shuffle, or in our case an explicit drone protocol — determines the answer; and (iii) that we should therefore write down a protocol, report whatever it gives, and treat any residual disagreement as philosophical noise the regulator can safely ignore.

What makes me doubt it is this. We can make our protocol perfectly explicit and it does not seem to settle anything, because the regulation does not ask for the output of a protocol; it asks for a sample uniform over the set of lines, which is supposed to be protocol-independent. And every protocol we can write down is uniform only relative to a parametrisation we have chosen — a launch point uniform in boundary arc with a heading uniform in angle is not the same distribution as a launch point uniform in the area of the reserve with a heading uniform in angle, and both are honestly described as "uniform by symmetry". There is also a mathematical worry I can only half-articulate: all three classical schemes are rotation-invariant, so the disc's symmetry group does not discriminate between them, whereas with a die the symmetry group seems to me to force the answer. But I cannot tell whether that is the crux or a red herring.
