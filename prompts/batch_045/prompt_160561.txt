"Kant's categorical imperative says: act only on a maxim that you can will to be a universal law. A maxim is the rule you are really acting on, stated together with your end, so this is a test on your reasons and not on your outward behaviour. You take your maxim, ask what it would be like if it became a law of nature — that is, if everyone in your circumstances did the same thing as automatically and reliably as a stone falls — and then see whether the result is thinkable. With the famous case of the deceptive loan, it isn't: if I act on 'when I need money I'll get it by promising repayment that I don't intend to make,' the universalized version destroys the practice of promising that my own maxim was exploiting, so the maxim annihilates itself and I have a perfect duty not to act on it. Other maxims can be conceived perfectly well, but I could not rationally will the world they produce: a world in which nobody ever helps anyone could get along well enough, as Kant himself grants, so there is no contradiction in conception there either — the only problem is that I might one day want help myself. Those yield the imperfect duties, developing one's talents and aiding others, and they are derived in exactly the same way as the first two; they simply leave you more latitude about when and how to comply. The upshot is that the test is essentially the golden rule with better bookkeeping, and that it is meant to be purely formal: nothing about what you happen to be trying to achieve can bear on whether an action is permissible, since all that matters is whether the rule has the form of a law. That formality is also the test's fatal flaw, and it's the reason hardly anyone defends it any more: because it refuses to look at content, it cannot rule out a universal law of universal indifference to others, and it can only extract the imperfect duties by smuggling in an empirical assumption about what we all necessarily want."
