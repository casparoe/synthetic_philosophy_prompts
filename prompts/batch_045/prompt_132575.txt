I teach a first-year seminar called Formal Reasoning: 24 students, one 75-minute session, and the only machinery they have is truth tables and enough probability to compute an expected value. Next week I want them to run into the surprise exam paradox rather than watch me derive it. Last year I wrote the standard announcement on the board and walked through the backward induction, and the response was a shrug: they said the teacher could just not give the exam, or that surprise only means unlikely, or that the whole thing was a trick with words. So this time I want a game they actually play, with a scoring rule and real (small) stakes, and the name of the paradox withheld until the debrief.

Here is the rough thing I have been turning over, which you should feel free to scrap if you have something better. Instead of trying to operationalize knowledge, operationalize surprise as a public forecast: the class is one team and must produce a single number each morning, the probability they assign to the quiz being that day. You (or a rotating student) play the teacher and seal a day in an envelope at the start of the week, in full view of everyone. When the quiz day arrives, the quiz only counts as a surprise if the class's number that morning was below a threshold, say 20%; the teacher's team scores for a surprising quiz, the class scores for accuracy, and I was going to use a quadratic scoring rule so that their number has to be an honest probability rather than a low one. My hope is that the conflict is the point: an accurate class makes surprise impossible, but a class that reasons its way to the last day has already made itself accurate about the last day, and so on down the week. I would also like rounds with different structures to see what the collapse depends on: a two-day week as well as a five-day one, and one round where the teacher writes the day down where everyone can see it versus one where the teacher privately hints at the day to a single student, because I suspect the whole thing is really about what is common knowledge and the difference between the two rounds will show that better than anything I could say.

What I need from you is the runnable version: rules, materials, the scoring scheme and a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes including at least three rounds and a debrief. Give me the debrief as a mapping from moments in the game onto parts of the argument — where the last day gets eliminated, where the elimination propagates, and where the students should be able to see both that the reasoning looks valid and that the teacher surprised them anyway. Tell me the misconceptions to expect in the order they will surface and the moment in the sequence to defuse each (my guesses: that unannounced non-occurrence is an escape, that surprise is just low probability, that randomizing the day resolves it, and that not being able to deduce the date is the same as being surprised by it), plus what will predictably go wrong when I run it — collusion, refusing to commit to one number, the degenerate final morning, someone announcing that mixed strategies settle it — and how to handle each. Be frank about which feature of the original problem the game cannot reproduce and which premise my forecast-for-knowledge substitution quietly changes, and tell me what would change your mind about the design: what you would see in the room, or what alternative operationalization, would convince you that the game teaches nothing transferable about the epistemic version of the puzzle.
