I am preparing a graduate seminar on Berkeley's economic writings, and I would like your help reconstructing the argument of the following passage from Berkeley's The Querist, first published in 1735, before I risk an anachronistic reading in lecture. The passage, consisting of Queries 21–26, runs as follows:

"21 Whether other things being given, as climate, soil, etc., the wealth be not proportioned to the industry, and this to the circulation of credit, be the credit circulated or transferred by what marks or tokens soever?

22 Whether, therefore, less money swiftly circulating, be not, in effect, equivalent to more money slowly circulating? Or, whether, if the circulation be reciprocally as the quantity of coin, the nation can be a loser?

23 Whether money is to be considered as having an intrinsic value, or as being a commodity, a standard, a measure, or a pledge, as is variously suggested by writers? And whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?

24 Whether the value or price of things be not a compounded proportion, directly as the demand, and reciprocally as the plenty?

25 Whether the terms crown, livre, pound sterling, etc., are not to be considered as exponents or denominations of such proportion? And whether gold, silver, and paper are not tickets or counters for reckoning, recording, and transferring thereof?

26 Whether the denominations being retained, although the bullion were gone, things might not nevertheless be rated, bought, and sold, industry promoted, and a circulation of commerce maintained?"

In my own attempt to get clear about this, I have been using a distinction between what I call "substantive" and "notational" accounts of money. A substantive account explains the power of a monetary object to transfer wealth by some non-relational property of the object itself, typically its metallic content or its scarce natural usefulness; a notational account explains that power entirely by the object's place within a practice of denominating, reckoning, and transferring claims, so that, in principle, any stable token could serve provided the notation and the practice of transfer survive. I do not need a bibliography, and I am not asking you to adjudicate the secondary literature; I am asking you to work with the distinction as I have defined it.

Please help me understand how the six queries hang together, and answer the following specific questions. (1) In Queries 21–22 Berkeley ties wealth to industry and industry to the circulation of credit, transferable by whatever marks or tokens one likes. How should we understand credit here? Is it a claim about the transfer of labor, about trust, or simply about accounting entries? Does this prepare the way for the ticket-or-counter claim in Query 23, or is Query 23 a separate claim of a different kind? (2) In Query 23, is Berkeley rejecting the whole list of candidates he associates with the writers he mentions — intrinsic value, commodity, standard, measure, pledge — or only the first item, leaving the others as potentially compatible with the ticket or counter idea? What work is the word altogether doing? (3) In Query 25, what is meant by calling crown, livre, pound sterling exponents or denominations of such proportion? Should exponent be read in a strict mathematical sense, as an index or power, or in the looser sense of a visible sign or name of a ratio? How does this bear on his claim that gold, silver, and paper are all equally tickets or counters for reckoning, recording, and transferring thereof? (4) Query 26 imagines that the denominations are retained while the bullion is gone. Does this establish, for Berkeley, that the material composition of money is eliminable in principle, or does his immaterialism — the view that reality consists exclusively of minds and their ideas — require that even a mere counter remain some perceivable idea, so that a wholly notational money without any perceivable token would be unintelligible to him? (5) Finally, does the passage as a whole amount to a reductive definition of money in terms of the notational-transfer role, or is it only a practical claim about what is sufficient to maintain industry and circulation, leaving the metaphysics of the token open? Please be explicit about where the text supports your reconstruction and where you are extrapolating, and tell me if my substantive/notational distinction distorts the passage rather than clarifying it.
