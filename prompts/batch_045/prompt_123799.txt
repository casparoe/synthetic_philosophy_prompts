Hey — I write long-form features on science and ideas, and last year I did a big one on cliodynamics (Turchin's secular cycles, structural-demographic models, the whole "can history be a forecasting science" question). It got enough attention that I've been asked to give the closing talk at a small invitation-only workshop next month, and I want to walk in actually prepared, because this room is not going to be friendly in a vague, general-interest kind of way.

The room: about twenty people, mostly historians — early modern Europe plus a few who work on imperial China and on the Roman/early Islamic material. They care about archival specificity, contingency, and narrative explanation, and every one of them has read something that looked to them like Spengler with differential equations. Then there are two formal modelers (political scientists who do dynamical systems and agent-based stuff; their reflexes are out-of-sample testing, parameter identifiability, degrees of freedom), one economic historian of the cliometric sort who will want to know exactly where a number came from and what it's a proxy for, and one anthropologist who I suspect will go after the category of "elite overproduction" itself. Moderated by a historian who has written skeptically about quantitative history in the past but is genuinely curious.

My thesis is a bit of a bait-and-switch on the field's own PR, and that's deliberate: I argue that cliodynamics is never going to be predictive in the way weather forecasting or even macro forecasting is predictive, that the famous pre-2020 instability forecast everyone cites is being oversold (broad window, loosely specified outcome, and nobody knows what the base rate is), and that the field's real epistemic value is something else entirely — that it forces you to write down every mechanism and every state variable in a form where a claim can be publicly, embarrassingly wrong, instead of a narrative that can absorb any outcome.

The concrete thing I floated at the end of the piece, and which I'll be defending: stop grading these models on single-society forecasts and grade them on what I've been calling divergence tests. Find pairs (or small clusters) of polities that the model says were in roughly the same structural state at some time t — similar population-to-resource pressure, similar elite-aspirant-to-office ratios, similar fiscal capacity — but that differ on exactly one of the model's state variables; then check whether their trajectories actually diverge, in the direction and roughly on the timescale the model implies. And crucially: the matching gets done by historians who haven't seen the model's output for the period, with the matching criteria locked in before anyone looks at outcomes.
