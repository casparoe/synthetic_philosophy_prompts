Here's the question I set:

Q3. Norms of assertion.

Burean is a language in which every declarative sentence has to carry an evidential ending: -si ("I perceived this myself"), -ru ("someone told me"), -ne ("I'm inferring or guessing this"). In every other respect Burean speakers are like us: they use declaratives to tell each other how things are, they criticise each other for saying what's false, they ask "how do you know?", they retract what they've said, and they get blamed for speaking out of turn.

Case: Ada says to Ben, in a context where nothing much is at stake: "The ferry leaves at noon-ne." The ferry does leave at noon. But Ada has no real evidence — she glanced at a schedule for the wrong day, misread it, and hit on the right time by luck.

(a) Consider four candidate norms:
KN — you may assert that p only if you know that p (this is Williamson's version)
TN — you may assert that p only if p is true
BN — you may assert that p only if you believe that p
JN — you may assert that p only if your assertion is reasonable or properly supported by your evidence
For each, say in a sentence or two whether Ada's utterance is permissible under that norm and why. Then: does it matter for your answer whether the -ne ending is part of what Ada asserted (so the content is roughly "the ferry leaves at noon and I'm guessing") or instead a separate marker of how strongly she is commiting herself, more like a hedge or a tone of voice? Explain.

(b) Set out, as charitably as you can, the argument from Moore's-paradoxicality ("The ferry leaves at noon, but I don't know that it does" sounds absurd) to the conclusion that KN is the norm of assertion. Then give the strongest objection to that argument that you can think of, short of just assuming KN is false.

Here's one student's answer, verbatim except that I fixed a couple of spellings:

(a) TN: permissible. The ferry does leave at noon, and TN is a norm on how the world has to be, not on how careful you were — a lucky guess that happens to be true satisfies it. It's a norm about permissibility, not about whether you deserve credit, so it doesn't matter that Ada was sloppy.

KN: not permissible. She doesn't know. Her belief is true, but it came from misreading the wrong day's schedule, so it's a case of luck rather than knowledge.

BN: permissible — she clearly believes it, she volunteers it and she isn't joking or acting.

JN: not permissible. "I glanced at the wrong schedule" isn't evidence, so her assertion isn't reasonable.

KN and JN will in general always give the same verdicts, since knowledge is justified true belief; the only difference is that KN also requires truth, so KN rules Ada out for basically the same reason JN does.

The -ne doesn't change any of this. Evidential endings are hedges — they tell you how confident the speaker is, they aren't part of what's said. It's like English "I guess the ferry leaves at noon": still an assertion, and the norms still apply to the content. So all four verdicts are the same with or without the ending.

(b) The argument: "p but I don't know p" is absurd, and that needs explaining. It can't be explained by belief, because you can perfectly well believe p while also believing that you don't know p — people do this all the time when they know their evidence is shaky. So the absurdity has to come from the speech act itself: in asserting p you represent yourself as knowing p, so tacking on "and I don't know" contradicts what you're doing with the words. That's exactly what KN says, so KN explains the data. The truth-norm people (Williamson's view) can't explain it at all, which is evidence for KN over TN.

The strongest objection is that Moore-paradoxicality might be explained by a norm on belief rather than a norm on assertion: "p but I don't believe p" is equally absurd and has nothing to do with assertion. But this objection fails, since asserting p does commit you to knowing p — that's the whole point of the data — so the Moorean argument goes through.
