I serve on a university committee that advises the administration on named buildings, plaques, and monuments, and I keep running into a tangle that I suspect is philosophical rather than political.

The historians we consult all warn us not to be presentists: the past must be understood on its own terms, and it is illegitimate for us to condemn a figure of 1880 by the standards of today. Yet our committee's actual working rule is what I would call the dissent test: we treat a historical figure as blameworthy for a practice only if we can find contemporaries of that figure who objected to it at the time. A slaveholder's defenders say nobody in his circle objected; we go looking for the abolitionist pamphlet, and if we find one, we say he should have known better. I have noticed two things about this rule. First, the objections we are able to find and take seriously tend to be the ones phrased in roughly our vocabulary, so the standard we declare to have been available to him starts to look like our own standard with a period accent. Second, our scruple is oddly selective. We say without any hesitation that the same man's medical theories were false, that his claims about the people he displaced were not supported by the evidence he had, and that his geology was confused. Nobody calls that presentism. Nobody says we lack standing to call a past belief factually mistaken. And when the one caution does arrive about factual verdicts, it is a different caution entirely: the standing taboo on narrating the past as a march toward us, which is about the shape of the story rather than about whether past people got things right.

A third thing: nobody on the committee, and no historian we have consulted, objects when we ask genuinely new questions of the same period, about women's labour or the environmental costs of a bequest. The taboo attaches only to verdicts, never to questions.

What I want from you is the implicit theory. What view of moral judgment, of blameworthiness, and of what it takes for a standard to be available to an agent does this cluster of habits presuppose? Is the anti-presentist rule really a claim about moral standards being indexed to a time, or is it only a claim about the epistemic conditions on blame, and if the latter, is there anything left for the two sides to disagree about? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits well with, and tell me whether several quite different theories would license exactly the same practice. Say whether its parts presuppose consistent things, and what our committee would have to change if it gave the presupposition up and judged the past with our standards straight.

Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to abandon the reconstruction you give, and where you think it is most likely to be wrong. Flag explicitly any place where you are guessing about how professional historians actually reason, as opposed to arguing about what follows.
