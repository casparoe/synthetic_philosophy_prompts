I use Bertrand's chord problem as a running example in some notes I'm writing on how to choose priors, and before I build anything on the position I've ended up in, I'd like to know whether the position is actually coherent. Roughly where I've landed:

(1) The problem has an objectively correct answer. If a chord of a circle is drawn at random, the probability that it is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle is 1/2. What settles it is the invariance (transformation-group) argument associated with Jaynes: require the distribution to be invariant under rotations, translations and rescalings of the setup, solve the resulting integral equations, and the answer is determined. The classical 1/3 and 1/4 answers each quietly assume a particular physical mechanism, and so import information the problem does not give you.

(2) The principle of indifference in its ordinary form — spread credence evenly over the alternatives you can enumerate — I take to be refuted by exactly this sort of example, and I appeal to it nowhere.

(3) As a general method for getting from partial information to a prior, I accept maximum entropy, and I think of it as the successor to the principle of indifference rather than as a competitor to it.

(4) On interpretation I am a subjectivist: probabilities are degrees of belief, and I reject the idea that a state of ignorance carries a uniquely rational credence function along with it.

(5) I also think the paradox is shallower than it is usually made out to be. Specify a chance mechanism — straws thrown at a circle, a needle spun about a point on the circumference, two points chosen independently on the circumference — and each of the classical answers is the correct answer to a well-posed question, so the three answers are not really in competition with one another.

Several pairs here feel uneasy to me, but I cannot tell which of the uneasy feelings track something real. Please audit the set: which commitments are genuinely in tension and which only look it, with the real ones ordered by severity. For each real tension, say what would have to be given up and what giving it up costs me elsewhere in the set. If a single revision would settle more than one of them, say which; and say explicitly if any of the five is doing no work in the presence of the others.
