I'm a philosophy PhD student writing about the ethics and epistemology of clinical risk-prediction tools, and I want to lean on the decision-theoretic result usually called Good's theorem — roughly, that the expected value of a costless piece of information is never negative for an expected-utility maximizer. To check that I had it right I read a chapter in a recent trade book on decision theory, which summarizes it like this:

'A result proved by the statistician I. J. Good in the 1960s settles the old question of whether ignorance can ever be preferable to knowledge. Good showed that for any expected-utility maximizer the expected value of information is never negative: if you are offered a piece of evidence for free, your expected utility given that you accept it is always at least as high as if you decline it, since you remain free to act exactly as you would have acted before you saw it. So a rational agent's attitude to new evidence is never negative — curiosity is vindicated, and "ignorance is bliss" is, for a Bayesian, provably incoherent. The result also tells you how much evidence to gather: every further datum contributes a non-negative quantity of expected utility, so you should keep buying data until the cost of the next datum exceeds its expected contribution. The theorem is officially about decision rather than belief, but it has an epistemological moral as well, since it explains why knowledge is more valuable than mere true belief. Philosophers who reply that information sometimes hurts — the patient who would rather not know her test result, the card player who would rather not see his opponent's hand, the investor who would rather not hear the rumour — are describing people who are not expected-utility maximizers, so their examples leave the result untouched.'

The chapter reads as confident and largely right, but I don't trust my sense of where it slides. Two moves worry me especially: the step from a decision-theoretic result to a claim about the value of knowledge, and the brisk handling of the "I'd rather not know" cases. I also have a half-memory that in markets and games public information can make everyone worse off, which sits badly with "never negative."

Separately, I need to know whether I can use the result at all in the cases I actually care about. Those differ from the textbook setting in three ways: the risk score is delivered to several agents at once (clinician, patient, insurer); it predictably affects the patient's own mental states; and the outcome it predicts is itself partly shaped by how those agents later respond to having seen it.

Please audit the passage claim by claim: what a decision theorist would accept as stated, what is a benign simplification, what is a substantive misrepresentation, and whether the chapter's practical and epistemological conclusions survive correction of the misrepresentations. Then say whether my three-feature case falls inside the theorem or outside its scope, and how much of the chapter's confidence survives there.

Flag where you are extrapolating beyond what the formalism actually settles, and tell me what would change your verdict — whether there is a reading of the result on which it really does bear on why knowledge is better than mere true belief, whether my case is better handled by comparing information structures or by game- or market-theoretic models of public information, and where you think your own reading is most likely to fail.
