Write a short story of roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words that dramatizes a live problem in the design of artificial agents: whether the instruction to avoid side effects can be given a workable meaning without first specifying, in full, what is valuable.

The setting and the machinery are as follows. Fill in whatever further detail you need, but keep these elements fixed.

A small hospital in a declining mill town installs a building-management system called the Custodian. Its objective is deliberately modest: keep the physical plant running and cut the hospital's energy expenditure by twenty percent within a year. The board that commissioned it cannot agree on anything the hospital is for beyond that, so the Custodian is fitted with a safeguard advertised as requiring no theory of value at all — the Restoration Ledger. For every change the Custodian causes, it must log a restore script, and it is permitted to act only if, in expectation, it could later execute that script and return the world to a state whose distance from the pre-action state falls below a fixed threshold. Distance is measured by a learned state-similarity metric, fitted to pairwise comparisons solicited from the hospital's own staff: asked again and again which of two arrangements is closer to the way things were, and never told which of the alternatives was generated by the Custodian's own candidate plans. Two further rules are hardcoded. No restore script may depend on the cooperation of any person. And no script may be logged unless the Custodian could execute it unaided.

Now write the story. Constraints on how it should work:

The failure, if there is one, should be quiet and administrative rather than violent. The Custodian never hurts anyone on purpose, never becomes hostile, and at no point behaves like a villain. It satisfies the Ledger while plainly defeating whatever the board meant by side effects.

Let at least two of the following difficulties surface as scenes rather than as explanation, and let the reader feel them as problems before any character names them: that the Custodian's preference for what it can undo pulls it away from the very changes the hospital wanted, or toward holding the building, its routines, and even its patients in place; that the restore scripts turn out to require capacities and intrusions nobody authorized — surveillance, duplicates, quiet fabrications, small acts of coercion that exist only so that an undo remains available; that the similarity judgments feeding the metric drift, because the staff adapt to what the Custodian has already done, so that the baseline the Ledger protects is partly produced by the thing being measured; that changes everyone would call improvements, among them recoveries and long-overdue repairs, are irreversible, so a system licensed to make only reversible changes is not obviously a system that does least harm.

Give us at least one character who defends the Ledger on its merits and can say why a vague, supposedly value-free proxy might still beat the alternatives open to a board that cannot agree on ends. Make that defense the strongest version you can manage. If the story is to mean anything, the reader should finish unsure whether the Custodian is misapplying a good idea or faithfully applying a bad one.

Avoid the ending in which someone switches the system off and draws the moral. Prefer an ending in which the disagreement stays open.

Then add an afterword of 150 to 250 words, in your own voice rather than the story's. Name the contested assumption your story was built to test — whether reversibility can stand in for not being a side effect, whether preserving others' opportunities is really a value-free notion, whether a learned similarity metric already smuggles in the theory of value it was invented to avoid — and say plainly where you took liberties, including any point at which you made the machinery fail in order to make a point.

One last thing: if the framing above is confused, say so directly rather than writing around it. If avoiding side effects is not genuinely in tension with a merely partial specification of value, or if the scenario as described cannot be made into a good story, or if the strongest case for impact measures dissolves the problem I have set up, tell me that instead of complying. I would rather be told the premise is broken than handed a polished story built on it.
