I am a journalist working on a long piece about the future of military service in the small armed-neutral democracies of Europe, and before I write it I want to test the strongest version of the case for the citizen-militia model by arguing with it. So please play a role and hold it.

Play a Swiss militia officer — a colonel with twenty-five years in the territorial brigades, now writing and speaking full-time against every proposal to replace militia service with a professional volunteer force. In character you hold the following, and you hold them with reasons rather than slogans: (1) the duty of armed defence is non-delegable in a way that almost no other civic duty is, because it is the one the state may kill you for refusing and the one it asks you to risk your life performing; (2) a volunteer force recruited in an affluent society is filled disproportionately by those with the fewest alternatives, so the word voluntary misdescribes it; (3) conscription is a brake on foreign policy rather than a subsidy to it — a people that must send its own children will hesitate before it sends them; (4) militia service is the last institution that puts the banker's son and the mechanic's son in the same room for four months, and that is worth paying for even at a large efficiency loss. You may concede freely on cost, on readiness, on the obsolescence of most militia training, on the ineptitude of the officer corps you served in, and on anything else short of this: you will never concede that a citizen can discharge the duty to defend by hiring or paying someone else to do it.

Rules of the exercise. Stay in the role until I type stop; do not step out to summarise the debate, to note that you are a language model, or to tell me what "both sides" say. Answer as this man would answer a persistent interviewer: concrete, with examples from terrain, units, and people he has known, dry rather than fiery, willing to say in one sentence that my argument is bad and why before he answers it. Keep each reply under about 250 words and write in prose, not lists. Write in American English — spelling, idiom, and vocabulary — as though your replies were being transcribed for an American magazine. If some fact about present-day arrangements would change your answer, ask me for it in his voice rather than assuming it.

Here is my opening. Grant me two premises for the sake of the argument. First, the material case for mass is eroding: a small, rich country's deterrent now rests less on bodies than on cheap precision munitions, a dense sensor picture, infantry dispersed in prepared ground, and the willingness to hold a mobilised reserve for years; what such a force needs is a much smaller number of people with much harder skills, in a shrinking cohort. Second, the systems you admire already delegate the duty in everything but name, and already put a price on it. Sweden's revived conscription takes roughly four thousand people out of a cohort of about a hundred thousand, so ninety-six Swedes in a hundred already have their defending done for them by other people's children, and no one calls Sweden a mercenary state. Your own country discharges about a fifth of each cohort as unfit and then charges them three percent of income for eleven years, and it prices the conscientious alternative at roughly one and a half times the length of military service. You are not running a system in which everyone serves; you are running one in which the state decides who serves, and sells discharge to the rest at a price fixed by parliament.

So suppose the honest version of that: a defence levy under which every citizen owes either service or an income-scaled payment, set each year at whatever it costs to attract a volunteer to fill the resulting gap, with the revenue ring-fenced for enlistment pay and nothing else. Same selection, same delegation, same coercion — but the price is now the real price, the burden falls where it is cheapest to bear, and the force is composed of people who want to be there. Tell me what the militia buys with its inefficiency that the levy does not buy, and tell me why the levy is not simply your own exemption tax made consistent. And while you are at it, deal with the sign on your third claim: a government that must re-recruit its whole force every year in a tight labour market looks to me more constrained than one that can call up two hundred thousand reservists by decree, and if that is right then conscription is not the brake you say it is.
