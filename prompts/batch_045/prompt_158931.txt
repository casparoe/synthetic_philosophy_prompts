Hi — I'm supposed to write a ~2,500 word essay for a reading group on truth, and I've got an argument I half worked out on a train yesterday and now genuinely can't tell whether it does any work or whether I've just reinvented something that's been done to death. Would you read the sketch below and tell me what you think?

Quick context on where the group is: we've been going through the deflationary/minimalist line — basically that there's no substantive property of truth to theorise about, that the whole content of "true" is exhausted by the equivalence schema instances ("p" is true iff p), and that truth-talk earns its keep as a device for generalising, for semantic ascent, for expressing infinite conjunctions and so on. The standard objections — that truth seems to explain success, that truth seems to be the norm of belief and assertion, that truth seems like it ought to have a nature like correspondence or coherence — all seemed to me to get defused pretty fast: the deflationist says the explaining is done by the specific contents of the beliefs, the norm "believe only what is true" collapses via the schema into "believe p only if p", and the nature-of-truth stuff is really a theory of warrant for a given domain. I don't want to re-run any of that. Here's the thing I actually want to try out — I'm calling it the "alethic audit" problem, provisionally:

Step 1. There's a distinctive thing we do: stand back from an entire practice or discipline and ask whether its standards are any good in the truth-relevant sense. "Are the methods of this field actually truth-conducive?" "Is this discourse even truth-apt, or is it just venting attitudes?" "Are we tracking the truth here, or just tracking what everyone in the room already agrees about?" Call that the audit.

Step 2. The audit can't be answered by the audited practice's own standards. If you certify your methods by checking that they satisfy your methods' criteria, you haven't done anything. So the audit presupposes a standard that isn't itself answerable to the practice's canons — something it makes sense to hold the practice to even when, by its own lights, it is in perfect order. And the word we reach for is truth: you can be fully warranted by the best standards available to you and still, apparently, be wrong.

Step 3. The deflationist can't supply that. Their only resources are the schema instances plus a theory of warrant. But the schema is exactly what makes the audit go vacuous: for any p your best standards license, you're entitled to assert p, and the schema hands you "p is true" for free. So any internally well-ordered practice counts as truth-conducive by its own lights, and the audit returns "yes" automatically.

Step 4. But we plainly don't treat the audit as automatic. We take it as a substantive and fallible question whether a whole discipline could be systematically off, and we don't hear that as a contradiction. So either the deflationist owes an account of the audit that doesn't collapse into self-certification (and it's not obvious they can give one without smuggling in a notion of correctness that outruns warrant, at which point minimalism isn't so minimal), or they have to say the audit is internal after all — Neurath's boat style, you patch the ship from inside using other bits of the ship — and then I want to know what's left of the worry that a discipline can collectively go off the rails.

Step 5, why this isn't just the old objections. The familiar complaints are about truth's role *inside* a practice: true beliefs explain success, truth norms belief and assertion. Mine is about truth's role in assessing practices from outside, and the deflationist's favourite move — deflating the generalisation back down to its instances — doesn't obviously get a grip, because what's being assessed is a method's long-run profile, not any particular claim you could disquote.

What I'd like from you, roughly in this order of importance: (1) which step is actually load-bearing and where does the thing break — I'd rather be told it doesn't work than encouraged. (2) The strongest reply a deflationist would actually give, not a strawman. The one I keep circling is: the audit is just "are our methods reliable, given how the world actually is?", and "how the world is" is supplied by first-order inquiry, so no substantive truth-property is needed. Is that reply good enough, and does it concede anything I can use? (3) Does my argument overgeneralise — would the same reasoning tell against a full-blown correspondence theory too, and if so is that a feature or a disaster? (4) Is the audit really distinguishable from the explanation and normativity objections, or am I repackaging? If it's a repackaging, just say which one and why; I don't need references. (5) Structural advice for the 2,500 word version: what goes up front, what gets cut, and what an opponent most needs to see answered before they'll take it seriously. And (6) a blunt verdict on whether it's worth writing at all, or whether I should bin it and write something on truth-value gaps instead, which is the other idea I'm toying with.
