Hey — I'm in a small group that's drafting a childcare and parental-leave chapter for a policy paper we're writing on weekends (long shot, nobody's paying us), and my gut is way more settled than my principles. So I want to do this backwards: here are my verdicts, you tell me what I actually think. We have to write the one paragraph of principles that goes at the top of the chapter and I've realised I can't.

The set-up, because it's a bit unusual: instead of maternity leave and a childcare subsidy as two separate things, we're toying with one per-child care-time account. Every newborn is credited with, say, 3,000 hours of paid care time, roughly eighteen months full time. Any adult can draw on that particular child's account and be paid a care-wage for the hours they actually spend caring for that child — either parent, a grandparent, a friend, a registered childminder — or the hours can be spent as a voucher at a licensed nursery instead. Hours can't be moved from one child to another. Whatever is left when the child turns five converts into pension credits for the two registered parents. The care-wage is flat, same for everyone.

My verdicts, case by case:

1. Father takes six months full time: obviously fine. And I want the account to include use-it-or-lose-it months for the second parent, because I don't trust free choice here to amount to anything.
2. So if both parents want to hand the entire account to the mother, that gets blocked. I'm fine overriding their stated preference and I'm not embarrassed about it.
3. Grandmother draws the hours to look after her grandchild: fine. Genuinely, enthusiastically fine.
4. A friend of the family, no relation, draws the hours: fine, but hesitantly.
5. Parents use the hours to pay a nanny, cash in hand, unregistered, uninspected: not fine. Except I said the licensed-nursery voucher is fine, and when I try to say why the nanny isn't, I get three different answers — that it's about regulation, that it's about the state paying for care that happens inside someone's home, or that I just have a prejudice that real childcare happens in institutions. I can't tell which one is doing the work and it bugs me.
6. A family that doesn't use all its hours sells the leftovers to another family: definitely not.
7. A live-in migrant care worker with no work rights draws the hours: I want to say yes and no at once. No, because the state shouldn't be routing money into irregular arrangements. Yes, because that's where a huge amount of actual care happens and excluding it makes the whole scheme regressive in practice.
8. Funding through a payroll levy on everyone, including people who will never have children: fine. I think of it like schools and I don't buy the why-should-I-pay-for-your-choice complaint.
9. The state should pay the care-wage, but employers should be obliged to hold the job open for eighteen months, with no exemption for small firms. Also, a six-person startup shouldn't have to hold a job open for eighteen months. I know both can't be true and I don't know which to drop or what replaces it.
10. Unused hours converting into pension credits: I like it, but I don't like that a parent who draws every single hour ends up with nothing at the end. They did more care. That seems backwards.
11. The one I go back and forth on: flat care-wage or earnings-related? Flat feels right — the point is to pay for care, not to replace a salary, and flat means the nurse and the lawyer get the same for the same hours. But flat also means the nurse can't actually afford to take it, so in practice the care-time goes to the rich, which is the opposite of what I want.

So: is there one principle, or at most two, that gets most of this? I keep reaching for several and they disagree with each other on 3, 5 and 7 specifically — something about care being a public good, or about the costs of care falling arbitrarily on one person's career, or about leave being for the child rather than for the parent, or about the state's job being to turn care into work instead of into a sacrifice. Give me the one or two you'd actually defend, stated precisely enought that I can test it, and say which clause is doing the work in which case. Then be straight about which of my verdicts it can't accommodate — I expect to lose 5 and 9 — and run it on three cases where I haven't told you my answer: a parent who wants to draw hours to look after a seven-year-old over the summer; someone who wants to draw hours to care for their own elderly mother; and a couple who split the account fifty-fifty but both keep working three days a week, drawing hours on the other two.

Please don't just survey the positions people have taken or name-drop theories at me, and no citations — I want the argument, and I want it in the form of something I could defend in a paragraph.
