I help run a small, informal film-and-philosophy group that meets monthly in the back room of a pub; none of us has any academic philosophy, and I am the one who prepares the questions, which I work out from reading around on my own. Our next session is Everything Everywhere All at Once, the 2022 film written and directed by Daniel Kwan and Daniel Scheinert, and I have got myself rather deep into the modal metaphysics it seems to invite: the dispute between Lewis's counterpart theory and the transworld-identity picture its opponents prefer. On my amateur reading the film is unusually self-aware about the choice. It gives us verses in which Evelyn flourished — a kung fu film star, a singer on a red-carpet premiere — alongside verses in which she is barely recognisable as a person, and it gives us verse-jumping, in which what looks like one consciousness inhabits a long series of other bodies and borrows their memories and skills. Those two devices pull in opposite directions, and I would like a reading that says which way the film actually leans, and whether it is entitled to lean both ways at different points in the story.

Three things in particular puzzle me. First, Lewis's own other worlds are supposed to be perfectly concrete, not mere abstractions, so a film in which the other verses are as solid as ours looks closer to his ontology than to the picture of merely possible ways things might have gone — yet he still needs the counterpart relation, because on his view individuals are world-bound and nothing literally recurs. Does that mean a concrete multiverse is no help to the defender of transworld identity, or has the film staged a case the textbooks do not cover? Second, I have started distinguishing two despairing arguments that I think the film runs together: modal despair, the thought that I might have done anything and so what I do here is arbitrary; and plenitude despair, the thought that every possibility is somewhere actually lived by someone, so that nothing I do here matters. Jobu Tupaki's everything bagel looks like the second, fuelled by seeing all the lives at once, and her search is for an Evelyn who can see what she sees. My suspicion is that counterpart theory defuses the second but leaves the first entirely intact, but I cannot get clear about whether Jobu's outlook even needs her to be identical with her other selves, or whether the resonance of their similarity is enough. Third, there is the relativity problem: Evelyn's counterparts differ wildly, so the relation holding them together cannot be straightforward similarity of intrinsic features. What is doing the tracking — psychological continuity, a role in a shared narrative, or simply being the person called Evelyn in that verse — and, if counterpart-hood really is interest-relative in that way, does Alpha-Waymond's judgement that this Evelyn is the greatest failure of all the Evelyns survive?

Where I expect the honest critic has to be sternest is the ending. Evelyn's turn towards kindness, the googly eyes, and Waymond's plea that in some other life he would have liked simply doing the laundry and the taxes with her all seem to require that this world and these people are the ones that matter — which is easy to say if the other Evelyns are only counterparts, and much harder if she is, in some literal sense, all of them. So does the film quietly trade on counterpart theory exactly where it needs the choice to feel like hers, and on transworld identity exactly where it needs the multiverse to feel crushing? I would like the scene-by-scene version of that verdict: which moments to point the group at — the janitor's closet where verse-jumping is explained, the montage of flourishing selves, the bagel, the rocks, the final laundromat conversation with Deirdre — where the modal reading illuminates them, where the film resists it, and whether, in the end, it contributes something to the counterpart-theory debate that the arguments alone do not.
