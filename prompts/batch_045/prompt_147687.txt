I host a podcast aimed at philosophy graduate students, and next week's guest is Dr. Naomi Ivers, who has just published a book-length defence of the contingent-identity answer to the statue-and-the-clay puzzle. Her version of the view runs roughly like this: the statue (call it Andromeda) and the lump of clay it is made from (call it Clod) are one and the same thing, and the appearance that they differ in modal profile — Andromeda could not have survived being squashed into a ball, Clod could have; Clod could not have been made of bronze, Andromeda could have — is not explained by there being two coinciding objects, nor by constitution-without-identity, but by the claim that de re modal predication is always relativised to a sortal, and that the sortal in play is fixed by the causal-historical chain through which the name or description was introduced. So there is one thing on the pedestal, and two true but sortal-relative ways of saying what it could have been like.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that push her past restating this. I want at least one question each on the following, and you should add whatever else you take to be the genuine pressure point.

1. The argument that contingent identity is incoherent: the move that abstracts a predicate like "could have been distinct from Andromeda" out of a modal context, notes that Clod satisfies it while Andromeda does not, and derives a violation of Leibniz's Law.

2. The bare-demonstrative probe I plan to use myself: if the relativisation is inherited from a name's causal-historical chain, then it should disappear when there is no name — so have her consider a speaker who simply points at the thing and says "this could have been a shapeless lump" and then "this could have been bronze", with no sortal supplied by any description in the context.

3. The squash case, and the case where the ball is reshaped into the original form two days later: does Andromeda survive the squashing, is "statue" therefore a phase sortal like "student", and does the statue return on the second moulding?

4. What constrains the available sortals. If the thing can carry a statue-profile and a lump-profile, why not a doorstop-profile, a "thing-shaped-just-so"-profile, and so on without limit — and if the answer appeals to the sculptor's intentions or to the thing's causal history, what is the modal profile of a lump that no one has ever taken an interest in?

5. Whether the sortal-relativity extends to temporal predication: on her view the thing existed before it was a statue, so "the statue was made on Monday" must be read relativised too. Is there any unrelativised fact about when the thing began and how it can change, or does the relativity go all the way down — and if it does, is there anything left of the object's own modal and temporal profile?

6. The extension to persons and their organisms: whether she is committed to saying that a human person is contingently identical to the animal she is, what that yields in a case where the organism persists without the person, and why — if the machinery is exactly the same — the person case strikes people as a substantive dispute about what we are while the statue case strikes them as verbal.

7. Whether her disagreement with the constitution theorist, who denies the identity and posits two coinciding objects, is substantive at all, and what would settle it.

For each question, give me three things: the question itself, written so that a listener who knows the puzzle but not the literature can follow it and so that she cannot answer it by repeating "one thing, two sortals" without cashing that out; a one- or two-sentence note on the reply she is most likely to give; and the follow-up that that reply makes available, ideally the one that turns her own concession against her. Keep each question answerable in about two minutes, keep them in her vocabulary rather than in the vocabulary of a rival view, and don't pad the list with anything about her career, her influences, or what the audience should read next.
