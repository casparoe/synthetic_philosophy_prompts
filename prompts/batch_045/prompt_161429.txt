I have been reading, on my own and with no academic background, a popular book about cultural evolution, and before I start repeating its claims to friends I would like to know how much of them I should trust. The central chapter, as I have been paraphrasing it to myself in a single paragraph, says roughly this.

Culture is a second Darwinian process running alongside the first. Ideas are replicators that live in human brains rather than in cells, the things Dawkins called memes. They are copied from one person to the next, the copies vary, and the variants that get copied more often spread at the expense of their rivals, which is all that fitter means. Selection is indifferent to the interests of the vehicle carrying the replicator, so a chain letter, an advertising jingle, or a self-destructive cult can be highly fit while leaving the people who carry it worse off; there is no presumption that a common idea is a good idea. What limits the whole process is copying fidelity. Below a certain threshold, which the author calls the transmission floor, a variant is not really copied at all but rebuilt by each new learner out of what that learner already believes, and nothing accumulates. Above the floor, the author says, Darwinian rules take over wholesale: useful variants are kept, harmful ones are discarded, and culture begins to ratchet, from handaxes to microchips. The payoff is supposed to be a method. Just as we ask what a heart is for, we can ask what a custom was selected for, and that gives us its function. The author adds that biologists have largely come round to this, because the population-genetics models of dual inheritance theory showed that a second system of inheritance obeys the same equations as genes, so the gene's-eye view of culture was right after all, merely slow to be recognised.

The first half feels right to me and I have been repeating it. The part I keep stumbling over is the method: every custom I try it on turns out either to have several plausible functions at once or none at all. I also have no way of checking whether the claim about a consensus among biologists is true, and it sounds like the sort of thing a popular writer would inflate.

Could you work through the paragraph in the order I have given it and separate, claim by claim, what a serious defender of this approach would accept, what they would wince at as a simplification, and what is simply wrong, telling me where a mistake is about words and where it is about substance? I am especially interested in the transmission floor: is crossing it really enough for the Darwinian machinery to do the work the author assigns to it, or is something further needed, and would the author's own examples count as crossing it? Finally, the book draws a practical conclusion I would like either to use or to discard: since selection is blind to the truth of what gets copied, dealing with misinformation online is a matter of lowering the copying rate, and arguing about content is pointless. Does that follow from the parts of the summary that survive correction, or does it depend on the parts that do not?
