I am a retired chemist who, over the last two years, has been sitting in on a graduate reading group in the philosophy of mind, mostly in the role of the person who keeps asking what the experimental evidence actually shows. We recently spent a session on pain asymbolia: patients who, by the ordinary clinical tests, perceive pain perfectly well — they can localise it, describe its character as burning, stabbing or crushing, and grade its intensity on the usual scales — and yet report that it does not bother them, display no avoidance or escape behaviour, do not want it to stop, and sometimes find the whole business faintly amusing. I came away from the discussion dissatisfied. It seemed to me that the case is standardly used to bludgeon a single conclusion out of the reader — either that pain is not really bad in itself, or that its badness must lie in some one privileged component — when in fact what the syndrome shows is that the components can come apart, and that we have no argument yet about which of them, if any, carries the disvalue.

So I want to use the syndrome as a diagnostic instrument rather than as a weapon, and to do that I need to fix three candidate accounts of what makes an episode of pain bad for the subject at the time she has it. Call the first the intensity account: a pain's disvalue is a matter of how intense its sensory-discriminative component is, the thing the patient is reporting when she says it is a seven out of ten and burning rather than dull. Call the second the aversion account: a pain's disvalue is a matter of how strongly the subject is moved that it cease, how much she minds it, where this is supposed to be independent of how intense it sensory-wise is. Call the third the occupancy account, which I have not seen defended anywhere and which I offer only as a foil: a pain's disvalue is a matter of how much of the subject's limited conscious capacity it involuntarily takes up at that moment — how much of her mental room it conscripts, how far it crowds out whatever else she might have been doing with a finite workspace — regardless of how intense it is and regardless of whether she minds it.

What I would like you to build is a single case, in one paragraph, in which the intensity account and the aversion account both return the verdict that the pain is not bad for the subject (or bad only to a negligible degree), while the occupancy account returns the verdict that it is bad for her to a substantial degree. The direction matters: I am not interested in the easy case in which asymbolia strips a pain of its badness, and I am not interested in a case where the occupancy verdict simply piggybacks on the pain's being intense or its being disliked. The constraints are these. The scenario must be clinically realistic: a ward, a clinic, a recovery room, a patient with a lesion or a drug on board or a postoperative state, ordinary tests and ordinary questions — no futuristic neurotechnology, no implant, no perfect measurement of anything. The subject must be a competent and cooperative reporter whose reports we have no independent reason to distrust, so that neither the case nor your discussion can be resolved by suggesting that she is confused, confabulating, or saying what she thinks the examiner wants to hear. Nothing downstream may do the work: no tissue damage, no prognosis, no anxiety about what the pain portends, no lost sleep or missed work later in the week — whatever badness there is must be badness of the episode itself, at the time. And the verdicts must fall out of the three accounts applied to the case's features, not be written into the case by fiat.

After the scenario, I would like the check. State each account's verdict separately and show the reasoning that gets you there from the details of the case, then tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work and demonstrate that removing it — and changing nothing else — collapses the case back into an unremarkable asymbolic patient about whom all three accounts say much the same thing. Finally, give me the strongest objection an advocate of the intensity or the aversion account would press against the case: I expect something along the lines of the occupancy theorist having confused a merely causal crowding of attention with something bad for the subject, and I would like to know how the occupancy theorist should reply, and whether her reply quietly reinstates the very consequentialist reasoning I tried to rule out by forbidding downstream harms.
