hey — I'm writing a novel set maybe forty years from now, and one of the characters is a mid-level compliance officer whose job is certifying whether large computing systems count as conscious. The regime in my book has built its whole legal test on something very like integrated information theory: consciousness as integrated information, cause–effect power, complexes, the minimum information partition, exclusion, the lot. I need an actual document to drop into the book as an insert — 800 to 1000 words — and I want it to be deadpan civil-service prose that only slowly reveals that the test is completely insane.

Form: an official "Guidance for Determining Whether a Computing System Is a Subject of Experience," issued by a made-up agency (give it a name and a self-important acronym). Numbered clauses in the flattened cross-referencing register real guidance uses — "for the purposes of this Part," "subject to sub-clause (4)(b)" — then a short FAQ, then one worked example. The worked example should be the case everyone in-world knows about and nobody brings up at conferences: a vast, uniform grid of trivial logic elements that does nothing useful at all and still scores far higher than any human brain.

The jokes have to come from the theory's real commitments, not invented ones. Use these:

- the quantity is defined intrinsically, from the system's it's own point of view, and in principle can't be read off from outside — and yet here is a form, filled in by an inspector with a probe. The guidance should handle this with total confidence and no awareness of a problem.
- exact computation is intractable for anything large, so the guidance authorises proxies — a perturb-and-measure style index: zap it, measure how complicated the echo is — and somewhere in the clauses the proxy quietly stops being a proxy and becomes the definition.
- exclusion: only the single maximally irreducible whole counts, so every overlapping or nested candidate, including the subsystems that are obviously doing the actual work, gets declared not conscious. The certificate therefore has to name which of several overlapping systems gets the standing and which gets switched off.
- the panpsychist edge: anything with a scrap of integration counts a little, so there has to be a threshold — and whatever threshold they pick, something silly (a thermostat with hysteresis, a smoke alarm) lands just on the interesting side of it.
- the shape business: on this view an experience unfolds into a geometric structure in a high-dimensional space of possible experiences, so the guidance should require applicants to describe or attach the shape of the applicant's experience.
- the feedforward loophole: a system with no recurrence has none of this, so a purely feedforward model reproducing a brain's inputs and outputs perfectly scores zero, while a nearly useless recurrent one scores positive. State it in a way that is obviously correct by the guidance's own lights and obviously mad.

And the grid: the agency knows about it. I want them to solve it with a clause rather than a concession — an exemption, a discount factor, a de minimis rule — that gets the right answer for the wrong reason, with no hint that the reason is wrong. That's the bit that should feel like a modest proposal: the doc follows the theory's logic, the logic delivers an outrage, and the beaurocracy absorbs the outrage instead of the theory.

Rules: no winking. Nobody in the document notices anything or cracks a joke; the horror is entirely in the competence. Someone who has actually read integrated information theory should feel it's a fair hit, and someone who hasn't should just enjoy a very badly designed government form. Invent a couple of citations to the guidance's own earlier versions, and one or two short quotes from a grateful regulated entity. Don't name any real researchers or real papers — keep everything in-world.
