Last week a colleague used it to end one: we were discussing whether better information and better planning could help people start hard tasks, and he said the flesh always wins at close range, so the only thing that works is removing the option — app blockers, the phone out of the bedroom, making the bad move unavailable. I suspect the sentence is doing several different jobs at once.

Give me exactly five numbered items, no introduction and no conclusion, each at most two sentences and each headed with a two- or three-word name. For each: the precise thesis the slogan would have to be claiming, a verdict (trivial, substantive and defensible, or false), and a yes or no on whether that reading actually supports his conclusion that information and planning cannot help.

Two things I want included. First, my own rough distinction between the pull growing (the reward looms as it gets near: snacks, infinite scroll) and the cost growing (starting the hard thing feels worse the closer it is, though the task itself has not changed) — which readings have room for that, and does a hyperbolic-discounting story explain the second case or get it backwards? Second, whether not acting (skipping the gym) and acting against your own judgment (refreshing a feed) are the same phenomenon on that reading, since from the inside they do not feel like the same failure.
