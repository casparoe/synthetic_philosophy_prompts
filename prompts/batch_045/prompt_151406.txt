I'm halfway through a paper on this and my supervisor keeps saying "define corruption" like it's a five-minute job, and I've realised after about six arguments with a friend from back home that my gut verdicts on concrete cases don't line up with any rule I can actually state. So here they are, and I want the rule. (1) Handing a clerk 40 bucks to release a passport that's been "pending" for five months and that I'm plainly entitled to: not corruption, just infuriating — a fee for not being ignored. (2) A mayor giving a city contract to a firm whose owner he's played golf with every weekend for ten years, nothing ever said out loud, no evidence the bid was worse: corruption, definately, and I don't care that nobody can point to a deal. (3) Where my parents grew up, a woman takes a goat to the village chief so he'll hear her land dispute this year instead of next year: I can't call that corruption, it's how the institution works there, and refusing would just mean she never gets heard. (4) A surgeon getting a case of wine from a grateful patient a week after the operation: fine, obviously. (5) The same case of wine arriving the week before the operation: not fine. (6) A drug rep buying a doctor's flights and hotel for a conference in another city, and the doctor prescribing more of that company's drug afterwards while honestly believing it's the better drug: corrupt. (7) The same rep dropping off sandwiches for the whole clinic: not corrupt, and I don't think it's just that the money is smaller. (8) A hospital nurse taking cash to move a patient up the surgery queue: corrupt. (9) The same nurse taking cash to stay two hours past her shift and treat patients privately in the same building: not corrupt. (10) A rich family endows a building at a university and their son gets in with grades that wouldn't have gotten him in: corrupt. (11) The same university admitting the children of alumni who've given nothing: not corruption, just unfair, or nepotistic, or something — I don't think it's the same thing as 10 and I can't say why.

The best I can do on my own is: an officeholder acts corruptly when a private relationship displaces the criteria their role exists to apply. But I don't know what to do with (3) — in that village the criteria seem to include being someone the chief owes something to — and my friend says that just shows I've smuggled in a Western standard and then called it universal, which feels like a cheap way out but I can't actually answer it. And (1) pulls against (5): in both cases something of value moves before the official does what I want, so if timing is what makes (5) bad then (1) should be bad too, and it isn't.

So: can you give me a principle — or two, honestly, if no single one will do — that gets as many of these as possible? State it precisely enough that I can apply it to new cases myself, tell me what it predicts for cases I haven't listed (my actual stuck one: my dad sits on the municipal planning committee, and a family friend whose firm has a rezoning application coming up sent him an expensive watch when my sister got engaged — customary in that community, and he recused himself from the vote, which passed anyway), and be straight with me about which of my verdicts some principle forces me to give up and which clash is the real one I have to decide.
