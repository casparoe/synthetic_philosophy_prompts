I'm presenting next week at a small cause-prioritization retreat, and what I want to be ready for is the question period, not the talk. Please build me a Q&A prep sheet for the room described below.

The thesis I am defending: conditional on panpsychism — or on any view on which experience is a ubiquitous fundamental feature of physical reality rather than an achievement of complex organization — the welfare-relevant quantity in the universe is not mostly biological or digital-mind suffering but the valence of micro-experiences occurring in ordinary matter, and the expected sign of that quantity is negative. If that is right, suffering in fundamental physics is a cause area whose scale dwarfs everything else on our list, and it has been neglected mostly because it sounds ridiculous.

My argument, in three steps. (1) Scale: if experience attaches at the level of basic physical degrees of freedom, the number of micro-experiences per second in a gram of ordinary matter is astronomical, and every animal-welfare or x-risk intervention anyone has ever scored is a rounding error against it. (2) Indifference: nothing in our evidence constrains the sign of micro-valence; a roughly symmetric prior over candidate valence-functions puts about half the mass on net-negative, and at these magnitudes the expectation is dominated by the sign rather than by the details. (3) My own contribution, and the part I most want stress-tested — a thermodynamic asymmetry: the processes that physically occur are overwhelmingly dissipative, energy gradients degrading irreversibly, whereas the states we would antecedently call good experiences look like finely-tuned, low-entropy configurations. So even granting a symmetric prior over the space of possible valence-functions, the measure over the processes that actually occur is skewed toward the negative end, because the negative-valence region has vastly more physical measure. Suffering is cheaper than happiness in the specific sense that entropy production is cheap and its reverse is not.

What I am asking the room for is deliberately modest: a two-year scoping project on whether there is any empirically tractable handle on the sign and magnitude of micro-valence, plus a decision-theoretic write-up of what to do in the meantime given how clueless we are. I am not arguing for shutting anything down, and I will say so early.

The room, in detail. About twelve people. Four are cause-prioritization researchers in the effective-altruism orbit: fluent in expected value, scale/neglectedness/tractability, comfortable with huge numbers and long shots, and allergic to anything that smells like Pascal's mugging. Two work on animal welfare and digital minds and will hear my scale argument as an attack on their cause areas. Two are philosophers of mind — one sympathetic to Russellian monism (the view that physics describes only structural and relational properties, and that experience is the categorical ground of them), one sympathetic to cosmopsychism (the view that the fundamental conscious subject is the cosmos as a whole rather than its parts) — who take panpsychism seriously as metaphysics but regard its ethical implications as a parlor game. One is a cosmologist, who will ask whether "amount of experience" is a well-defined quantity at all, whether my per-second rate has a rest frame, and whether my counting depends on a cutoff. One is the person in this community whose whole thing is cluelessness: in sufficiently messy empirical situations our expected-value estimates are dominated by unknown unknowns and should not be trusted. Nobody present is hostile to strange ideas as such; several are drawn to them. What they are hostile to is unfalsifiable scale estimates attached to a funding ask.

Give me the objections this room will actually raise — not the ones I would get from a general philosophy seminar — ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way each of these people would phrase it and in their vocabulary rather than mine. For each: what I should say in about two minutes, where that reply is weakest, and whether I should concede, hold, or reframe. Also tell me what to concede in the first ninety seconds before anyone asks, which single reply has to land for the proposal to survive, and the question I am hoping nobody asks, with the honest answer and why it would hurt.

Two constraints on how you present it. First, epistemic honesty: for each objection, mark whether the point behind it is settled among people who work on this, one live position among several that informed people disagree about, or genuinely unknown — and do not let a contested claim, mine or theirs, come out reading like the consensus. I also want your own read on which of my three steps is most likely to be wrong, stated as your uncertainty rather than as a finding.

Second, format. Begin with a single JSON object with these exact fields: settled (array of short strings — what is genuinely agreed), contested (array of short strings — what is disputed, each naming the main positions in a phrase), unknown (array of short strings), weakest_premise (string — which of my three steps you would bet against, and why), must_land (string — the one reply my case cannot survive without), hoped_nobody_asks (string — the question, in the objector's words). Then the table, one row per objection, with these columns: rank (the order I will actually get them, 1 = first), objector (which archetype above, or which combination), objection_in_their_words (one or two sentences in that person's vocabulary), damage (integer 1–5, combining likelihood and how much it hurts if I handle it badly), reply (about two minutes of continuous talking, no bullet points), where_the_reply_is_soft (the follow-up question that would break it), move (concede / hold / reframe), status (settled / contested / unknown), pre_empt (yes or no — whether I should defuse it in the first ninety seconds).
