I spent last night arguing with a friend who has lately become a thoroughgoing constructivist. He insists that classical mathematics is a game with meaningless symbols, that an existence proof without a witness is a promissory note, and that a mathematics department could simply vote to switch to intuitionistic foundations over one long vacation. I think he is largely right about the mathematics and entirely wrong about the management consultancy. Rather than argue further, I would like to satirise him.

Write, in British English, a deadpan corporate 'migration guide' — the sort of change-management memo a large organisation circulates before a compulsory platform upgrade — announcing that a mathematics department has migrated from classical to intuitionistic foundations, effective Michaelmas. About 700 words, with headings and numbered sections.

The comedy should come from the technical content being right rather than from parodying mathematicians. Among the things the memo should have to handle: the double-negation translation as a compatibility layer that lets legacy proofs keep running, and the fact that it only rescues some of them; excluded middle as deprecated rather than refuted, with double negation of a disjunction still shipping and triple negation collapsing back to single; the disjunction and existence properties advertised as features, so that every existential theorem now arrives with a witness attached; the loss of truth-table semantics; the loss of decidable equality on the reals and its replacement by a positive apartness relation, with the consequence that every total function on the reals comes out continuous and the intermediate value theorem survives only in its approximate form; the fan theorem as an optional licence to be installed separately; the Choice add-on quietly restoring excluded middle, by Diaconescu's theorem; and the three supported builds — Brouwerian, Bishop-style and Russian or Markov — as mutually incompatible distributions with different baggage, differing over choice sequences, over whether classical results count as theorems, and over Markov's principle. Weak counterexamples built from the creating subject belong under Known Issues.

Two constraints. First, the piece should be fair: the target is the reformist, manifesto-style rhetoric of a change of logic and the managerial voice it is dressed in, not constructivism itself, and a constructivist should be able to laugh without feeling misrepresented — no technical claim in the memo may be false. Second, the standard objections to constructivism should never be stated outright; a reader should feel the costs through the memo's euphemisms, workarounds and small print rather than be told them.
