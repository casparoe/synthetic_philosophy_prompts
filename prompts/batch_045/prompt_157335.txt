I convene a philosophy-of-biology reading group attached to a behavioral ecology lab, and for roughly two years now we have been going around in a circle about adaptationism. The pattern never varies: someone presents an optimality model for some trait and argues that the fit between the trait's design and the problem it solves is itself the evidence that selection shaped it; someone else replies that the fit is exactly what you would expect if the trait were a structural by-product, a developmental constraint, an exaptation, or the residue of drift, and that nothing in the presentation rules those out; the first person says that this is unfalsifiable; and we finish where we began. This is obviously a small-scale version of a dispute that has run in evolutionary biology and its philosophy since Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique, and neither side has moved in decades. I want a diagnosis, not a verdict.

Here are the three positions as they actually get defended among us. First, the optimality-first or reverse-engineering position: the right way to study a trait is to ask what problem it would be optimal for, treat the engineering analysis of form as the primary evidence about history, and regard alternatives as things that must be positively demonstrated rather than merely gestured at. Second, the pluralist position: developmental bias, allometry, phylogenetic inertia, exaptation and drift are not exotic exceptions but a large share of the causal background, the adaptationist strategy of treating selection as the default generates unfalsifiable narratives, and the burden of proof should sit with anyone claiming a selective history for a particular trait. Third, the deflationary position: the critique was always just a demand for methodological rigor, contemporary work with explicit alternative models and comparative tests has absorbed it, and what continues is institutional inertia and a taste for rhetoric rather than a live disagreement.

My own hypothesis — please treat it as a hypothesis and tell me if it is wrong — is that two separate stalls are stacked on top of each other. One is not empirical at all: it is a dispute about what the default hypothesis should be and who carries the burden of proof in a single-case study, where no amount of data about other organisms settles anything, because the sides differ about the standard of evidence rather than about the cases. The other is a hidden base-rate question that both sides implicitly answer and neither can measure, because of what I will call the engineered-trait sampling filter: the traits that enter the literature are pre-selected for looking designed, since a trait that looks like a solution to a problem is what prompts someone to model it, while the critic's examples are drawn from a different, oppositely biased pool — traits chosen because they do not look designed. If that is right, both sides can produce indefinitely many confirming instances forever without converging, and the empirical question underneath the fight is unanswerable with the sample we have.

I can also see two other readings, and I want them adjudicated against mine. One is verbal: perhaps 'is trait T an adaptation?' has different truth conditions on the two sides because traits are individuated differently — by current function on one side, by developmental or structural unit on the other — so that a trait can be an adaptation under one individuation and a spandrel under the other without contradiction. The other is a mismatch of explanatory target: one side may be answering why the trait arose, another why it is maintained, and another why it has this shape rather than an alternative one, with all three questions traveling under a single heading.

To make the diagnosis bite, consider a thought experiment I have been using on my group. Suppose that for a few model organisms we had a complete genealogical audit: for every trait, a reliable verdict on whether its presence is explained by selection for its current function, by selection for a different function followed by co-option, by its being a side-effect of selection on something else, or by drift and constraint alone. Suppose the audit returns a fairly middling result — say a bit over half of traits are direct adaptations for their current function, a large minority are co-opted or by-product, and the rest are drift or constraint. Would that close the dispute? My own suspicion is that each side would declare victory on the same numbers, which would be evidence that the disagreement was never about the frequencies. But I am not confident, and I want the alternatives argued: whether such a table would in fact settle it, whether the dispute is instead about which traits belong in the table, or whether the disagreement survives even the best possible evidence because it is a difference about explanatory standards or about the individuation of traits.

What I am asking for, concretely: tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is, taking the three positions as I have stated them and telling me which of my candidate diagnoses is doing the real work and why the others are not; then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question, a change in what is treated as data — or argue that no progress should be expected and explain why.

Please write in American English. Do not give me a survey of the literature, a reading list, or citations: I have the literature, and what I lack is the diagnosis. Argue for your answer rather than summarizing the state of play, and be willing to tell me that the question as posed is ill-formed if that is what you think.
