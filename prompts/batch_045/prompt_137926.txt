I'm 71, retired after thirty-odd years as a research hydrologist (groundwater modelling, mostly), and in the last couple of years I've started going to — and now running — a monthly ideas evening at the village hall. About fifteen people, nearly all retired: several engineers, two GPs, a former accountant, a couple who used to teach. Sixty minutes, a whiteboard, and a house rule that nobody looks anything up mid-session, since a few of them barely use the internet at all.

Next month's mine and I've rashly promised them "how to think about how long things like countries last." What I actually want across is the outside view: you forecast a case by putting it in a class of comparable cases and looking at what happened to them, rather than by building a story about this one. I did a session last year on the inside/outside contrast, including that story about the team of experts writing a school curriculum who each privately thought they'd be finished in two years, so they've met the basic move and I don't want to spend the hour doing it again. What they haven't met — and what I've realised I can't cleanly explain myself — is the reference class problem: that any case sits in indefinitely many classes, you pick one, and the pick does most of the work. And then the bit where I think the real content is, which is residual life: the mean duration of things of this kind from their founding is not the same quantity as the expected further duration of one that has already lasted 220 years, and you don't get the second by subtracting the 220. That smells to me like the inspection paradox wearing a hat, but I'm not at all confident I can say why without losing the room.

So: don't explain the outside view to me. Plan how I explain it to them. What I want from you:

1. The order. What has to be in place before what, and what breaks if I reorder it. I'm assuming the inside/outside contrast goes first because everything else is a refinement of it, but after that I genuinely don't know whether "here is how you pick a class" has to come before or after "here is what the numbers in a class can and can't tell you," and whether residual life is an early framing device or a late payoff.

2. The carrying case. I've been playing with a made-up polity — call it the Kaveri Dominion: 220 years old, formed out of nine successor states after a long war, currently running a fiscal system everyone agrees is unsustainable, no contested succession in sixty years, and no external rival it can't out-produce. Scrap it or change its features if you like. What I want is an argument for which single example should carry the hour and what that example has to have and has to lack. My instinct is that it needs just enough narrative detail that the inside view is genuinely tempting — a bare label makes for a rigged demo — and that it has to lack any feature that makes one particular class obviously the right one. But I'd rather you committed to a case than agreed with me.

3. The misconceptions, with positions in the sequence. Here's my list, which is probably wrong in places: (a) the outside view is just the base rate, so more data is always better and you should widen the class to get n up; (b) if you can't find twenty comparable cases the method doesn't apply; (c) the outside view is what you fall back on when you don't understand the mechanism, so it's second best; (d) they'll quietly assume the reference class is given by the world rather than chosen by them, which I think is the big one and the one the example has to set a trap for. Tell me which of these actually show up, which I've invented, what I've missed, and at exactly what point I should head each off — pre-empt it before it forms, or let it get said out loud so I can work with it.

4. A thing I made up and can't place. I've been fiddling with a quantity I call the strain ratio: contested successions in the last fifty years, over the number of times the state has had to reinvent how it pays for itself in the last fifty years, with some sort of interval around it from the small counts. Is that an outside-view instrument — a way of choosing a class without telling a story — or is it a theory smuggled in wearing statistics? Give me a one-sentence rule I could actually say out loud to the room for telling the two apart, and tell me whether it belongs in this session at all or is a distraction for this audience.

5. What to leave out, and say out loud that I'm leaving it out. I'm inclined to name and skip two things: whether any of this licenses a real forecast at all (I think it licenses considerably less than people want it to), and the question of whether "empire" or "state" is a stable kind of thing in the first place. Tell me if I'm wrong about either of those, and what else should be on the chopping block for sixty minutes with a whiteboard.

6. A check. One question to put to them at minute fifty that they can't get right by repeating "use the outside view" — where getting it right shows they have both the class-choice point and the residual-life point, and where the plausible wrong answers tell me which one they missed.

Be committed: give me one order, one example, one rule on the strain-ratio question, with reasons, not a menu of options I then have to choose between. And where you think there's a real chance I'd lose the room, or where you're just guessing about what retired engineers will and won't follow, say so and say what you'd do instead.
