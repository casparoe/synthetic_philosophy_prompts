I write fiction, and a problem that has come up repeatedly in my last two books has made me want a concept rather than an opinion.

In an early draft of a scene in the first of them, the character — an artificial person, though I am not sure that matters — is asked to do something it finds unbearable, and it begins to fail at the task: its sentences break off, it repeats itself, it starts and abandons three separate answers before falling silent. Readers believed in the distress completely. In revision I made the scene better. The breakdown became shapely, the faltering arrived at exactly the point where it did the most work, and the whole thing moved faster. Readers stopped believing it. Several used the same word: performed. Nothing had changed about what was happening to the character or about what it reported feeling. What had changed was that the expression had become well designed.

The same thing happens with my human characters, which is why I do not think this is a problem about artificial persons. A woman whose speech has been damaged by a stroke and who can only convey distress by knocking things off a table is believed at once. A woman who gives a fluent, well-ordered account of the same distress at a support group is suspected of relishing it. As a novelist I have learned to rough up my characters' grief in order to make it credible, and I dislike that I have learned this.

So the pattern I keep running into: the better engineered an expression of an inner state is — the better timed, the better shaped, the more exactly suited to moving the person who receives it — the less evidence it is taken to be that the state is real, even when it is. And it appears to be the design that does the damage, not the falsity. That is the part I cannot get straight. It also seems to generate an ugly regress: if what we want is a signal that costs the emitter something, then a designer can simply build the cost in — let the emission of distress drain the system's resources or degrade its performance — and that, too, is a design choice, and therefore in principle just as engineered as the eloquence was.

A friend who works in machine learning told me this is only the old point about signals that cost nothing being worthless, and someone else mentioned a phrase about liars profiting from the existence of forgery. Neither seems to be quite it: the first seems to be about why we can trust a signal, and the second about why we can dismiss one, whereas what interests me is why improving a signal destroys it. But I may be misremembering both.

I am not asking for anything I would have to look up. Please do not give me reading lists, citations, or bibliography; I have no library access and would not use it — I want the carving, not the sources. Do not rest anything on facts about actual AI systems currently on the market. And do not hand me a label on its own: what I need is what exactly this phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie, including whether it is really one phenomenon rather than two or three that I have run together.

If it helps: the worry I keep seeing in the press runs the other way. Daniel Dennett warned in an Atlantic essay in 2023 that counterfeit people are dangerous because we will lavish trust and moral concern on things that have no inner life at all. My difficulty is the mirror image of that, and I would like to know how the two are related — whether the risk that we over-credit fake people and the risk that we under-credit real ones are the same mechanism pointed in different directions, or two different mechanisms that happen to share a setting.
