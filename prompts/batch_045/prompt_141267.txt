someone in my house groupchat dropped this and i've been going in circles on it for two days. context: five of us run a tiny grantmaking thing and we try to publish actual "house views" with the reasoning attached, so questions about whether groups can have views at all are not academic for us. the argument is dressed up as being about courts but i'm pretty sure it eats itself, and i'm pretty sure it eats me too.

(1) standard setup. three judges, contract case. defendant is liable iff the contract was valid (P) and it was breached (Q). judge 1 accepts both, so liable. judge 2 accepts P, rejects Q, so not liable. judge 3 rejects P, accepts Q, so not liable. every judge is individually consistent. vote premise by premise: P passes 2-1, Q passes 2-1, so liable. vote on the conclusion: 2-1 not liable. same people, same votes, opposite verdicts, and the only thing that changed is which propositions got put to the vote.

(2) and it's not an artifact of that one example. the arrow-flavored impossibility result for judgment aggregation: you can't have all of universal domain, anonymity, collective rationality (the output is always consistent and complete), and systematicity (the same proposition-by-proposition rule on every issue) at once; and if you drop anonymity to get out of it, what you get back is a dictator. so there is no privileged way to get from individual judgments to a group judgment.

(3) so "the group's view" isn't discovered, it's manufactured by a procedural choice that nothing in the members' judgments settles. it's straight framing sensitivity. call this the artifact conclusion.

(4) now, the reason people think individuals are safe from all this is that individuals are supposedly already unified: no aggregation problem, because there's nothing to aggregate.

(5) that assumption is doing all the work and it isn't free. a person over time is a collection of judgment-bearing states: you at the start of looking into something, you after one more paper, you at 3am, you the next morning. they disagree with each other constantly — that's just what changing your mind is. sub-personally it's messier: things your visual system "knows," things you know propositionally, and they come apart.

(6) and here's the part i can't route around. the impossibility theorem is a piece of math about functions from profiles of judgment sets to judgment sets. it does not say "groups of organisms." its conditions are conditions on the function. so feed it the time-slices of a single person, or the sub-systems if you prefer. if your occurrent belief set is the output of something satisfying universal domain + anonymity + systematicity + collective rationality, it's dictatorial: one slice fixes everything and the others are decoration, in which case "you" are that slice and the rest of your mental life isn't yours. if it doesn't satisfy all four, you are collectively irrational — your total view is inconsistent or incomplete, exactly like the court.

(7) and the obvious dodge doesn't help. if you say the person just *is* the temporally extended structure rather than any slice of it, then you've granted that "S believes p" is a fact about a four-dimensional pattern and not a state of anyone in particular, which was the conclusion anyway.

(8) either way: no subject of belief. "S believes that p" is a predictive shorthand, not ontology. and if nobody believes anything then nobody knows anything, and whoever typed this argument doesn't believe it either, so i can't be moved by it, and the whole thing collapses. that last bit is why i think something is broken, but it's also the cheapest objection available and i want the real one.

stuff i want to keep: (1) and (2) — i've checked the vote counts and the theorem seems right to me. and the first sentence of (6): the proof really doesn't mention organisms or skin or separate nervous systems, and i would of thought i'm special-pleading if i say it applies to five people in a room but not to five states of me. i'd drop (8) happily. what i can't do is point to the step where the argument stops being a fact about committees and starts being a fact about minds, and until i can i don't feel entitled to laugh it off.
