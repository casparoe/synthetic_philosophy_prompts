For the past two years I've been the engineer responsible for the data platform at a regional hospital network — a multi-tenant warehouse over clinical, billing, and research data, with view-based access control, row- and column-level policies, attribute-based rules keyed on role and treatment relationship, lineage tracking through derived tables, retention jobs with per-table TTLs, and a differentially private release layer (per-analyst epsilon budgets, composition accounting) for anything that leaves our perimeter. On top of that we run a homegrown scheme my team calls purpose binding: every record carries a purpose tag, a query is admitted only if the purpose declared by the requester is on that record's allowlist, derived views inherit the intersection of their parents' allowlists so that purposes can only ever narrow as data flows downstream, and when a consent "lease" expires we have to revoke or recompute everything downstream of it. We use the word purpose drift rather than leakage for the failure mode, and the distinction turns out to matter in ways we didn't anticipate. At a design review last week a colleague argued that this machinery doesn't protect privacy at all, only confidentiality, and that what we've built is really a compliance artifact; I think she's wrong but I couldn't say why, and I've been reading some philosophy of privacy on the side to figure out whether there's a real disagreement here or just two usages of a word.

So here is what I'd like. Take the accounts of what privacy is that I keep running into in that reading — (i) the control account, on which privacy just is a person's having control over information about herself, so that a loss of privacy is a loss of control rather than a loss of secrecy; (ii) the limited-access or reduced-access account, on which privacy is a matter of how accessible a person is to others, with the familiar trichotomy of secrecy, anonymity, and solitude as the dimensions along which access varies; (iii) the contextual-integrity account, on which privacy is neither control nor concealment but the fit between a given flow of information and the norms appropriate to the context it happens in, so that the same disclosure can be perfectly fine from a clinician to a specialist and a violation when it shows up in a marketing dataset; and (iv) the line of argument that treats privacy as valuable because it gives people room — room to try things out, to form relationships, to become a self — and hence as something worth protecting even when no downstream harm can be identified. Restate each one, in turn, as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use: principals and policies, read permissions and views, threat models, inference and side channels, epsilon and composition, lineage and revocation, purpose tags and allowlists, de-identification and re-identification risk. I want four translations, not one blended one, and I want each to be a genuine attempt to preserve what the account asserts rather than a restatement that quietly makes it look like something I already agree with.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. For each account: what carried over without loss, what you had to bend to fit my vocabulary, and what has no expression in it at all — and whether the residue is evidence that my vocabulary is too thin to build what we think we're building, or evidence that the philosophical claim was less substantive than it looks. In particular I want your verdict on three things: whether our purpose-binding scheme captures anything real about contextual integrity or merely implements the control account with extra bookkeeping; whether the monotonic narrowing of allowlists through derived views is doing any philosophical work or is just an engineering convenience we've mistaken for a moral principle; and whether any of these four accounts, if true, would actually change what we should build next quarter. Finally, be explicit about where your own translation is least trustworthy: flag the places where you suspect you're substituting an engineering assumption — that there is one identifiable principal whose preferences are the policy input, that data flows are enumerable and auditable, that harms arrive at individuals rather than at groups — and calling it a translation. Mark your confidence on each of your main claims, and tell me what would change your mind: which concrete case, system behavior, or argument would lead you to abandon the translation you've given, and where do you think your reasoning is most likely to fail. Please write in American English, address it to me rather than to a general reader, assume I'm fluent in the systems vocabulary and have no patience for being taught it back to me, and keep it to roughly a thousand to fifteen hundred words.
