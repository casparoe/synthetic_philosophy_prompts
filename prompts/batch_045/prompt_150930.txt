I’m putting together a short packet for my undergrad philosophy club — we went over Hume on miracles last week, and everyone left feeling like the argument was obviously unfair but nobody could put there finger on why. I thought a fake bureaucratic document would be the funniest way to show it, so here’s the ask. Please write a parody of an official guidance document or FAQ page (about 800–900 words) titled Evaluating Miracle Petitions: Guidance for Caseworkers, issued by a made-up agency called the Office of Uniform Experience in a world where Hume’s argument has been written into administrative law. The voice should be deadpan civil-service compliance-speak: calm, jargon-heavy, endlessly reasonable-sounding, the style of a risk-assessment memo or a claim-form instruction page. The target is the Humean evidentialist case against believing miracle testimony — and, just as much, the modern administrative mindset that would try to operationalize it. So the humor has to come from the content: it should get the real moves of Hume’s argument right and just follow them, straight-faced, to a place where the reader sees that basically no miracle can ever be approved.

To make this more than a rehash, build the memo around a few invented procedures, but explain them enough that nobody needs outside reading. First, a caseworker starts with the definition: a miracle is an event that contradicts a law of nature, and a law of nature is established by completely regular, unbroken experience; the reasonable person proportions belief to the evidence. Second, have the agency maintain an Ordinary Course Ledger — a giant database of ordinary, repeated experiences (dead people stay dead, water does not become wine on its own, sticks do not turn into snakes, etc.) — that supplies the overwhelming prior against any petition. Third, the core decision rule is the balancing test: testimony can only establish a miracle if the falsehood of that testimony would be even more contrary to regular experience than the event reported; call it the Miracle Exception Threshold, and let the memo note that, in practice, almost nothing clears it. Fourth, include a Competing Miracles Desk: when rival miracle traditions — incompatible religions, say — report conflicting wonders, the evidence cancels out and both petitions are denied, because the two testimonies destroy each other. Fifth, add a short bias-training section on why reports of wonders are unreliable — the love of being surprised, the thrill of wonder, ordinary human credulity, and the fact that reports from far away, long ago, or very unfamiliar cultures are especially easy to invent and hard to check — treated as a neutral HR module. The document should contain at least one sample claim form with caseworker annotations: a denied petition (a healing, a resurrection, a weeping statue, whatever) where the annotations show the prior, the balancing test, and the reason for rejection; plus one absurd near-miss or borderline file that the office cannot process, to show what would have to be true for the testimony side to win. The bureaucrats should never break character by appealing to God, faith, mystery, or the idea that maybe the law of nature is just incomplete; they accept Hume’s definitions and just apply them. The piece should be funny to someone who knows the argument (they should recognize the real moves being exaggerated, not invented) and readable to someone who doesn’t, and by the end a reader should have felt the reductio: a framework this scrupulous makes miracle belief practically unreachable, and the office thinks that is simply good governance. No need for citations, page numbers, or anything you’d need to look up — just write the piece.
