I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy background (some blog posts, one very long argument with a coworker who did a philosophy minor, which is how I got into this). We disagree about whether LLMs understand anything and she finally wrote her side out as an actual numbered argument. I think the conclusion is wrong but I can't point at the step that breaks, and I'd like you to tell me which one it is — and what it costs to give up.

Her argument, as close as I can reconstruct it:

(1) To understand a sentence is at least to be in an internal state with truth conditions — a state whose content is fixed by what the world would have to be like for the sentence to be true. Getting the continuations right isn't enough; a very good imitation of a map isn't a map of anywhere.

(2) A system trained purely to minimize next-token prediction loss on a corpus recieves, in its training signal, only facts about which tokens co-occur with which. Nothing in the objective or the data carries information about non-linguistic reality: no perception, no intervention, no feedback from acting.

(3) Token statistics underdetermine truth conditions. For any assignment of referents and truth conditions that fits a corpus, you can build an alternative that fits exactly as well: permute the referents systematically, everywhere at once (swap copper and iron throughout, or shift every object a foot to the left). Because the permutation is applied to the whole language at once, every statistical relation among tokens is preserved.

(4) So two models — same architecture, same objective, training corpora that are exact mirrors of each other — could have internal states with completely different truth conditions, and nothing in the training setup or in the models' behaviour on text distinguishes which one has which.

(5) If nothing distinguishes which truth conditions a system's states have, there is no fact of the matter about what those states are about.

(6) If there is no fact of the matter about what a system's states are about, it doesn't understand.

(7) Current LLMs are pure next-token predictors in the relevant sense. Instruction tuning, RLHF, prompting, tool use: either more of the same signal or downstream of weights set by it. Scaling changes the quantity of the signal, not its kind.

So no present or future LLM, however big, understands anything. Its all, as she puts it, extremely sophisticated pattern matching on shapes.

Here's my problem. The argument seems to run just as well on things I'm not willing to give up:

- A human who learns everything from text. Say someone raised in a sealed room who reads the whole internet and never sees the sky. If (2) through (6) are right, she doesn't understand 'rain'. I think she does.
- Me and electrons. Nobody has perceptual contact with an electron; all I have ever encountered is symbols in a theory. If (2) does the work it seems to do, my understanding of electrons is in the same boat as the model's. I dont want that.
- The Chinese Room thing (I think that's what it's called) — she says yes, that's the same argument, and she's happy to bite that bullet, which makes me suspicious.

So here's my own attempt at a tie-breaker, which I want you to evaluate rather than just accept: the swap test. Take a moderate-sized training corpus, systematically exchange two natural-kind terms everywhere they appear, including in the passages that state their downstream consequences, retrain a small model on the swapped corpus (I've got a couple of consumer GPUs, this is a weekend of work), then probe it. My prediction: the swapped model isn't silent or random about the world. It will confidently say things that are false in a structured, consequence-tracking way. And 'confidently false' looks like it requires content — you can't assert a falsehood without saying something. But if the swapped model has content despite its training data being severed from the actual referents in exactly the way (2) says is fatal, then either the permutation in (3) doesn't buy (5), or the original model had content all along.

I'm not sure whether that actually breaks the tie or whether I've just begged the question — maybe the honest description of the swapped model is that it understands a false theory while the original understands a true one, which assumes the original already had content.

What I want from you:

- Name the step you'd reject and explain why it looks fine on first reading. If the argument is actually valid and the real question is which premise is cheapest to drop, say so and price the options.
- I'd rather keep (1) and (2) and the claim that the text-only physicist understands electrons than keep (5). Tell me what keeping all three commits me to, and which of the standard positions — the grounding/causal-contact one, the functionalist one, the dispositional/inferential-role one — can keep them and which can't, and what each pays.
- Does the swap test actually discriminate between the two sides, or does it smuggle in what it's supposed to test? Is there a version of her argument that survives even if the permutation step is junk — one that leans only on (2)?
- Is there a reading where both she and I are right, because 'understand' is doing two different jobs? If so, which one is the one people actually care about in this debate, and does answering that question dissolve the disagreement or just relocate it?
