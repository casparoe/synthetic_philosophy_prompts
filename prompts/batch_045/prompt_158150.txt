I teach a final-year undergraduate seminar in philosophy of physics and I am writing the exam paper. I want one essay question on the reality of the quantum state that cannot be answered by writing down the ψ-ontic/ψ-epistemic distinction and reporting that a famous theorem refuted the epistemic side.

Constraints on the question itself. It must be answerable in 45 minutes, in about 1,000 words, closed-book, with no mathematics beyond whatever the question supplies. So state every distinction and every result the candidate needs inside the question. In particular, give the ψ-ontic/ψ-epistemic contrast in its ontological-models formulation (a model counts as ψ-epistemic just when distinct quantum states can be represented by overlapping probability distributions over the underlying physical states, so that a complete specification of the underlying state is compatible with more than one quantum state), and paraphrase in your own words the content of the 2012 no-go result of Pusey, Barrett and Rudolph: no model in which the quantum state is mere information about an underlying physical state, and in which independently prepared systems have independent physical states, can reproduce the predictions of quantum theory. Nothing in the question should depend on the candidate having read any particular paper or on their remembering a name.

The crux I want candidates forced to pull apart has three parts. First, the ψ-ontic/ψ-epistemic question as just defined. Second, a different question: whether the wavefunction is a physical object — a field on a configuration space of very high dimension — as opposed to something nomological, a codification of the dispositions of matter or of the dynamics rather than a piece of the furniture of the world. Third, the option of keeping a ψ-epistemic model by denying the independence assumption. A candidate who runs these together has missed the point; one who distinguishes them but assumes that settling the first settles anything about the second has half-missed it.

To stop the third part being a gesture, require the candidate to construct the strongest version of the independence-denying reply and then to say what it owes us: how two preparations we treat as independent could nonetheless come out correlated in their underlying states, and how that move differs from — or collapses into — the familiar complaint against superdeterminist responses to Bell's theorem.

I also want the question to end somewhere harder than taxonomy. Require the candidate to compare the cost of giving up independence with the cost of accepting a wavefunction on a configuration space of enormous dimension (including the difficulty of recovering the appearance of ordinary three-dimensional goings-on from it), to say whether those two costs are denominated in the same currency at all, and either to describe what a tie-breaker between them would have to look like or to argue that none is available and that the dispute is therefore idle. Candidates must take a position, not survey the options.

Please give me:

1. The question exactly as it will appear on the paper.
2. A model answer of about 900 words.
3. A marking scheme out of 25: five must-hit points at 5 marks each — the three parts above, plus the point that Bohmian and Everettian theories are both ψ-ontic while giving incompatible answers to the object-or-law question, plus the candidate's own stance on the tie-breaker. For each point say what earns 3, what earns 4, and what earns 5. Then give a one-paragraph A/B boundary: what an A answer must contain that a B answer may lack. I want the boundary to turn on whether the candidate separates the first question from the second and whether they commit to something on the tie-breaker, not on how much terminology they deploy. Add one line per point on how candidates typically get it wrong.

Two instructions about the model answer's epistemic posture. Separate what is settled from what is contested among people who work on this: the content and logic of the no-go result, and the fact that Bohmian and Everettian theories are ψ-ontic, are settled; whether denying independence is as costly as it looks, whether a nomological treatment of the wavefunction is genuinely a ψ-ontic option or a way of changing the subject, whether configuration-space realism can recover the appearance of three-dimensional space, and whether the dispute as a whole is one that further experiment could settle, are all contested. The model answer must not present any of the contested items as the consensus, and the marking scheme should reward a candidate who flags an item as contested even when they dissent from the model answer's stance, while penalising one who asserts a contested claim as received doctrine. Finally, close with a note of three or four lines on where the model answer takes a stance that a good candidate could reasonably reject.
