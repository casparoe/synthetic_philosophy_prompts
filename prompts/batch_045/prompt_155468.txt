So I'm in this little Marxist reading group — eight of us, meeting in someone's front room, working through the base/superstructure material — and last night we got properly stuck. Someone was arguing that a serious wealth tax plus sectoral bargaining laws could shift class power without anything changing at the level of who owns and controls the means of production, and Dave (there's always a Dave) just said "the base determines the superstructure" and sat back like he'd won the argument. I don't think he'd won, but I couldn't say why, because I realised I had no idea what the slogan was actually claiming.

Can you lay out the distinct precise things "the base determines the superstructure" might be asserting? I want a menu, not a verdict: for each reading, what exactly it says, whether it's near-trivial, whether it's substantive and arguable, and whether it's probably false. Then at the end, say which of them would actually do the work Dave needed — i.e. rule out law feeding back into the relations of production in a way that changes anything — and whether that one survives.

A few things I want you to engage with rather than talk past:

1. I made up a distinction on the way home and I want to know if it's any good. Call it channelling vs composing. On the channelling reading, the base narrows the space of stable superstructures — legal forms, ideologies, state structures that can't reproduce the relations of production get filtered out over time — without picking which one you actually get; lots of very different superstructures can sit on one base. On the composing reading, the base generates or fixes the specific content, so you could in principle read off the law or the religion from the property relations. Is the channelling version strong enough to be an interesting thesis at all, or does it collape into something nobody denies? And which of the two does the slogan usually get used as, in practice, by people who aren't being careful?

2. Someone in the group who reads more than anyone says the whole thesis is unfalsifiable, because whenever you produce a counterexample — some legal form or ideology that clearly isn't explained by the economy — defenders either reclassify it as "really part of the base" or retreat to "in the last instance". I think half of that is just the old Engels hedge, but she might be making a sharper point: that "economic" ends up functioning as a label for whatever does the explaining, so the thesis is true by definition and says nothing. Is that a fair way to put the worry? And is it the same worry as the "in the last instance" retreat, or is one of them a scope restriction (only long-run, large-scale change gets explained) and the other a genuine get-out? Please separate them if they're separate.

3. Then there's the reading where "determines" is functional: the superstructure is as it is because being that way stabilises or reproduces the base. I've heard defenders say this is the only way to make the thesis non-mysterious and that functional explanations are respectable elsewhere in science. But how do you cash out that "because" without it being spooky? What would the mechanism have to look like? And how would you ever test it against the boring alternative — that whoever holds power stabilises whatever happens to suit them, and the fit between base and superstructure is largely coincidence or ex post rationalisation?

4. A test case to run the readings against: if the base really determines the superstructure, what should we predict about two countries with very similar property relations and very different legal systems — say a common-law system versus a civil-code one? If the honest answer on most of your readings is "not much; superstructures can differ wildly under one base," then say plainly how much determination is left over, and whether what's left is enough for Dave.

Also: Gary — different guy — kept insisting that "determines" is being used in a special sense, closer to "sets the limits" or "poses the problem" than to "causes", and he cited that bit in the 18th Brumaire (I think) where Marx says people make their own history but not under circumstances of their own choosing. Is that one more item on the menu, or just a restatement of one of the others in nicer language? If it's the slogan plus a shrug, say so.

Format: a numbered menu of readings, one crisp line each on what it claims, then roughly a paragraph on plausibility and on what it would take to defend or refute it, then a short closing section on which one Dave was reaching for and whether it holds up.
