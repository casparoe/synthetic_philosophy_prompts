I teach a first-year undergraduate seminar, "Courts, Committees and Collective Reason," in a politics-and-law programme, and I have one fifty-minute session to get students to the point where they can state, and then explain to someone else, why a bench or committee that settles every question before it by majority vote can end up collectively committed to a set of judgments that no individual member holds and that cannot all be true together — the discursive dilemma. Almost none of them have done any formal logic, they arrive thinking of voting as a matter of choosing between candidates or options, and a few will assume the topic is really about jury bias or about how to run a meeting well. Plan the session for me rather than explaining the material to me.

Order. Give me the sequence in which the components have to appear, with the dependency: the move from voting over options to voting over connected propositions, the point at which a logical relation (two premises that jointly entail a conclusion, neither entailing it alone) has to be on the board relative to the vote profile, and the point at which I draw the collective judgment set and let them see the collision. Mark any step where the natural order tempts me to move faster than they can follow.

Carrying example. I want one invented example — not the textbook one, since I want to be able to tune it — that carries the whole session. It must have: a small odd-numbered decision-maker; two premises that look like genuinely separate questions and a conclusion entailed by their conjunction; a profile on which each premise is majority-accepted while the conclusion is majority-rejected; and it must be invertible, so that by flipping the profile I get the mirror case in which the conclusion is majority-accepted although neither premise is, letting me show that premise-first and conclusion-first procedures can come apart in either direction. Tell me what the example must contain and what it must avoid: the premises must not look like restatements of each other, the conclusion must not be a rewording of one premise, and the example should not turn on a dispute about the facts rather than about how a rule applies, because that confusion will consume the session if the case invites it.

Contrast. Several will have met majority cycling over three or more candidates. I need them to leave able to say what is different here — that no individual member is inconsistent and that there is no cycle to point at; the defect is in the collective set. Tell me where in the sequence, and with what board work, to make that contrast land.

Misconceptions. I expect at least these: that the majority is simply contradicting itself and can be charged with irrationality; that the remedy is to vote only on the final outcome, where I need them to see the cost — a body that owes reasons cannot just announce a verdict, and the premise route can deliver an outcome most members would reject; that it is an artifact of having three members or of a rigged profile; and, subtlest, that they will miss that the majority for the first premise and the majority for the second can be made of different people. Give me the further misconceptions I should expect, and for each the exact moment in the session to defuse it and the single sentence that does the defusing.

Refusals. I will not open the door to general impossibility results about aggregation rules, and I have no time for weighted or probabilistic voting. Tell me what to say, and what to say I am declining to say, when the strongest student in the room asks whether some rule avoids the problem entirely.

Check. Give me one closing question, set on a structurally different case — a panel applying a written rule with three conditions to a grant application, say — whose answer tells me whether a student has the idea or has merely learned to fill in the table.
