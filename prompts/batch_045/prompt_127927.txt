I write a column on science and ideas, and I'm filing a long piece on whether the Enlightenment is best read as the source of our best tools or as the intellectual front end of domination. Before I write it, I want an audit of my own position, because I'd rather find the inconsistency myself than have a reviewer find it for me. Here is where I've landed, stated as plainly as I can:

1. Epistemic universalism about the sciences. Claims about the natural world are not true-for-a-culture; there is no separate Indigenous physics or Western physics, and I think the move of insisting that there is one condescends to the people it is meant to honor.

2. A genealogical claim about how that universality was made. The practices that made universal science possible — the survey, the census, the botanical and anthropological collection, the standardized table — were developed in and for colonial administration, and were valuable in large part because they made land and populations legible to people who wanted to rule them.

3. A name for the resulting problem, which I've been using in my notes: credibility laundering. The authority that scientific institutions are able to spend in public today is not only earned by successful prediction; a good part of it is credit acquired under conditions the institution no longer acknowledges, refinanced each generation without an audit.

4. Laundering is not refutation. I'm willing to accept the findings of nineteenth-century comparative anatomy and to keep using the taxonomic instruments while condemning what both fed into. That a tool was used for domination is a reason to watch it, not a reason to distrust its outputs.

5. A method for intellectual history: judge ideas by their downstream effects rather than by the intentions of their authors. Heroes and villains are both bad historiography; what matters is what an idea made possible once other people had it.

6. An asymmetry. I accept a cumulative story about scientific knowledge — we genuinely know more than an eighteenth-century naturalist did — but I'm skeptical of the parallel story about morals, which strikes me as whig history with better manners.

7. A verdict on a case I keep returning to. When a museum is asked to return human remains and objects gathered during a colonial survey, I think the objects should go back: the conditions of acquisition were coercive, and present standards are the right ones to apply. I also think the museum may keep and publish the measurements, casts and scans already taken, on the grounds that data are not property and that suppressing them helps no one. I don't think presentism is a valid charge against either half of that.

8. The remedy I actually believe in. The correction for Enlightenment error is more Enlightenment: more unrestricted inquiry, more organized fallibilism, more public criticism. I want to say that without retreating into the both-sides settlement — source of progress and of domination — which seems to me a way of declining to give a verdict at all.

Please audit this set. Which of these commitments really pull against each other, which only look as though they do, and where am I relying on a distinction I haven't earned? Order the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally serious; for each, say precisely what would have to give and what that would cost me in the argument I'm trying to make. If one of the commitments is defensible only on a reading narrower than the one I've stated, tell me which, and what the narrower version is. Finally, name the single revision that would clean up the largest part of the set at the least cost to the piece.
