I am working through a difficult passage in Leibniz's fifth paper against Clarke, in the 1715–16 Leibniz–Clarke exchange. The text is more than three hundred years old and compressed, and I want help understanding one step in his argument for the principle of the identity of indiscernibles, not a general explanation of the principle or a survey of later debates.

The relevant passage runs, in paraphrase, as follows. Leibniz claims that there are never two things in nature that are perfectly indiscernible: even if two drops of water or milk appear exactly alike, a sufficiently powerful microscope would reveal some difference. He connects this to the principle of sufficient reason by saying that if two things really were indiscernible, there would be no reason why God should place one here and the other there, nor why there should be two rather than one.

I want to test a specific reading of this step, which I will call the no-unanchored-symmetry reading. On this reading, the point of the microscope is not primarily that our unaided senses are unreliable; it is that the drops are embedded in a wider relational order, and it is that wider order, not the drops taken in isolation, that supplies the difference. The microscope stands in for an external anchor: the rest of creation, the observer's frame, or the divine intellect. The implicit principle would then be that genuine numerical difference requires a symmetry-breaking relational difference, not merely some relation or other. To make this precise, say that a relation is irreflexive when nothing bears it to itself, and asymmetric when if x bears it to y then y does not bear it to x. A merely symmetric irreflexive relation, such as being two miles from the other sphere in an otherwise empty universe, does not, on this strong reading, break symmetry enough to ground distinctness.

Now apply this to Black's spheres. Call the hypothesis that a universe containing only two perfectly similar spheres, two miles apart, with no external relata, no observer, and no other asymmetry, is possible: the symmetric-duplicate hypothesis. In that world the only salient relation is the symmetric distance relation, which is irreflexive and symmetric.

My question is how to read the Leibniz passage in a way that makes it informative here. Does the argument from sufficient reason require the strong asymmetry reading, so that the symmetric two-mile relation would fail to make the spheres numerically distinct and the symmetric-duplicate world would have to be impossible? Or is a weaker reading, on which any irreflexive relation, even a symmetric one, is enough for what is sometimes called weak discernibility, compatible with the passage, so that the spheres are already discernible and not a counterexample? I am especially unsure what the microscope is doing: is it doing metaphysical work by importing an external relatum that makes the relation asymmetric, or is it only an epistemic illustration that apparent indiscernibility is always superficial? Please explain which reading better fits the move from indiscernibility to a violation of sufficient reason in the passage, and say where the argument would fail if the only available relation were the symmetric distance in Black's universe. Assume we are not allowed to appeal to primitive haecceities or brute thisness; the issue is whether the relational structure alone can supply the needed difference.

Answer in no more than 550 words, in three short numbered sections: (1) what the microscope step is doing, epistemic versus metaphysical; (2) what relational requirement the argument from sufficient reason actually needs; (3) the verdict on the symmetric-duplicate hypothesis and the biggest cost of your reading. Do not quote or cite any other text, and do not provide a bibliography or reading list.
