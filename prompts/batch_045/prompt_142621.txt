I am writing a paper for a volume on the metaphysics and ethics of virtual worlds, and I have stalled on a claim in its second half that I can no longer tell whether I believe. The claim is about a case I built deliberately to defeat the deflationary line I usually give my students — that virtual objects are nothing over and above patterns in the physical states of servers, and that virtual harms are therefore ordinary harms with an unfamiliar causal history. Call the world in my case Palimpsest. In Palimpsest the game state is held in a rotating, erasure-coded store that migrates continuously across machines: at no time is there a determinate set of physical states that realizes any given item, the physical description that would pick out a single item is wildly disjunctive and different from moment to moment, and no counterfactual about the hardware depends on whether that item persists. What persists is entirely at the level of the world: shared, resistant to any individual player's will, norm-governed, and available for projects that last years. No money changes hands anywhere in Palimpsest. Now: A spends four years cultivating an item that the world's rules make unique and non-transferable; B destroys it by means the code permits and the community's norms forbid. The proposition I want a number on is this: B's act is a genuine moral harm to A — it gives B a non-instrumental reason not to do it, and a bystander would be right to prevent it — and this is not merely a cause of, or a metaphor for, some further physical or psychological harm. I will count the question as resolved only if, once the sub-questions are settled, no disagreement remains about whether B wrongs A; I do not count it as resolved if everyone simply agrees to apply the word harm.

My own credence is about 0.65, and I expect it to be unstable. A colleague in social ontology sits at about 0.9 and thinks this is straightforwardly the same kind of harm as burning someone's letters, while a metaethicist in my department sits at about 0.15 on the ground that only setbacks to welfare are harms and everything else here is either a fiction or a real effect on other people's attitudes. What I want from you is your own number and, more importantly, the machinery that produces it. Please decompose it over the sub-questions you think actually carry the weight — I have candidates, but use your own: whether a harm must consist in a setback to some physical or psychological state; whether a harm can be essentially object-dependent at all, and whether object-dependence survives when the object's physical realization is indeterminate, as in Palimpsest; whether the thought that this is only a game is constitutive of what players owe one another or merely a heuristic about how much people in fact invest; whether harm requires the victim's uptake, so that the case where A never logs in again and never learns would not be a harm at all; whether the moral kind here is identical to that of destroying a physical keepsake or a distinct and lesser kind, and whether my paper's conclusion even needs the identity claim; and how much of the answer is hostage to whether harm is an explanatory notion or an irreducibly normative one. Give a number and a one-line justification for each term you use, show how you combine them and why that combination rather than another, and tell me which term your final number is most sensitive to and which you are least confident about.

Then the part I actually care about: given your decomposition, where do the three of us most likely disagree? Is the gap between my 0.65 and the 0.9 mostly about object-dependence, or about whether the game's norms are constitutive? Is the gap between me and the 0.15 one sub-question doing all the work, and if so is it a question anyone could settle? And what would move your number most — I have three candidates and would like you to rank them and add better ones if you have them. First, an empirical result we cannot actually get but can imagine: a longitudinal study of players in a Palimpsest-like world whose unique projects are destroyed, tracking whether the severity of their reported setback follows the fate of the item or their uptake of it, and how it compares to the loss of a physical keepsake of similar biographical weight. Second, a formal or semantic result about whether singular reference to, and quantification over, an item with an indeterminate and ever-changing realization is even coherent — if it is not, I think my deflationary fallback collapses and the realist owes me an account of what the item is. Third, a distinction I might be missing that dissolves the question rather than answering it, perhaps something in the neighborhood of the distinction between a harm and a setback to someone's practical identity, or between constitutive and merely regulative norms of a game. Please do not spend your words surveying the literature or naming positions — I have the bibliography and I can place your answer in it myself; I want your number, the derivation, and an honest statement of what you would have to learn to change it.
