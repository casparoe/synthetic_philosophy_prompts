I'm on the grants committee for a small literacy funder, and we're about to switch from narrative reports to a handful of headline numbers for the thirty-odd nonprofits we fund. Every time it comes up somebody says "Goodhart's law" and the conversation stops, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's a real objection or just a reflex. So before I write the memo: give me the desiderata for a precise, usable statement of Goodhart's law — the kind that could actually tell us whether to adopt a metric, and how hard we're allowed to lean on it.

I keep bumping into what look like four different things and I can't tell whether they're one phenomenon wearing four hats:

(a) plain statistical drift — the proxy is only correlated with what we care about, so pushing on the proxy partly pushes along the bit of the correlation that isn't causal;
(b) strategic adaptation — the orgs change what they do to move the number, including quietly redefining the activity the number was supposed to track (our version: tutoring sessions get shorter, or the kids who turn up get easier);
(c) constitutive cases — where the target is partly built out of the measurement, so moving the number just *is* moving the target, and it isn't obvious anything bad has happened at all;
(d) signal decay with nobody behaving any worse — the number stops tracking because the population shifted or the job got harder.

There are two slogans that get repeated in this space, one about statistical regularities collapsing once you put control pressure on them and one about a measure ceasing to be a good measure once it becomes a target. I want a spec that says what each of them is missing, and which of (a)–(d) is the central case a statement of the law has to be about rather than an edge case.

Requirements I already think belong on the list — each with the case behind it — but tell me which of these are real desiderata and which are just the prejudices of one camp:

- it has to be falsifiable, i.e. it has to say what a pressure-resistant measure would look like; otherwise it isn't a law, it's a mood
- it has to be about degrees rather than a cliff: we need to know how much pressure is tolerable and what sets the exchange rate
- it has to seperate the target getting worse from the signal getting worse, because a spec that conflates them will make us throw out metrics that are working
- it has to handle single-optimizer cases (the ML "specification gaming" thing, where one system with a lot of search power finds the cracks) and many-agent cases separately, if they really are different
- it has to say something about second-order measures: we're also considering a metric whose whole job is to detect gaming of the first metric, and I'd like to know in advance whether that's a treadmill

Then: score those two slogans briefly against the list, name the single requirement that does the most work in excluding candidate formulations, and flag any pair of desiderata you suspect can't be jointly satisfied — if the list turns out to be unsatisfiable as a whole, that's the most useful thing you could tell me.

And the bit I'll actually read: what would change your mind? Which requirement would you drop first if I argued it was a prejudice rather than a desideratum, and where is your judgement about which of (a)–(d) is the central case most likely to be wrong? Skip citations and anything you'd have to look up.
