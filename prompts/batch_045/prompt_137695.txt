Setup (correct me if i'm mangling it): in GEB, when Hofstadter asks where meaning is located, he splits a message into three layers. The frame message is carried by the sheer fact that the object is a vehicle at all — it says there is a message here and it can be decoded, plus something about what kind of vehicle this is. The outer message is what you get by transcribing the signal itself, the marks or grooves or codons, without yet knowing what they are for. The inner message is what shows up once a decoding mechanism has gone to work on the outer message, and in principle the same outer message can yield different inner messages in different decoders.

The case i want: two variants of one situation in which the frame message differs, the outer message is held fixed, and the inner message comes out different — in content, not just in mood or emphasis. Constraints, and these are the ones that matter:

- The outer message has to be genuinely identical across the two variants. If you photographed or scanned just the signal at high resolution you couldn't tell which variant you were looking at. So no changing a word, no changing the font if the font is doing semantic work, no extra readable material that the decoder takes in as content.
- Same decoder: one ordinary adult human, same background knowledge, same language, no difference in what they were told beforehand or happened to overhear earlier. If the variants differ only because the receiver knows different things, the case is worthless to me — that just relocates meaning into context, and I already know context matters.
- The difference has to come from the frame alone: what kind of object this is, what it is made of, where it sits, what it is attached to, what it is for.
- Realistic and small. A workplace, a hospital, a pub, an airport, a kitchen drawer. No aliens, no archaeologists, no lost civilisations, no artefacts from the future. One short paragraph for the scenario.
- The two inner messages have to be incompatible — the decoder can't rationally take both to be what was meant — and the difference must be in what is being said or done, not just in how it lands emotionally.
