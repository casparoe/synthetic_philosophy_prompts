I'm writing a term paper for a philosophy of medicine seminar, and before I commit to defending any one account of health and disease, I want the specification. Fix the purpose narrowly: the theory is to be used to decide whether a condition goes on a list that determines insurance coverage and research funding, so adequacy means adequacy for that classificatory job, not for everything.

Please set out the desiderata such a theory must meet — the cases it has to classify, the distinctions it must not collapse (having a disease versus how serious it is; being unhealthy versus being badly off; disease versus injury; the health of an organism versus the health of its parts or of a trait), and the empirical results it must not contradict — each with the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and each tagged as either non-negotiable or a preference of one camp.

Three cases my seminar handout did not cover, and which I want the list to be tested against rather than stipulated around: (i) a heritable variant that lowers a statistically typical capacity but, in the environment its bearers actually live in, both extends their lives and raises their lifetime wellbeing; (ii) a population in which everyone receives the same intervention at birth that permanently lowers some capacity, so the lowered level becomes statistically typical and something no one is worse off for; (iii) an asymptomatic person some years before the onset of symptoms, where the question is whether health must be gradable at a time at all rather than only over a life — I suspect the answer to that one changes what several of the other requirements cost. Also please flag, as you go, which requirements secretly presuppose a theory of function, since I have not picked one.

Then say which subsets of your requirements are, as far as you can tell, jointly unsatisfiable — if the list is itself a result, that is the part I most want — and which single requirement does the most work in excluding the standard contenders.

Please use these sections, in this order: "Requirements" (about 700 words, numbered, each entry with its motivating case and its tag); "Conflicts" (about 250 words, on the jointly unsatisfiable subsets); "The binding constraint" (about 150 words, on the one requirement that rules out the most candidates and why); "Scorecard" (a compact table, under 200 words, scoring against your list only three views: a Boorse-style biostatistical naturalism, a Wakefield-style harmful-dysfunction hybrid, and a normativist view on which health consists in the ability to achieve one's vital goals). No citations or reading list — I can supply those myself.
