I'm running a 75-minute session next week in an upper-level undergrad seminar on rational choice and decision theory — 22 students, mixed majors, they've done expected utility, backward induction and a bit of game theory, and I know for a fact that at least four of them have already seen the dollar auction demo online because one of them brought it up in office hours last week.

Which is my problem. I want them to feel the pull of escalation of commitment, and the standard dollar auction — I mean the one where a dollar bill goes up in small increments and both the highest and the second-highest bidder pay whatever they bid — is exactly the demo I'd normally reach for, but it's not going to work on a room that's already wise to it. Half of them will refuse to bid on principle, someone will open at a dollar to shut the whole thing down, and the ones who do bid will be performatively self-aware about it the entire time.

So I need something better designed than what I'd improvise. Constraints:

- 22 people, 75 minutes total, and I want at least 30 minutes of actual play. Happy to break into groups of 4-5 running their own auctions at the same time.
- No real money. Points, chips, a token prize, whatever — but losing has to sting a little, I dont want them giggling through it.
- I can be the seller if I have to be, but I'd rather not be, since they'll assume I'm running a trick on them. Suggest how to handle the credibility thing.
- They shouldn't hear the words "sunk cost" or "escalation" from me before the debrief, and they shouldn't be told which condition they're in.

What I actually want them to hit is not "sunk costs are a fallacy," which they already believe and can recite. I want them to run into three different things and not be able to tell them apart while they're in them: (1) the pure trap, where once you're in, continuing really is the better move given the rules and the whole mistake was entering; (2) the case that looks the same but isn't, where continuing really is throwing good money after bad because nothing new will be learned by continuing; (3) the case where continuing past a loss is genuinely right, because further investment is the only way to find out whether the thing is worth finishing — so the naive rule is the wrong rule there.

To make (3) live, and to make (1) and (2) harder to separate, my idea was to give each player a private and different value for the prize. Then someone who keeps escalating past everyone else might be a trapped fool, or might be the one person for whom the prize is actually worth more, and nobody — including the other players watching them, and including me — can tell which from the outside. If that's a good idea, tell me concretely how to assign and keep the private values secret. If it's a bad idea, say so and give me something else that does the same job.

What I want back: rules and materials, how to run the rounds and keep time across simultaneous groups, a short prediction sheet they fill in before they play, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the room onto the three-way distinction above — including the point that something can be rational locally and irrational as part of a plan. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: the non-bidders, the colluders, the groups that finish in ninety seconds while others are still going at twenty minutes, the student who bids a dollar immediately — and what I should actually do in the room in each case. And be honest about what the game can't reproduce about real escalation in organizations: there, nobody knows which of the three situations they're in, the goal itself keeps getting quietly redefined, there isn't a clean endpoint, and walking away costs you your face in front of colleagues. If the activity can't produce that part and I have to supply it in the debrief, say so plainly and tell me what to say.
