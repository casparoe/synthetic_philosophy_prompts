I have been asked to give the retirement toast for my doctoral advisor, Miriam Kessel, and I am finding it much harder than the eulogy I gave for my father, which at least had a conventional shape. Miriam spent thirty-five years in a sociology department arguing that generations are not real kinds, and she was not a deflationary writer by temperament — she simply thought the evidence pointed one way. Her positive view, which I need to get right because a third of the room are her colleagues and former graduate students and will wince audibly if I mangle it, ran roughly as follows. Birth cohorts are real and causally potent, but what makes them real is administrative rather than natural: school-entry cutoff dates, draft lotteries, pension eligibility thresholds, the state of the housing and labor markets in the year you turned twenty-two. She called these "administrative kinds" — partitions that do genuine causal work because institutions act on them, not because they cut nature at its joints — and she liked to say that cohorts are causes while generations are characters. Her other constant theme was a level confusion: an aggregate difference between two cohorts can be robust, replicated, and large, while the distributions still overlap so heavily that almost no individual member of either cohort has the trait in question, so that "Millennials are X" is at best a claim about a mean and at worst a pure artifact of period effects or of a small, unrepresentative subgroup that the label was built to describe. She also held that generational labels can acquire a second-order reality by being taken up and acted on by the people they are applied to, which for her was a reason to be careful with them rather than a reason to think they were vindicated. The complication is that I now make my living as a VP of generational insights at a brand consultancy; my team published, three weeks ago, a report claiming that Gen Z detects inauthentic brand voice within roughly eight seconds, it was widely mocked, and Miriam's email to me consisted of the sentence: "I raised you better." She also wrote in the invitation that if anyone at the dinner says she shaped a generation of scholars, she will get up and leave.

So I need a five-to-seven-minute toast that threads this. It has to work for two audiences at once: her department, who will catch every sloppy inference about cohorts and who know exactly which claims in that report she would have anatomized in a seminar; and the rest of the room, which is her ninety-one-year-old brother, her two granddaughters aged nineteen and twenty-three, and my own sixteen-year-old daughter, none of whom want a lecture on age-period-cohort identification. It should be affectionate rather than apologetic, and it should not be either a recantation — I do not intend to stand up and announce that my job is nonsense, because I do not think that and because it would be its own kind of self-dramatization — or a defense of the eight-seconds report, which I would rather acknowledge honestly and briefly and then move past. There is one inside joke for the sociologists somewhere in it, ideally built out of her own distinction rather than out of nomenclature, and it should land for the non-specialists too. The department chair also asked me to say something about where her work leaves the field, but her work's upshot is deflationary, and I would rather convey that in the grain of the toast than tack on a paragraph of prognosis. Please write the toast: it should not use "Boomer," "Millennial," or "Gen Z" to ascribe a trait to anyone, though it can mention them as labels under discussion, and it should end in an actual toast that a room can raise a glass to.
