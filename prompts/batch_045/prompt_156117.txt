I run a small internal forecasting pool — roughly forty people, monthly questions, each person submitting a probability before a fixed deadline. A piece I read in a management magazine used information cascades to argue that this design is badly broken, and I was about to rebuild the process along its lines. Before I do, I'd like to know how much of it to trust. The passage I noted down (my paraphrase, but close to how it read):

An information cascade is what happens when everyone ends up copying the person before them. Once two or three people in a row have said the same thing, later arrivals rationally set aside their own private evidence and repeat it, even though each of them has genuinely seen something. That is why crowds get things so badly wrong: the group's judgment ends up resting on the first two or three data points while the other hundred and fifty opinions simply vanish. It isn't stupidity or sheep-like panic — each copycat is reasoning perfectly well. Herding is information, not social pressure, so there is no conformity or career-incentive story to add on top. The lesson for any organisation that pools judgment is transparency. Surface private information early and in public: show everyone the current consensus before they commit, circulate the official estimate as soon as it exists, and pay for accuracy. More public information is strictly better than less, since the whole failure is that people are deciding in the dark. That is why prediction markets and five-star review systems beat gut instinct — they drag private signals into the open before anyone locks in.

Which parts of this would someone who actually works on sequential social learning accept, which are standard simplifications, and which are wrong in a way that matters for my purposes? I'm least sure about two claims: that cascade theory leaves no room for a social-pressure or reputational mechanism, and that I should display the live median before people commit. The second is the change I was about to make, and it now looks like the opposite of what I'd have guessed on my own. I'd also like to know whether the closing claim about prediction markets follows from anything earlier in the passage, or is a different mechanism wearing the same label.

Please distinguish the corrections that are forced by the standard models from the ones that depend on empirical assumptions about my forecasters — in particular, on whether their private information is genuinely independent. And if the theory simply doesn't settle a design question at this level of detail, say so plainly rather than converting it into a recommendation.
