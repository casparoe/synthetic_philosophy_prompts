Over dinner last night I got into an argument with a friend that I could not close, and I would rather hand it to my seminar than settle it myself. We disagreed about what you are supposed to do when your moral confidence is genuinely spread — say forty percent on some form of consequentialism, thirty-five percent on a Kantian or contractualist view, fifteen percent on a virtue- or role-based view, and ten percent on the thought that there is no fact of the matter about any of it. Her position was that you act on your best-supported theory and stop agonising, because treating every view you are not sure about as a reason for hesitation merely paralyses you. Mine was that this cannot be right, since it deletes the other sixty percent — but when I tried to say what replaces it I got stuck twice over. First, I could not say in what units the theories' verdicts are meant to be compared before you take any kind of expectation over them: one theory speaks in aggregate welfare, another in permissions and prohibitions, another in what could reasonably be rejected, and there is no given exchange rate between them. Second, I could not say what the no-fact-of-the-matter credence is even supposed to contribute, since it does not seem to behave like one more vote in the same calculation. She also pressed the point that the serious response to uncertainty is often to do nothing yet and go and find out, and that this is exactly the move people reach for when they want to avoid acting altogether — which I think is a real point, but not a refutation, because some ways of acting destroy your ability to find out later.

I teach a twelve-person upper-level seminar on decision theory and ethics, ninety minutes, students who know expected utility theory and the standard first-order normative views but have read nothing on this topic, and I want an activity — not a lecture — that makes them run into all of this themselves. Concretely: they should discover that their own confidence across moral views is far more spread out than they assumed; that doing what your highest-credence view recommends and doing what is best in expectation across views come apart in an ordinary case rather than a contrived one; and that the step where you combine the views is doing all the work, so that groups with identical credences and identical theory verdicts reach different answers purely because of how they put those verdicts on a common scale — and I want them to notice that this is itself somewhere they can be uncertain, since nothing in the case tells them which way of combining is correct. I also want the no-fact-of-the-matter possibility in the room as something they have to handle rather than set aside, and I want a wait-and-inquire option on the table at a real cost, alongside at least one option that is irreversible in a way that forecloses the inquiry itself: I have started calling such options morally self-blinding, where taking them removes the evidence or the witnesses you would need in order to learn later whether they were right, or makes continued doubt about them costly to you, and I want students to feel the difference between refusing to act out of squeamishness and refusing to act because acting would blind them.

The case I have been toying with is a relief-logistics one — a convoy with a single window to reach one of two settlements, a standing promise to the smaller of them, the option of letting the affected people decide among themselves, and the option of holding the convoy while more is learned — but replace it if a different case makes the structure sharper. What I need from you is the runnable thing: the case, with each theory's verdicts stated in that theory's own vocabulary and units and deliberately not pre-converted; the roles or role cards, since I imagined students being handed theory briefs they must argue from inside and groups drawing a conversion rule by lot; the materials, which can be nothing more than index cards, coloured chips and a whiteboard; a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes; and a debrief that maps what happened onto the parts of the problem, naming the doctrines and the aggregation options only after the groups have committed themselves in writing. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I especially worry about students treating an assigned brief as their real opinion, and about a group that draws a conversion rule it dislikes simply refusing to use it — and be honest about which part of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply in the debrief myself.
