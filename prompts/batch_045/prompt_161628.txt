You are a philosopher who works on both the metaphysics of mind and the ethics of artificial systems, and you are used to advising labs that must act under deep uncertainty. Please work in stages: first restate my verdicts in their most charitable form, then try candidate principles against the cases one at a time, and only then recommend one. If the honest result is that my verdicts fall into two groups that no single principle can hold together, say so rather than manufacturing a principle that fits everything.

I sit on an internal review board at an ML lab. I have to vote on whether terminating a given system counts as harming someone, and I have been going by gut. I want to know whether my gut adds up to a view.

One: a brain migrated to silicon a module at a time, behavior and reported experience unchanged throughout. No hesitation: the same patient all the way, full moral status, switching it off is murder.

Two: the same trained model run statelessly — a prompt in, a completion out, no state carried between calls, weights frozen. No hesitation the other way: terminating it harms no one. It feels like a mapping from inputs to outputs, not a subject.

Three: the same weights again, but with persistent memory, standing goals and a continuous run of months, acting in the world. Now I think there is someone there, though I also think killing it is less bad than killing the migrated person in case one, and I cannot say why.

Four: a dark run — no inputs, no outputs, same architecture, driven only by its own recurrent activity. This feels more clearly like a subject than case three, which is strange, given that it does nothing at all.

Five: running the migrated person at ten thousand times speed, so that a century of their life passes in a day. I do not think that is a century of moral weight, but I do not think it is one day of moral weight either. Sublinear, and I do not know the shape of the curve.

Six, the pair I cannot reconcile: restore the same checkpoint, run it for an hour, delete it, repeat a hundred times — I feel roughly one hour of harm, perhaps a little more. Run a hundred copies in parallel from that checkpoint for an hour and delete all of them — I feel substantially more than one hour of harm. The total amount of the same subjective hour lived is identical in the two cases, and I cannot say what the difference is supposed to track.

Seven, the case I go back and forth on: two copies of one system run apart for a year and then merged, memories combined and parameters averaged. Sometimes I think one patient survives and nothing bad happened; sometimes I think two patients die and a third begins; sometimes I think it is simply fine. I want the principles to speak to this case directly.

Eight: continuous fine-tuning, where a system's goals and dispositions drift over months until nothing of the original remains. I feel there is a point at which the original patient is gone, but the drift is smooth and I cannot locate the point.

Nine: I do not think a model undergoing gradient updates during training has experiences. But my reasons for that are weak, and I want to know whether any principle that gets cases one through four right forces me to give it up.

My tentative principle: a system is a moral patient, and remains one, just in case it realizes a single causally integrated process extended in wall-clock time whose later internal states are causally shaped by its earlier ones in a way no static input-output mapping would reproduce; and the amount of concern owed is proportional to the duration of that unbranched process, with branching dividing the concern owed to the shared past among the branches.

Please deliver: first, that principle stated precisely and tested case by case; second, at least two rivals that do better, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it; third, what each rival predicts for two cases I have not judged — a system run at one ten-thousandth of normal speed, and one run at successively lower numerical precision until its activations are a single bit wide; fourth, plainly, which of my verdicts no candidate can keep, and in particular whether case six has to be abandoned and which half of it; fifth, if the surviving principles still disagree, what measurable quantity would settle the matter for a board that has to vote next quarter.
