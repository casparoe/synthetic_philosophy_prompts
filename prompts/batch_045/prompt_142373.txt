I'm writing a short position paper for a workshop on decision-making under normative uncertainty — half the room will be philosophers, half people who have to make funding and research decisions on behalf of others, so I've tried to state my views in plain terms and without hedge-words — and before I circulate it I want to know whether the package I've assembled actually hangs together. Here, as compressed as I can make it, is where I've landed.

(a) The right response to normative uncertainty is to hedge: assign each of the first-order normative theories I take seriously my credence in it, weight how much it favors each available option by that credence, and choose the option with the highest weighted total. I individuate theories coarsely for this purpose — utilitarianism, pluralist deontology, contractualism, a virtue-theoretical view — rather than splitting each into variants. (b) But I resist a move that (a) appears to need. Most of the theories I give non-negligible credence to are not consequentialist: they contain constraints, options, and supererogation, and they do not rank options by how much value each produces. So I don't think every theory can be made to yield a cardinal measure of the degree to which it favors an option, and I especially don't think a deontic constraint should be represented as a very large disvalue — that strikes me as misdescribing what such a theory says, not merely as leaving something unmeasured. (c) I reject what I'll call the domination of tiny chances: I don't accept that for any small credence there is a payoff large enough that the product swamps every competing consideration, and in particular I am unmoved by arguments of the form that some small chance of shaping an astronomically large future beats everything else. (d) At the same time I am a strict probabilist about credence: credences satisfy the probability calculus and update by conditionalization; the practical stakes of a decision do not get to raise or lower the credence itself; and adjusting credences at the point of decision — discounting them, capping them, treating small ones as zero because the payoff makes me nervous — is ad hoc. (e) I take the persistent disagreement of people I count as epistemic peers in moral philosophy to be genuine evidence, and I think it should flatten my credence distribution substantially toward rough parity among a handful of views even where my own arguments point one way; and I don't see why this should stop at the first-order level, since my peers disagree about how to respond to normative uncertainty itself. (f) I think I have weighty obligations to particular others — my collaborators, my students, my own long-standing projects — that are not merely instrumental contributions to impartial value and that do not disappear under aggregation. (g) And here is the case I test everything against: a research protocol would expose one identifiable, non-consenting person to a small risk of death; I have about 0.9 credence in a pluralist view that forbids this regardless of the aggregate benefit, and about 0.1 in a utilitarian view on which the protocol has roughly a one-in-a-thousand chance of unlocking something that would go extremely well for an astronomically large number of future people and is otherwise worthless. I think the answer is that we don't run it; I think I'd give that answer if the chance were one in a million and the payoff a million times larger; and I think I'd go on giving it as my credence in the utilitarian view rose, until somewhere near the very top of the range the answer would switch — but not gradually, and not because the stakes had grown. (h) Finally, I think that very often the right thing is to postpone the decision and go do more moral philosophy first, and I want that to be an option inside the deliberation rather than an excuse for avoiding it.

What I want is an audit, not a survey: I don't need the literature, a reading list, or a history of who said what. My collaborators keep insisting that (c) and (d) are the pair that cannot both be true; I'm not convinced, since it seems to me I might keep strict probabilism about credences and locate my resistance to astronomical-payoff reasoning somewhere else entirely, though I can't say exactly where — so please don't simply ratify their diagnosis either. Rank the tensions by how severe they actually are instead of listing them as equals; for each genuine one, say precisely what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would be; tell me which of the apparent conflicts dissolve under inspection and why; and where one of my commitments is not really inconsistent but merely underspecified, identify the missing parameter and how much work it is doing. If I had to surrender exactly one item to make the rest cohere, which should it be, and what would surrendering it cost me?
