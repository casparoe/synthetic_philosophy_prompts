I sit on the therapeutic use exemption committee of an international sport federation. Our code prohibits a long list of substances and methods, but allows an athlete to use a prohibited item when a documented medical condition requires it; the committee's job is to decide whether the proposed use is therapeutic — restoring the athlete's functioning — or an enhancement, taking an already-healthy athlete beyond what their own body would do. We receive a few dozen applications a year and decide them in fortnightly meetings, roughly twenty minutes and one file per application, with no power to request further tests: we rule on the file in front of us. Three of us are physicians and one is a lawyer; none of us has training in philosophy. Grants are published in anonymised form with a short statement of reasons and can be appealed to an independent arbitral body. Using a prohibited substance without one is a ban, and athletes who hold one sometimes win medals with it.

Please write a decision procedure we can actually run on an application inside twenty minutes, grounded in what the philosophical literature on the therapy/enhancement distinction really says, and honest about where that literature gives us nothing usable.

- Give the questions in order, and for each, what counts as a sufficient answer drawn from a file — stated so that a physician can fill it in without knowing the theory.
- Mark where the procedure stops with a yes, where it stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Those value judgments should be named as value judgments, not disguised as medical criteria.
- Our reasons are published, so add a publicity test: any step whose real justification we could not print in our published reasons should be flagged, and either reformulated honestly or dropped.
- Say which distinctions drawn in that literature must survive the simplification and which we should abandon as unusable in our setting, and why.
- Most of our disagreements, I suspect, reduce to a choice between three different baselines: the athlete's own earlier level of functioning, the statistical normal for a reference population, and the level of functioning the sport itself demands of its participants. Say whether that diagnosis is right, and if it is, make the procedure force us to choose one baseline and to state which we are using in the published reasons.

The applications that break us, which the procedure must handle:

1. A male cyclist in his mid-thirties with documented hypogonadism whose testosterone is low as a consequence of anabolic steroid use earlier in his own career. His levels are below the population normal but above what they were while he was doping.
2. A nineteen-year-old endurance runner, clinically normal on every measure, whose history of stress fractures makes her physician expect a breakdown under the training load she intends to carry next season; the application is for a prohibited agent to build bone and muscle beforehand, so that she can train at the level her event requires.
3. A forty-year-old marathon runner applying for a drug that reverses age-related decline well enough to restore him to the performance he had at thirty — his own earlier baseline, but above the normal for his age.

Run the procedure on all three. At least one of them must be a case where the procedure returns an answer my committee will find unwelcome; tell me plainly which one it is and why we should accept it anyway. Then state what no procedure of this kind can settle, including whether any protocol can avoid taking a position on what the sport is for. Do not cite or name any literature: I need the distinctions to survive being restated in our own words.

Begin with a single sentence giving the procedure's governing principle — the one sentence we could put on the wall above the table — then give the procedure and the worked applications, with no preamble and no restatement of what I have asked for.
