Everyone in the memory-politics reading group I'm in keeps falling back on the same analogy: a community's relation to a past atrocity is basically like an individual's relation to a repressed trauma, so working through it is healtheir than letting it go. The duty-to-remember people use it to argue for memorials and truth commissions; then the pro-forgetting side runs it backwards, saying that for a collective it's the rehearsal that keeps the wound open, so what's needed is less like therapy and more like a statute of limitations.

Can you audit the analogy itself rather than the politics? Give me the correspondence table — what plays the patient, the trauma, the therapist, recovery — then the places the mapping breaks, and whether either conclusion survives once those are on the table. Two things I want weighed specifically: whether the analogy smuggles in a single remembering subject with an interest in its own health, when in these cases what counts as the community is exactly what's being contested; and my own attempt at a fix — I've been distinguishing veto-memory from archive-memory, where the first is memory kept live so it can license present claims (this land, this veto, this grievance) and the second is just kept on file. Does that track anything real, or am I re-describing the arguement in fancier words? And if the trauma picture is the wrong one, is there a better analogy available — from law, or property, say?
