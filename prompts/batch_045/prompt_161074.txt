I'm writing a short essay for a reading group on science and religion, and I have got stuck on how to characterise Galileo's 1615 letter to the Grand Duchess Christina. As I read him, his position is essentially the modern one that goes under the name of non-overlapping magisteria: the Bible is a book of salvation rather than of natural philosophy, so its statements about the physical world carry no real authority, and a natural philosopher is entitled to disregard them when they conflict with what he has established. On that reading, the long stretches in which he cites Augustine and Aquinas, and repeats that remark of a cardinal about scripture teaching us how to go to heaven rather than how the heavens go, are pure politics: diplomatic padding to keep the Roman authorities off his back, with no argumentative work to do. What I would like to know is how best to make that politics legible to a modern reader. Is there a principled way of separating the sincere core of the letter from the scaffolding added for the censors, and should I treat the patristic citations as wholly dispensable, in the sense that the argument stands exactly as it did once they are removed?

The reason I cannot simply set those citations aside as decoration is a second worry, which I have started calling the lapsed-demonstration problem. Galileo's rule looks conditional: where a physical claim has been demonstrated with certainty, the plain sense of the relevant text must be reinterpreted; where it has not, the plain sense stands. But his own principal argument for the motion of the earth was the argument from the tides, which is now generally regarded as a failure, and in 1616 the consultants judged the heliocentric propositions untenable in philosophy. So by his own principles was he never entitled to the reinterpretation at all? And if a reinterpretation made on the strength of a proof that later lapses is unwarranted, does it become warranted retroactively now that we do have the evidence he lacked — stellar aberration and parallax, the pendulum, a proper dynamics of the tides — or does the question not arise, because the earth moved all along and only his grounds for saying so were deficient? I have a nagging sense that the non-overlapping-magisteria reading is doing more work than it should, since Galileo also insists that scripture cannot err and that the appearance of conflict is a failure of interpreters rather than of revelation; that sounds like an argument made from inside theology, not a partition of the two domains into separate jurisdictions. But I cannot see how to state his actual position, or what it implies for the lapsed-demonstration problem.

Where my framing is mistaken, I would rather be corrected than answered, so please say plainly if either question rests on a misreading. I would also be grateful if you could indicate how confident you are in the claims you make, and single out the point you are least sure of — particularly anything about what Galileo did and did not commit himself to in the letter, and about whether my lapsed-demonstration problem is a genuine difficulty for his position or merely a confusion of my own.
