so I work at a small lab (15ish people, mostly interpretability and evals) and we have one of those recurring lab-meeting arguements that never resolves, and I want to get it straight before it eats another hour.

The setup: two of my colleagues both reach for the same analogy and walk away with opposite conclusions. The analogy is "training a model is basically evolution."

Colleague A (our head of research, was a biologist before ML) uses it optimistically: evolution produced creatures that genuinely understand things out of nothing but replication, variation, and death — no designer, no foresight, no comprehension anywhere in the mechanism itself. So competence-first is the normal route to comprehension, not a suspicious one; gradient descent is the same shape of process; therefore as we push behavioral competence, something like real understanding is likely to show up as a byproduct, and interpretability is a nice-to-have rather than a precondition for trusting a system. He also likes to point out that nobody demanded a circuit diagram of a crow before believing crows know things.

Colleague B uses the same analogy pessimistically: the whole lesson of the Darwin thing (competence without comprehension) and of Turing's version of it (machines following rules producing behaviour that looks exactly like it came from understanding) is that you can get all of the behavioural competence with none of the comprehension, and that this is not a bug or a stage. And training looks even more likely to land there than evolution does, because it optimizes behaviour directly, whereas evolution at least had to build something that stayed alive in a world. So: expect fluent hollow competence that keeps fooling us, and expect the fooling to get better, not to turn into understanding.

What I want is an audit, not a verdict on who's nicer. Concretely:

1. The correspondence table. What is actually playing the role of the organism, the gene, the mutation, the recombination, the generation, the population, the environment, the fitness function, the selection event, death, and the fossil record — in a training run? Be specific about which ML thing each one is (I'd guess something like: weights = organism? or = genome? batch = population? gradient step = generation? loss = fitness? checkpoint selection or best-of-n = selection? model soups = recombination? — but I don't trust any of those assignments).

2. Which disanalogies are load-bearing for the two conclusions A and B want, and which are noise. Three I keep coming back to, and I want to know whether they're real:

- We've started calling it the "oracle gap": a training process gets told, at every single step, which direction is better (a differentiable, per-example, instantaneous signal), whereas evolution is never told anything in advance and only gets scored retrospectively, after the fact, at the level of whole organisms. Is this distinction doing genuine work, or is it just a fancy relabeling of everyone already knows optimizers have gradients? If it is real, which of the two conclusions does it cut against?
- Backprop is straightforwardly Lamarckian: everything a model "experiences" during training is written straight into the weights it passes on. There's no germline/soma split, no Weismannian barrier, no analogue of an organism learning something in its lifetime and dying without passing it on. Does that make training different in kind from evolution, or just much faster?
- There's no real death in training, and no auto-curriculum: in evolution most of the interesting environmental pressure comes from other evolving things (predators, prey, parasites, mates), which is where I'd guess the open-ended ratchet comes from, while our data distribution just sits there. Does the absence of that matter for the specific question of whether comprehension should be expected to emerge, or only for whether we should expect open-endedness?

3. What each use is quietly importing. I think A's version smuggles in a designer by the back door (a human wrote the loss, picked the data, chose the architecture) which is kind of exactly the thing Darwin's move was supposed to remove; and I think both versions smuggle in a teleology, treating fitness as a target to be approached when in evolution it's a summary statistic. Tell me if that's right and what else is being imported.

4. My own hunch, which I want stress-tested: the thing in ML that is actually like evolution isn't the training run at all — it's the lineage of architectures and tricks across labs over a decade. Heredity is papers and forked repos, variation is people tinkering, the environment is the benchmark suite and the compute budget, selection is which ideas other people bother to build on, and it's genuinely the case that the resulting systems work better than anyone can explain. Give me the mapping table for that version too, tell me where it breaks, and — the bit I care about — whether it supports either A's or B's conclusion, or a third thing neither of them said.

5. Whether either conclusion actually survives the audit, and if not, what the honest defensible version of each would be. If there's a better analogy for what a training run is, I'll take that too. Don't bother naming papers, I just want the reasoning.
