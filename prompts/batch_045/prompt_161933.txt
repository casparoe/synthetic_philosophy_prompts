I am a senior at a large public high school, and I convene the school's Philosophy of Science Society: roughly sixteen members, aged sixteen to eighteen, meeting once a week for a single seventy-minute period after school. About half of them are taking AP Biology; the rest have little science background beyond the required courses. All of them have strong opinions and a taste for argument. I want to run one session on the credibility of evolutionary explanations of human behaviour, and I want it to be a session in which they run into the difficulty themselves rather than one in which I summarise a dispute for them.

The problem I am trying to design around is this. Several members have absorbed, from short-form video and from arguing online, both the habit of explaining every human quirk as an adaptation and the equally confident retort that such explanations are unfalsifiable stories. If I open the session by naming that dispute, or by naming the well-known critics of adaptationism, both camps will recite positions they already hold and the vocabulary will do the work instead of the experience. So the structure should produce the problem first and supply the words only afterwards.

What I have in mind, which you should feel free to scrap or improve: a first round in which small groups are handed a human trait and asked to produce the most convincing explanation of it they can, competing for a small prize, with instructions that say nothing about evolution. My hope is that a large share of them reach for an ancestral or functional story unprompted, which is itself worth noticing. Then a second round in which the same groups are told to explain the trait as an adaptation, and are told that half the traits in the packet are real and widely reported and half are ones I invented, without being told which. After the reveal the point should be unavoidable: by their own judgement, their explanations of the invented traits were no worse than their explanations of the real ones.

But I do not want the session to end at evolutionary psychology is unscientific, and I do not want it to end at evolutionary psychology is fine. I want members to leave able to tell apart several distinct ways an evolutionary explanation can fail, and to say which of those failures is repairable. The distinctions I care about, each of which should have its own moment in the activity rather than being listed on a handout, are these:

1. The account predicts nothing that could not be accommodated: whatever the data had been, a story of the same shape would have been available. Make this vivid by having each group write the equally persuasive adaptive story for the opposite trait, then have another group, blind to which of the two was the real one, vote on which story is more convincing, and treat a split vote as the diagnostic result.
2. The trait needs no adaptive story at all, because it is a by-product of something else that was selected, a consequence of how the mechanism is built, or plain developmental variation.
3. Even granting the function, the thing being explained may not be a heritable psychological mechanism: it may be learned, culturally transmitted, or the output of a general-purpose capacity applied to local content, so the adaptation story has been attached at the wrong level.
4. The account rests on a specific claim about an ancestral environment nobody can observe, that environment is under-described, and several incompatible functions fit the same trait equally well.

Then a final round that turns the screw in the other direction: give the groups one of the traits they have already worked on and forbid stories. They must instead say what would refute the explanation, name the comparison class in which the prediction could come out differently — another population, another species, a measurable trade-off — and state the observation they would need. I want them to discover that some traits resist this treatment almost entirely while others do not, and that the ones that resist it are the ones where the objection bites.

Please give me: the complete rules and materials, including a packet of eight to ten traits written in the dry, neutral register of a textbook, half real and half invented, with a note on which of your invented ones I should verify before using and how to rephrase any that turn out to be real by accident; a minute-by-minute timeline for seventy minutes with four groups of four and no money available for prizes; the exact wording of the instructions I read aloud at the start of each round, with the opening instructions mentioning neither evolution nor adaptation nor any critic by name; a debrief that maps specific moments in the session onto specific points and introduces the vocabulary only after those moments have occurred; an honest list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how I should handle each — I expect at least one member who has read about this already and defuses the reveal, groups that smuggle empirical claims into their stories, the reveal landing as a trick that makes them trust nothing, and someone concluding that no evolutionary claim about humans can ever be tested; and a frank statement of what the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself in closing.

One constraint on the real traits: use only items that are uncontroversial common knowledge, and flag any you are not confident about, since I cannot check the specialist literature. If the session works, my members should leave able to say what the difference is between an evolutionary explanation that is merely a story and one that is a hypothesis, and why the same objection does not carry the same force against both.
