I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy working on formal decision theory, and an argument that came up in a joint reading group with the econ department has me stuck. I'll define the gambles from scratch so nothing hangs on labels or on anybody's memory of the literature.

Toss a fair coin until it first lands heads, and let N be the toss on which that happens, so P(N = n) = 2^{-n}. The Pasadena game P pays (-1)^{n+1} · 2^n / n when N = n: it pays +2 if the first heads comes on toss 1, -2 if on toss 2, +8/3 if on toss 3, -4 if on toss 4, and so on. The probability-weighted terms are therefore (-1)^{n+1}/n, i.e. 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., which sums to ln 2 in the order given, while the sum of absolute values is the harmonic series and diverges. A friend in the group used this to argue that expected-payoff ranking has no verdicts at all, not even about gambles as ordinary as a sure dollar against nothing. His reconstruction:

(1) For monetary gambles at stake sizes where utility is effectively linear in money, the rational ranking of gambles is the ranking by expected payoff, and a gamble's fair price is its expected payoff.

(2) A gamble on a countable outcome space has a defined expected payoff just in case the probability-weighted payoffs converge absolutely; when the positive and negative parts both diverge, there is no expectation, and hence no fact of the matter about what the gamble is worth.

(3) In P the terms are conditionally convergent: by the rearrangement theorem, for every real r and for ±∞ there is a permutation of the outcome space under which the probability-weighted sum equals r. So P has no expectation.

(4) No enumeration of a countable outcome space is privileged -- the ordering of states is a fact about how we write the gamble down, not about the gamble. So P's value is not merely unknown: every verdict about a comparison involving P can be reversed by an equally admissible evaluation. Undefinedness here entails unconstrainedness.

(5) Separability: for any gamble C independent of G and H, G is preferable to H if and only if G + C is preferable to H + C. This is the independence axiom in another guise, and it is what licenses cancelling shared components.

(6) Take any two gambles G and H with perfectly ordinary, well-defined expectations differing by some positive amount, and let C = εP for any ε > 0. The composites G + C and H + C have no expectation either, by (2) and (3). Worse, the composite payoff lives on a product of outcome spaces, and summing over a product space one of whose components is only conditionally convergent is not order-invariant: without absolute convergence the shared component need not cancel, so the usual inference from the value of G and the value of C to the value of G + C is unavailable. Hence expected-payoff ranking delivers no verdict on G + C versus H + C.

(7) But by (5), the verdict on G + C versus H + C just is the verdict on G versus H. So expected-payoff ranking delivers no verdict on G versus H either.

(8) G and H were arbitrary, so no two gambles are rationally comparable and expected-payoff ranking is empty even where everyone agrees it is well defined.

He also has a compressed version. Let P* be the anti-Pasadena played on the same coin with every payoff negated, so the package P combined with P* pays exactly zero in every state and is statewise identical to receiving nothing. If undefinedness licenses arbitrary admissible values, then the two components can be evaluated independently as any r and r', and additivity across components of a package makes the surely-null package worth r + r' rather than zero. So either additivity goes or (4) goes.

Two things make me think the pathology is not a knife-edge curiosity that I can quarantine. First, in the family P_α with payoff (-1)^{n+1} · 2^n / n^α, every α in (0,1] yields conditionally convergent probability-weighted terms and hence no expectation; absolute convergence arrives only at α > 1. Second, and this is the wrinkle I contributed: conditional on the first k tosses all having landed tails, the natural-order expected payoff of what remains is 2^k times the tail of the alternating harmonic series from term k+1, whose magnitude grows roughly like 2^k/(k+1) and whose sign alternates with k. So the game has a tidy unconditional value of ln 2 in the natural order while its conditional values, along the only paths where anything is still at stake, are unbounded and oscillate in sign.

I'm most reluctant to surrender (1) and (5), though I haven't earned that reluctance. So: which step do you reject? Explain why the step looked innocent -- what principle or habit of reasoning makes it seem like a trivial bookkeeping move -- and then price the alternatives: what does it cost to reject each of the plausible candidates, including the options of treating some gambles as simply incomparable with everything, of adopting a weakened or principal-value notion of expectation, of restricting separability to integrable components, of moving to a merely finitely additive probability, and of abandoning (1) for a bounded or otherwise non-linear treatment of money? Finally, given your diagnosis: is there a defensible number to pay for a single shot at P, and if so what is it and on what basis -- and if not, say plainly what it means for a decision theory to be silent about a gamble you can be handed.
