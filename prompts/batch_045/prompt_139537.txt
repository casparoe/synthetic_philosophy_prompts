I write novels rather than history, and I am currently finishing one set in London and Washington between August 1990 and March 1991. Its three central characters — a British junior minister, a retired diplomat who was a young man in the 1930s, and an American planner at the Pentagon — reach for historical analogies almost every time they open their mouths, and I want the arguments they have to be the arguments such people would actually have had, not the slogans that end up in newspaper columns. So I need someone to think the case through properly on my behalf.

Please respond as a professional philosopher with a working knowledge of decision theory and of how historians reason about evidence, and take the following as a commission for a case analysis rather than for a survey. Think carefully before you commit to anything: begin with a short phase in which you sketch, for yourself, the candidate analogies in play, the different things an analogy might be doing in an argument, and the ways a given episode could fail to fit the moral that is usually drawn from it. Only then write the analysis. Do not hedge every sentence; I would rather have a reading that turns out to be wrong than a balanced overview. And please write throughout in British English (analyse, behaviour, -ise endings). I am not asking for sources, citations or further reading — I could not use them, and I do not want them.

The episode. Analyse the crisis that ran from Iraq's invasion and annexation of Kuwait in August 1990 to the ceasefire of early 1991: the assembling of an unusually broad coalition, the UN authorisation, the air campaign, the very short ground war, and the decision to stop short of Baghdad. Take as given the bare facts of that sequence. The analogies in circulation were, overwhelmingly, two. Munich 1938: a dictator who must be stopped early, the shame of appeasement, the claim that restraint now purchases a larger and costlier war later. Vietnam: quagmire, no exit, the military's insistence on overwhelming force, clear objectives and an exit before committing, the memory of an open-ended land war in Asia. If you think the structural story about a rising power and an established power — the sort of thing people now summarise as the Thucydides trap — adds anything here, say so, but I suspect it fits this case badly and I want to be told if you agree.

The lens. I have been working with a distinction of my own, and I want it tested rather than flattered. It seems to me that an appeal to a lesson of history can do at least three quite different jobs, and that distinguishing them explains more than any general complaint that analogies mislead. First, a predictive job: the analogy places the present case in a reference class whose outcome distribution is supposed to be known, and thereby supplies a probability or a base rate — this sort of situation, left alone, ends in a big war with such-and-such frequency. Second, a procedural job: the analogy operates as a decision rule that reallocates the burden of proof, so that in doubt one acts, or in doubt one refrains. Third, an authorising job: it supplies a moral narrative that makes a course of action sayable in public and legible to an electorate, without, so far as anyone can tell, changing any actor's estimate of what would happen.

My working hypothesis, which I would like you to argue with, is that the first of these jobs is in practice the rare one and the second and third are the rule: that when politicians invoke a lesson they are almost never making a checkable prediction with a reference class behind it, and are usually shifting a burden of proof and licensing a course of action they have already settled on. I have two further hunches that go with it. One is that the stock of episodes available to be used as lessons is selected for moral clarity and narrative closure — a recognisable villain, a clean before and after — and that these are precisely the features that make such episodes unrepresentative of whatever class they are meant to represent, so that the currency is biased towards the vivid and atypical. The other is that the two families of lesson are epistemically symmetrical but not psychologically symmetrical: a lesson drawn from a failure of restraint and a lesson drawn from a failure of action have the same standing as evidence, but they are not held with the same weight or for the same length of time, and where both are available a speaker can nearly always find one that fits what they wanted to do anyway. Which raises a question I care about: whether the analogies in 1990 did any independent work at all, or were epiphenomenal, decorating decisions taken on other grounds.

What I want back is a disciplined mapping in both directions. On the side of the case: for each analogy, who the relevant agent is, what the options on the table actually were, what would have counted as evidence for or against the analogy at the time, and what a genuinely predictive use of it would have required — a specified reference class, a stated expectation, something that could have turned out otherwise. Then say whether either analogy was in fact better supported by what was knowable in 1990-91, or whether the honest verdict is that the pair cancelled out. On the side of the lens: whether my three jobs are genuinely distinct or collapse into two, where the case confirms my hypothesis and where it embarrasses it, and — this matters most to me — where the whole framework is silent. I assume it cannot tell me whether the war was right; I want to know exactly what else it cannot tell me, and whether there is some fourth job an analogy does here that I have missed, perhaps something about structuring attention or making a situation thinkable at all.

Finally, and this is the part I shall read first: close with a short note on how confident you are in your overall reading, expressed plainly, and single out the one claim in your answer you are least sure of, together with the consideration that would most quickly change your mind about it. If any step in your reconstruction of the analogy's role rests on your being unsure what a particular actor knew or believed, say so at that point rather than in general.
