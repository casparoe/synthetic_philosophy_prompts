hey — I run a small reading group and two of the regulars have been going at each other about what emotions even are for about a year, and it's got to the point where they just restate there positions in slightly different words. So I want to make them produce the document they should have written six months ago: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration.

The setup. Priya is a judgment / construal theorist: emotions centrally involve evaluative states that are belief- or judgment-like — they represent their object as dangerous, insulting, loss-worthy, whatever; they can be correct or incorrect; they answer to reasons; and they're the kind of thing you can be criticised or irrational for having. Marco is a bodily-feeling theorist: emotions are perceptions of, or states constituted by, patterned changes in the body — heart rate, breathing, gut, facial posture, readiness to act — and it's that somatic side that makes something an emotion rather than just the thought that something is dangerous. Neither has convinced the other, and I don't want a verdict on who's right. I want the text both of them could put their names to.

What needs to be in it:

A numbered agreed-claims section. Real claims, not filler like "emotions are complicated and multi-faceted". I mean: do emotions have objects, and what kind of object; can emotions themselves (not just the actions they cause) be assessed for rationality; do infants and non-human animals have them; where do moods fit; can you have an emotion without noticing you have it; is the bodily change a cause of the emotion, a part of it, or a mere effect; and what a theory of emotion is supposed to explain in the first place — the phenomenology, the way emotions rationalise action, the fact that they come in recurring kinds with characteristic expressions? If they can't actually agree at that level, say so and move it to the disagreements part instead of fudging it.

Then a disagreements table, with columns roughly: the crux; Marco's claim in wording Marco would accept for himself (not a caricature); Priya's claim the same way; whether the disagreement is substantive or only about which thing gets to be called "the emotion" — and if it's verbal, which part and why; the case or argument both of them agree would bear on it; and each side's prediction about what that case will show, written down now so nobody can move the goalposts later.

The bearing-cases I want used as tests, since this is where I think the action is — replace any of them with something sharper if you think of it:

Recalcitrance. Someone is fully convinced the glass-bottomed walkway is safe, says out loud that they know there is no danger, wants to be there, and still freezes and has to be helped off. Is that an emotion? Both need an account of it, and each should say in advance what they'd conclude if the same subject also reported no felt bodily change whatsoever.

The denervated subject (my hypothetical, fix it if it's badly set up). Someone whose interoceptive feedback is largely blocked: heart races, gut churns, none of it reaches them, while their ability to size up situations, act, and talk about what matters to them is intact. Do they have emotions? What would each side predict about whether they describe themselves as feeling things less intensely, and about whether their emotion-guided behaviour degrades?

Emotion with no situation. A subject in an empty room whose autonomic profile is made to match fear exactly — drug, stimulation, whatever — with nothing to be afraid of, no thought that anything is amiss, and nothing they're inclined to do. Emotion, bare feeling, or neither?

Fear of fear. Someone who isn't afraid of heights or dogs or dying but is terrified of having a panic attack, so that the object of the emotion is a bodily episode of their own. This one seems to me to cut against at least one of them, and I can't work out which.

Fiction and music. Being genuinely moved by a film you know is fictional, or by a piece of instrumental music that doesn't represent anything.

Offline emotions. Dread about a meeting next Tuesday, embarrassment about something from 2009: the object isn't present to the senses at all, and the bodily side is faint or missing.

One more thing I want handled: where does the "perceptual" or "attitudinal" option fit — emotions as perceptions of value, neither judgments nor bodily feelings? Is that a third camp they've both been quietly ignoring, or is one of them already committed to it without admitting it? If a chunk of there dispute is really a fight over which of three things deserves the name, tell me which chunk, and what's genuinely left over.

Length is up to you, but I want the agreed-claims section to be something I can read out loud to both of them without either one objecting to the wording. And don't give me names of authors, citations or a reading list — I know the literature fine, I just want the document.
