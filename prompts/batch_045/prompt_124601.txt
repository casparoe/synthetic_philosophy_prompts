I'm putting together a short talk for a film-and-philosophy night — nothing academic, twenty minutes max — on Kurosawa's Rashomon, and I want to do it through Jain many-sidedness (anekāntavāda plus the nayavāda and syādvāda machinery that goes with it) rather than the usual epistemology-of-testimony angle. Not the so-called Rashomon effect, the thing about eyewitnesses being unreliable, which is true enough but isn't what I'm after. I want to know whether the four accounts are really four partial-but-genuine standpoints on one event, in the Jain sense, or just four motivated distortions of a standpoint somebody could in principle occupy.

Here's the distinction I keep getting stuck on, and I'd like you to work with it rather than around it. Call many-sidedness vertical if standpoints are ranked and there's a maximal one — the omniscient view a kevalin is supposed to have — that contains the partial ones and shows each to be true only in its own respect. Call it horizontal if there's no such standpoint even in principle, and all you can do is hold explicitly indexed claims side by side without contradiction, which feels like a much thinner, more deflationary picture. My hunch is that Jainism officially wants the vertical version and the film pushes you toward the horizontal one, because the closest thing to a privileged standpoint we ever get is the woodcutter's late version, and he's the least trustworthy narrator in the movie. But I might be wrong in both directions, and I'd rather find out now.

So: does the film actually dramatize either version, or neither? Where does the lens earn its keep on the scenes — the trial, the medium channeling the dead husband, the framing at the gate, the baby at the end — and where does it distort things? Two things I especially want right. First, the Jain point that a one-sided assertion isn't vaguely half-true but is an outright error when stated unconditionally, while the corrected conditioned in-some-respect form is not skeptical hedging and not a maybe; I think that's exactly the distinction people mangle when they wheel Rashomon out as the poster child for relativism. Second, whether the ending does any philosophical work at all: the rain stopping, the woodcutter with the baby, the priest getting his faith back. Is that a move from one-sided assertion to something like a standpoint-transcending truth, or just a moral warm bath poured over an epistemic mess that the film has no interest in resolving? Be willing to tell me the reading doesn't fit — I've seen enough bad film-philosophy to know when a lens is being forced.

Also, since I'll be saying this out loud to people who know more about both Kurosawa and Indian philosophy than I do: tell me how confident you are in the reading overall, and single out the one claim you'd least defend if a specialist pushed back on it. I'd rather cut that bit than have it collapse in the Q&A.
