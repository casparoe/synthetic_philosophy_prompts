You are a research philosopher in animal and environmental ethics whose second specialty is population ethics, and you are used to defending your numbers in front of ecologists who check them. Work in stages. Begin with a short brainstorming phase of no more than 150 words in which you sketch three candidate sites or conversion mechanisms for the case and say, in a clause each, why you are discarding two of them. Then commit to one and build it. Do not look anything up and do not cite anything: every theoretical and empirical ingredient you need is supplied below, and part of the exercise is to build from these ingredients alone.

Background. Three positions, defined here so that no prior familiarity with any literature is required:

Symmetric Net-Welfare Totalism (SNWT). Add up welfare, in a single unit, over every sentient animal that lives over the relevant horizon. Positive and negative welfare count equally, and an animal that never exists contributes zero. Choose the option with the larger total.

Suffering-Prioritarian Totalism (SPT). First minimise total suffering; that aim has lexical priority. Only if two options tie on suffering do you turn to positive welfare, and then only as a tiebreaker.

The Present-Possessor View (PPV). A deontic side-constraint, not a maximising view: animals who already exist have a claim against being killed or made worse off for the sake of benefits that would accrue to different animals who would not otherwise exist. PPV says nothing about preventing future animals from coming into existence.

The task. Design one realistic decision about one piece of land such that SPT favours converting or destroying the habitat, SNWT favours preserving it, and PPV favours preserving it — where all three verdicts follow from the numbers you supply plus the definitions above, and not from anything smuggled in alongside them.

Design constraints.

(a) Matched cohorts. The two options must involve the same total number of sentient animal-years and the same total number of deaths, each within five per cent, across the horizon. This is the point of the exercise. The familiar argument that habitat destruction reduces suffering works by shrinking how many sentient lives there are; that lever is to be removed, so that whatever verdicts remain must arise from the shape of the lives involved — the rate of positive welfare while alive and the cost of the way each life ends — rather than from their number.

(b) Realism. A temperate-zone site, a decision plausible within the next fifty years, an ordinary planning, drainage, agricultural or offsetting mechanism. No science fiction, no genetic engineering, no species-level extinction, no miraculous predator removal. Only animals whose sentience is philosophically uncontroversial — vertebrates, and decapods if you want them — may enter the ledger.

(c) Both options must be options a real planning authority could genuinely weigh: lawful, fundable, and not obviously precluded by existing conservation duties.

(d) State the mechanism explicitly: are the animals currently on the site killed, displaced, sterilised, or merely prevented from breeding? PPV turns on exactly this, so it cannot be left vague.

(e) Fix a welfare unit and use it consistently. For each option give, per representative life: the positive-welfare rate per animal-year, the mean length of life, the number of deaths, and the one-off cost of dying (the suffering contained in the terminal event, whether predation, starvation, exposure, disease, or a managed killing).

(f) No rescue and no catastrophe. The preservation option may not be saddled with an extra disaster the conversion avoids, and the conversion option may not turn out to be, in effect, a rescue operation for animals who would otherwise have suffered.

Deliverables, in this order.

1. The scenario, in no more than 200 words: the site, the two options, the horizon, and the mechanism.
2. The ledger: a compact table with the numbers required by (e) for each option, plus the totals. Before continuing, check it for internal consistency — deaths, mean lifespans, and total animal-years have to fit together, and the matched-cohort constraint has to actually hold.
3. The three verdicts, each derived, not asserted: show the arithmetic that yields the SPT verdict and the arithmetic that yields the SNWT verdict, and state which clause of PPV the conversion option violates.
4. Which single parameter does the work? Give a sensitivity analysis: how far would it have to move before one of the verdicts flips, and is a move of that size empirically credible?
5. The flip test. Describe a minimal variant of the case — same site, same ledger, one feature changed — in which PPV alone switches to permitting conversion, and explain why that change moves PPV while leaving the other two verdicts intact. If you think no such variant exists, say so and explain why: an honest negative is worth more here than a contrived positive.
6. The best objection available to a defender of SPT who wants to reject your case, stated as strongly as you can make it. Then classify it: is it a dispute about empirical facts, about the choice of welfare unit, about the lexical-priority clause, or about what counts as one life? Say whether you think it succeeds.
7. A closing note of no more than 100 words on what the case does and does not establish.

Purpose and failure conditions. The case is for an interdisciplinary working group that wants to know whether the practical disagreement between suffering-prioritarian and symmetric welfarist approaches to habitat policy is robust, or whether it collapses once the size of the population is held fixed. Accordingly, the following count as failures: letting either option win by changing how many animals exist; stipulating welfare magnitudes a reasonable field ecologist would dismiss as fantasy, or leaving those magnitudes implicit; letting preservation win by appeal to anything other than welfare, such as biodiversity, naturalness, wildness, beauty, ecosystem integrity or duties to humans; and producing a scenario in which the three verdicts are stipulated rather than generated by the numbers and the definitions.

Audience and style. Write for a room containing two philosophers and two ecologists who will check your table. Be precise and unhedged where the arithmetic is clear, and explicitly hedged where it is not. Aim for 800 to 1,100 words in total, excluding the ledger.
