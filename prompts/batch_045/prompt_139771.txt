I sit on the editorial board of a university press series on contested pasts, and we have to rule on a submission. Our track record so far:

- rejected a manuscript arguing that Atlantis was a real Bronze Age thalassocracy, resting on Plato read as reportage plus comparative mythology;
- rejected an ancient-astronauts manuscript, on the ground that it inferred extraterrestrial instruction from engineering the author could not explain;
- rejected a New Chronology style manuscript holding that the whole documentary and dating record is a retrojected forgery;
- published, with a rejoinder by a specialist, a tightly scoped argument for pre-Columbian trans-Pacific contact built on botanical and linguistic evidence;
- published a monograph arguing that an apparent dark-age gap in a regional record is an artifact of preservation rather than a real collapse, and separately a monograph arguing that a colonial-era excavation looted and misdated a site — both methodologically conventional, both modest in what they overturn, and the second explicitly framed as reparative;
- rejected, on a split vote, a well-executed manuscript arguing from a small corpus of Neolithic marks that they encode a lunar calendar and push the origin of writing back two millennia, our letter saying the load-bearing evidence is too thin for the size of the revision, though we published the same author's narrow paper on the distribution of a single sign.

The new submission: from a reanalysis of one submerged harbour site, with a single radiocarbon series on short-lived seed material that a specialist says may come from a redeposited layer, the author argues that a culture the textbooks place in the second millennium BC in fact flourished eight centuries earlier and exported its script to two neighbouring regions, which would force the redating of three regional sequences. The manuscript works from the primary excavation reports, answers every mainstream objection in a forty-page appendix, states in advance what would refute it, predicts that a buried quay will be found at a named spot if the new dating is right, and offers to appear alongside a critic's rejoinder. Its preface says the point is to give a people back the antiquity that a colonial chronology took from them. The quay will not be excavated before we must decide.

A colleague proposes that we stop asking what would refute a heterodox claim and instead apply a symmetry test: demand of the revisionist thesis only what the received chronology it attacks itself meets, and no more.

Which rules fit all six of our verdicts, and where do they come apart on this one? Give me the decision that best coheres with our own precedents, the closest analog among them, an honest statement of the principle we would be adopting either way, and the single change to this case — or the further case — that would tell the surviving rules apart, including whether the symmetry test belongs in the set.
