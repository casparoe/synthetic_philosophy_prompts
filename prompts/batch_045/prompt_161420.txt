A political philosopher I have corresponded with for years has given me verdicts, case by case, on whether there is a duty to vote, and I want to predict how she will rule on a case she has not seen. Her track record:

1. A national election between parties with sharply different economic policies, polls at 50–50, 40 million voters, polling place five minutes away: yes, there is a duty.
2. The same election, except that voting costs a day's pay and six hours of travel: no duty — she says you are excused.
3. A purely advisory municipal poll on a matter of no consequence, free and five minutes away: no duty.
4. A legislative race in which her preferred candidate is 25 points behind in the polls with a week to go, stakes moderate but real, no cost worth mentioning: no duty.
5. A town meeting of 400 voters on a funding issue the town is split over, real stakes, trivial cost: yes.
6. A binding statewide referendum on a genuinely important policy, her side leading 68–32 in large polls, trivial cost, no turnout requirement in the enabling law: no duty.

The new case: a binding referendum on a genuinely important policy in a jurisdiction of 2.4 million registered voters. She favours the measure, is reasonably well informed about it, and voting costs her fifteen minutes. Large polls put the measure at 68–32, so which way it goes is not in doubt. But the enabling law voids the result unless at least 40% of registered voters cast a ballot, and turnout is expected to land somewhere between 39% and 41%.

What I want from you: (i) the rules that fit all six of her verdicts jointly and that come apart on this one — I have two candidates in mind, one tying the duty to the chance that her own ballot flips the result and one tying it to whether this is a decision whose result or validity could turn on whether people like her show up, but I would rather you map the space than confirm my two — together with what each candidate rule predicts here; (ii) your best guess at her verdict, with the reasons you prefer it over the alternatives; (iii) the single variation on this case that would best separate the rules that agree here, and what her answer to it would show.

Please also state how confident you are, which step in your reasoning is most likely to fail, and which further verdict from her — or which fact about this case, if it turned out differently — would move you off your prediction.
