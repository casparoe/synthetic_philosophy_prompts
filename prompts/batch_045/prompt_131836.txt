I'm in the last year of a PhD and next month I'm presenting a chapter to my department's work-in-progress seminar. It's the chapter where I try to say, as exactly as I can, what I am committing myself to by calling myself a particularist about reasons, and my supervisor's complaint about the last draft was that I had produced a list of things particularists tend to like rather than a position with a shape. Before I try to fix that I want an audit from someone who isn't invested. So here is everything I am inclined to assert in the chapter, written as flatly as I can, including one constraint I invented myself and one case I lean on hard:

(a) Holism about reasons: whether a feature counts in favour of an action, and which way it points, depends on what else is true of the case; the same feature can be a reason in one case, no reason at all in a second, and a reason on the other side in a third, and there is no finite, general, informative specification of the conditions under which it does each. (b) Anti-principlism: the rationality of moral judgement does not depend on a supply of moral principles; the competent judge responds to the salience of the case rather than subsuming it, and principles are at best crutches that a good judge would outgrow. (c) Reasons are not the only reason-relevant features: some features enable, disable, intensify or attenuate without themselves counting in favour of or against anything, and this distinction is what stops (a) from collapsing into arbitrariness. (d) Anti-triviality: I want the dispute with the generalist to remain a genuine one, so I cannot win it with a manoeuvre that my opponent could use too — in particular, I cannot let any awkward feature be demoted to a mere enabling condition whenever a principle would otherwise break. This means I owe some non-ad-hoc account of what makes a feature an enabler rather than a reason. (e) The account I've proposed myself, call it Detachability: a genuine enabler or disabler must attach to a type of consideration in advance of the case, so that you can say which kind of reason it switches on or off without already knowing the verdict you want in the particular case; if the only thing you can say about a feature is that adding it here flips this verdict, it is a hedge and not an enabler. (f) I concede, against many of my allies, that for any body of particularist verdicts there is some hedged, disjunctive, thoroughly uninformative principle that matches all of them; I take the real disagreement to be about explanation, deliberation and what moral thought depends on, not about which verdicts are available. (g) I accept supervenience: no moral difference without a non-moral difference, necessarily and without exception. (h) I think moral deference is real and often the right response to disagreement: someone with better moral perception can tell you what you should do and you can thereby come to know it, not merely comply. (i) The case I rely on most. Devi and Tomas are colleagues with a standing arrangement to alternate the weekly departmental coffee duty; in week one Devi skips her turn unannounced, in week two Tomas does the same. I stipulate that everything that could matter is held fixed and equal across the two weeks: same arrangement, same twenty minutes of extra work for the other person, no further promise, no further need, no third party affected, and the same amount of inconvenience, the same being-let-down and the same annoyance. The only difference is that in Devi's week the two of them are in the middle of a low-grade feud in which each is quietly collecting grievances against the other, while in Tomas's week they are on cordial terms. My verdict is that what Tomas did is fine and what Devi did is not; and I want to insist that the feud is not itself a reason against anything, that it is not that the breach hurts more or that anyone is more upset, since I have stipulated all of that away — it simply switches on the small consideration already present, so that the twenty minutes counts in Devi's week and is silent in Tomas's. I also want to insist on something that makes this harder rather than easier: I cannot say in advance which kind of consideration the feud switches on, because in a case with a different trifle lying about it would switch on that one instead.

What I want from you is an audit, not a survey. Tell me which of (a) through (i) are genuinely in tension with one another, and rank those tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally serious; for each real tension say what exactly would have to be revised, restricted or abandoned, and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set; and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Please also mark off the pairs that merely look like they conflict, and say briefly why they don't, since I would rather have a short list of real problems than a long list of things I already knew were uncomfortable. I don't know yet which of these I would be willing to give up, so don't assume an ordering of my attachments, and don't assume that the item I added myself is the weak one — or that it isn't.
