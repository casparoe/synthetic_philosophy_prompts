I have been using the following argument in a short paper. My aim was to show that the frame problem is not a parochial defect of classical symbol-system architecture but a structural obstacle to any account of belief revision. A friend read a draft and objected that if the argument is sound it establishes something nobody could accept: that no finite agent, of any construction, has ever rationally revised a belief. I think she is right that this is what it establishes, and I am not willing to accept it, but I cannot find the bad step. I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

Set-up. Let an agent's standing beliefs be represented by a probability function P over a large set of variables V = {V₁, ..., Vₙ}. Evidence arrives as an observation Q (the values of one or more variables). Call Vᵢ *Q-sensitive* iff P(Vᵢ | Q) ≠ P(Vᵢ): those are the beliefs that Q rationally moves. A *relevance filter* is a function f that assigns to each possible observation Q a candidate set f(Q) ⊆ V — the beliefs the agent actually re-examines. Call f **safe** iff it never omits a Q-sensitive variable, i.e. f(Q) ⊇ {Vᵢ : P(Vᵢ | Q) ≠ P(Vᵢ)}. Call f **cheap** iff computing f(Q) and revising only f(Q) costs substantially less than order n, so that the filter does its work without canvassing the whole belief state.

(1) *No local bound on relevance.* Whether Vᵢ is Q-sensitive is settled by the entire joint distribution. Probabilistic relevance between Q and Vᵢ can be carried by chains of intermediate variables of arbitrary length, and can be created or destroyed by the value of any third variable: whether a shift in the price of grain in a distant province should move your beliefs about a colleague's reliability can depend on a dozen intervening links, and can be screened off or unscreened by the value of any one of them. So there is no way of reading off, from Q and Vᵢ together with any fixed bounded neighbourhood of them, whether Vᵢ is Q-sensitive. Equivalently: for every cheap filter f there is some prior P and some observation Q on which f is not safe.

(2) *Rationality is conditionalization.* A revision rule that is not safe is not one that Bayesianism sanctions. Conditionalization on Q moves every Q-sensitive variable, so an agent who ends up leaving some of them where they were has arrived somewhere no Bayesian update would have put them.

(3) *Boundedness.* Any agent that gets anything done — a robot, a human, a trained network — must finish revising before the world moves again, and V is enormous. So any real agent must use a cheap filter. Revision that is order n is simply not available to it.

(4) *Therefore no real agent revises rationally.* By (3) revision must be cheap; by (1) no cheap filter is safe; by (2) an unsafe revision is not rational. Note what (4) is not: it is not a complaint about symbol systems. It applies to anything with a large body of standing beliefs and finite time — to scientists, to online learners, to embodied agents, to whatever you prefer. So the argument cannot be deployed the way I was deploying it, as an indictment of one research program. That is the sense in which it seems to me to prove too much.

(5) *The obvious repair fails.* Store an explicit relevance map R alongside P — a structure recording, for each variable, which observations can affect it — and consult R instead of computing sensitivities on the fly. But R is itself part of the belief state. After Q arrives, R may need revising too, since Q can reveal that the map was wrong; revising R requires a filter; that filter requires a further map R′; and so on. The repair relocates the problem one level up.

(6) *A friend's variant of the repair.* Do not store a full relevance map. Store instead a *shadow structure* S: for each variable, a list of those other variables whose *relevance entries* it can affect, and nothing more. She claims S is sparser than R by an order of magnitude, and that the regress halts because updating S in response to Q only ever requires touching the shadow entries of the variables in f(Q). I cannot tell whether this is a genuine stopping point or just (5) with better bookkeeping.

Where does the argument go wrong — which step, exactly — and why did that step look secure enough to carry the weight I put on it? I would like to keep (1) and (3) if I can, since (1) seems to me to be close to a theorem and (3) to a plain fact about the world; but I would take a diagnosis that sacrifices (2) instead if that is cheaper. Please price the main alternatives rather than just naming one: what has to be conceded if the trouble is in (1) — for instance, that relevance is in fact structurally tractable and that the holism which made the problem look deep was overstated, together with whatever then has to be said about where that structure comes from — and what has to be conceded if the trouble is in (2), where the cost looks like losing a unified standard for belief revision and having to say which approximations are permissible and why. And please tell me what you make of the shadow structure in (6): is halting the regress there a substantive empirical claim about the sparsity and stability of higher-order relevance, or is it the same regress in disguise?
