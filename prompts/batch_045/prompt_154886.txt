I cover science and ideas for a newspaper, and this term I am setting and grading the final paper for an upper-level seminar on moral status that I co-teach. The module ends on the question of whether any fact about brains can do moral work, and I need one essay question on that crux: whether brain size — or any gross neuroanatomical quantity in the same family, such as absolute volume, encephalization quotient, or the move to counting cortical neurons rather than weighing whole brains — is ever morally relevant at all, and if it is, whether it is relevant as evidence of capacities we already care about or as something that partly constitutes moral standing.

Every version I have drafted so far can be answered by reciting the argument from marginal cases and the charge of speciesism, and by concluding that what matters is psychological capacity rather than anatomy. I want a question that makes that answer insufficient, so build it around cases the standard readings do not handle. Two I keep returning to: the intraspecies variation problem — stipulate for the purposes of the question that a human infant and an adult in advanced dementia differ enormously in the capacities we care about while differing little in neuron count, so any quantity that survives that contrast cannot be tracking very much; and the extension to artificial systems, where the analogue of brain size would be parameter count or training compute, and where there is no independent behavioural track record to calibrate the proxy against. Use those, or a better pair of your own, and make the question a single answerable essay prompt suitable for a ninety-minute exam.

Deliverables: the question as it would appear on the paper; a model answer of about 700 words; and a marking scheme out of 25 built around five must-hit moves at five marks each, with a one-line note on how students typically get each move wrong. The A/B boundary should turn on two things: whether the candidate notices that rejecting brain size as a criterion may only relocate the arbitrariness rather than remove it, since the underlying account of which capacities count is itself contested; and whether they distinguish relevance for moral patiency from relevance for moral agency and responsibility, where an anatomical proxy might do different work.

Finally, the model answer should mark which claims are settled and which are live disputes among people who work on this — whether sentience is sufficient for equal consideration, and whether law and policy may legitimately rely on proxies known to be imperfect, are both contested — and should not present either as the consensus. The marking scheme should say that a candidate who adopts a contested premise without flagging it as contested loses marks even when the reasoning from it is valid.
