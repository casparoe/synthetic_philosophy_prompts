I'm a retired protein crystallographer — well, I spent the last decade of it mostly running a facility rather than doing anything I'd call science — and about three years ago I drifted into a philosophy group at the local lifelong-learning place, which has become the thing I actually look forward to on Tuesdays. We're doing a one-off session on whether lying and misleading are morally different, and I volunteered to bring in a case study because I got annoyed at how bloodless the discussion was in the last meeting: everyone agreed there's "a difference" and nobody could say why it should matter to anybody. I picked Othello, which I've read twice and seen once, because it struck me that Iago is the most interesting figure here — everyone in the play calls him honest, constantly, and yet he seems to engineer an entire catastrophe while very rarely writing down, so to speak, a sentence he can be pinned to. He asks questions, breaks them off, arranges what people see, lets them draw the conclusions. That felt like the phenomenon the philosophers are circling.

The bit I've been pushing at the group, and that I want tested rather than flattered, is what I've started calling the subcontracted falsehood: the idea that what's distinctive about misleading is that the speaker gets the hearer to manufacture the false belief herself, using her own inferential machinery, so that (a) there's no false assertion anywhere on the record — I keep thinking of it in lab-notebook terms, there's nothing you'd have to retract — (b) the speaker keeps deniability, not just legal deniability but deniability to herself, and (c) the damage isn't merely that the victim ends up with a false belief but that she ends up as co-author of it, which corrupts something different: she can't repair the situation by deciding not to trust Iago, she has to distrust her own judgment. My hunch is that the play is on my side about (c) in particular — Othello demands the sort of proof he thinks he's entitled to, and the tragedy is partly that the proof gets built out of his own reasoning — but I also suspect I'm over-romanticizing it, and that a version of this reading makes Iago into something cleaner and more modern than he is, and quietly lets Othello off the hook.

So: give me a reading of the play through the lying/misleading distinction that takes both sides seriously. Where does my subcontracted-falsehood thing actually illuminate a scene or a choice, and where does the play resist it — including the possibility that Iago isn't a pure misleader at all and that the "he never actually lies" line is something a philosopher would like more than a Shakespearean would. Tell me which two or three scenes I should put in front of the group, and what you'd say about them. Please don't reduce the play to an illustration of a distinction, and equally tell me plainly if the distinction I'm pushing is wrong and the play is the thing that shows it. And no reading list or citations — our group's library is three shelves of Penguin paperbacks and I'm not chasing journal articles at my age; I want the argument, not its bibliography. It's a twenty-minute intro to about fourteen people, half of them retired scientists like me who will happily accept a diagram and half literature people who will not, so I need something that works for both without being bland.
