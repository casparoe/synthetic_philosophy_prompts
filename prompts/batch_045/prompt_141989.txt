I'm a backend engineer and somehow ended up running a biweekly lunch thing at work that's vaguely about "ethics in engineering" — it's mostly just me and 12-16 other engineers in a room for an hour, mixed levels, maybe two people who've read any philosophy at all, one guy who thinks the whole enterprise is performative guilt, and one or two who will go full utilitarian about ten seconds after you say "greatest number." Last month I tried to run a discussion on that Singer essay — the old one about famine, where he starts with a kid drowning in a shallow pond and you'd ruin your clothes to pull them out, then slides to "so why not send the money to strangers far away" — and it was a mess. Half the room kind of shrugged and agreed with the conclusion in a way that meant nothing, the rest treated it as a gotcha about how we should all donate everything and live in a tent, and nobody actually felt anything. I want a redo, as an activity rather than a discussion: people doing something with rules, not sitting around opining.

Two things I care about, and then the design constraints. First, I don't want anyone to hear the name Singer or the pond case up front — I want them to build the escalation themselves and only find out at the end what they'd been arguing about. Second, the currency shouldn't be money. If it's dollars, everyone's brain goes straight to their salary and charity overhead and it stops being about the structure of the argument. What I'd rather use is the thing my team actually fights about: interruption. So give everyone a budget of "focus" — like on-call paging budget, a limited number of context switches per hour — and make the cost of spending it *convex*: the first interruption is cheap, the third one in the same period wrecks your whole afternoon. I have a suspicion that a convex cost curve gives you something interesting here, like a stopping point that isn't just an arbitrary line you drew, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's a real answer to the argument or whether it just relocates the arbitrariness one level down — I'd like the activity to put the group right on that fork so they have to say which it is. I also want a mechanic for the "someone else would have handled it anyway" move: give each rescue a backup responder who usually but not always acts, so people can watch their own marginal contribution go to zero and then have to decide whether that dissolves the obligation or just launders it. And somewhere in there the distance thing has to show up — cases right in front of you versus a name on a list versus something you can only see as a statistic — but as a feature of the rules, not as me asking "but does distance matter, guys?" No real money, no actual donations, no leaderboard anyone cares about.

What I need from you: an activity I can literally run in 60 minutes with whatever I can print or hand out on index cards. Rules, materials, a payoff or scoring scheme that doesn't need real stakes, a minute-by-minute timeline, and then a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the specific moves in the argument — including where I should finally say the name out loud and how. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time: the two utilitarians derailing it, the guy who tries to game the mechanics instead of engaging, the person who checks out because it feels preachy, the risk of it turning into a fight about real charities. And be honest about which part of the argument the game can't reproduce — the bit where you just have to stop and tell them something, because no set of rules will get them there. Success for me is everyone leaving able to state the strongest version of the argument and their single hardest-to-shake objection to it, rather than "so I guess we should give more money," which is what I got last time.
