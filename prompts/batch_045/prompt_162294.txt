I am a final-year doctoral candidate in philosophy, and next week I am presenting a work-in-progress chapter to a joint seminar of my department's philosophy of action group and the university's behavioural science group. The paper is provisionally entitled Present Bias Without Discounting: Why Hyperbolic Curves Do Not Explain Akrasia. What I need from you is a preparation sheet for the question period, not a defence of the view.

The thesis I am defending is this. Hyperbolic (and quasi-hyperbolic) discounting is the wrong kind of thing to explain weakness of will. What a curved discount function can generate is diachronic inconsistency: an agent who at one time prefers the later larger good, and at a later time, when the smaller good has become immediate, prefers that instead. The reversal depends entirely on the two options standing at different temporal distances from the point of evaluation. But the paradigm cases of akrasia, on the standard Davidsonian picture, are synchronic: at the very moment of action the agent judges, all things considered, that A is better, and yet intentionally does B. Evaluate both options from a single point in time and any discount function multiplies equally dated consequences by a common factor, so the shape of the curve drops out and no reversal is produced.

I concede that the quasi-hyperbolic model has one feature that survives this argument: the discontinuous present-tilt parameter, the beta that discounts everything not available now. My central claim is that beta is not a rate of time preference at all. A discontinuity at the present has no interpretation as discounting the future more steeply; it is better understood as sensitivity to mode of presentation. Perceptually present options are evaluated as if at temporal distance zero, irrespective of when their consequences actually arrive; merely imagined or described options are placed at their objective distance. I call this the presentation-sensitivity account. It carries a testable corollary: manipulations that make a future option perceptually vivid (photographs, physical samples, odours, virtual reality walkthroughs) should reduce present bias, and within the near range the size of the effect should be comparatively insensitive to the objective length of the delay. If the account is right, the practical moral shifts too: the remedy for weakness of will is not primarily pre-commitment or the bundling of choices, but the deliberate recruitment of perception in the service of the agent's own all-things-considered judgement.

The audience is about fourteen people. Roughly half are philosophers of action: two work in the planning-theory tradition and treat intention as a distinct normative-explanatory kind, one is a neo-Aristotelian who thinks of continence and practical wisdom in terms of character rather than choice architecture, and two are general sceptics about whether dynamic inconsistency is a rational failing at all. The other half are behavioural scientists: one experimental economist who uses the beta-delta model and thinks of it as well-confirmed, one psychologist of intertemporal choice who works in a construal-level / psychological-distance framework, and a mixed group of graduate students who mostly want to know what experiment would settle it.

Please give me the objections I should expect, ordered by how much damage they would do rather than by the order in which they are likely to be asked, and phrased the way each of these constituencies would actually phrase them rather than in my vocabulary. For each: a reply I could deliver in about two minutes, and an honest note on where that reply is soft or question-begging. Tell me separately what I should concede in my opening two minutes to disarm the room, which single reply has to land if the paper is to survive at all, and the one question I am quietly hoping nobody asks. Assume roughly twenty-five minutes of questions. Please write the sheet in British English, and do not pad it with references or suggestions for further reading: I know that literature, and I need the dialectic, not the bibliography.
