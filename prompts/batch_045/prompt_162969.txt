What follows is the argumentative core of section 3 in note form; the prose version will run about twice this length.

Working title: "Subtraction, and What It Cannot Buy You"

1. The case, as I'm taking it: a human being is brought into existence all at once, mature and intellectually intact, suspended in the air with his limbs splayed so that they do not touch one another or anything else, with no sensation, no acquired belief, and no occurrent awareness that he has a body at all. Avicenna's claim is that such a person would still not fail to affirm that he exists, and that this shows self-awareness to be primitive — not derived from, or analysed in terms of, awareness of the body — and hence that the self is not the body.

2. Thin versus thick self-intimation (my first distinction). Thin: the subject is in a state that presents its own existence, non-inferentially and without presenting what the subject essentially is — knowledge that I am, with no knowledge of what I am. Thick: the subject is aware of itself as an immaterial, body-independent substance. My claim is that the thought experiment's first-personal report supports only the thin reading, while the dualist conclusion requires the thick one, and that nothing in the set-up bridges the two, because a state can present the existence of its subject without revealing that subject's essence.

3. Subtraction creep (my main objection). If the test is "whatever I can self-attribute without attributing P, I am not P," then run it for every P not analytically contained in I exist: memory of a past, agency, persistence through time, having a temperament, even being a person rather than a momentary subject. Self-intimation plausibly survives each subtraction, since none of these is required for the bare affirmation of one's own existence. So either the method overgenerates and leaves us with a self that is hardly anything at all, or it was never a reliable test for non-identity in the first place. The natural dualist escape — that these are all properties of the self while the body is something the self merely has — seems unavailable, because having a body is as much a property of the self as having a memory is, and because a property-less bare subject is not a self Avicenna's own account of the soul's faculties would recognise.

4. The addition probe (my constructive suggestion). Everyone has run the argument in one direction only: strip away bodily awareness and see what remains. Run it the other way and ask what happens to self-intimation when full bodily awareness is present — sight, touch, proprioception, appetite. Three outcomes seem possible: fusion, where self-intimation and bodily awareness become one indivisible state reporting one thing under two descriptions; accompaniment, where they are two states each of which could in principle occur alone; and replacement, where bodily awareness crowds out self-intimation. My claim is that the subtraction probe cannot discriminate between substance dualism and an identity-under-a-description view, since both predict accompaniment; only the addition probe could, and nobody has run it — so the move from the thought experiment to dualism has simply not been earned yet.

5. The content-vehicle gap (my reply on behalf of the non-dualist). A mental state can be realised by a bodily mechanism without having that mechanism, or any bodily feature, as its intentional object. Self-awareness is a candidate: its content is exhausted by the bare first-personal affirmation of existence and carries no bodily specification, even if its vehicle is entirely bodily. So "self-awareness has no bodily content" is compatible with "self-awareness is a wholly bodily state," and the inference to immateriality fails, because immateriality would have to be inferred from the vehicle — which is exactly what the thought experiment, being first-personal, says nothing about.

Where I suspect I may be cheating: my advisor remarked offhand that nobody has ever reached substance dualism from the flying man without an additional premise, and I take my subtraction-creep point to be that premise's difficulty made vivid — but I can't tell whether that is an insight or just a restatement of the advisor's remark. I also worry that the addition probe may be introspectively undecidable, in which case my conclusion should be the much stronger one that the needed work cannot be done at all, and I should either defend that or drop section 4.
