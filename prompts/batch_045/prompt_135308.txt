I work with a national space agency's Biosignature Classification Panel, which has to rule, for planetary-protection and sample-handling purposes, on whether candidate entities count as living. A difficult case comes before it in March, and I need both a prediction of how it will rule and a view on how it ought to rule if consistency with its own precedents is the aim. I don't want citations or reading suggestions; I want the reasoning.

The precedents, as I read the panel's published decisions:

- Influenza virion: not alive.
- A giant virus carrying several translation-related genes: not alive, but 4–3, the minority holding that the difference from a cell is one of degree.
- A bacterial endospore recovered from a sealed container two centuries old: alive; dormancy is irrelevant, since what matters is what the entity can do once supplied with what it needs.
- A prion: not alive. A viroid: not alive.
- A defective viral genome that replicates only in cells simultaneously infected by a helper virus: not alive.
- A mitochondrion inside a eukaryotic cell: not alive, though the panel accepted that its free-living ancestor was.
- A mule, and a sterile worker ant: alive; the panel said individual sterility is neither here nor there.
- A mammalian cell line, including cryopreserved stocks and cultures that persist only because technicians passage them weekly: alive.
- A siphonophore colony: alive, and a single organism, its feeding and stinging units being parts rather than organisms.
- A bacterium whose genome was chemically synthesised and then transplanted into a cell from which the native genome had been removed: alive. Note that the chassis was inherited from an existing cell line.
- Self-replicating computer programs that mutate, compete for processor time and evolve new functions: not alive; the panel's note said the decision turned on embodiment, not on evolvability.
- A robot that assembles copies of itself from parts supplied in a factory: not alive.
- A crystal that grows, fractures and seeds daughter crystals, transmitting lattice defects that affect growth rate: not alive. A flame: not alive.

The March case is a laboratory construct I'll call KS-4. It consists of fatty-acid vesicles enclosing a network of ribozymes with these features. The ribozymes catalyse the conversion of fatty-acid precursors present in the medium into fresh fatty acid, so the vesicle grows and divides, and the container is produced by its own contents. The same ribozymes catalyse templated copying of themselves with errors; variants differ in division rate, and over two hundred generations the population has adapted to a higher-salinity medium, though nothing more complex than a ribozyme has yet arisen and its builders disagree about whether it could ever sustain open-ended evolution. Every catalyst is RNA; there is no protein synthesis. The system requires experimenter-supplied activated nucleotides and pre-formed fatty-acid precursors, and does not make either from simpler starting materials. If that supply stops, the system decays and does not recover; it has no dormant stage. Placed in an unmaintained rock pool, it does not persist.

I cannot tell which test the panel is running. The precedents seem to me jointly consistent with something like a self-construction-plus-evolvability-plus-material-embodiment rule, with a stricter autonomy or closure rule, and with something more historical about descent from existing life, but I cannot get them to diverge in my own head.

What I would like from you: the candidate rules that fit all the precedents, stated precisely enough to apply; what each predicts about KS-4 and why; your best guess at the actual ruling, with the closest precedent identified and your reasons for preferring it; the single further case you would put to the panel to separate the surviving rules, with what each rule would say about it; and, if the precedents genuinely underdetermine the March decision, what the panel would be committing itself to under each option, and what it should therefore decide if coherence is the goal.

Please answer in British English.
