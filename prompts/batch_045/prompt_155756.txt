I sit on a committee that reviews the clinical risk-prediction tools used across a large hospital system, and over about four years of these reviews I have kept encountering the same shape of disagreement, for which I have no word.

Two instances. A patient is being considered for a cardiac procedure. The model everyone accepts as well-calibrated puts the chance of a serious complication at 6%. One surgeon says the patient is 78, and that among 78-year-olds with this comorbidity profile the rate is 11%. Another says the patient is a lifelong distance runner with exceptional functional capacity, and that in that subgroup the rate is 3%. Nobody disputes anyone's figures. The disagreement is about which of several true statements about different groups is the one that should govern this one patient.

Second instance. A tool estimates the probability that a discharged patient will be readmitted within thirty days. The same patient can be placed under patients discharged from this service, patients with this diagnosis, patients living alone, or patients with this diagnosis living in this postal district. Each grouping is one the data genuinely supports, the groupings cross-cut one another rather than nesting neatly, and the resulting number varies by a factor of two or more depending on which is used.

What interests me is not the multiplicity itself but a further feature I have come to expect. Which grouping a speaker reaches for tracks what the number is going to be used for. When the number will be used to justify withholding an intervention, speakers reach for the broadest available grouping and emphasize that this is what the population-level evidence supports. When the number will be used to justify overriding what a patient has said she wants, the same speakers reach for the finest-grained grouping they can construct and emphasize that this is an individual judgment rather than a statistic. The move is made in the open, is not regarded as dishonest by anyone in the room, and is not dissolved by gathering more data, because refining a grouping until it is genuinely tailor-made eventually leaves a group of one, where the frequency carries no information at all.

Is there an established concept for this configuration: the simultaneous availability of several admissible comparison groups for a single case, together with the sensitivity of the choice among them to the decision the number feeds and to the argumentative work the number is needed to do? I know there is a literature on the difficulty of getting from frequencies in a group to a probability for an individual case, and I have seen Simpson's paradox invoked loosely in this vicinity, but neither seems to capture the second feature, the shift in preferred grain with argumentative purpose. If the established notion is narrower than what I have described, I want to be told precisely where it stops and which nearby notion does the most work on the remainder; if there is no name for it, I would rather have a careful definition with boundary cases than a label. Three things I specifically want distinguished: how this differs from ordinary cherry-picking of subgroups, where the alternatives are concealed rather than displayed; how it differs from a plain reversal under partitioning, where both numbers are simply correct and there is nothing more to say; and whether there is a version of the move that is entirely legitimate — say, a case where shifting the grain of the grouping between a budgeting decision and a bedside decision is exactly what one ought to do, rather than a failure.
