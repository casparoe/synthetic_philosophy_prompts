I run a rationality meetup — about 20 regulars, mostly programmers plus a few grad students, we get a borrowed seminar room for 90 minutes on Saturdays, whiteboard but no projector, and I've got maybe 20 minutes to set up before people arrive. I want to do a session on herding, and I want people to *feel* why copying the person in front of you can be the locally correct move and still dump the whole room into a confident consensus that's wrong. I've seen the vanilla version described on blogs — private signal, public guess, repeat down the line — and I actually tried it once last year and it mostly flopped: I let people ask clarifying questions out loud, which collapsed it into a seminar, and a solid third of the room had already read about cascades so they were self-consciously either conforming or showing off by not conforming, and I couldn't tell afterwards which behaviour I'd actually produced. So: no real money, nothing that needs a laptop, and the concept shouldn't be named until after we've played.

What I'm after is something sturdier than the vanilla version, with a couple of wrinkles I haven't seen anyone do. First, I want the same surface behaviour to show up twice with different guts underneath: one round where your payoff depends only on you being right, and one where there's also something in it for you for being near the consensus — or for not being the conspicuous odd one out — so people can feel the difference between "their move was evidence" and "their move was a place to stand", and see that the two look identical from outside. I don't know how to do that without it feeling contrived, so fix it if it is. Second, I want a buy-in mechanic: each player, before committing, can spend part of a fixed token stake on either a second private signal or on having their announced move hidden from everyone downstream. My直觉 is that this makes the public record into something like a public good that individuals have no incentive to feed, but I might be wrong and it might just add noise — tell me if the wrinkle breaks the math. Third, after the reveal I want the room to bet, as a group, on how confident they *should* have been given only what was publicly visible, and then discover they can't distinguish "cascade that happened to be right" from "cascade that happened to be wrong" from the public record alone. People should walk away seeing: that everyone can act reasonably and the aggregate can still be worse informed than any single member was; that the group loses not just information but the ability to notice it lost information; and that the fix for informational copying and the fix for reputational copying are different fixes, even though the behaviour is the same. Also give the three or four people who already know the concept something to do that isn't "participant who knows" — maybe a role where they only see the public record and not the private draws.

Then the debrief: map the specific moments in the session onto the specific parts of the idea, including which formal feature of the setup each moment is actually demonstrating and which one it only superficially resembles. Be honest about what the game can't reproduce and what you have to just tell people afterwards — my guesses would be real-world herding where signals are correlated rather than independent, where payoffs are set by what everyone else does rather than fixed in advance, and where reputation is a long-run multi-dimensional object rather than a round-by-round bonus — and say which of my three wrinkles is doing real work and which is decoration. And the epistemic bit: separate the stuff that's genuinely settled (what follows from the setup itself, what the lab experiments do and don't show) from the stuff that's contested among people who actually work on this (how much real-world herding is informational versus reputational versus something else, whether the "rational" framing is doing any work or is just a label, whether any of this carries over to things like science or markets or the replication mess that got me interested). Don't smooth a live disagreement into a consensus, don't guess at papers — I can't check them and won't read them — and flag explicitly anywhere you're extrapolating from the clean setup to a messy real case. Oh, and tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, given the room I just described.
