I've got a philosophy-of-art podcast and next week's guest has a new book on why we seek out sad art, so I want questions that go past her restating the view. Her position, sketched so you don't have to guess: the sadness we feel at a tragedy is literally the same emotion as grief at a real loss — same phenomenology, same felt badness, genuinely unpleasant — except that the practical downstream is suspended, so there's no belief that anyone actually suffered, no pull to intervene or repair, no plans to revise. She calls this sheltered sorrow. On her account we're not enjoying some extra pleasure that cancels or converts the pain, and there's no catharsis story; what we enjoy is the sting itself, which we want for its own sake. She also argues that fictionality is doing no essential work, since instrumental music moves us the same way and there's nothing there to believe in or imagine.

Give me eight to ten questions, each with the answer she's most likely to give and then the follow-up that puts pressure on that answer. The places I want pressed: (1) wanting an unpleasant state — if it's really unpleasant, isn't wanting it either straightforwardly akratic or evidence that it isn't really unpleasant, and how is this different from the person who visits a grave to feel bad, which we'd normally describe as a cost borne for a benefit rather than an enjoyment? (2) keeping the emotion while dropping the action tendency — anyone with appraisal-theory sympathies will say the tendency follows from appraising something as lost, so either she's dropped the appraisal as well, in which case what makes the state sadness rather than a free-floating bad mood, or she's kept it, in which case the pull to do something should still be there. (3) the music move — if there's no object and nothing imagined, is what we feel an emotion at all rather than a mood we're in, and why, on her view, do we still vastly prefer fiction to the real thing? (4) if the practical machinery is switched off by the frame, fictionality is doing the work after all and her music argument collapses. (5) finally, what would actually count against her: what finding or argument should change her mind, and does the view predict anything anyone could go test — plus why people differ so much, both from each other and from one week to the next, if the mechanism is as general as she says.
