Writing up my Marx position for a reading group and want a consistency check before I embarrass myself. Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. Exploitation is a real, in-principle measurable relation: the capitalist appropriates the gap between what workers produce and what they're paid, and that's the descriptive core of the critique in Capital.
2. But I want to hold that without signing upto the labour theory of value as a theory of prices — surplus-value accounting should still work even if relative prices are set by production prices or supply and demand rather than by labour times.
3. Exploitation isn't a moral category. Within capitalist categories the wage contract is an exchange of equivalents, the "capitalism is unjust" reading is a mistake, and the critique isn't a complaint about unfairness.
4. The normative weight is carried by alienation instead: producers don't govern their own productive activity, and the product confronts them as a power they don't control. That's the bad-making feature.
5. Methodologically I'm an immanent-critique person: the force of the critique is that capitalism's own dynamics generate crises and throw up the agents who can overcome it, not that it gets measured against some external ideal of human flourishing.
6. Three case verdicts: (a) a rentier living off dividends who has never employed anyone is still an exploiter; (b) a well-paid unionised worker in a rich country who's a net beneficiary of global exploitation is still exploited; (c) worker co-ops competing in a market would end exploitation but not alienation, and that's a real but modest gain.

Which of these actually pull against each other? I suspect some pairs only look like they're in tension.
