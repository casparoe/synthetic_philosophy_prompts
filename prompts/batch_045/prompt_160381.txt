Ok so I've been chewing on Samuel Scheffler's Death and the Afterlife (2013) for a while and I've ended up somewhere a bit different from where he does, and I want to know how exposed my version actually is.

The view I hold, roughly: what makes human extinction bad over and above all the deaths involved isn't that humanity-as-a-biological-kind ceases, and it isn't only that each of us loses something we were counting on. It's that extinction breaks a chain. What I think there's reason to care about is that there keep existing beings who inherit what we made — read our books badly, misremember us, argue about us, build on the mess — and that we were links in that chain rather than the end of it. I'll call it the relay view, since I need a name for it. So extinction is a distinctive, non-additive bad because it's the end of transmission.

Two consequences I'm committed to, and that I take to be where I diverge from the straight Scheffler line. First, a handoff wouldn't be nearly as bad: if we deliberately dissolved ourselves into succesor minds — uploads, uplifted animals, whatever — who genuinely inherit our languages, our arguments, our grudges, then on my view that's a much smaller loss than extinction, even though no humans remain. Second, a humanity that persists for a million years with no transmission going on at all — locked in, static, nobody inheriting anything, everyone alive and content — is on my view nearly as bad as extinction proper.

I also think this gets the timing asymmetry right in a way rivals don't: I'm definitely less bothered by extinction thirty years after my own death than by extinction next year, and less bothered by either than by the same number of deaths landing on people mid-project, and I want an account that reproduces that ordering rather than one that just says everyone loses their afterlife.

So: what would count against the relay view? I want the defeaters ranked fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to distinguish what a sympathetic defender of the general afterlife-conjecture would themselves concede counts against it from what only a critic would say. Please work the cases that actually bite: the last person; sterilization with no deaths at all; a succesor species that inherits everything versus one that inherits nothing versus one that inherits a falsified, Disneyfied version; a chain that runs backwards (they study us the way we study Rome, no continuity of practice); and the possibility that the chain is doing no real work and I'm just re-describing ordinary concern for future welfare under a fancier label. Also tell me whether I've qualified this into unfalsifiability — if I get to say "well then the chain was never really there" whenever a case goes against me — and whether that's a problem for a claim of this kind. And say what the view predicts that its nearest rivals don't, since if it predicts nothing distinctive that's itself a strike.

What would move me most is either a case where the chain clearly continues and I still have to say something terrible happened, or a case where I have to admit I was counting welfare all along.

Don't give me citations or a reading list — I've read the obvious stuff, I want arguments not pointers. Be blunt about whether the view is genuinely risking anything or whether I've insulated it into mush. Final line, exactly in this form:

\answer{one of: already defeated / needs revision / survivable exposure / no real exposure; your own probability the relay view is false, as a number between 0 and 1}
