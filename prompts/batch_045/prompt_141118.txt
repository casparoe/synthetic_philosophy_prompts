THE QUESTION (both parts compulsory; 12 marks for (a), 13 for (b))

Handout recap, supplied so that candidates use the same terminology:

— P: the complete physical truth about the actual world — every microphysical fact, including the laws and the total distribution of matter.
— Q: the complete phenomenal truth.
— Materialism: no possible world agrees with the actual world in all physical facts and differs in phenomenal facts. Equivalently: the physical facts metaphysically necessitate the phenomenal facts.
— A zombie world: a world physically identical to the actual world in which nothing is conscious.
— Grades of conceivability, as distinguished in the lectures: (i) picturability; (ii) narrative coherence — the scenario can be described without overt contradiction and sustained through a story; (iii) ideal negative conceivability — no contradiction is derivable from P together with not-Q by ideal a priori reflection; (iv) ideal positive conceivability — ideal a priori reflection yields a positive modal seeming that the scenario obtains.
— The two-dimensional framework: a sentence is 1-possible if true at some scenario considered as actual (primary intension); 2-possible if true at some world considered as counterfactual (secondary intension).
— The type-A materialist holds that, on ideal reflection, P a priori entails Q: there is no epistemic gap to explain. The type-B materialist grants the epistemic gap — P and not-Q is ideally conceivable — but denies any ontological gap, and explains the gap by appeal to the distinctive structure of phenomenal concepts (the phenomenal-concept strategy). A third position, sometimes called type-C, suspends judgement on what the gap shows.

(a) Set out the conceivability argument against materialism. Be explicit about which grade of conceivability the argument requires; about why a posteriori necessities such as the identity of water with H₂O are the materialist's standing counterexample to a naive conceivability-to-possibility bridge; and about how the two-dimensional argument is meant to answer them. [12]

(b) In the seminar I introduced a variant case that I called the Permutation World: a world with exactly the same physical facts as the actual world, in which phenomenal characters are systematically permuted — every physical state that in fact realises phenomenal character C instead realises a different character C*, the permutation being one-to-one and preserving all similarity and structural relations among characters, so that the difference surfaces nowhere in behaviour, judgement, discrimination, memory or functional organisation. Assess whether the Permutation World does the same anti-materialist work as the zombie world. You should address: (i) whether a materialist who has a story about why zombies are ideally but misleadingly conceivable must be able to tell the same story about the Permutation World; and (ii) what would follow, for either side, if the Permutation World turns out not to describe a genuine difference at all. [13]

THE SCRIPT (Candidate 17)

A zombie is a being physically identical to a conscious human being but with no conscious experience at all; a zombie world is a world physically identical to ours in which nothing is conscious. The conceivability argument runs: (1) a zombie world is conceivable; (2) whatever is conceivable is possible; (3) so a zombie world is possible; (4) if a zombie world is possible, there is a possible world with all our physical facts but different phenomenal facts; (5) so materialism is false.

The argument needs grade (iv), or — on the more careful version — grade (iii) together with an extra premise I come to below. Grades (i) and (ii) are useless to it: we can perfectly well narrate the discovery that Hesperus is not Phosphorus, and nobody can picture a space of a billion dimensions. Only an idealised, a priori grade carries modal weight, and even then the materialist has an objection ready.

The objection is Kripke's. Water is H₂O, necessarily and a posteriori. Yet one can describe, without deriving any contradiction from the concepts alone, a scenario on which the watery stuff is composed of some other compound. If conceivability entailed possibility, necessary a posteriori truths would be impossible. So the naive bridge at step (2) is dead, and the question is whether something weaker does the work.

The two-dimensional framework supplies something weaker. A sentence's primary intension maps scenarios-considered-as-actual to truth values; its secondary intension maps worlds-considered-as-counterfactual to truth values, with reference fixed by actuality. *Water is not H₂O* is 1-possible but not 2-possible: once we evaluate counterfactually, *water* rigidly picks out H₂O and the sentence is false at every world. Phenomenal terms are unlike natural-kind terms: they are not deferential, their reference is not fixed by a contingent mode of presentation, and their primary intensions are not conditional. So a scenario verifying P and not-Q considered as actual should also verify it considered as counterfactually possible. The move is not free, though: it needs a premise linking the primary intension of P and not-Q to its secondary intension — in the standard version a scrutability premise, to the effect that the physical truths together with the indexical and that's-all truths pin down the actual world well enough for the diagonal argument to go through, which the materialist is under some pressure to accept and which the anti-materialist rejects. This, rather than any crude bridge, is the strongest form of the argument.

The materialist's replies divide cleanly. The type-A response — the phenomenal-concept strategy, defended by Loar and Papineau among others — concedes that P and not-Q is ideally conceivable, but argues that this is a fact about our concepts rather than about the properties: phenomenal concepts are recognitional and categorical, they refer directly rather than through a contingent mode of presentation, and nothing in their nature guarantees an a priori entailment relation to physical or functional concepts. Conceptual independence is therefore compatible with metaphysical identity of the properties, exactly as in the water case. The type-B response, associated with Dennett, is more aggressive: it denies that there is any genuine epistemic gap, holding the appearance of one to be an artefact of our incomplete grasp of our own physical concepts, so that ideal reflection would reveal the conceivability of a zombie world to be illusory. Type-C, as the handout says, merely suspends.

(b) The Permutation World does none of the extra work claimed for it. The phenomenal characters of the actual world are, on any view worth taking seriously, essentially tied to the physical states that realise them: a character is not a free-floating item that might have been attached to a different realiser, but is the state that realises it. Given that, a world with exactly the same physical states must contain exactly the same characters, and the supposed permutation is impossible — there is nothing there for the permutation to permute. So the case is either the zombie case in confusing dress, or it is available only to someone who has already helped herself to the denial of materialism, in which case it cannot serve as an argument for that denial. As to (i): yes, the type-A materialist's explanation carries over unchanged, since the phenomenal-concept strategy explains the conceivability of any recombination of characters with physical states in just the way it explains the conceivability of their absence; the two intuitions stand or fall together.

As to (ii): if the permutation preserves every similarity and structural relation among characters, then arguably there is no more to the permuted world than a redescription of this one, and the anti-materialist gets nothing from it — she must fall back on the zombie case, where the difference between presence and absence is not a mere relabelling. That does not damage the zombie argument, which was never about relabelling.

In conclusion, the conceivability argument is the strongest weapon the anti-materialist has, but its fate is decided at scrutability rather than by any intuition about zombies.
