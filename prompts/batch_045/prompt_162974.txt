I write a column on science and ideas, and I am turning about a year of reporting into a short book on multipolar traps: races to the bottom, arms races, competitive dynamics that everyone involved says they dislike and nobody seems able to stop. Before I commit to a position in print I would like someone to audit the set of things I have come to believe, because I suspect some of them are doing work the others do not permit. Here is the set, roughly in the order I arrived at it.

One. I am a structuralist about diagnosis. When I cover one of these races — ever more invasive ad targeting, latency competition in trading, admissions and test preparation, fisheries, frontier AI labs — I do not look for the villain. The explanation is the shape of the payoff structure, not the character of the participants; nobody has to be unusually greedy, short-sighted, or stupid for the outcome to arise, and I want to say explicitly in the book that no individual has to be blameworthy for the collective outcome to be bad.

Two. I think what makes these cases more than a many-player prisoner's dilemma is not that defection is tempting but that the process is cumulative and path-dependent. Every step up the ladder is, at the moment it is taken, an improvement for whoever takes it; the bad destination is reached by iterated steps nobody would have chosen as a package, and it then becomes hard or impossible to climb back down because the intermediate positions have been foreclosed. So I want a diachronic account — selection over a sequence of moves — and not just a payoff matrix with a deficient equilibrium in it.

Three. I think the only real exits are changes to the payoff structure: binding rules, monitoring, enforcement, credible penalties, and the narrowing of the option set itself. I am dismissive of unilateral restraint and I have said so in print; my line is that one actor stepping back mostly changes who wins rather than whether anyone wins. I will add, because it may matter, that every binding arrangement I admire was built by somebody going first at their own expense, and I notice I have never reconciled that with the previous sentence.

Four. I do not want any of the above to function as amnesty. I want to keep saying that the justification I hear most often from participants — that they had no real alternative, because someone else would have taken the step anyway — is a bad justification and not merely a causally ineffective one, and I want to keep naming names in reporting. I am not willing to let the structural story launder the participants, and I am aware that one and three are the two commitments most likely to be doing exactly that.

Five. My own instrument is publicity, and I think it cuts both ways. Naming a trap seems to me the main lever available to a writer, because coordination needs common knowledge and someone has to supply the shared description around which the parties can converge. But an accurate account of a race also appears to feed it: a piece that spells out how each player is reasoning about the others is close to the most useful thing I could hand them, and it can harden the very expectation of defection that keeps the trap shut. I have been calling this the Cassandra fee — any diagnosis precise enough to coordinate people against the trap is also precise enough to tell each of them that the others are about to run — and I want to keep both halves rather than resolve it with a note about being careful.

Six. I think the Moloch frame is drastically over-applied. Most of what gets described as a race to the bottom looks to me like competition doing what competition does, and the rhetoric flatters the writer by turning an ordinary tradeoff into a tragedy, while encouraging a fatalism that my own prescriptions in three depend on not spreading. At the same time, I think the frontier-AI case is different in kind, and different enough to justify binding global coordination with genuine enforcement, including constraints I would regard as illiberal anywhere else. I do not have a principled marker that separates the case I exempt from the cases I dismiss. Two people whose judgment I trust have pressed me on exactly this pairing; I have not found the pressure convincing, but I am including it because I would rather have it tested than quietly lean on it.

Seven. This is the one I have been told is flatly inconsistent and I believe is fine. Every step up the ratchet is rational for the actor taking it, and the end state is worse for every actor than the starting state was, and I do not want to call anyone irrational in order to bridge that gap. As I understand it, this is just the difference between evaluating a move locally and evaluating a trajectory globally, plus the path-dependence claim in two. A philosopher friend says I am smuggling something and I would like to know what.

What I am asking for: go through the seven and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against each other, how badly, and what would have to give in each case; tell me separately which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection and why, since I would rather not be handed seven problems when there are three. Rank the real tensions by severity instead of listing them as equal, and for each one name the cheapest revision and what it costs me — specifically, which revisions cost me a story I want to tell and which cost me only a sentence I can rewrite. Please argue against the set rather than summarise it, and do not pick the thing to be dropped on the basis of what you infer I most want to keep; I am asking which commitment the set cannot actually hold, not which one I would find least annoying to lose. Note that the book is for general readers, so flag anywhere I am relying on a distinction I would have to spend a page explaining. Finally, and this matters as much as the ranking: say what would change your mind about your own ordering, which argument or consideration would make you reorder it, and where your audit is most likely to be wrong. I would rather have the failure mode named than have the verdict sound confident.
