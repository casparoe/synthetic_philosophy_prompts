I work in machine learning — mostly on overparameterized models, and on the question of when two models that behave identically should be treated as the same model — and I've been reading about the hole argument in a reading group that is mostly philosophers of physics, where I'm the only person who doesn't know differential geometry. I'd like you to do a translation job for me, and, more importantly, to tell me where the translation breaks.

Here is the argument as I currently understand it, so you can check my grip on it before you start. Take a generally covariant spacetime theory, pick a region of spacetime that contains no matter — the hole — and fix everything, metric and matter fields alike, outside that region and on its boundary. Because the equations are generally covariant, you can apply a smooth map that is the identity outside the hole but shifts things around inside it, and the result is a second solution to the same equations: one that agrees with the first everywhere outside the hole and disagrees with it inside. So the total state outside the hole fails to fix what happens inside it. The philosophical payoff, as it's been explained to me: if you think spacetime points are things with their own identity, independent of the fields defined on them, then the two solutions describe two genuinely different ways the world could be that nonetheless agree on everything we could in principle observe, so you owe me either indeterminism or a commitment to facts no one could ever verify. If instead you hold that two solutions related by such a map are just two descriptions of one and the same physical situation, the argument has no purchase. I'm told this is the version associated with Earman and Norton in 1987; that Einstein himself hit on something close to it late in 1913 and initially took it as a reason to reject generally covariant field equations; and that there is a standard move where the substantivalist hangs on to the points but gives up the idea that a point keeps its identity across the two models.

What I want first: restate the whole thing — the setup, the step that manufactures the second solution, and the two horns — as carefully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use. Concretely: parameters versus the function computed; non-identifiability and flat directions in the loss; symmetries of a parameterization and quotienting the parameter space by them; canonicalizing or gauging a network before comparing two of them; the gap between two models computing the same function on observed data and computing the same function everywhere; and the difference between a model's predictions and the trajectory by which it arrived at them. Please don't stop at the one-line answer that the switch is only a reparametrization and the moral is that the formalism carries surplus structure — I can produce that sentence myself, and it is everything after it that I don't understand.

In ML, two parameter vectors that agree on the training distribution can diverge wildly off it, so agreement on observed data is weak evidence of sameness, and I keep wanting to read the hole as a held-out region where the two solutions come apart. But as far as I can tell that is wrong, because the two solutions are not merely unobserved-and-different inside the hole; the claim is that no possible observation could separate them, since any specification of where in the hole to look has to run via the fields themselves.
