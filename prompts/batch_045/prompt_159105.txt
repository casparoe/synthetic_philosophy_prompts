I am a PhD student in control engineering, and my thesis work is on the verification of hybrid automata: systems with finitely many discrete modes, a continuous state evolving under mode-dependent dynamics, and the usual furniture of guards, invariants, reset maps, executions and reachability questions. A large part of my working life is spent on Zeno behaviour — executions containing infinitely many discrete transitions in finite time, accumulating at what we call the Zeno time — and on the standing practice of assuming non-Zenoness as a well-posedness condition, or of treating Zeno behaviour that shows up in a model as evidence that the abstraction has broken down rather than as a prediction the model is making.

I have been reading the philosophical literature on supertasks for a reading group I run with some philosophers, and I want a translation, not a summary. Please take the following four items, which I take to be the live claims in that literature, and restate each of them as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I have just sketched.

First, the lamp: a lamp switched on and off at successive half-intervals before noon, with the argument that it can be neither on at noon (every switching-on is followed by a switching-off) nor off (every switching-off is followed by a switching-on), the conclusion drawn being that the sequence of switchings cannot be completed at all, and that this is a matter of logical rather than merely physical impossibility.

Second, the standard reply to that argument: the story fixes what happens at every instant before noon and says nothing about noon itself, so the correct verdict is not that the description is contradictory but that it is incomplete; any verdict about the state at noon is an addition to the story rather than a discovery about the lamp.

Third, the move that relocates the problem: the claim that what is really at issue is not the lamp's terminal state but the completion of an infinite sequence of tasks, together with whatever the literature says about the difference between being able to do each of infinitely many things, being able to do them in a finite time, and there being no last step.

Fourth, a different case I want put through the same mill. An infinite array of identical balls at rest, spaced so that striking the first sets off a cascade in which every ball is struck and set moving within a finite time, with the result that some ball is moving although no ball's motion has a first cause inside the array, and the momentum and energy bookkeeping fails. I want this restated too, because it looks to me like a Zeno phenomenon in a system with infinitely many components, which is a configuration my vocabulary handles very differently from the lamp's.

Two things I would like you to work with rather than around. The first is a distinction I have been using in my own chapter and want tested. I separate convergent Zeno, where the continuous state has a limit at the accumulation point so that a Filippov-style continuation or sliding-mode law is available, from what I have been calling discrete chatter, where the continuous state converges perfectly well but the discrete component oscillates without limit, because the mode space carries no topology in which that sequence could converge. The lamp looks like a pure case of the second: its only continuous state is elapsed time, which converges, whilst the switch has no such structure. Does this distinction track anything the philosophers are arguing about, or does it cut across their categories?

The second is a methodological question I actually have to answer. In my chapter I must decide whether to regularise Zeno behaviour away by supplying a continuation rule past the Zeno time, or to model post-Zeno behaviour as a genuine object of study. I would like to know whether the philosophical dispute has content that survives into my vocabulary at all, or whether, once translated, it collapses into the single engineering remark that the model is incomplete at the accumulation point and needs a regularisation.

So, alongside the translation, give me a residue report. State which of the four items mapped cleanly onto hybrid-automaton notions; which mapped only after you had to fudge something, and exactly what you had to fudge; and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. My own suspicions about the residue, which I would like you to confirm or reject rather than echo: that the modal distinction between logical and physical impossibility has no role in a framework that only ever asks whether a model admits executions; that talk of tasks performed by an agent has no counterpart in a framework with no agents; that there may be no way to express the difference between saying the story fails to pin down a fact and saying there is no fact to pin down, since both look like model incompleteness from where I sit; and that the lamp's paradox may partly be an artefact of demanding a terminal state for an execution whose natural stopping point is the Zeno time and not noon.

For each piece of residue, tell me whether you think it indicts my vocabulary — something real that hybrid-automaton modelling cannot see and should — or whether it indicts the philosophical claim, which was doing less work than its presentation suggested. I would rather have an honest account of what did not survive the translation than a confident translation, and if you think some part of the debate is genuinely undecidable between those two verdicts, say so and say what would settle it.
