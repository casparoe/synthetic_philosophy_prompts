my philosophy study group is doing Jackson's Mary case this week, and one of the grad students in it keeps killing every objection with the same move. He says the whole thing is like the difference between reading a complete orchestral score and actually hearing the orchestra play it. The score says everything there is to say about the piece — every note, every dynamic marking, every fingering — and the performance doesn't add a single note to it; it just puts you in a new relation to stuff that was already written down. So Mary doesn't learn a new fact when she walks out of the black and white room, she aquires a new ability or a new kind of acquaintance with facts she already had, and physicalism is fine.

First: can you lay out the mapping explicitly? I want to see what's playing the role of "all the physical facts," what's playing the role of the performance, what's playing the role of "what Mary gains," and where Mary's own brain state is supposed to sit on the music side — since hearing a symphony is itself a physical event, and the score sure doesn't include your auditory cortex.

Second: where does it break? My worry is that the reason the score/performance analogy feels so convincing is that real scores are incomplete — notation doesn't fix timbre, room acoustics, exact tuning, how hard the violins dig in — so "nothing is added" is doing work it wouldn't do if the description really were complete. But "all the physical facts" is total by hypothesis. So is the analogy smuggling in an incompleteness that Mary's setup rules out by definition, or is there some other feature of the music case that survives the fix?

Third, and this is the part I actually care about: someone in the group proposed a tweaked case to test it. Call her Mary 2 — same black and white room, same complete physical knowledge, but she also gets a fully stocked machine shop and unlimited time. She can build any physical system she likes, including a device whose internal states are physically just like the color-processing states of a normal human visual system, and she can hook instruments up to it and read off everything physical that goes on inside it. She just can't rewire her own brain. Does Mary 2 leave the room knowing what red looks like? I keep flip-flopping: if yes, then whatever Mary gains was reachable from the physical facts all along and the music analogy looks okay after all; if no, then the thing she gains looks irreducibly first-personal or indexical in a way that "reading the score vs. hearing it" doesn't capture at all. And I genuinely can't tell whether the analogy is helping me think about this or just giving me nicer words for my confusion.

So: audit it. Give me the correspondence, split the disanalogies into the ones that matter for the use he's putting it to and the ones that don't, say whether the physicalist conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if the score thing is a bad analogy here, hand me a better one.
