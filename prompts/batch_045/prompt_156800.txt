I teach a third-year philosophy of mind seminar, and I am rewriting the take-home final: candidates get forty-eight hours, a 1,200-word limit per question, and this one is worth twenty marks. We have just spent three weeks on consciousness and sleep, reading Norman Malcolm's Dreaming (1959) against Dennett's essay "Are Dreams Experiences?" — I don't care about the bibliographic details — with a survey chapter on REM neurophysiology alongside. The question I set last year was a straight compare-and-contrast, and the scripts were almost uniformly useless: a tidy summary of each position, and then either the announcement that the disagreement is merely verbal, or that Dennett wins because the question is empirical, or that the whole thing is a stalemate and we shall never know. This year I want a question that can't be coasted through by having understood both authors separately. I want candidates to have to bring the two positions to bear on something, and to have to say which of them, if either, would have to move.

The "something" should be a body of evidence that I invent myself and hand them in the question, so that nobody can go and look up what has been said about it. My rough sketch, which you should make precise and tighten — or improve, or partly replace — is this. A laboratory works with experienced lucid dreamers who are trained to signal with a distinctive pattern of eye movements during polysomnographically verified REM sleep. First: when an experimenter speaks a single concrete noun into the room, dreamers produce the signal within five to ten seconds on a substantial majority of trials, and their waking reports mention that object, or something closely associated with it, at rates well above chance. There is real-time responsiveness to the environment during sleep. Second: the object is never reported as arriving. Dreamers say the kettle was simply always there; the dream narrative has been reconstructed around it, and nobody ever reports hearing a voice or noticing an intrusion. Third: in a separate condition, a decoder trained on the same subjects' waking visual cortex activity is run continuously across the REM period. It recovers content matching the eventual dream report only for the final twenty to thirty seconds before the awakening; for the preceding minutes of the same episode it recovers structured content that corresponds to nothing in the report at all. Add one further finding if it sharpens the crux — a control condition in which the dreamer is woken a couple of seconds after the word is spoken but before signalling, for instance.

What I want from you is: (a) the question exactly as it should appear on the paper, self-contained, requiring no citations, with the vignette stated precisely enough that candidates can't quietly read their own assumptions into the gaps; (b) a model first-class answer of about 800 words that takes a position rather than splitting the difference; (c) a marking scheme out of twenty built around five must-hit moves, with the marks attached to each and a one-line note on the characteristic way candidates botch that particular move; and (d) two or three sentences on what separates a high 2:1 from a first, which is where my marking always gets arbitrary. The crux I keep circling, and which the question should force, is that "are dreams experiences?" may be two questions rather than one: whether there is anything it is like for the sleeper at all during REM, and whether the dream as subsequently reported is something the subject actually underwent. The vignette seems to pull these apart — real-time responsiveness looks like evidence for the first, retroactive rewriting and late decoding look like evidence against the second — and the interesting position, which neither of my two authors occupies, is that there is experience in sleep but the report is not a record of it. A good answer should also have to reckon with the fact that neither finding is one-sided: the anti-experientialist can absorb real-time responsiveness as mere subpersonal discrimination without consciousness, while the experientialist can absorb retroactive rewriting as the kind of postdictive confabulation we now accept in waking perception, and can ask what cross-state decoding is even supposed to establish. I'd also like the scheme to reward candidates who explain why Malcolm thinks the experience-view of dreaming leads to Cartesian scepticism about the waking world, and why Dennett's refusal to let first-person authority settle the matter isn't just a refusal to be a verificationist. But I don't want the scheme to reward name-dropping anything beyond the set texts: anything else a candidate brings in — the currently fashionable suggestion that dreaming is better modelled on imagining than on perceiving, say — should be marked as an optional extra, not required. Please write the whole thing in British English, and don't give me a bibliography or suggestions for further reading.
