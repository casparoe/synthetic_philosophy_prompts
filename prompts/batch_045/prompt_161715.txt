You are a professional philosopher of science specialising in economic methodology. Please think the problem through carefully: sketch the candidate translations and where each one starts to break before you commit to a final answer, and write the whole thing in British English. Argue from the concepts themselves rather than from the literature — I have no library access to check references, so please do not cite, attribute or recommend any reading.

My background: I spent forty years as an experimental physical chemist and have only recently begun reading philosophy. The vocabulary I actually trust is the vocabulary of measurement and applied statistics: a data-generating process; a parametric model fitted to noisy data; identifiability; nuisance parameters; systematic versus random error; calibration; in-sample fit against out-of-sample prediction, cross-validation and pre-registration of test data; the bias–variance trade-off; model choice by information criteria; robustness and sensitivity checks; and the standing remark that every model is false but some are useful.

The task: restate, in precisely that vocabulary and as faithfully as you can, the methodological dispute around Friedman's as-if defence of unrealistic assumptions — the claim that the truth of a model's assumptions is irrelevant and only predictive success matters, together with the move critics call the F-twist, in which irrelevance quietly becomes a virtue and highly unrealistic assumptions are presented as the mark of a powerful theory. Do the same for the main objections to it. Take Friedman's own illustration of the expert billiard player who is said to behave as if he were solving the equations of classical mechanics for his shots, and give me its translation alongside the general claim.

A distinction of my own that I would like you to test rather than politely accept: an assumption can be false but idle — the model would do at least as well with a truer one, and the error it introduces cancels, or washes out asymptotically, or is confined to parameters nobody estimates — or it can be false and load-bearing, in the way that a deliberately biased estimator or a regularising penalty is, where the model performs well partly because the assumption is wrong in a particular direction. My suspicion is that the F-twist needs the load-bearing case to be what is at issue, while most defenders of unrealistic assumptions are actually trading on the idle case, and that the debate keeps sliding between the two. Does that distinction survive translation into the statistical vocabulary, or is it a distinction my vocabulary simply cannot draw?

Finally, the residue report. Tell me which claims in the dispute translate cleanly into my terms, which translate only with distortion (and say what gets bent in the process), and which have no counterpart at all — I suspect, for instance, that nothing in my vocabulary corresponds to asking whether an assumption is true or realistic, as opposed to asking whether it costs me anything. For each piece of residue, give me your view on whether it marks a real gap in the vocabulary of applied statistics or a sign that the methodological dispute is less substantive than it looks. Where you think my distinction is confused rather than original, say so bluntly.
