You are a professional philosopher who works at the intersection of epistemology and philosophy of language, with a specialty in the speech act of assertion. Take your time: begin by brainstorming the logical space of possible answers, and only then build the tree.

I am preparing a handout for an upper-level seminar on the norms of assertion, and I want a decision tree — not a prose survey — that leads a student from their verdicts on concrete cases to a named position. Six to eight branch nodes, and no more than nine leaves.

Two design constraints. First, every node must be phrased as a case or a claim a student can answer without knowing which position it leads to: nothing like "do you think knowledge is the norm?" Second, the case list has to include at least these four, which in my experience do the real diagnostic work: (a) the lottery ticket — you have overwhelming statistical evidence that the ticket lost but you have not checked the result; (b) the stationmaster — you assert a schedule to the guard who has just confirmed it and who knows more than you do; (c) the undercover officer — you assert something true to an audience you know will take it to confirm a falsehood, and staying in character requires you not to hedge; (d) the relapse — you assert that a patient is recovering, on impeccable evidence, it is in fact true, and you later learn the evidence was misleading; are you obliged to take it back?

The axes I want the tree to be organized by are: whether the normative status of an assertion can vary with the audience (audience-invariant versus audience-relative norms, including the sub-case of telling someone what they already know); whether the same norm governs retraction as governs assertion; and whether the norm is fundamentally a permission, a duty, or a prohibition — that is, whether assertion is ever required of anyone.

Leaves should include a knowledge norm, a truth norm, a belief or justification norm, a responsibilist or warranted-assertability view, a contextualist or subject-sensitive variant, and the skeptical view that there is no constitutive norm of assertion and only prudence and etiquette remain. Give the standard label where one exists and write "unnamed" where it does not. Where your tree cannot separate two leaves, flag that explicitly instead of inventing a branch to force the split, and for each unoccupied region of the logical space give one sentence on why it is empty — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Close each leaf with a single sentence on its most awkward commitment. I want verdicts and structure only: no bibliography, no citations, and no appeal to what some author said.
