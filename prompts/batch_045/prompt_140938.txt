I am putting together a one-page, self-scoring handout — about ten minutes to complete — for a reading group of intelligent non-specialists: people who argue about practical ethics online, have strong reactions to concrete cases, and have no vocabulary for what they are doing. The topic is what a person ought to do when their moral views themselves are uncertain. I want the handout to sort readers by what they say about cases rather than by which label they recognise, so my main constraint is that no question may name a position, an author, or a first-order moral theory: nobody is allowed to be asked whether they are a consequentialist.

What I want from you is the classification instrument itself, built in this order.

First, the logical space rather than the list of names. Give me the three or four load-bearing questions whose combinations of answers generate the cells, and say which of them does the most work. I have views about which distinctions must carry the tree: (i) whether the reader's uncertainty is moral at all or is instead uncertainty about some non-moral matter of fact with moral consequences — I suspect many readers will be mis-sorted if this is not a branch; (ii) whether what is uncertain is which option is best versus which options are permitted or forbidden, i.e. whether the currency being aggregated is value or deontic status; (iii) whether the response aggregates across the views one holds at all, and if it does, whether "how much better" can be compared across views and how the comparison gets scaled; (iv) whether the reader is choosing for herself or for a group whose members disagree; (v) whether the rule arrived at is one the reader expects to be self-effacing, and whether uncertainty about the rule itself is settled by another application of the rule.

Second, locate the actual positions in that space as the leaves — ten to twelve of them, each with one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Among the leaves I need at least: a view that aggregates expected performance across the moral views one holds; a view that says to act on whatever one actually believes and not to hedge; a pessimistic or risk-averse view that gives lexical or near-lexical weight to avoiding the worst outcome; a compromise or proportional-satisfaction view that splits or mixes rather than maximises; a view aimed at not acting wrongly or not being blameworthy rather than at producing the best outcome; and a leaf for the reader who thinks the entire problem is spurious, whether because there is nothing here to be uncertain about or because hedging is itself a vice. Name each leaf only if you are confident it has a standard name in the literature; write "unnamed" otherwise. Do not invent a name and do not attach one to an author or a work.

Third, the diagnostic material. Every internal node must be a question a reader could answer without knowing where it leads, and wherever a distinction can be carried by a verdict on a case it must be: phrase it as "what do you do here?", not as a principle. Where no case can carry it, phrase it in plain language that avoids "expected value", "choiceworthiness", "intertheoretic comparison", and the rest of the jargon — operationalise instead. The tree has to contain verdicts on at least these four cases: a tiny-credence case, where a view you barely believe makes some ordinary action astronomically bad; a scaling case, where two equally credible views disagree in opposite directions and the question is whether either difference can be said to be larger; a splitting case, where two equally credible views each strongly favour a different use of the same money and a mixed option is available; and a deontic case, where a view you assign only modest credence says an option is forbidden while every view you take seriously says it is better. I would also like, if they can be made to carry a branch: a case whose uncertainty concerns whether some kind of being has moral status at all, a case where you allocate a shared budget across people who disagree, and a case where you can now cheaply preserve an option in case you later come to hold a view you currently reject.

Then four shorter things. Identify at least two combinations the space allows that nobody occupies, and say for each whether the reason is that the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, and say whether any further case could separate them. Tell me which leaves require a pattern across several verdicts rather than a single path, and how I should score those, given that the rest of the instrument is path-based. And tell me where the handout will mis-sort someone — the reader who answers "it depends" at a scaling node, or who produces the aggregative verdict for non-aggregative reasons — with a recommendation for each such node about whether to branch again, to add a "you are not sorted by this tree" leaf, or to drop the distinction at this level and say so.
