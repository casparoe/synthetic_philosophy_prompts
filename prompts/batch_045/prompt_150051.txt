I host a monthly discussion evening at my local library — a dozen adults, no philosophy training, forty-five minutes on the floor — and next month they want me to lead the question I got stuck on myself: whether a person could have come from a different sperm and egg. I've thought about it enough to know there are two rough positions (that I am stuck with the gametes I actually came from, versus that a slightly different sperm would still have produced me), but I can't teach an ordering I haven't got straight myself, so I'd like a plan for the session rather than the explanation.

What I need:

1. What has to be in place before the question even bites. My instinct is that the group has to first see that the issue is not whether I *would have been different*, but whether *I* would have existed at all versus someone very like me existing in my place — and that they need some grip on 'could have been otherwise' meaning something other than 'is unlikely' or 'nobody planned it'. Tell me what else, and in what order.

2. The one case that should carry the session. I've been turning over three: (a) an IVF clinic where two sperm were available that day and only the technician's choice differed, and the resulting genetic difference is a few hundred base pairs; (b) the frozen embryo, a genetic sibling of mine, that was created at the same time and never implanted — was that me, or a sibling who never existed; (c) the zygote that splits a few days later than it in fact did, so instead of me there would have been two of me. Pick one, and say what the case has to have and has to lack in order to do the work — how small the genetic difference should be and why, whether the timing or mechanism should be stated explicitly or left vague, whether anyone should be given a name — and why each of the other two is a worse carrier rather than a better one.

3. The misconceptions, and where in the sequence to defuse each. I'm predicting: 'different DNA, so obviously a different person'; 'same parents, same upbringing, so still you'; a slide into 'identity must then be a matter of degree'; and the thought that this all dissolves if you just mean something practical by 'same person'. Give me the order they'll actually arise in and the point at which each has to be dealt with so it doesn't harden.

4. One closing question that separates people who have understood the structure from people who have only been told an answer. My candidate is to give them a case I never used — two embryos from different parents fusing into one body in the first week — and ask them what the view I built implies about it. Say whether that works and what I should be listening for.

5. What I should openly decline to settle at this level, and say so out loud. I'm thinking the implications for abortion, anything turning on a soul or on when a person begins, and whether the same reasoning applies to artefacts.

Please keep the whole plan under 650 words, and give it as a numbered running order with rough minute marks rather than continuous prose.
