I am writing a position statement on the substantivalism/relationism dispute for a graduate seminar in the metaphysics of physics, and before I defend it in front of people who will enjoy taking it apart, I would like an audit of whether the package actually hangs together. Here is where I have landed, stated as flatly as I can.

1. Newton's rotating bucket argument -- and, more cleanly, his case of two globes joined by a cord -- establishes something real. In a universe whose entire material content is a pair of globes connected by a taut cord, there is a determinate fact as to whether the system rotates, and that fact cannot be read off any relation of the globes to other material bodies, because there are none.

2. What this shows, though, is only that dynamics needs absolute structure: inertial, or affine, structure that is not itself a pattern of relations among bodies. Newton overreached when he inferred from this a substance of space over and above that structure, equipped with its own absolute positions and states of rest. So I take the case to refute the relational reduction of motion without vindicating Newton's own metaphysics.

3. I accept the moral usually drawn from the hole argument: models related by a diffeomorphism represent one and the same physical possibility, and there are no haecceitistic differences between possibilities that differ only over which bare point plays which role.

4. Accordingly I count myself a sophisticated substantivalist. Spacetime is a real concrete particular and not an abstraction from relations among bodies; but its points have no primitive identity across models and no identity independent of the structure and matter that occupy them.

5. I also hold that the dispute between substantivalism and relationism is a genuinely substantive metaphysical disagreement rather than a verbal one, and I take two things to be evidence on the substantivalist side: the globes case, and the explanatory role of geometry, since on the substantivalist picture the fact that a body traverses a geodesic is explained by the curvature of something that exists.

6. Methodologically, however, I feel the pull of the Leibnizian and Machian suspicion of absolute structure: other things being equal we should prefer theories that do not posit background structure that no possible observation could reveal, and I find attractive the Machian demand that inertial structure not be a brute unexplained given. My sense is that general relativity honours that demand only partially -- there is something Machian in the way matter drags inertial frames around -- while still letting inertial structure float free of the matter distribution through boundary conditions and vacuum solutions.

7. Finally, a verdict on a hypothetical. Suppose someone produced a Machian twin of general relativity: a theory that agrees with it on every observation anyone inside a universe like ours could make, but which predicts that the cord in the two-globe universe goes slack. My instinct is that this twin is a genuinely different theory, that we could never decide between the two from in here, and -- the part I am least sure of -- that in that situation there would be nothing to be right about, so that choosing between them would be a pragmatic matter rather than a factual one.

What I want is the following. Which of these commitments genuinely pull against each other, and which only look as though they do? Rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad. For each, say what would have to be given up, what that would cost me, and what the cheapest available repair is. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and whether the set commits me to anything further that I have not noticed and might not want to accept. Where you think a tension is merely apparent, say exactly what dissolves it. I am deliberately not indicating which item I would be willing to surrender.
