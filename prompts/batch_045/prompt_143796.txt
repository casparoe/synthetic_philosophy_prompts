I teach a graduate seminar on the epistemic foundations of game theory — common knowledge, common belief in rationality, and the work that higher-order beliefs are supposed to do in selecting among equilibria. I want to test that framework against an actual episode rather than another puzzle, so: analyse the failure of Silicon Valley Bank in March 2023 through it, and then tell me where the framework stops being informative.

I will stipulate the facts. SVB had invested a large share of its swollen deposit base in long-dated Treasuries and mortgage securities, whose market value fell as the Fed raised rates; unrealized losses on the held-to-maturity portfolio exceeded fifteen billion dollars. On 8 March the bank announced that it had sold over twenty-one billion dollars of securities at a loss, borrowed fifteen billion, and would raise 2.25 billion in equity. Prominent venture investors publicly advised their portfolio companies to move their money. Customers withdrew funds totalling forty-two billion dollars by the following day; the bank was seized on 10 March. Roughly eighty-nine percent of its deposit liabilities were uninsured. Two days later the authorities announced that all depositors, insured or not, would have full access to their funds, and the immediate panic subsided — while First Republic, with a comparably uninsured deposit base, was closed on 1 May despite thirty billion dollars in deposits placed with it by eleven banks in March.

What I want is a disciplined mapping, not a narrative: who the players are, what the option set and payoff structure actually looked like for a depositor on the morning of 9 March, what has to be true of first- and higher-order beliefs for mass withdrawal to be an equilibrium rather than merely individually rational, and how the public guarantee should be modelled — as a change in the payoffs, or as an intervention on beliefs, and if the latter, what the theory predicts about the difference between its working at SVB and not working at First Republic.

Then the return direction, which interests me more. First: was this a coordination failure at all? SVB's deposits paid close to nothing while short-term government paper yielded around five percent, so withdrawal may have been a dominant strategy irrespective of what anyone else did. If so the model is imposing a self-fulfilling structure on what was really a common adverse shock. Say what would distinguish the two readings, and whether the framework can distinguish them from the outside; if it cannot, is that a defect in the theory or a limitation on its use? Second: the depositor base was unusually small, densely networked, and able to watch one another's reasoning almost in real time. Does that transparency move the situation toward or away from the theory's idealised conditions, and — since transparency plainly does not by itself say which equilibrium gets coordinated on — whether near-common knowledge behaves like common knowledge here or departs from it disproportionately. Finally, the normative question: if staying and running are both equilibrium outcomes under common belief in rationality, can the framework say that anything went wrong at SVB, or does it have to import something from outside itself? Where it has nothing to say, say so rather than forcing it to speak.
