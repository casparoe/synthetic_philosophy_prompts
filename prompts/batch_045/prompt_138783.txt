Was reading about split-brain patients on a long flight and I think I've made it worse by bringing it up at work. Context on where I'm coming from: I'm a backend engineer, no philosphy past whatever was required freshman year, and the week before the flight our cluster had a network partition — two nodes couldn't see each other, both kept accepting writes, we had to do a merge afterward — so of course the second I read that they cut the corpus callosum I said out loud, oh, it's a partition. The version I've been describing to my team goes like this: the hemispheres are two nodes, the callosum is the network link, the surgery is a clean partition. Show something in the left visual field and the right hemisphere gets the write but can't pass it to the left hemisphere, which is the only one wired up for speech anyway, so it's like the node that serves the API answering with something plausible instead of returning "I don't know." (There's a case I half-remember where someone was shown a chicken foot to one side and a snowy field to the other, picked a chicken with one hand and a shovel with the other, and then explained the pair by saying the shovel is for cleaning out the chicken coop — that confident nonsense explanation is the part that got under my skin.) And there is no merge step afterward; the left side just never finds out what the right side did. There's even the one where a guy was pulling his pants up with one hand and down with the other.

My friend — also an engineer, also not a philosopher — says this settles it: the hemispheres must always have been two separate subjects of experience and the callosum was only ever a sync channel, so "one person, one mind" was always a story the talkative node was telling itself, and if it's true for split-brain patients it's probably true for the rest of us too, we just happen to have the cable plugged in. I don't buy that but I can't say where it breaks, mostly because I can't tell the difference between the analogy being wrong and the analogy being fine but him reading conclusions off it that it doesn't carry.

So: audit it properly. I want the correspondence laid out explicitly — what plays the node, the link, the partition, the consensus protocol, the client, the failed merge — and then the disanalogies, sorted into the ones that actually matter for the conclusions he wants and the ones that are just quirks of the experimental setup. Some things I keep circling on: our nodes were separate machines, built separately, each with its own clock and its own failure modes, and nothing that happens to one of them is *like* anything for it, whereas the hemispheres are maybe more like two threads in one process that share nearly all their memory — a teammate suggested that swap and I think it's doing a lot of quiet work, so say what changes if we call them threads instead of nodes. Also: the partition isn't really total (there's still subcortical traffic, and the eyes move together, and these people mostly get through their day fine), the "two" is a choice among many possible cuts rather than a fact about there being exactly two things in there, and a node in a cluster isn't an agent with goals in the way a person is. Then tell me what survives: does the two-people conclusion hold for the split-brain case itself, does it hold for ordinary brains, does anything here support "the self is a confabulation," and is there a better analogy than the distributed-systems one, or is my whole framing a dead end?
