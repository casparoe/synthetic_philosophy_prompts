I teach a large introductory philosophy course each fall — roughly four hundred students — and I keep the department's shared bank of thought experiments: about sixty cases that my colleagues and our graduate instructors draw on for lectures, discussion sections, and exams. Two or three times a week during term I have to make a call about one of them: whether to put it in front of first-year students who have no theory at all, whether to present it as raising a question or as settling one, and whether it is safe to grade on. The same question comes up in a different register a few times a year when I referee, where what I have to judge is whether a manuscript's case is carrying a conclusion it has not earned.

My own practice has been a small set of habits I picked up long ago and never systematized: restate the case in flat prose and see whether the verdict survives, look for the word "surely" doing the work of an argument, ask what the case is implicitly being contrasted with. These habits do not compose into anything I can hand to a graduate instructor, and they tell me nothing about the cases where my students divide evenly, or where the division shifts depending on the order in which I present two versions.

Please turn whatever is defensible in the notion of an intuition pump — the term as Dennett uses it, together with the standing worry that a case can persuade by its framing, its imagery, or the order of its questions rather than by its content — into a procedure my teaching team can actually run on a candidate case, in twenty or thirty minutes, with no training in the methodology literature. I want the questions in the order they should be asked; what would count as a sufficient answer to each; the point at which the procedure stops with a no; and the point at which it returns the decision to a person because the question is one of values, of emphasis, or of what we want the students to notice, rather than one the case can settle. Please also say plainly what such a procedure cannot do. If my three habits do not survive the translation, discard them, but tell me what is wrong with them.

Three features of the procedure matter to me. First, the cases in the bank are not all doing the same job. Some are meant to elicit a verdict that we then treat as evidence about how a concept works — the way we treat a judgment about whether someone knows, or acted freely, or intended a result. Others are meant only to make a conceptual possibility vivid: to show that a certain combination is not excluded by the concepts alone, without asking anyone to endorse a verdict about it. A third kind does neither and is pure scaffolding — it gets students to see that a distinction is available before we argue about where to draw it. I want the procedure to sort a case by the job it is meant to do before it tests it, and to apply different tests accordingly: the same case can be a poor verdict pump and an excellent possibility pump, and conflating the two is the error I most want to avoid. Second, the procedure has to tell me which cases are safe to put on an exam, where a grader needs a determinate answer, as opposed to which belong only in discussion. Third, it has to have something to say when the verdict is unstable across a room and across order of presentation.

Please run the finished procedure on the three cases below.

One. I have opened the unit on free will with this case for fifteen years. A device, implanted without your knowledge, monitors your deliberation and adjusts one parameter — your threshold for acting on a consideration. Whenever your deliberation would have issued in a selfish decision, the device raises the threshold until you decide generously instead; whenever your deliberation would already have issued in generosity, it does nothing at all. Over the following months you act generously again and again; the generous acts flow from your own reasons in the ordinary way; you take pride in them, and you become known among your colleagues as a generous person. Are those acts free, and are you praiseworthy for them? This is the case I would be most reluctant to give up, and that is exactly why I want the procedure applied to it without sympathy.

Two. A system produces fluent, responsive answers in a natural language over decades of interaction. Internally it consists of a million clerks, each applying a single rule for rewriting one symbol into another, each with no idea what any of it means, connected so that the output of one is the input of the next. Does the system understand what it says? I have always presented this as a possibility pump, not a verdict pump, and I want to know whether that classification is honest.

Three. The vice-president of a company is told that a new program will, as a side effect, help the environment. He does not care about the environment at all; he approves the program purely for profit, and the environment is helped. In the paired version the side effect is harm rather than help, and he is equally indifferent to it. Did he bring about the side effect intentionally? My students divide on this, and the division moves with which version I present first. Tell me whether the procedure treats this as a usable case, what it says about the instability, and where, if anywhere, it hands the decision back to me.
