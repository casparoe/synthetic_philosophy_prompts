I work in the appraisal unit of a national transport authority. Every year we allocate a fixed safety budget across candidate projects — junction redesigns, median barriers, bridge retrofits, level-crossing upgrades — by monetising each project's expected reduction in fatality risk using a single official figure, expressed in currency, for what is called the value of a statistical life. That figure comes from studies of how much extra pay workers in hazardous occupations have to be offered to accept additional risk of death on the job. We convert each project's expected lives saved into money at that rate, rank the projects by net monetised benefit, and fund them in that order until the budget is spent.

Three features of this procedure strike me as pulling in different directions, and I can't tell whether they are in tension or whether I am missing the view that reconciles them.

First, we use one number for everyone, even though the underlying studies report quite different trade-offs depending on whose behaviour you look at and in what occupation, and even though the individual circumstances of the people protected by any given project vary enormously. When I asked why we discard that variation, I was told that a single uniform number is what keeps the procedure non-judgmental: we are deferring to the trade-offs people actually make rather than substituting our own views about whose life matters more. But discarding the variation looks to me like the opposite of deference.

Second, we refuse to count what people say. The authority also runs consultations in which residents are asked how much they would pay for safer junctions near them, and those answers are excluded from the ranking on the ground that hypothetical questions are not real choices. Yet the wage evidence we do rely on is also, in the end, a money-for-risk trade-off, inferred rather than requested; and the people whose risk we are reducing were never asked anything at all.

Third, the procedure is justified entirely by deferring to each individual's own trade-offs, and then simply adds the resulting sums across different individuals — including, in the nature of the case, people who will never enjoy the benefit attributed to them.

A consultant has now proposed that we stop averaging: infer each person's own valuation of risk from their actual behaviour — occupational choice, seatbelt use, smoking, and similar — and weight safety spending accordingly, so that those who live as though they set less store by their own lives receive less protection.

What I want from you is a reconstruction of the implicit theory. What view of utility, or of what it is we are putting a number on, would make this procedure coherent? Which parts of it strictly require that view, and which are merely habits that would survive under rival views? Which rival views does the procedure quietly rule out? Is it internally consistent — in particular, in treating market behaviour as evidence while treating stated willingness to pay as none? Does the consultant's proposal follow from the same view, or does it rest on a misunderstanding of what a choice can reveal? And if we replaced the presupposed view with one on which what matters is how well lives actually go, what exactly would have to change in how projects are ranked, and would the resulting rankings differ much in practice?
