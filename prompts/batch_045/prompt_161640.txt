I've been reading about the insect protein industry — the mealworm and black soldier fly farms that are supposed to replace soy and fishmeal — and I can't work out what the standard way of doing things there actually assumes. As far as I can tell, a commercial insect operation kills the entire batch at once, by freezing, blanching, or just grinding it, and no welfare law anywhere requires a humane kill for insects, or even asks whether one is possible. At the same time, the UK recognised animal sentience in law a couple of years ago and its scope runs to all vertebrates plus some invertebrates like octopuses and lobsters, and the same societies that give insects nothing will spend a fortune rescuing a single stranded whale. So: vertebrates yes, cephalopods and decapods yes, insects nothing at all.

A friend who works on animal advocacy made an argument I can't shake. Suppose, she says, your credence that insects feel pain is 1%, or even 0.1%. Farmed insect numbers are already counted in the trillions per year, so on almost any credence the expected amount of suffering in that industry swamps everything happening in vertebrate farming by orders of magnitude, and the cheap interventions — change the kill method, fund the research that would settle the question — ought to sit at the top of the welfare agenda rather than at the very bottom, where in fact they sit. Everyone I've mentioned this to treats it as obviously absurd, but nobody can tell me what's wrong with it or what they think instead.

So what does the current practice presuppose? Does it strictly require the claim that insects aren't sentient, or only something weaker: that obligations track relationships we've created, or that a tiny probability multiplied by an astronomically large population just isn't how you decide anything? Is it inconsistent for a legal order that extended protection to lobsters on the strength of a scientific review to give insects nothing, or is that line defensible? And if you did take the expected-value version seriously, what would the practice have to become — I don't mean "spend more on insects", I mean what the rule would literally say, because something like 'regulate any taxon where the number of individuals handled per year times your credence in their sentience crosses a fixed threshold' can't be right as stated, can it? It feels like it collapses into a Pascal's-mugging style worry once the numbers get big enough. I'd also like you to separate what the practice strictly needs to assume from what it merely fits well with, and to say if it's just underdetermined — several quite different theories all licensing the same nothing.
