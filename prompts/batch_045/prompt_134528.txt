I've been reading the rationalist blogs since the Sequences were going up, and I've been chewing on the Dennett/Caruso thing for years — the book version, the recorded talks, the old SSC comments sections, my local meetup — and it has this frustrating property where both sides concede basically everything and still don't converge. As I read them: Caruso says there's no basic desert, so retributivism is out, and we should shift to something like a public health/quarantine model where sanctions get justified purely forward-looking (incapacitation, deterrence, rehabilitation) and the reactive attitudes get retrained. Dennett says fine, no spooky desert, and he doesn't want retributivism either — but he wants to keep the practice and the vocabulary of holding responsible, reformed rather than abolished, because the practice does real work (norm elaboration, scaffolding for self-control, the thing that lets a community coordinate on saying no), and he thinks Caruso's cleanup costs more than Caruso thinks. They agree on the metaphysics-ish stuff, they agree dangerous people have to be off the streets, they agree manipulation cases are where the crux is, and then they just stop.

My hypothesis is that the free will part is a decoy and the actual fight is about what a moral vocabulary is *for*, and I've been using a test on people to try to isolate it — call it the Swap Test: imagine two jurisdictions with identical sentences and identical outcomes, one justifying every sanction in desert language and the other in pure quarantine/deterrence language, and ask whether anything of value is lost in the second. The Dennett-ish answer seems to be yes: the vocabulary is load-bearing, it's how a community tracks norms, and it's cheaper to run than spelling out the forward-looking reasons every single time. The Caruso-ish answer seems to be no, and that if you keep saying it anyway you're running a noble lie on people who cant talk back. I've also been toying with a version of this I haven't seen either of them use: desert-talk as lossy compression, like prices, which summarize a pile of supply-and-demand facts without being an extra value in the world — so the question becomes whether the compression loses something load-bearing or just makes the system cheaper to operate. But I definately don't trust it: I land on Dennett's side and I can't tell whether that's an argument or just attachment to my existing moral vocabulary, and I can't shake the feeling that whether desert-talk does work is itself an empirical question nobody can run the experiment on.

I want a diagnosis, not a survey and not a verdict: what kind of disagreement is this, actually — verbal, substantive, two different questions under one heading, different standards for what a practice has to be to be worth keeping, or bedrock intuitions about fairness and disgust that neither side can argue for? And is there a hidden question doing all the real work: an empirical one about whether desert-talk changes behavior, a conceptual one about what it would be for a practice to be earned, or something about whether were ever allowed to keep a useful falsehood? Assume I've read both sides' best arguments and don't need them restated.

Give me exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, no intro and no conclusion: one on what kind of disagreement this is, one on where the Swap Test framing goes wrong (or why it's the right lens after all), one on whether there's a hidden question doing the work and what it is, one on what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a result, an experiment, a change of question — or on why no progress should be expected, and one on which of my own claims I'm most likely to have to eat.
