I chair the prioritisation panel of a regional transport authority, and I am writing after an argument with a friend last night that we could not finish and that I have decided to settle by changing how my panel works.

Twice a year we must return a single strict ranking of the forty to sixty schemes in our capital programme — a city-wide cycle network, a junction redesign, a bridge strengthening, a rural bus subsidy, a school-routes safety scheme — together with a one-line published reason for every place on the list. We work by pairwise comparison, because nobody can hold forty multi-attribute schemes in mind at once, and we are under a duty to give reasons a member of the public can follow. Every round ends with a list. Every round, I now suspect, ends with a list that conceals something.

My friend's position was that 'better than' is transitive as a matter of logic, so any cycle in our pairwise judgments is merely a record of carelessness, and the remedy is care. Mine was that a fully careful panel could still endorse a cycle. I had in mind the standard spectrum structure: scheme A is better than B because it delivers a benefit spread over many people, offset by a loss to a few that is individually so small it is not worth mentioning; B is better than C on the same pattern; and so along a chain; and then the final scheme is plainly better than A when the two are set side by side, because the individually negligible losses have accumulated into something that plainly is worth mentioning. She said this was the sorites in a cheap suit. I said a points system would not settle the matter but only assume it settled, since any additive scoring rule builds transitivity in by construction and so cannot even represent the possibility in dispute. There we stopped.

What I want from you is the method note I now have to write, not a survey of the debate — I have read enough summaries, and my friend can supply the references if I ever want them. Please write me a procedure my panel can actually run.

Requirements:

1. Ordered steps, in language an engineer or planner with no philosophy training can follow inside our two-day panel. For each step give the question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to it, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a named person on the grounds that the question is one of values rather than of fact.
2. It must distinguish a cycle produced by aggregating several dimensions that are each separately transitive — an artefact of weighting, which reweighting can cure — from a cycle produced inside a single dimension, which no reweighting can cure. I regard that distinction as the whole crux and I want an operational test for it, not a definition.
3. It must force any 'not worth mentioning' threshold into the open rather than letting it do its work tacitly, and it must say what we publish when the panel is shown its own cycle and declines to revise any step in it. My friend would have us fake a strict ranking; I would rather publish an honestly partial one, but I need to know what that looks like on a page that has to list forty schemes.
4. It must include a test I thought of only after she left. Schemes can be submitted in phases, and we face the same programme every six months. If our pairwise judgments cycle, a promoter who knows the cycle can slice a proposal so that we approve phase one against the status quo, then phase two against phase one, and arrive somewhere we would have rejected outright at the outset. That is the practical shape of the old exploitation argument, and I want the procedure to test for it rather than assume it away.
5. Then run the procedure on three cases: (i) a three-scheme cycle involving a city-wide cycle network, a junction redesign and a rural bus subsidy; (ii) an eight-scheme chain of the spectrum kind described above; (iii) a cycle caused by one panel member shifting which dimensions count between two comparisons. Case (iii) should be the one that returns an answer I will not like: the authority has already announced that the flagship scheme will be funded, and I expect your procedure to tell me we may not publish a strict ranking that places it first.
6. Finally, say plainly what the procedure cannot do — which questions it leaves open, and at which step it offers an appearance of rigour it does not possess.

Constraints: the method note itself should be no more than about 1,200 words; it must be runnable within the two days; it has to be signed off by our Chief Executive, who will ask what it commits us to; and it should contain no citations or reading suggestions.

After the note, and with nothing following it, give a single JSON object with exactly these fields:

- steps: an array of objects, each with the keys step_id (values S1, S2, S3 and so on), question, sufficient_answer, on_failure (what the panel does when the answer is not sufficient), and type, whose value must be either factual or value.
- judgment_points: an array of objects, each with step_id, what_the_procedure_cannot_decide, and sign_off (the role that must decide it).
- applications: an array of objects, each with case_id, cycle_kind (one of aggregation_induced, value_induced, dimension_shift, none), stopped_at_step, published_output, and disliked_by_asker (true or false).
- phasing_exploit: an object with the keys test, triggers_on, and remedy.
- failure_modes: an array of short strings, each naming one way the procedure will misfire in use.
- out_of_scope: an array of short strings, each naming a question the procedure expressly declines to answer.
