I'm taking a graduate seminar on the philosophy of perception, and I've been stuck on something since last week's session. We were going over the color phi phenomenon — the Kolers and von Grünau setup in which a red disc is flashed on the left, a short blank interval follows, and then a green disc is flashed on the right, with subjects reporting a single disc gliding across the gap that turns green somewhere around two-thirds of the way along its path, i.e. a colour change they seem to undergo before the green disc has actually been presented. The discussion then turned to the two stories about what must be going on inside the subject, the ones Dennett labelled Orwellian and Stalinesque. On the Orwellian story the subject first has an ordinary, unrevised experience of the red disc, and only after the second flash does some later process go back and rewrite the record, so that it now seems to the subject, from the inside, as though the motion and the colour change had already happened: history is falsified after the fact. On the Stalinesque story nothing is presented to consciousness until after the second flash at all; the composed, backdated content is what gets written in the first place, so there was never an earlier experience for a later process to falsify: the record is doctored in advance. Someone in the room asked which of these is actually going on in the subject during that interval, and whether that is even the same question as the question about when the subject's experience of the colour change occurs — before the second flash, after it, or smeared across the interval somehow.

Our TA shut the question down with an old pragmatist-sounding motto: a difference that makes no difference is no difference. Since, he said, there is nothing the subject could notice, say, or do that would tell the two stories apart, there is no fact of the matter about which kind of revision took place, and the question about the timing of experience is dissolved rather than answered. That felt too quick to me, for two reasons. First, a classmate pointed out that the hypotheses may diverge in their counterfactual consequences even if they don't differ in how things seem in the actual case: suppose you could intervene during the interval — a TMS pulse over early visual cortex, or a backward mask, timed somewhere around 50 to 80 ms after the second flash and calibrated to disrupt whatever process is still consolidating the percept. If the revision is Orwellian, there is an earlier, unrevised experience whose trace you might rescue or leave standing, so interference should sometimes yield a report of a red flash, a gap, a green flash, and no motion. If it is Stalinesque, there is no earlier content to rescue, so the same interference should only degrade or abolish the phi report altogether. Even granting that no such experiment could be conclusive, the two stories seem to differ in what they predict, which is a difference of some kind. Second, a friend suggested a third option the TA's dichotomy ignores: maybe there is no fact here not because there are two candidates we can't separate, but because experiences aren't the sort of thing with a determinate temporal location at the millisecond grain, so that asking whether the change was experienced before or after the second flash rests on a false presupposition. That strikes me as a very different claim from the TA's, even though both get summarised as no fact of the matter.

So what I want from you is a disambiguation rather than a verdict on the original question. Please lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses the motto a difference that makes no difference is no difference could be being used to express in this setting — for instance a purely epistemic claim about what we're warranted in believing, a semantic or verificationist claim about truth conditions, a Leibniz-style claim that indiscernible processes are identical, a claim specifically about consciousness (that all the facts about experience are introspectively or behaviourally available, so there can be no hidden fact of experience), a deflationary claim that experience-talk here is vague or up for stipulation, a methodological claim about which questions are worth pursuing, a presupposition-failure claim of my friend's kind, and anything else you think belongs on the list. For each reading, tell me what exactly it would have to mean by difference — difference to whom, detectable when, in the actual case or under possible intervention, in experience or anywhere in the causal story; whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or something false or circular in this context; and whether it would really support the TA's conclusion that the timing question dissolves, or only the weaker conclusion that we can't tell. Then say which reading you think someone in his position is almost certainly relying on, and whether that reading survives my classmate's TMS-style intervention argument — or whether the most defensible version of the motto turns out to be considerably less interesting than it sounds.
