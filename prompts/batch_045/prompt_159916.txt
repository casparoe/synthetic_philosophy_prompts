I teach an introductory course on personal identity, and I am pairing Christopher Nolan's The Prestige (2006) with Parfit's teletransportation case and what it is meant to show: the reductionist claim that personal identity is not what matters in survival, the proposal that what matters is psychological continuity and connectedness however caused, and the suggestion that once this is seen clearly the fear of death and the weight of self-interest should loosen their grip. I want honest criticism rather than a philosopher's toy example with a film attached to it, so please hold both sides fixed: the plot as it actually is, and Parfit's argument as it actually runs.

Three departures from the standard case are where I think the work has to be done.

First, Angier's machine does not destroy the original in scanning; it produces a duplicate at a distance and leaves the original where he stood, so each performance involves a brief branching in which there are two equally well-qualified candidates, one of whom then drowns beneath the stage. How much should that overlap matter on Parfit's own terms? And does the film's stipulation that both have uninterrupted memories, and that each takes himself to be the real Angier, settle anything, or does it stipulate the answer?

Second, before the machine exists Angier performs the trick with a lookalike, Root, and bitterly resents remaining beneath the stage while Root takes the applause. Nothing branches there; continuity of the act, and of something psychologically continuous with him, is preserved, and what galls him is that he is not the one who continues. Is that a datum against reductionism — evidence that we care about being the person who survives rather than merely about someone psychologically continuous with us surviving — or is it, on the reductionist's own account, competitiveness dressed as metaphysics?

Third, Borden turns out to be two identical twins alternately living one life, which is the inverse of fission: one social person and one apparent stream of experience realised now by this organism, now by that one, maintained at the cost of self-mutilation and of the people around them. Does the film present this as a picture of what a person really is, and so as vindicating the psychological view, or as a reductio of it — a case where the stream is shared while the persons are not? Say what you make of the fact that the arrangement only works by being hidden.

Then the verdict on the film as a whole. Its horror — rows of water tanks, a hundred deliberate self-killings, Angier's dying uncertainty about who he is — reads as a rebuttal of the claimed practical upshot, since death here is as bad as anywhere. But how much of that horror is detachable: an artefact of deliberate death, of first-personal uncertainty about which candidate one will turn out to be, of a genre that needs corpses, rather than of the metaphysics? And where does the film resist the reading altogether — where is it a melodrama about rivalry and recognition rather than about identity, and where does it depend on confusing numerical identity with what matters?

Finally, mark where your reading is most likely to fail, and say what would change your mind about whether the film supplies a genuine counterexample to reductionism or only dramatizes an intuition that reductionism already explains away.
