Two people on my team — Marta and Dev — have been going back and forth for over a week about what fairness criterion to use on a fraud-screening model, and I'm on the same team but this isn't my call and I genuinely cant tell who's winning. Setup: about 2,000 insurance claims come in a day, reviewers can only look at 40 of them, so the model produces a risk score and we take the top 40 — there's no threshold, its a ranking with a fixed budget. Two claimant groups we report on separately.

(1) Marta opens with what she calls pull-rate parity: we should pull roughly the same *share* of each group's claims. Her argument is that being pulled is itself an imposition (payout held, documents requested, sometimes weeks of delay), so the burden should be shared in proportion to group size rather than concentrated. Her second argument is epistemic: we only have confirmed-fraud labels for claims we already investigated, and everything we didn't investigate is recorded in the database as no-fraud. So any criterion computed from labels is computed on a sample the model itself helped pick, and pull-rate parity is the only one of these metrics we can actually verify.

(2) Dev says parity just relocates the disparity. Our recorded fraud rates differ a lot by group (~2% vs ~8%), so if we pull the same share of each group, the hit rate in the pulled set differs by roughly the same factor — he ran it and got something like an 11% hit rate in one group and 46% in the other, same top-40 cut. Reviewers see the group on the file. So a flag is much stronger evidence for a claim from the high-rate group, and reviewers will reasonably require less to deny those claims. The disparity you took out of the model reappears in the humans' heads. He wants equal hit rate (precision at k) instead, so that a flag means the same thing regardless of group.

(3) Marta writes an identity on the board: catch rate = (hit rate × pull rate) ÷ base rate, where catch rate is the share of that group's actual fraud that we catch. So, she says: (a) if base rates differ you cannot have equal hit rates *and* equal pull rates *and* equal catch rates — at least one has to break, so Dev hasn't escaped a tradeoff, he's picked which one to break. (b) If you insist on equal hit rate and equal catch rate, the pull rates have to be proportional to the base rates, which on our numbers means pulling several times the share of the high-rate group — more burden on the group that's already scrutinized more, not less. (c) When she actually tried to hit equal hit rates at 40 slots she couldn't: the only routes there were pulling something like two-thirds of the high-rate group's claims, or deliberately adding noise to the score. So equal hit rate isn't on the menu — it's a criterion you can only buy by making the ranking worse.

(4) Dev concedes (a) and (b) but says her verifiability argument proves too much. Pull-rate parity is checkable exactly because it's close to contentless: pulling 40 claims at random satisfies it perfectly while catching nothing, so its a constraint on how many people we bother, not a fairness criterion. He also says the 2%/8% gap isn't purely an artifact of what we chose to investigate — there's a real component (different fraud types, different documentation requirements) — so we can't wave away every label-based metric as circular, even if he grants the recorded base rates are contaminated. And, he adds, her parity only delivers equal catch rates on the assumption the score is equally good in both groups, which we have no way to check and which he doubts, since we have far less training data for the smaller group. If the score is worse there, parity gives you unequal catch rates too and then her criterion keeps only one of the three.

(5) Marta's fix: run a random audit slice — investigate a sample of claims regardless of what the score says, so we get unbiased base rates and can also test whether the score is even equally good in each group — and then pick a criterion with real numbers instead of fighting about identities.

(6) Dev's answer: the audit slice is itself the imposition we're arguing about, so we need a fair allocation of audits in order to estimate the base rates we need in order to decide what a fair allocation is. And if we allocate audits proportionally, we'll get tight estimates for the big group and very noisy ones for the smaller one, so whichever criterion we pick gets enforced with wildly different confidence across groups — which looks like its own kind of disparity.

They stopped there and I have no idea who's right. And say what, if anything, both of them are missing — something upstream of the choice of metric that neither has touched.
