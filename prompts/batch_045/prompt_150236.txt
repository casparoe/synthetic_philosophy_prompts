I'm a couple of years into a PhD on decision theory, writing the chapter where I have to say what the "unit of decision" is for an agent who doesn't take flat expectations of utility — the risk-weighted view, where you run the cumulative probabilities through a transformation r before combining them with the utilities (Buchak's version). And I keep bumping into something I don't have a word for, and I've been staring at it for to long now.

Here it is in two versions. Take r(p) = p², utility = dollars, and a 50/50 gamble paying either $0 or $100, offered at a price of $30. Weight on the bad outcome is 1 − r(0.5) = 0.75, weight on the good one is 0.25, so the risk-weighted value is $25 and you decline. Now take two independent copies of exactly that gamble, sold together as a single package for $60: outcomes are $0, $100, $200 with probabilities .25, .5, .25; weights are 1 − r(.75) = .4375, r(.75) − r(.25) = .5, and r(.25) = .0625; weighted value $62.50. That's more than twice $25 — so you accept the pair. But accepting the pair and accepting each one separately produce the very same distribution over final wealth, so nothing about the options changed; only how they were bundled. And it goes the other way too: with r(p) = √p a single gamble is worth about $70.71 and the pair is worth about $136.60, which is *less* than twice the single value, so at $70 each you'd happily take each gamble on its own and refuse both together. The direction of the effect flips with the curvature of r, which makes me suspect I might be looking at two different things wearing one description.

I don't think the usual labels cover it. "Narrow bracketing" and the choice-bracketing literature are about people who deviate from a benchmark that's assumed to be correct, and the prospect-theory stuff about segregating gains and integrating losses is doing its work through reference points and loss aversion, neither of which my agent has — she has no reference point, just a probability transformation. It isn't a sure-thing-principle failure either, since that shows up inside a single decision. And it isn't a money pump, as far as I can tell: if she just resolves to always aggregate as widely as possible and re-plan at every node, nobody can pump her. But "aggregate as widely as possible" is doing all the work, and I can't say what fixes the width, or why a life, or a decade, or an afternoon is the right unit.

So: does this packaging-sensitivity have an established name, and if so, is it one thing or two (does the dependence on the curvature of r split it)? How exactly does it differ from bracketing-effects as they're understood in the descriptive literature, and is there a principled answer about the unit of decision, or is that an unfixed parameter of the view? If there's no name, I need to coin one for a paper chapter, so I'd want a definition plus boundary cases — including the awkward one, which is that if the honest answer is "always bracket maximally widely," then the Allais-ish pairwise intuitions that motivated the risk-weighted view in the first place were elicited one pair at a time, and I'm not sure the view survives being told to stop doing that.
