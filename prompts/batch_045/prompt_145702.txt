I have been reading about secession and national self-determination on my own, with no academic background in politics or philosophy, and I have found that my reactions to particular cases are far more confident than anything I can say in general. I would like to know whether there is a principle behind them, or whether some of them simply have to go. I have listed real cases I have followed in the news alongside invented ones I made up to test myself, and in each case I have given the verdict my gut delivers.

1. Scotland 2014. The vote was authorised in advance by London, held on a clear question, and lost about 55 to 45 on a turnout of nearly 85 per cent. My verdict: this was the right way to do it, and the result was rightly accepted. I do not think Scotland was being oppressed, and I do not think that matters.

2. Quebec 1995. Lost by about 50.6 to 49.4 on a very high turnout, on a question that bundled sovereignty together with an offer of a new partnership with Canada. My verdict: a margin of that size should not dissolve a country, and the question was not clear enough for the vote to settle anything. I find myself agreeing with the later Canadian position that both the question and the majority have to be clear, and with the Supreme Court's view that a unilateral secession would not be lawful.

3. Catalonia 2017. The referendum was ruled unconstitutional and suspended, held anyway, and won over 90 per cent of votes cast on a turnout of about 43 per cent, with a declaration of independence following. My verdicts here do not fit together: I think the police violence used to stop the vote was wrong; I do not think the declaration that followed was legitimate; and yet I cannot accept that the matter is closed by a constitution's declaration that the nation is indivisible. The Catalans are not an oppressed people, and still it seems wrong to me that a durable majority in Catalonia may never be able to obtain a lawful vote. This is the case I keep changing my mind about.

4. A wealthy province that contributes much more in taxes than it receives, with no distinct language or history of its own, votes 60 per cent to secede, and the campaign argues openly about money. My verdict: not a good enough reason. But if I change the case so that the province has its own language and 70 per cent support, I find it very hard to say no, even though everyone knows the money is also part of the motive. The motive test seems to collapse under pressure, and I do not know what to do about that.

5. A border region whose population shares the language and ancestry of the neighbouring state votes 62 per cent to leave and be absorbed by that neighbour. My verdict: this strikes me as much worse than ordinary secession, more like annexation with extra steps, even though I have no reason to doubt the votes were genuine. If what matters is what the people want, why should the destination matter?

6. Within that same border region, a coastal district votes 70 per cent against joining the neighbour and would become a small minority in the new state. My verdict: the district should get its own say, either through a border adjustment or through the right to remain. But I can see that this threatens to recurse down to towns and neighbourhoods, and I cannot say where it should stop.

7. A linguistic minority spread thinly across a whole country, a majority nowhere, asking not for a state but for control over schools, language and land use. My verdict: this is the strongest case on my list, and it makes me suspect that self-determination is not really about statehood at all, which sits badly with how strongly I react to case 1.

8. A region whose departure would leave the remainder with a debt it cannot service, or would cut off its water supply or break a defence treaty. My verdict: the remainder is entitled to a say, and you cannot simply walk away from joint obligations. What worries me is that this hands a rich remainder a veto over a poor region's departure, and that in the reverse case it gets used to tell people they are trapped.

9. A small island that was a separate country until it was absorbed into a federation sixty years ago, with 60 per cent support for independence, economically viable, no oppression, and a federal constitution that forbids secession. My verdict: it should be allowed to leave. So I reject any rule on which mistreatment is the only thing that generates a right.

10. A region votes 55 per cent to secede on an explicit platform of stripping the minority language of official status. My verdict: genuinely unsure. I recoil from it, but making the liberal character of the prospective state a condition seems to let a parent state veto any secession by alleging that the successors are bigots.

I have gathered that writers distinguish a right to secede as a remedy for mistreatment from some sort of standing right of a people to decide its own political future, but I may be mangling that, so please put it in your own terms if it is relevant.

Is there a single principle, or a small set of candidates, that captures these verdicts or nearly all of them? Please state it precisely, say which of my cases motivates each of its clauses, and tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and would have me give up. I would also like it applied to two cases I have not listed: a unilateral declaration of independence made after a parent state has repeatedly refused to permit any vote at all, and a secession that a majority of the whole country supports as well as a majority of the region. Finally, tell me which of my reactions you think is not a considered judgement but a prejudice dressed up as one, and why.
