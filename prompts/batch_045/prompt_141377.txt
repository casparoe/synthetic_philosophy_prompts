Me and a friend have been stuck on the same arguement for about six years: would there have been something recognizably like Nazi Germany without Hitler? He's on the structural side — the Depression, Versailles, the stab-in-the-back story, a conservative elite that wanted a strongman and thought it could ditch him later, the army, fear of the left. I'm on the other side, and not in some vague great-man way: I mean his specific package of tactical patience with legal forms, appetite for enormous risks, and the personal loyalty he drew from people who'd have quit on anyone else. We both know the standard moves — I've steelmanned his side back to him and taboo'd "Nazi Germany" with him and neither one ended it — so write us the adversarial-collaboration document instead: the joint statement we could both sign.

Build it on one distinction I think we keep sliding past. There are three different counterfactual moves you can make on a person: deletion (he disappears in 1930, no agreed successor), runner-up substitution (swap in the most plausible actual alternative for that slot — a right-wing authoritarian without his gifts or his obsessions), and re-roll (redraw the occupant at random from the distribution of plausible occupants of that slot). Pair that with the grain of the outcome variable: "a one-party fascist regime that starts a war of annihilation in the east and attempts the murder of European Jewry" versus "some authoritarian nationalist German regime or other". I suspect a lot of our fight is that we're each answering a different cell of that grid.

Deliverable: a numbered section of claims we'd both sign (including about what the counterfactual even is, what would count as answering it, and which structural facts are held fixed), then a disagreements table where each row is a claim phrased so its own side would accept the wording, then the cruxes — each with a case or argument both of us accept as bearing on it, and both our predictions recorded in advance. Add a node-by-node pass over the obvious hinge moments (the appointment, the enabling act, the rearmament gamble, the invasion of the USSR) where each of us says how much of any divergence is the man and how much the structure. Flag any part of the dispute that's really about words, and say which words. Keep it tight, no footnotes.

Finish with a last line beginning Verdict: containing the single crux you'd bet the most on, ten words or fewer.
