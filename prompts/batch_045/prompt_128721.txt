hi — I'm a journalist, I mostly cover science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about how population ethics ended up inside the longtermist/effective-altruism policy world. Before I repeat any of this in print I need someone to tell me how much of my one source is actually right.

The source is a draft white paper from a small foresight think tank that was forwarded to me by a PR person (I'd rather not name them). I've rewritten it slightly so it isn't searchable, but the substance is theirs. The background section says:

"Population axiology asks which of two outcomes is better when the outcomes contain different people. Total utilitarianism answers: add up everyone's lifetime welfare and prefer the bigger total. In the 1980s Derek Parfit showed that this commits you to the Repugnant Conclusion — that for any population of ten billion people all living wonderful lives, there is some vastly larger population of people with lives barely worth living that would be better. So total utilitarianism says we ought to be trying to fill the universe with trillions of near-miserable people. Since nobody accepts that, the total view is dead. The natural alternative is average utilitarianism: maximise welfare per person. But that says it's a bad thing to add a happy person who happens to be slightly below average, and it implies the best possible world contains exactly one ecstatic person. So researchers in this area have mostly moved to 'critical-level' views, which subtract a fixed neutral level of welfare — the level at which a life counts as worth living — from each life before adding them up; these avoid the Repugnant Conclusion and don't have the other problems. Really, the Repugnant Conclusion is just the Mere Addition Paradox restated, and both come down to nothing more than our discomfort with very large numbers, which psychologists have shown we're terrible at."

Then they pitch their own fix, which is the part I actually care about. Call it rank-weighted totalism: you keep total utilitarianism, but before summing you sort everyone from best-off to worst-off and weight the person at rank r by 1/r — the best-off life counts fully, the second counts half, the third a third, and so on. They claim this "smoothly dampens the value of headcount," stays "close to ordinary total utilitarianism," and "blocks the Repugnant Conclusion, because however many barely-worth-living people you add, the later ones barely count." They add in a footnote that it doesn't matter how you break ties between people at the same welfare level.

So: go through the background section and tell me what a population ethicist would accept, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong — and whether "total utilitarianism is dead" follows from the parts that are right. My instinct is that the history is basically fine and the 1/r gimmick is where the bodies are buried, but the whole thing reads so smoothly to me that I don't trust my own reaction. And on their fix specifically: does it actually avoid the Repugnant Conclusion or just push it out to numbers no reader can picture, and what happens to everyone's weights when you add one person who's better off than all of them?
