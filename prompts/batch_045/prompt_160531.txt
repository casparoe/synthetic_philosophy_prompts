In my phil of maths seminar two people would not stop arguing after class and I stupidly offered to adjudicate. Background: we'd just done Benacerraf's dilemma — the face-value semantics for mathematical sentences makes them about abstract objects, but our best story about knowledge wants some causal or explanatory link between knower and known, and abstract objects can't supply one.

Malik's opening (1): take the plenitudinous / "full-blooded" way out and stop demanding a unique intended model. If every consistent mathematical theory is true of some part of the mathematical realm, then being right isn't a matter of matching one privileged structure, so there's no access problem left — all we have to do is not land on an inconsistent theory.

Tess (2): that just relocates it. "Our theory is consistent" is itself a mathematical claim of exactly the sort we were supposed to be giving an account of, so you've swapped truth for consistency and still owe an epistemology.

Malik (3): fine — abductively. Mathematics gets applied; a contradiction would make a theory trivial and useless; the bits of maths science leans hardest on have been leaned on for centuries without blowing up; so the success of applications is evidence that our methods track consistency.

Tess (4): that reaches applied analysis and linear algebra and maybe nothing else — not large-cardinal set theory, not the huge bulk of pure maths nobody applies — and anyway the abductive argument uses mathematical reasoning to certify mathematical reasoning.

Malik (5), which he called "local reliability": we don't need reliability across all possible theories, only across nearby ones. For any claim p we believe, look at the theories that agree with ours except possibly about p and ask whether our methods pick a true one. On plenitude every consistent alternative there is true somewhere, so local reliability comes almost free.

Tess (6): then it's worthless — a coin flip about Goldbach would come out locally reliable too, so the notion can't tell proof from guessing.

Malik (7): last move — don't count the plenitude equally, weight it. Only require reliability across the "natural" region: structures that are rich and extendable rather than degenerate one-offs, and say our methods track that region.

Tess (8): that weighting is either a fancy way of restating which theories we happen to like, in which case reliability is self-certifying, or a real mathematical fact about the space of structures, in which case we're back to needing an epistemology for it.

Can you go through it move by move, 1 to 8, say who's right about what at each step, and what (if anything) both of them are missing about the dilemma itself? I care less about who "wins" than about which specific claims survive — but say if you think one of them is more right overall. Also, are (5) and (7) really seperate moves, or is (7) just (5) with extra machinery bolted on?
