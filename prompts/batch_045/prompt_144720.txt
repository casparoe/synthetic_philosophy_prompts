You are a professional moral philosopher who has also served as an adviser to mass-claims compensation schemes. Think carefully and slowly about the task below, and before you write anything, spend some time in a private scratch analysis: list the features of the case, try out at least two incompatible ways of stating the objection, and discard the ones that give you no traction. Do not show me the scratch work; show me only what survives it.

Six weeks ago I was appointed to the distribution committee of a compensation fund set up after the fertilizer-plant explosion in the town where I have lived for twenty years. Thirty-one people died, several hundred were injured, and the fund will cover perhaps a quarter of the provable losses. I am not a philosopher; I am a retired hospital administrator who now has to vote on a set of rules that will tell grieving neighbors what their father or their daughter was worth. We keep being told to look at the September 11th Victim Compensation Fund as the obvious precedent, and I have been reading about it, but every discussion I find either treats it as a triumph of pragmatic administration or condemns it for pricing lives by income. I want something more exacting than either.

So: analyze the 9/11 Victim Compensation Fund through the lens of the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism. I know the canonical sources (Rawls on the distinctness of persons, Nozick on the moral balancing act, Taurek on whether the numbers count) and I do not need them summarized. What I need is the mapping, and it has to run in both directions.

Treat the following features of the fund as stipulations even where you would quibble, because I want the analysis, not a fact-check. Congress created the fund within weeks of the attacks as part of a larger airline-stabilization statute, and capped it in the neighborhood of seven billion dollars including administrative costs. Participation was voluntary but conditional: a claimant accepted an award and gave up the right to sue the airlines. A single Special Master wrote the regulations, ran the process, and personally heard a large share of the appeals, which were deliberately non-adversarial and informal. Awards were built primarily out of projected lifetime earnings plus a separately capped component for non-economic loss, adjusted for dependents, with offsets for collateral sources such as life insurance, pensions, and charitable donations. The Special Master worked with an explicit rule of thumb about narrowing the gap between the largest and smallest awards, on the rough idea that the bulk of the money should not flow to a small share of the highest-earning claimants. And the set of claimants was itself contested and later expanded: rescue and recovery workers with slowly developing illnesses, downtown workers and residents with respiratory claims, businesses as well as individuals, and eventually, years later, a reopened fund for cancer and other latent disease.

Here is the part I most want you to work on. The objection was built for cases of sacrifice — one person made to bear a cost for the good of others — and a compensation fund distributes a fixed pie after the harm is done. So it is not obvious that the objection applies at all, and if it does, it is not obvious what it forbids. I have been toying with several formulations and I cannot tell which is the real one:

- The no-substitution reading: one person's loss cannot be made good by anyone else's gain, so each award must track the loss of the person it is paid to, and never the size of the total.
- The reasons-that-are-yours reading: the constraint is on justification, not outcome; the ground for my award must not be a fact about your award or about the aggregate, even if the resulting distribution is identical.
- The no-mere-channels reading: the metaphysical complaint that persons are not conduits through which welfare flows, so any rule that treats the claimant population as a single loss-bearing subject has misdescribed what exists.

Apply each of these to the specific features above and tell me where they diverge rather than agreeing: the earnings-based formula (which tracks each person's own loss but prices persons differently), the narrowing-the-gap rule of thumb (which is explicitly aggregative in its justification), the offsets, the non-adversarial appeal, the waiver of suit, and the exclusion or inclusion of hard-to-classify claimants. Then tell me which reading you take to be the actual content of the objection, and why the others are either weaker or not the same claim.

Three complications I want you to take seriously rather than wave at. First, the identity problem: in wrongful-death compensation the person whose loss it most directly was cannot be paid or acknowledge anything, so whose separateness is supposed to be respected — the decedent's, the survivors', or the estate's as a legal person? Second, the constituency problem: the boundaries of the claimant group are partly drawn by the allocation rule itself, so does the objection constrain who counts as a separate claimant, or only how a fixed group divides things? Third, the counterfactual-claimant problem: because the total was capped while the population was not, each award was a function of how many other people showed up, which no one chose. Is that the kind of aggregation the objection targets, or is it an exogenous scarcity that the objection simply does not reach?

Also tell me honestly where the theory goes silent. Does it say anything about what to do when the fund is far too small? About whether an individualized adversarial process is more respectful of separateness than a uniform administrative formula, or less? About whether a public fund's expressive function — the state addressing a crowd rather than a person — is something the objection itself rules out, or a separate dignity-based consideration I would be smuggling in? And give me one observable symptom by which an outside observer could tell that a fund had violated the objection, if there is one.

Finish with five design recommendations for my committee's rules, each tagged with the reading it comes from and marked as required, merely permitted, or not addressed by the objection — plus two decisions that you think the objection leaves us to make on other grounds, named plainly. Write in American English, do not cite or recommend anything to read, and keep the whole thing under about fifteen hundred words.
