I write historical fiction, and my latest novel is narrated by the scribe of a captured city whose records were burnt in its fall. At a panel last month a fellow novelist, asked whether a writer in my position owes anything to what actually happened, answered that history is written by the victors, and treated that as the end of the matter: if the surviving record is only ever the winner's story, there is nothing left to be faithful to.

I would like the line taken apart. My own rough taxonomy distinguishes five things it might mean — that the victors produce and preserve most of the surviving documents; that they decide which accounts get copied, taught and translated; that the very categories in which events become intelligible (liberation, treason, restoration) are theirs; that the evidence is now too thin and too one-sided for method to recover much; and the strong claim that there is no fact of the matter about the past apart from whichever account wins. Please set out these readings and any I have missed, ordered from platitude to radical, with a verdict on each — trivial, defensible, or false — and, where defensible, say at what scale of event and over what span of time it holds.

Two follow-ups matter more to me than the taxonomy. First: only one of those readings could do the work my colleague wanted of it, releasing me from any obligation to the past; say plainly which that is, whether it survives scrutiny, and which reading she was most likely reaching for. Second: in my draft the scribe forges a tablet that makes the conquerors' own campaign chronicle look fabricated, and early readers have taken his success as a claim that the whole chronicle was a lie. Does any reading of the slogan license that inference, or does the line, properly understood, cut against the use I have made of it? I would rather be shown where my own story repeats the thing the slogan complains of than be told the slogan is simply crude.
