i teach a final-year epistemology seminar and I'm writing the take-home final. I need one question on higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia, and I want one that students can't answer by reciting the conciliationism-vs-steadfastness debate or by name-dropping the Equal Weight View — half the class has read the standard peer-disagreement material and will just reproduce it, so please don't frame the question as a peer disagreement case at all.

What I actually want to test is the deeper crux: whether there is any coherent rational response to higher-order defeat at all. I want them forced to say what the level-connection demand comes to (the thought that your higher-order assessment of your own reliability should govern your first-order attitude), and whether it can be satisfied jointly with ordinary first-order evidentialism, or whether it collapses into either akrasia or some self-effacing, fragmented rationality where the advice you should follow isn't advice you can follow.

The case I want built in: an agent gets higher-order evidence that her reasoning on some question is unreliable, and then a third-order piece of evidence that people who recieve warnings like that systematically over-correct and become too deferential to the warning — so her distrust may itself be an artifact. Sharpen that into a precise, self-contained case for the exam, and use it to force the regress question: where, if anywhere, does the rational response stop, and does anything coherent remain?

Deliverables: the question itself, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit points with marks for each, plus a one-line note on how students typically get each point wrong. The A/B line should turn on specific moves the answer has to make (distinguishing wide- from narrow-scope requirements, whether the third-order evidence is self-consuming, what a requirement can demand of an agent who can't follow it), not on polish or on who they've read.
