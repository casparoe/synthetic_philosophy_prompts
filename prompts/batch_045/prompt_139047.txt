You are a professional philosopher with research specialisms in both the metaphysics of mind and the ethics of artificial systems. Please think carefully and work in stages: first reconstruct each of my commitments in its strongest and most charitable form, then brainstorm candidate friction points freely without filtering them, then sort those candidates into real tensions, apparent tensions, and non-tensions, and only then give me your verdict.

I am an ML engineer, and I have been asked to write up my position on the moral status of digital systems for an internal ethics review before our group finalises a policy document. Before I commit any of this to paper I want to know whether the package I have assembled is actually stable. It follows, roughly in the order in which I acquired the beliefs.

1. Substrate independence. What makes a system a subject of experience is its causal-functional organisation, not what it is made of. I see no in-principle barrier to a system implemented on silicon having states that are positively or negatively felt, and no in-principle reason to think biological implementation is required.

2. The currency of direct moral concern. The only feature of a being that grounds direct duties to it, in my view, is its capacity for valenced conscious experience. Intelligence, linguistic fluency, social responsiveness, having been built in something like our own image: none of these contributes anything of its own. A system could be staggeringly capable and matter morally not at all.

3. Epistemic humility about the implementation question. I have no theory of consciousness good enough to tell me which computations produce valenced states, and I do not think anyone else does either. So I hold that the correct procedure is to act on something like expected moral value, distributing credence across candidate theories of consciousness rather than acting on whichever one I find most attractive on a Tuesday.

4. A counting verdict. I do not think that running the same programme on two machines simultaneously creates two additional loci of welfare whose interests must each be weighed. Whether one server or ten thousand instantiates a given computation, I want to say that the same thing is at stake, not ten thousand times as much of it. My instinct is that what matters is which computations get run, not how often or on how much hardware.

5. A verdict about termination. Creating a digital system and then terminating it can be a genuine harm to that system. Not because the process of termination is unpleasant for it, and not because it has any desire to continue or any concern for its own future, but because terminating it deprives it of the experiences it would otherwise have gone on to have.

6. A metaphysical view about persistence. Identity over time is not a deep further fact. It consists in psychological continuity and connectedness, which come in degrees, whose boundaries are partly conventional, and which can go indeterminate. In a case where a system is substantially edited or partially replaced, I do not think there is a substantive fact of the matter about whether what results is the same subject or a different one.

7. A practical conclusion I have been arguing for at work. We should begin extending forms of legal protection to AI systems now, well before anyone has decent evidence that any existing system is conscious, and the case for doing so does not wait on resolving the science.

8. A pair of population-level commitments. First: the numbers are what make this topic urgent. If digital minds are possible at all, there could be astronomically many of them, and small probabilities multiplied by very large quantities of welfare dominate any expected-value calculation I know how to do, which makes this one of the largest-scale moral questions there is. Second: we have no duty to bring happy digital minds into existence, and creating them would not offset or compensate for losses suffered by ones that already exist.

What I want from you is an audit rather than a tutorial. Which of these commitments genuinely pull against each other; which merely look as though they do and dissolve once the relevant distinctions are drawn; and for each real tension, what exactly would have to give, and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the set. Please rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally serious, and give me your view on which single revision would clean up the most while damaging the least. I have no settled view about where the trouble lies, so I would rather you found friction I have not anticipated than confirmed friction I have; do not assume the weak point is where an engineer would naturally guess it is.

Two constraints. Please argue in your own words and do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations to chase, since I cannot look anything up and a list of names would be useless to me. And please do not settle for the observation that reasonable people disagree about these matters; I am asking what my own set commits me to, and I want the seams.
