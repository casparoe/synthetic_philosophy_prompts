I teach a twelve-person upper-level seminar on philosophy of language — one ninety-minute session a week, a whiteboard, index cards, and a room without a projector. We have already had one session on Austin and the students can recite the vocabulary, which is exactly the problem: they can recite it while still thinking nothing is at stake. I spent last night until about one in the morning arguing about this at a friend's kitchen table — she is a court interpreter, no formal philosophy, and extremely stubborn — and her position, which I am now convinced is the position several of my students are quietly holding, went like this: that "I promise" differs from "I will" only in that the former is conventionally used to produce a certain state of mind in a hearer, so that whatever is special about it is just its reliable perlocutionary profile; that an infelicitous utterance is simply one where that effect fails, which she was perfectly happy to call a kind of falsity; and that uptake is nothing over and above being understood. I think all three claims are false and I want the seminar to feel that they are false, but I have found that the standard illustrations — christening, pronouncing a couple married, "I do" — let students nod along and reproduce the terms without ever having to adjudicate anything, because in those cases every condition is satisfied at once and nothing has to be decided.

So what I want from you is a runnable simulation, not a handout of cases. Constitute a miniature institution that the class then has to conduct its business inside — I was imagining a tenants' association annual meeting with a chair, a secretary who keeps the minutes, a treasurer, and ordinary members, but propose whatever makes the mechanics cleanest — with these features. First, there must be a single public minute book that is the authoritative record of institutional reality: who holds which office, whether the meeting is in session, what is owed to whom, whether an apology is on record, whether a resolution was carried. During play, every dispute gets settled by appeal to the minute book, and the only way anything in the book changes is that somebody says or writes something. Give me the utterance cards with exact prescribed wording, the agenda of business to be conducted, the roles, the materials, and a realistic minute-by-minute timeline. Second, and this is the constraint aimed straight at my friend: for the duration of the game nobody may use the words performative, illocutionary, felicity, or uptake, and nobody — including me — may justify a ruling by appealing to anybody's psychology: not the speaker's sincerity, not whether anyone was reassured, persuaded, annoyed, or satisfied, not whether anyone "felt" apologized to. If that constraint leaves some disputes unresolvable, I want to know which ones and why, because that is data.

Third, the rules have to force the group into three engineered trouble cases before anyone names a distinction, and they should hit them by trying to get the association's business done, not by being handed a puzzle. (a) A split addressee: the association owes an apology to a three-person committee, the formula is addressed to all three jointly, and one of the three refuses to accept it while the other two do. Has the apology been performed, and if so, for whom — and what does "for whom" even mean here? (b) An inscription-conditional act: for at least one kind of act — adjournment, or the incurring of an association debt — the standing rules say the words count only once the secretary has entered them in the minute book, so that an authorized, well-timed, properly witnessed utterance that never gets written down leaves the world exactly as it was, while a secretary who writes down something rather different from what was said changes it anyway. The group should have to argue about whether the voice, the speaker's office, or the record is doing the work. (c) Ventriloquized authority: the correct formula is spoken at the correct moment by the wrong person reading a card the right person wrote, and separately by the right person speaking words handed to them by someone with no standing at all. Where does the act live? If you can also fit in a fourth case where the formula is uttered correctly by someone with full standing, in jest, during a rehearsal, or under a threat — something hollow that nonetheless gets the job done — do, because I want them to have to separate hollowness from failure to act at all. The three or four cases should be built so that declarations, promises, and apologies come apart in what they each require.

Finally, give me the debrief: map each trouble case onto the distinctions I actually care about — misfire versus abuse, uptake versus perlocutionary effect, what a declaration needs as against what a promise or an apology needs — and, for each of my friend's three claims, say precisely where in the session it breaks and what a defender of her view could say to squirm out at that point, since I would rather know the escape routes in advance. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this: I expect at least one student to try to win by gaming the wording of a formula, and at least one to try to collapse every dispute into a vote, and I want a prepared response to both that does not simply stop the game. And be honest about which part of the phenomenon the simulation cannot reproduce — the bit I will have to say in so many words myself at the end, and which no arrangement of cards and rules will make them feel.
