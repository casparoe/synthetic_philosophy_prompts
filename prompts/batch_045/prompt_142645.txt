Reading the cyclical theories of history — the ones that treat a whole civilization like an organism with a youth and an old age — I keep tripping over a move I don't have a word for.

Two instances. Enormous public building, and being very open to foreign people and foreign ideas: in the chapters about the confident middle phase both get described as energy and reach, and in the chapters about the late phase the very same things get described as bloat and loss of nerve. Same with inequality and with religious skepticism — vigor on the way up, rot on the way down. The feature itself never changes, only where in the story you've decided you are. And it definately cuts both ways, because civilizations that didn't infact collapse get the same traits written up in the prevous chapter as dynamism.

My first guess was hindsight bias, but that feels too narrow: that's about judging how likely something was, whereas here it's about which description of the evidence is allowed to stick. It's also not just plain unfalsifiability, since the theory really does commit itself to a shape — it just never commits itself in advance to what would count as a symptom. So: is there an established concept for this in the philosophy of history or nearby, or for the nearest thing to it? I don't want a bare label — I want to know exactly what the move is, what it is not, and where the boundary sits, since re-describing the evidence must sometimes just be learning more. And does it also affect linear progress theories, or is it special to cyclical ones?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning — no preamble, no restating my question. American English, please.
