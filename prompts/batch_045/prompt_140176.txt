im working on a short piece on humes essay On Suicide and i keep going in circles. The way i read it, the engine of the argument is the providence bit: my choices, like everything else, run on the general laws god set up, so nothing i do can count as a rebellion against him, and diverting those laws to end my life is no different in kind from diverting them to prolong it — taking medicine, turning aside a falling stone, building a dam. My roomate says the whole thing collapses: if determinism is whats doing the work then nothing is really up to me, so calling suicide permissible has no content, and it proves far too much anyway — if a human life matters no more to the universe than an oyster's, then killing my neighbour is fine too, and anyone who costs other people more than they contribute is positively obliged to kill themselves. My fix was to say that the harm doing the real work is harm to the person who dies: murder steals someone's future, whereas suicide leaves no surviving victim to be robbed of one, so the two aren't parallel cases at all. He says that's just me stapling a modern deprivalist view onto a text that argues no such thing. Which of us is confused, and where exactly does humes actual argument stop and my roomates extrapolation begin? Is the determinism premise load-bearing at all, or is he up to something else in that part of the essay? And what's the overall aim — is it that suicide is sometimes positively the sensible option, or just that it isn't a crime anyone may punish?
