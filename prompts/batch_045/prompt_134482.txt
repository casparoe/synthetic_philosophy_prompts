I teach a graduate seminar on decision-making under normative uncertainty, and I am assembling a handout in which I use my own settled position as the worked example. Before I put it in front of students, I would like an independent audit of whether the position is actually internally coherent. I am not asking for references or reading suggestions; I want the arguments themselves. Please write your answer in American English.

Here is the position, in eight theses.

(1) Meta-normative: I am a realist. There is a single correct answer about what there is most reason to do in any given situation, and my uncertainty about which moral theory is the true one is purely epistemic, not a fact about the metaphysics of normativity.

(2) Method: the right response to that uncertainty is decision-theoretic rather than epistemological. I should not try to settle the first-order question first, and I should not simply act on my favourite theory; I should rank my options by expected choice-worthiness, understood as the credence-weighted sum, across the theories I take seriously, of how good each theory says each option is. Since the theories' scales are not comparable, I normalize each theory's scale by rescaling it so that the values it assigns to the options currently under consideration have unit variance. The point is to stop a theory with an inflated scale from swamping the others, without privileging any one theory's units. In cases involving empirical uncertainty I take expectations over the empirical possibilities first, then normalize the resulting option-values, then weight by credence.

(3) A constraint on the method: whether one option outranks another should not depend on which further options happen to be on the menu. If I add, or strike, an option I was never going to choose, the relative ranking of the survivors should be undisturbed. I hold this loosely, but I would give it up only under real pressure.

(4) I reject fanaticism. I do not accept that for any finite cost there is some sufficiently tiny probability of a sufficiently great good such that taking the gamble is mandatory. A procedure that cannot say no to a Pascalian gamble is, to me, defective.

(5) My credences, roughly: 0.25 in hedonistic total utilitarianism; 0.2 in a deontology with agent-centred constraints; 0.15 in a contractualism on which wrongness consists in being disallowed by principles no one could reasonably reject; 0.15 in a person-affecting view of the asymmetric sort I gesture at in (6); 0.1 in a virtue-ethical view on which the right act is what a virtuous agent would characteristically do; the remainder spread thinly over views I have never bothered to articulate.

(6) Substantively, I find the asymmetry about existence plausible: that a person's life would be happy is not in itself a reason to bring them into existence, though that it would be miserable is a reason not to.

(7) I take it that I should sometimes act against the verdict of the theory I currently think most likely to be true, when hedging across theories recommends doing so. That seems to me to be what uncertainty requires, not a kind of incoherence.

(8) A concrete verdict, which I have let settle a real decision: choosing how to deploy a large donation, between a well-evidenced intervention that improves the lives of beings who already exist, and a speculative intervention aimed at improving the very long-run future, where the case rests on a small probability of an enormously better trajectory and much of the value comes from there being vastly many more welfare subjects later on. Running the procedure in (2) with the credences in (5), the long-run option comes out ahead by several orders of magnitude.

What I want from you: go through this set and tell me which theses genuinely conflict, which only appear to, and for each real conflict what exactly would have to be given up and what giving it up would cost me. Rank the conflicts by severity rather than flagging them all as equally bad, and treat each apparent conflict as something to be tested rather than asserted. Where a conflict is technical, show me the mechanism: a small toy example with two or three options and two theories is worth far more to me than a paragraph of prose. Where a conflict admits more than one repair, tell me which repair is cheapest and what it leaves me unable to say. Finally, tell me whether the verdict in (8) is one that the procedure in (2), as I have actually specified it, can deliver.
