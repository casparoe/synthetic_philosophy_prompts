I'm a high school senior writing an extended research paper on assisted dying — specifically on what the law should permit when a competent adult asks a doctor to prescribe or administer a drug that will end their life. My teacher's main criticism of my drafts is that I argue case by case: I produce an example, show that some view I dislike handles it badly, and declare victory. She wants me to say first what any adequate view would have to be able to do, and only then test views against that. So that is what I am asking you for: the specification, not the verdict.

The purpose the view has to serve: it should be good enough to guide an actual legal rule in a country with decent palliative care but very uneven social support, where doctors are fallible and overworked, where some patients are poor or isolated, and where some depend on relatives for daily care. If a view is only adequate for ideal institutions or ideal families, mark that as a limitation instead of letting it slide in unnoticed.

What I would like from you:

1. The list of requirements a view has to meet, each with the case or consideration behind it, so I can see why it belongs on the list rather than just take it on faith.
2. For each requirement, whether it is non-negotiable, a preference of one camp, or genuinely negotiable — and please tell me plainly if any of the things people demand in this debate are really just the prejudices of one side dressed up as standards.
3. The pairs of requirements that I suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, with the argument for why not. If the list turns out to be unsatisfiable as a whole, tell me which requirement you would drop and what that costs.
4. Which single requirement does the most work in ruling out popular positions.

The cases I think a view has to handle — please use them, correct them, or replace them with sharper versions, and add any that are missing:

- The request that is partly socially caused: a young person with a severe disability, competent and stable in what she says over many months, whose suffering is real but is largely produced by inadequate care funding, inaccessible housing, and isolation. The same state that underfunded her support is being asked to authorise her death. I do not know whether the right requirement is that the view must handle this at all, or that it must say something specific about it: I keep finding that views which appeal only to autonomy have nothing to say, and views that appeal only to injustice end up telling her she is not really choosing.
- The deadline case: someone whose suffering is unbearable now but who, on the best available evidence, would probably be glad in two years that the request was refused. Whatever the view says here has to confront the fact that we routinely let competent people take large risks of death for reasons we think unwise, and sometimes refuse them things for their own future good.
- The cases we already permit: refusing life-saving treatment, having treatment withdrawn, deep sedation at the end of life, and high-dose pain relief given foreseeably but not intentionally to hasten death. Any view must deal with these, and I think it fails if the only difference it can point to is who physically performs the last act.
- The professional-role case: why a doctor, and what follows if no doctor will participate. Is the appeal to medicine's internal norms doing real work, or is it gatekeeping?
- The scope case: someone who clearly stated years ago that she wanted assistance if she ever reached advanced dementia, and now cannot reaffirm it. Is covering or not covering this a legitimate choice of scope, or is a view incomplete without an answer?
- The expressive case: whatever rule we adopt is also a message to people whose lives resemble the lives being ended. I am unsure whether being required to handle this is a real desideratum or a political one.
- The administrability case: if the test asks whether suffering is unbearable and beyond improvement, someone has to decide, under uncertainty, with an error rate. I want to know which direction the burden of proof should fall and whether any view can avoid this problem rather than just relocate it.

Finally, only in the last few lines, score the four positions I keep meeting — an appeal to the sanctity of life, an appeal to autonomy, an appeal to the relief of suffering, and an appeal to the integrity of medicine — against the requirements you marked non-negotiable. Keep that part short; the list and its motivating cases are the part I care about. Please do not give me sources, reading lists, or citations, and please do not just restate the autonomy-versus-sanctity-of-life debate, which I have already read plenty of.
