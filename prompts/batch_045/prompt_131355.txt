Take the Wright brothers against the "scientific" aeronautical establishment — Lilienthal, Langley, the Smithsonian crowd — and read it through Darwin's and Turing's strange inversion: the view that comprehension isn't the source of competence but a late, optional gloss on it, produced by a process that doesn't itself understand anything.

Do the mapping properly. Who's the competent-without-comprehending system here: the brothers, the Flyer, the wind-tunnel programme with its couple of hundred wing shapes, or the whole tinkering-plus-selection process? And don't let "comprehension" stay vague — I've been using a three-way split that helps me: quantitative-predictive grasp (can you compute the lift in advance?), causal-handle grasp (do you know which knob to turn, and which way?), and justificatory grasp (can you say why it works to someone who doubts it?). Say which of those the Wrights had in 1903, which came later, and which Langley had; I'd guess the three come apart in this case, but argue it, don't just agree with me.

Then run it the other direction. Two things nag at me about reading the episode as a confirmation: the brothers measured their way to a corrected pressure coefficient when the textbook number turned out to be wrong, which looks less like blind competence and more like comprehension arriving on schedule by the very method the theorists were supposed to be using; and the theory of lift that came afterwards is exactly what lets you skip a thousand wind-tunnel runs, which suggests comprehension does real forward work rather than merely telling a story about a fait accompli. And if the case is just a bad fit — a decade of bicycle-shop tinkering isn't a mindless substrate, and the "competence" is spread across a community rather than sitting in one system — say so, and tell me what a clean instance of the inversion would look like instead.
