I'm taking an advanced seminar on the ethics of war, and there is one analogy that keeps doing heavy lifting in our discussions without ever being audited. My teaching fellow introduced it informally, and one of my classmates now reaches for it in every paper: war is a boxing match. Two fighters agree to enter the ring; once the bell rings, each has exactly the same permission to hit the other as the other has to hit him, and neither becomes a murderer by landing a punch. The rules of the sport tell you what counts as a foul, a fighter who stays inside them has nothing to answer for, and the further question — whether either fighter had a good reason to be in the ring at all, or is fighting for a promoter's money under a lie — is treated as external to the contest itself. The analogy is then used to license the moral equality of combatants: every soldier on every side has the same permission to kill enemy soldiers, none of them is blameworthy for doing so provided they respect the laws of war, and the wrong of an unjust war belongs entirely to the political leaders who ordered it, the way a crooked bout belongs to the promoters who staged it.

The reason I'm suspicious is a case I invented for my final paper and can't find treated anywhere, which my classmate's version of the analogy handles very badly. Imagine a state that deliberately engineers the epistemic situation of its own soldiers: it draws its conscripts from an informationally closed population, feeds them a fabricated account of the conflict designed to be credible to them, screens out recruits with independent access to outside sources, and does all this as policy, on the explicit reasoning that a soldier who non-culpably believes his cause is just is morally harder to blame and harder to target than one who knows better. Call it excuse farming. Note that on the ground nothing about the soldiers' conduct changes: they still fight and kill soldiers of the other side who are fighting justly. Now I want to know what the boxing picture says about this. Is excuse farming like doping — a foul that voids the bout and discredits its organizers while leaving the permissions inside the ring untouched? Does it instead break the analogy's own logic, since the fighter's consent, which is supposed to do the whole moral work, is now manufactured by one of the parties rather than given by the fighter? And crucially, the analogy seems to fuse two things that the farming case pries apart: a permission to fight (a right to kill, and a liability to be killed in return) and an exemption from blame. In the ring, consent supplies both at once, because the person who consents both waives his complaint and makes his opponent blameless. But a farmed conscript looks blameless while still being an unjust threat someone may defensively resist — and if so, the equality the analogy buys may be only an equality of excuse, which is a thinner conclusion than the one it was wheeled in to support.

So I'd like a proper audit rather than a verdict. Give me the correspondence table explicitly — the ring, the bell, the referee, the gloves, the consent to be hit, the fouls, the promoters, the purse, and, if it has one, an analogue for civilians, who seem to be either the audience or the corner staff and fit neither — and then sort the disanalogies into those that matter for the conclusion being drawn and those that don't. I'm especially interested in what the picture quietly imports into war: that there is a referee, that the rules are constitutive rather than instrumental to some independent fact about who is in the right, that the parties are symmetric agents with symmetric standing, that permission is an all-or-nothing status rather than something that can hold to a degree, and that blame tracks only rule-breaking rather than the justice of what one is doing. Then run the excuse-farming case through both this picture and the rival one my seminar reads it against — the rescuer-and-culpable-aggressor framing that revisionists prefer — and say which handles it better and why, including whether the farming state's leaders become more rather than less liable on each view. Finally, if there is a better analogy for the moral situation of combatants, say what it would have to preserve that the boxing match loses.
