i'm writing a novel where my narrator — a retired taxidermist's assistant, dont ask — keeps a notebook of everything she has seen that was not black. Near the end she writes a poem in it, and I want that poem to be the thing that actually makes the reader feel the raven paradox rather than just nod at it. Can you write it as a villanelle whose two refrains are the hypothesis and its contrapositive (roughly "every raven is black" and "nothing that is not black is a raven" — keep the sense, fix the meter)? The returning lines should do the work: each time they come back they should have to mean something a little dfferent, and when they finally sit together in the last two lines the reader should see why one pale shoe gets to count as a witness at all. Two things need to be right, because she's meant to be sharper than the reader: the poem should notice that a black raven is no evidence whatsoever for the contrapositive version even though the two sentences are logically equivalent, and it should land on the thought that the shoe's confirmation is real but so small it's almost an insult — degree, not kind. No logic lecture, and please no cute wink about the shoe.
