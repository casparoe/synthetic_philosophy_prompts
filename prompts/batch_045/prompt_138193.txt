I am putting together a two-session unit on Jain anekāntavāda for a final-year seminar on relativism, pluralism and the limits of assertion, and I keep running into the same expository crutch. Every popular presentation of the doctrine — including the one a colleague in religious studies gave to my students last term, which is where I first encountered it — introduces it with the parable of the blind men and the elephant: several people each touch a different part of one animal, describe it incompatibly, and are each partly right. My colleague then used the parable to carry a fairly heavy load: because every doctrinal system is a partial grip on a single complex reality, he said, no tradition is strictly speaking wrong, the right stance toward conflicting religious and metaphysical claims is tolerant pluralism, and the real target of the doctrine is dogmatism rather than any particular first-order claim. I want to know whether the parable can bear that weight, or whether it is quietly supplying the very premises that need arguing for.

So: audit the analogy as it is being used here. First, lay out the mapping explicitly — a table is fine — with the elephant, the blind men, the parts touched, the incompatible reports, and the sighted onlooker who can see the whole animal on one side, and reality, finite standpoint-holders, nayas, the conflicting assertions, and whatever plays the role of the onlooker on the other. Two features of the parable worry me especially. The first is the onlooker. The parable is normally told with someone who sees the whole elephant and could in principle settle the dispute by description, and anekāntavāda does seem to have a candidate for that role in kevala-jñāna, the unobstructed cognition the tradition attributes to the liberated. Is the doctrine committed to a standpoint from which the elephant is seen whole? If so, does the many-sided view collapse back into the one-sided absolutism it was introduced against, or is the thing the omniscient knower grasps different in kind from the sighted person's elephant — such that the sighted onlooker in the parable is itself a misleading element? And if there is no such standpoint, what happens to the idea that the partial reports are composable into one complete account by simply conjoining them? The second worry is that the parable models disagreement as ignorance about which bit of a determinate, part-structured object you have hold of, while syādvāda looks like a thesis about the conditions under which a predicate may be truly applied at all: syād asti, syād nāsti, the mode in which a thing both is and is not thus-and-so, and the inexpressible mode, together with the further combinations, seem to be available even to a knower who is ignorant of nothing. Can the parable represent the conjunctive is-and-is-not mode or the inexpressible mode at all, or does it structurally exclude them? I have drafted two variants to test this and would like your verdict on both: one in which the sighted onlooker is deleted, and one in which the elephant is replaced by a river, so that touching a stretch of it gives you no stable part of a persisting whole. A physicist at a talk I gave then suggested the whole thing is just complementarity in quantum mechanics under another name; if that comparison does better or worse on the same scorecard, say which and why.

Please separate the disanalogies that actually matter for the use my colleague makes of the parable from the ones that are merely incidental, and give me a straight answer on whether the tolerant-pluralist conclusion survives once the incidental ones are set aside — or whether the parable was carrying it all along, for instance by presupposing that every partial report is veridical, that partial reports are mutually composable, and that reality is a single object with determinate parts. If the conclusion can be reached, say what extra premise does the work; if it cannot, say what a better analogy would look like, and what it would have to build in that the blind men lack. I don't need citations, a reading list or textual references — I have the syllabus and I want the conceptual structure: the mapping, the load-bearing disanalogies, and the verdict.
