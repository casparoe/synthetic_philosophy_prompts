I'm running a discussion section on Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" next week and I'd rather do it as a session about intuition pumps than as yet another utilitarianism seminar. So: read the story as a pump. Not "what does it say about utilitarianism" — I want the machinery. What is it doing to make you feel the thing it wants you to feel, and is that machinery honest or is it rigged.

A few things I actually want you to get into:

1. The option set is clamped. The stipulation is that the whole city's joy causally depends on that one child's misery, and that nobody can rescue the child, reform the city, redistribute the cost, or take the good without the horror. That's like five exits boarded up at once. So is the intuition it produces even about utilitarianism, or is it an intuition about a situation that more or less never happens — and if it's the latter, does the pump have any scope at all, or is it just a very effective way to make people feel something about a case they'll never meet?

2. It self-tunes, and I want you to say whether that's a strength or a cheat. The narrator tells you at the start to imagine the city however you like because they certainly can't suit everyone, and later basically says: if you believe in all this happiness, good, and if you don't, then let me describe one more thing. A pump that adjusts itself until you produce the target reaction is doing something argument can't do — but it's also very hard to break. What would you call that move, and is there a version of it that's legitimate (say, in moral philosophy where the reader's imagination is part of the data) versus a version that's just unfalsifiable?

3. Run a checksum on the stipulations. Here's a thing I've been playing with: for each feature of a thought experiment, flip it and see whether the intuition survives. If nothing changes, the feature was decoration; if the intuition inverts, it was load-bearing. Do that for: the number of children (one vs ten thousand), the child being a child, the child being right there in the city rather than somewhere far off, the causal dependence being magical/metaphysical rather than economic, and the fact that everyone is shown the child as a kid and most of them end up accepting it. Which ones are load-bearing, and what does the list tell you about what the pump is really pumping — is it even one intuition, or two or three that the story runs together?

4. The ending. The people who walk away don't help the child, don't try to change Omelas, don't organise; they just leave alone, and the story says nobody knows where they go and that the place they're going may not even exist. Is that a third option the story is smuggling in — so the real question was never "stay or fix it" but "who are you, in relation to this" — or is it the story giving up on its own pump, an admission that the interesting question can't be staged in fiction? And does refusing to describe where they go make it a better pump or a cowardly one?

5. What does six pages of festival, bells, horses and weather do that the two-sentence philosophy-paper version doesn't? I want the honest version here: is the vividness doing philosophical work — teaching you what "accepting the bargain" would be like from inside, from the point of view of someone who has already said yes — or is it just making the intuition stickier, and is stickiness a defect in a pump or a feature?

Also: Le Guin gave credit to William James for the central idea (and Dostoyevsky used it first, apparently, though she said she'd forgotten that), and I have a vague memory of her saying somewhere that she never had an answer to the question the story asks. If that's right, does it change the reading — is a story that disclaims a thesis a worse pump, or a better one, or just a different animal? Don't go hunting for citations or a reading list, I can't use them and my students won't read them; just tell me what you'd actually say in the room.

Last thing: be honest about were the reading stalls. If you think the intuition-pump frame is the wrong frame for this story — that it's a parable or a psychomyth or something else and calling it a pump flattens it — say that, and say what reading you'd use instead. I'd much rather hand my students a fight than a tidy answer.
