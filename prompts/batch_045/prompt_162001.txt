Something I keep tripping over in the "what could someone reasonably reject" / many-vs-one literature, and I dont know whether it has a name.

The case everyone argues about is: a million people each get a mild headache, or one person loses a hand. Non-summers say the headaches never add up to the hand, and I get the shape of that argument. But there's a class of cases where the many do seem to win without anyone adding anything, and its when their complaints arent really separate complaints. Case one: a town of 5,000 loses its only water source. Each person's complaint is about the very same loss, so saying "each of the 5,000 has a complaint" counts one loss 5,000 times — but it also seems wrong to say the town's complaint is the same size as one person's. Case two: a policy badly hurts one person, and everyone else in the community is horrified about it. Their horrifiedness isnt a harm to them, and it isnt the same event repeated either — it's 5,000 responses to one event — and yet the number of people responding seems to matter.

So the pattern: cases where counting one complaint per head either inflates (a shared loss gets counted over and over) or sweeps in complaints from people who arent the ones being hurt (distress about someone else's treatment). It looks like aggregation but I dont think it's the "do the numbers count" question, because the disagreement isnt about whether small harms sum — it's about whether these are N separate complaints in the first place. People usually just declare that every person's complaint gets counted once and move on, which papers over both directions.

Does this have a name? I dont want just a label. I want to know what the nearest established concepts are, how my thing falls outside each of them, and which one actually fits; and if none does, coin something and define it tightly enough that I can tell in a new case whether it applies.

Give me a JSON object with these fields: nearest_concepts (a list, each entry with: name, what_it_actually_picks_out, where_it_fits_my_case, where_it_doesnt, and fit as exact/partial/no), verdict (one sentence on whether anything established covers this exactly), proposed_term (term, definition, one case it clearly covers, one case it clearly doesn't, and a yes/no test I can run on a new case), and boundary_cases (three cases, each with whether the term covers it and why).
