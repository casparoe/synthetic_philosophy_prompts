I sit on my school's Student Integrity Board, which rules on whether a particular gift or favor counts as corrupt. I would be grateful for help reading our own track record.

Our rulings so far:

1. A club treasurer accepted a box of pastries from a bakery owner, given as thanks after the club had finished buying there for the year. Not corrupt.
2. A student sent a $50 gift card to a teacher with a note asking her to reconsider a grade that had not yet been submitted. Corrupt.
3. A parent donated new jerseys to the athletic department and then emailed the coach asking that his daughter start at point guard. Corrupt — the Board said the connection did not have to be stated out loud.
4. A student gave each of her teachers a jar of preserves at the start of the year, as her family always does. Not corrupt.
5. A parent offered a contribution to the principal's discretionary fund while his son's suspension appeal was pending, explaining that in the country he grew up in such gifts to officials are ordinary. Corrupt; the Board added that its standard applies regardless of local custom. It has also hinted, without ever saying why, that a standing relationship of favors is worse than a one-off offer.

The new case: for three years a family that owns a restaurant near the school has hosted a free dinner each June for the whole Student Council and its faculty adviser — no request attached, and Council took no vote for months afterward. This year the dinner is moved to the week before a vote on a student-activities-fee increase that would pay for more catered school events, and the invitations go only to the eleven voting members: the alternates and the adviser, who were always included before, are not invited.

What should the Board decide if it wants to be consistent with itself, and which precedent is the closest analog? I am less interested in the verdict than in the rule we are implicitly running: is it a test about the giver's intent to influence, about whether a donor with a financial stake is buying access and indebtedness, about whether the gift is targeted at the actual decision-makers, about customary practice as an exemption, or something else? If our rulings genuinely underdetermine the case, tell me what principle we would be committing to with each choice, and which single further case would best separate the rules still in play.
