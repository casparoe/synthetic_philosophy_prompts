You are a philosopher of social science who also reads life-course demography and survey methodology. Please work carefully, and begin with a short, explicitly labelled brainstorming phase: before committing to anything, list the candidate concepts, distinctions, and literatures that bear on the phenomenon I describe below, including candidates from outside philosophy (demography, measurement theory, social psychology, historical periodization). I am a PhD student in political sociology, finishing a dissertation chapter on cohort replacement and political attitudes, and I keep running into a pattern that I do not have a word for. That costs me a paragraph of explanation every time, so I want either the established term or a properly carved new one. Three instances:

(1) Description drift under fixed boundaries. The birth years attributed to a much-discussed cohort stay roughly where they were, but the traits attributed to it change substantially within a few years: the same cohort is described first as confident, commercially optimistic and digitally native, and then, without anyone announcing a revision, as anxious, risk-averse, and alienated from institutions. The new description is then "confirmed" by fresh survey data in which respondents who accept the label report the new traits. Nobody treats the earlier descriptions as falsified; they are quietly dropped.

(2) Seam repair. The contested boundary is always the one adjacent to whichever cohort is currently the live topic: the trailing edge of the cohort before it, not its leading edge. At each contested seam, small interstitial labels appear — portmanteau names for people born in the gap — and their function seems to be to absorb the individuals who would otherwise be counterexamples to the stereotype. The individuals do not change; the partition is patched around them.

(3) Elicitation feedback. If a survey offers the cohort label as a response option, self-classification is noisy in a systematic direction, and respondents who accept the label then answer trait questions in line with the stereotype associated with it. Re-running the analysis with the label removed, or with birth year recorded continuously, weakens the trait differences considerably.

My own hypothesis is that the label, not the birth years, is doing the explanatory work: the label attracts and then stabilizes a description, and the boundaries are maintained by repair rather than tracked by discovery. I have a vague memory of a literature on classifications of people feeding back on the people classified — something about looping — but that looks to me like a different case: there, the classified people change in response to being classified. Here the people stay put, the description and the boundary move, and the movement is asymmetric in the way I described. A colleague mentions an "identification problem" in cohort analysis, but as far as I can tell that is a statistical worry about separating age, period and cohort effects in a linear model, not about what happens to a named partition in public and scientific use.

So: does this configuration have a name of its own, in philosophy or in the neighbouring disciplines? If it does, tell me how closely it fits and where it comes apart from what I have described. If it does not, coin a term, give it a definition that does real work, and specify the boundary cases — including the case that looks superficially like mine but should not be counted as an instance, and the case that does not look like mine on the surface but should be. A bare label will not help me. I need the carve: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
