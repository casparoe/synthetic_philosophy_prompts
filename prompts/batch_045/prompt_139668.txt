I'm writing a paper on the argument from the poverty of the stimulus, and I've got stuck on how much work the argument can do even when you grant it everything it asks for. So I want you to consider a composite case — not a real language, not a real experiment, a stipulation I'm constructing to isolate the logic. Please take the stipulations as given rather than querying them.

Stipulate, then, that in a well-documented language there is a constraint forbidding the formation of a question by moving a question-word out of a certain kind of embedded clause, where the constraint tracks hierarchical structure rather than linear order, semantic role, or frequency. Stipulate further that: (i) adult speakers converge on the judgment that the relevant sentences are deviant, without instruction, even though the corresponding declaratives are flawless; (ii) corpus work over the entire recorded child-directed speech of the community — millions of words — finds no instance of the deviant structure and no sentence that would discriminate between the adult constraint and the simpler linear-order rule; (iii) children converge on the adult pattern by age four, their errors are omissions rather than the overgenerations the simpler rule would predict, and correction of the deviant structure is essentially absent from the input; and (iv) a neural sequence model trained from random initialization on exactly that corpus, at the same token count, with only the inductive biases that come with the architecture, matches adults on a held-out probe of the same contrast.

Call K the claim that the knowledge underlying the adult pattern is innate in the sense of not having been acquired from the child's linguistic experience by domain-general learning.

Give me your credence in K given (i)–(iv), decomposed. I want a number and a line of justification for each of the terms you use, then the combination. The terms I think belong, though add or replace: whether (ii) establishes underdetermination at all, given that we are never in a position to enumerate what would have counted as a disambiguating datum; whether (iv) supports or undermines the innateness inference, and specifically whether the architectural inductive biases that let the model succeed are themselves domain-general or a Universal Grammar smuggled in by designers who knew in advance what they were building; whether innate here has to mean genetically encoded rather than merely canalized, species-typical, or inherited from non-linguistic constraints on attention and memory; whether the constraint posited by the argument is psychologically real in the four-year-old or merely the most economical description of the language she ends up speaking; whether the argument's second premise is indexed to whatever learning theory happens to be available at the time, so that the case for innateness can be eroded by progress in machine learning rather than by any discovery about language; and whether the residual dispute between nativists and their critics is empirical at all rather than a disagreement about which learning machinery qualifies as domain-general.

Then tell me which term your number is most sensitive to, which one you are least confident about, and what single further stipulation — for instance, that the model in (iv) had failed — would move the number farthest and in which direction. Finally: my credence in K is around 0.35 and my supervisor's is around 0.85. Tell me where you think that gap most likely lives, and whether it is a gap about the case or about what K is asserting. And separately, give me a number for the credence that this dispute is the kind of thing that has a determinate answer at all.
