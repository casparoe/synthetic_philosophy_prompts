I've been going in circles on the absent-minded driver problem for like a week and I think I've built an argument that shows you can beat the planning optimum, which is obviously wrong, but I can't find the joint. Setup in case you use different numbers: two indistinguishable intersections, exit at the first gets you 0, exit at the second gets you 4, blow past both gets you 1, and you know in advance that you won't be able to tell them apart or remember passing one.

(1) Restrict to strategies of the form "at every intersection, continue with probability p" — you've got nothing to condition on, so that's all there is. Expected payoff is p(4 − 3p), maximized at p = 2/3, value 4/3.

(2) When you're actually at an intersection, your credence that it's the first one should be the visit frequency: you hit the first for sure and the second only with probability p, so P(first) = 1/(1+p). Call it q.

(3) So at the intersection: exiting is worth q·0 + (1−q)·4. Continuing is worth q·(4 − 3p) + (1−q)·1 — if you're at the first and continue you arrive at the second, where the rest of your plan is worth 4 − 3p, and if you're at the second and continue you just get 1.

(4) Run the planning-optimal plan, p = 2/3, so q = 3/5: exiting = 4(2/5) = 8/5, continuing = (3/5)(2) + 2/5 = 8/5. Both come out to 1.6.

(5) But 1.6 > 4/3. The ex ante optimum says 4/3 is the most you can get, and yet once you arrive both of your options are worth 1.6. Maybe per-visit value just isn't the same quantity as ex ante value, fine, but it's a bad smell.

(6) Now plan p = 1, always continue: q = 1/2, so exiting is worth 4(1/2) = 2 and continuing is worth (1/2)(1) + 1/2 = 1. Exiting is worth 2, which is way above the supposed maximum of 4/3.

(7) And in general exiting is worth 4p/(1+p), which rises with p and beats continuing exactly when p > 2/3. So the better your plan is, the more you should deviate from it, and as p → 1 the value of deviating at the intersection approaches 2.

(8) So the ex ante calculation is a red herring: adopt some plan to fix your credences, then do the other thing when you get there, and collect nearly 2 instead of 4/3. Which is absurd — exit at every intersection and you get 0, continue at every one and you get 1, and you can't beat the max over strategies by planning one strategy and executing another.

Two things I'd rather not give up. First, (2), the visit-frequency credence — becuase I'm a thirder about Sleeping Beauty and this looks like literally the same move. Second, the principle doing the work in (4) through (7), that what you should do at the intersection is maximize given the credences you actually have there; if I drop that, "don't adopt a plan you know you'll abandon" becomes empty and I have no idea how to think about sequential selves at all.

My own guess is that the rot is in (5): averaging over the two visits counts the same eventual payoff twice, since if I'm at the second intersection the 4 is still entirely ahead of me while if I'm at the first it sits downstream of this very choice. But here's why I don't trust that guess. Run the same computation on a variant with two duplicate drivers instead of one driver twice: computationally identical, one at each intersection, both present at the same time, payoff fixed by what the pair does (driver at the first exits → 0, driver at the second exits → 4, both continue → 1). Now there's no shared future to double-count and the credence is a flat 1/2 whatever the strategy is, and at p = 2/3 I get exit = 4/3 and continue = 4/3 — the inflation in (5) is gone. But at p = 1 I still get exit = 2 against continue = 1, the exact absurdity of (6). So the double-counting story doesn't touch the part that actually bothers me.

Which step do you reject, why does it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost me? And whatever you reject, tell me what's left of the idea that there's a decision problem at the intersection at all — because if the driver can't discriminate then "exit" isn't an act available at one intersection, it's a change of rule, and the credence is a function of the rule, and it starts to feel like the whole thing dissolves into "your only real choice is the ex ante one," which can't be the right moral either. Also tell me honestly whether the duplicate-driver version is a genuinely different problem or just the same problem with the confusing part surgically removed.
