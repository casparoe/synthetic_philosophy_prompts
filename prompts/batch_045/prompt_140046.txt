I write a long-form science column and sit in on a graduate reading group in evolutionary biology, and last week we spent a whole session on Gould and Lewontin's 1979 paper "The Spandrels of San Marco and the Panglossian Paradigm." Two people came away using the architectural analogy in exactly opposite directions, and I can't tell which of them is leaning on the analogy and which is leaning on something else. My friend Priya uses it to argue that the adaptationist programme is unfalsifiable in reverse: for any trait anyone proposes as an adaptation, you can always instead tell a story in which it is a byproduct of selection on something else, so "it is a spandrel" is as cheap and as unfalsifiable as "it is an adaptation," and the two hypotheses can never be made to bite on each other. My supervisor uses the very same analogy for the opposite conclusion: he says a byproduct is only identifiable as a byproduct against a background of structure whose point you already grasp—you can only call something "left over" if you know what the arches and the dome were for and which of their features were chosen—so the spandrel critique secretly presupposes the adaptationist's capacity to identify adaptations and therefore cannot be used to discredit it. I don't need a summary of the adaptationism dispute or a reconstruction of Gould and Lewontin's intentions; I know that story. I want the analogy itself audited.

To keep the audit honest, here is a concrete case from my lab-mate's system that we argued about afterward. In a genus of desert annuals, most populations carry a bright magenta band on the stem just below the flower. Field work finds no measurable effect of the band on pollinator visits, herbivory, or water loss; it costs roughly four percent of the plant's nitrogen budget; and it occurs only in populations that also have a deeply lobed leaf. Lobed leaves do affect fitness, because they run several degrees cooler. The band's pigment is synthesized two steps upstream of a compound that stiffens the stem. We listed four candidate histories: (i) the band itself was built by selection for some function we have not yet detected; (ii) the band is a correlated response to selection on leaf shape, through pleiotropy or linkage; (iii) the band arose as a consequence of the stem-stiffening pathway and was only later recruited for a signaling function; (iv) the band drifted up during a bottleneck and is now selectively near-neutral. I have also been trying to sort explanations into three boxes of my own: necessitated byproducts, where the trait had to be there given the rest of the design, as the triangular spaces had to be there given the arches; co-opted byproducts, which arose for no current-use reason and were later recruited for one—what Gould and Vrba labeled exaptation in 1982; and faded adaptations, which selection built for a job they no longer do.

Please do the following, in American English, in about 800 to 900 words. First, give me an explicit correspondence table: element of the building onto element of the organism, and say where the mapping is forced and where it is a choice. Second, separate the disanalogies that matter from the ones that do not, and say which disanalogy does the real damage to each of my two interlocutors: Priya's claim that spandrel-talk makes adaptationism unfalsifiable, and my supervisor's claim that spandrel-talk presupposes adaptationism. In particular I want to know what happens to the analogy's central feature—that the architectural space is necessitated by decisions taken elsewhere, and is only subsequently available for decoration—when it is carried over to a trait that nothing forced to exist. Third, tell me which of my three boxes the analogy can actually model and which it conflates, and which of the four candidate histories for the magenta band it can help us tell apart and which it is simply silent about, so I can see whether the analogy is doing any work in my lab-mate's case or merely renaming the problem. Fourth, one member of the group insists that the structures in San Marco are not really spandrels at all, that other solutions were available to the builders, and that this ruins the example; I don't want your answer to hang on the architectural history, but tell me whether, if he is right, the mapping changes in a way that matters. Finally, say whether either conclusion survives, and if the building is the wrong source domain, name one that carries the distinction better.
