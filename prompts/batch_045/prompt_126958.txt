The argument of the chapter is this. I claim the famous 1979 Gould and Lewontin paper is best read not as the empirical claim that lots of traits aren't adaptations — which is the version everyone argues about, and which the comparative-methods literature has more or less answered — but as an attack on what I call "trait-wise separability": the working assumption that you can assign a fitness contribution to a single trait while holding the rest of the organism's design fixed, so that an organism decomposes into independently optimisable parts. My follow-up claim is that separability is built into the very tools that are supposed to have answered the critique. A selection-gradient analysis, a one-trait-at-a-time manipulation, even a fairly fancy phylogenetic model with lots of covariates, all estimate the fitness effect of a trait against whatever background phenotype happens to be sitting there in your population, so they can't test separability without already assuming it. As the positive alternative I propose measuring what I call a "coupling profile": how the fitness return to changing trait X varies with the state of the rest of the phenotype. My evidence for why this matters is my own beetles — horned dung beetles, where I ran factorial manipulations of horn length, body size and rival density, and the mating advantage of a longer horn actually reverses sign below a certain body size, whereas a one-at-a-time analysis on the same animals would have spat out a single flat postive gradient.
