Hey — I somehow ended up on my university's commemorative naming committee (don't ask, I made the mistake of replying to a thread), and now we have to apply our written standard to three cases at once: a Columbus-named scholarship fund, a Jefferson-named residential college, and a Churchill-named lecture series. I've been arguing about it in a group chat full of LessWrong-adjacent people for weeks — I've been reading this stuff since the Sequences were going up — and we keep going in circles, so I want someone to actually do the philosopher thing and tell me what the rule we wrote presupposes.

The rule, roughly: (1) we strip a name only if the person's worst acts were condemned by at least some of their own contemporaries — call it the availability test, the idea being that you can only be faulted for not knowing better if somebody in fact did know better; (2) we explicitly don't strip a name just because the person held odious beliefs that were near-universal at the time, which is where the "man of his time" defence keeps getting wheeled out; (3) if the name was attached as a condition of a bequest from the honoree's own estate, we won't touch it, because honoring him was the deal we made and the deal is what paid for the building — Chesterton's fence and all that — while for a name the university chose on its own initiative, clauses (1) and (2) apply in full; and (4), which I think is quietly doing the most work, when a name has been around so long that nobody on campus knows or cares who it refers to and it just functions as an address, we leave it alone, because (in one member's words) "at that point it's not honoring anyone, it's just a signifier."

Here's my problem. (1) and (2) together look like they presuppose that blameworthiness is comparative rather than absolute — that what matters is being worse than your peers, not being bad — and I can't tell if that's a coherent view of blame or just a slide from blame to praiseworthiness, since honoring might be contrastive in a way that blaming isn't, and commemoration might be some weird hybrid of the two. Then (3) and (4) seem to pull the opposite way: (3) makes the wrongness of a name depend on what the namer meant, and (4) makes it depend on what the name currently does, and those can't both be the whole story — unless you treat commemoration as an ongoing speech act that can lapse, which I half believe but can't spell out. Also can you steelman clause (3) for me, because my side of the chat thinks it's obviously just money talking.

So: reconstruct the implicit theory of blame, honor, and historical judgment that this policy only makes sense under; be explicit about what it strictly presupposes versus what merely sits comfortably with it; tell me whether (1) and (2) are even consistent given that beliefs cause acts and acts flow from beliefs; say what the policy would have to become under a rival view — someone proposed treating honor as a lease rather than a deed, with automatic review of every name every fifty years, which I like but which seems to have its own commitments; and flag the places where the policy is just underdetermined, where several quite different theories would license identical votes, so that our disagreements aren't evidence of anything. Under 650 words, prose rather than a bullet dump, since I'll be reading it on my phone on the bus to the meeting.
