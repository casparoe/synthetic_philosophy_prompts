I work as head of security at a large hospital, and before that I spent fifteen years on the door at bars and clubs in a rough part of town. I have no college education and no training in anything academic. I ended up reading about nuclear strategy because about a year ago I got curious about how anyone could hope to prevent a war by threatening one, and it struck me that the problem looks a lot like the problem of stopping a fight in a room full of people who are all watching each other and all deciding what to do next based on what everyone else is about to do. Since then I have read a fair amount of popular writing on it, and it keeps sliding out of my hands. I would like it said back to me in the words I actually use at work.

The vocabulary I mean is this: reading a room, escalation, de-escalation, backing someone down versus backing down yourself, letting a man save face, sending a message, the regulars watching, the guy who cannot control himself, the guy who has to be seen to be reasonable, the point where it stops being talk, calling the police as an outside force that neither side controls, and the standing fact that once it gets physical both of you have already lost, somebody may end up in the emergency room and I may end up in court.

There are three claims I keep running into in my reading. First, the claim that a threat is more believable if you have arranged things so that you could not call it off even if you wanted to, and that letting part of the outcome depend on chance is supposed to be a strength of a threat rather than a defect in it. Second, the claim that it helps to be thought irrational, or not fully in control, or the sort of person who might go further than a sensible person would stop. In a bar I think that is true, but I am not sure it is true for the reason they give. Third, the claim that making your weapons more accurate, or building something to protect your own people, can make the fight more likely rather than less, because one side begins to think it could actually win, and the other begins to think it had better go first while it still can.

There is also something from my own work that I have never seen in anything I have read, and I want you to deal with it. The person who most reliably stops a fight, in my experience, is not the loud one and not the unpredictable one. It is the one who plainly does not want it, who is obviously going to get hurt, and who clearly will keep going anyway past the point where a sensible man would quit. That combination of reluctance with the look of somebody who can absorb a beating is more convincing than any threat I have ever heard shouted. I do not know whether that is the same thing as what the books call a secure second-strike capability, or whether it is a different thing entirely, and I would like you to tell me which and why.

So here is what I am asking. Take those three claims, and take my own observation, and restate them as carefully and as honestly as you can in my vocabulary: the room, the regulars, the outside force, the moment it stops being talk. Keep the arguments intact rather than filing them down into something that merely sounds like something I would say anyway. Then give me the residue report. What came across cleanly? What did you have to bend or stretch to fit my words? And what is there in these arguments that my vocabulary simply cannot carry, such that no sentence about a bar, a crowd, or a fight manages to say it? For each piece of that leftover, tell me honestly whether you think it points to something missing in the way I think about a room, or to something in the writing on deterrence that is vaguer than it sounds. I would rather be told that a claim does not survive the translation than be handed a version of it that flatters me.

One more thing. I have had thousands of nights to learn this work on, and the night tells me whether I got it right. The people writing about deterrence have had, in the whole history of the thing, no night where it actually went all the way, and so no way of finding out whether any of it was right. I suspect that difference is doing more work in their arguments than they admit. Tell me whether it is.

Please answer in continuous prose, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables. Write it the way you would write a letter to someone who is intelligent and experienced but has never read a book on this subject.
