Two people at a reading group I sit in on got stuck on a case, and they asked me to referee; I couldn't, so I'm passing it to you.

The case: a man leaves his home town at eighteen after a bitter falling-out, emigrates, changes his name, and for forty years lives in a city where nobody knows his history. He works, pays taxes, is civil to neighbours, forms no real attachments. The one person who still thinks of him is the sister he has not spoken to since he left.

The question is whether, on a relational or Ubuntu-style conception of personhood — the family of views on which one is a person through one's relationships and standing in a community rather than through some inward capacity like rationality or self-consciousness — he counts as a person during those forty years.

Amara: Yes. The relations that matter are normative, not psychological. His sister's continuing recognition, plus the plain fact that his life is threaded through others at every point — language, food, institutions — is enough. And any reading that strips personhood from the isolated licenses mistreating them, which is the opposite of what these views were for.

Jonas: Then the view has no content. If being available for relationship suffices, it is just a capacity view in relational costume. If it means anything, personhood is constituted by actual mutual recognition and by occupying roles in a web of obligation, and this man has opted out of all of that.

Amara: That makes personhood hostage to other people's attitudes. A community that refuses to recognise someone — a racist or caste-bound one — would thereby succeed in un-personing them, and nobody should accept that. So the view needs a normative correction: what matters is being someone who can and should be recognised.

Jonas: The correction is what empties the view. Now relational facts do no work, and personhood gets more secure the less recognition there is, which inverts the whole slogan. And look where the achievement reading actually ranks people: a torturer who is trusted, promoted and honoured by the community he serves comes out a paradigm person, while the exile comes out a bare human organism. If that ordering doesn't trouble you, you were never reading the view as a claim about who counts.

Amara: Fine — distinguish two things. Personhood as moral status, which he never lost, and personhood as a thick, achieved excellence, which he plainly lacks. Conflating them is what forces the choice between un-personing the isolated and emptying the view.

Jonas: That is a retreat. It concedes the view was never a rival answer to the question of who counts, only a picture of what human flourishing looks like.
