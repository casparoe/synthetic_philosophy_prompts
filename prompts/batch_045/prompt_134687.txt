writing the torture section of my dissertation chapter on emergency powers (I'm in political theory, not philosophy, so sorry if I mangle the vocabulary) and I've realised I've never actualy checked whether what I believe here is one position or two that I rotate between depending on who's asking. Here it is, roughly:

(a) Absolutist about the rule. No torture warrants, no codified emergency exception, no necessity defence available to officials, ban stays flat. (I do think the necessity defence is fine for ordinary people — speeding to get someone to hospital — and I think officials are different because their claim of necessity is self-certifying in a way the driver's isn't.)
(b) Not an absolutist about the act. In a genuine ticking bomb case — fully stipulated: you know this person planted it, you know he'll talk, you know the information will work, there's no alternative — I think torturing the person who planted the bomb is what should be done, and I don't want to say it's merely excused.
(c) Given (a) and (b), the official breaks the law, is prosecuted, and I think he should be convicted and serve time: no retroactive indemnity, no pardon, no quiet well-done afterwards. I don't think that's hypocrisy, because a legal exception would be worse than the occasional broken official.
(d) My reason for (a) is mostly institutional rather than victim-focused. I'm persuaded by the line that torture is in some sense the wrong law exists to prevent — an attack on the relation between the state and the person, not just a harm to the person. Relatedly, I don't think torture is worse than death, I think the pain-is-what-makes-it-uniquely-awful rhetoric is confused, and I don't think accepting foreseeable civilian deaths in war is inconsistent with the flat ban.
(e) But there's a large difference between torturing the bomber and torturing his eight-year-old daughter to make him talk. The second is off the table even in the fully stipulated case, and not merely because it's less reliable.
(f) I lean hard on the empirical case — torture produces confessions rather than intelligence, the practice migrates to the innocent, agencies lie about necessity — and I think it's doing real work rather than being window dressing.
(g) I think the dirty hands framing is right: in (b)-type cases there's a genuine moral remainder, the official has done something bad, he should feel guilty, we shouldn't celebrate him.

Two stress tests I use, steal them if useful. First, the readout: some technique that reliably extracts what someone believes, no pain, no lasting damage, and verifiable within minutes against independent evidence. My (f) obviously gives out there; I still find myself wanting to say the daughter case is impermissible and the bomber case is fine, but I can't say why, which bothers me. Second, the hundred-case version: not one bomb a decade but a standing situation, thirty a year, each as well-evidenced as the single case. I currently think it's still right each time and that repetition changes the politics, not the morality.

What I want is an audit, not a survey and definitely not a literature roundup. Which of these genuinely pull against each other and how badly, which pairs only look like they conflict, and for each real tension what would have to give and what the cheapest repair costs. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Rank by severity — don't hand me seven equally-weighted worries.

Also, please be blunt: if one of my commitments is doing no work, if the whole thing is two views stapled together, or if my stress tests are badly posed, say so instead of being polite. Mark how confident you are in each tension you claim — I'd rather have three you're sure about than seven you've talked yourself into — and say explicitly where you're guessing about what I mean by something. Don't assume I'd rather drop any particular one of these.
