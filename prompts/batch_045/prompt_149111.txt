I work as a backend engineer and have been reading about al-Ghazālī's occasionalism with two friends from a book group, none of us with any philosophy training, and we keep getting stuck in the same place. Every presentation we have found spends its energy on the negative half of the view: that we never observe any necessary connection between cause and effect, only one thing following another, so the link must be held in place by God's will rather than by anything in the objects themselves. His famous illustration of fire and cotton, where the burning is created at the moment of contact rather than produced by the fire, is the piece we keep circling. But none of these presentations tell us what the view is supposed to change about a prediction someone actually has to make on a Monday morning. So I want to see whether two rival accounts of causation can be forced to disagree about something practical, and I want a purpose-built case rather than an illustration.

What I need is a thought experiment, realistic and confined to a single paragraph, set anywhere non-theological, in which an occasionalist and someone who believes in real causal powers inhering in nature are given identical evidence and asked the same single question about a future event, and where the occasionalist comes out *more* confident than the powers theorist. That direction is the whole point: I want the inversion of the usual complaint, which is that occasionalism guts induction and leaves science unmotivated. Hard constraints: no miracles, no scriptural or revelatory premises, and no stipulating that God does anything special in this case, so that whichever divine activity the scenario contains is exactly equivalent to what the laws of nature are doing and the disagreement is generated by the structure of the two accounts rather than by a premise I smuggled in. Both parties must also agree on the past data and on the description of the object or event in question, so the split comes from what each takes a regularity to be grounded in, not from a quarrel about the facts.

My own attempt, which I think is broken, leans on novelty: if causal powers inhere in a thing's nature, then a genuinely new kind of stuff, say a newly synthesized compound or an unfamiliar alloy, is precisely the case where we do not yet know the nature, so the powers theorist must suspend judgment while the occasionalist, whose uniformity comes from God's habit and is not keyed to how well we have characterized the substance, can extrapolate straight across. A friend objected that this is only a recycled argument about induction, and that the two views collapse together again the moment the powers theorist is allowed to appeal to family resemblance with substances we already know; I suspect he is right, and I also cannot tell whether making the object novel is itself the cheat. So fix it, replace it with something better, or tell me plainly that the inversion is unavailable and which extra premise it would require. After the scenario I want the verdict each theory gives with the reasoning shown, the one feature of the case doing the work, what happens to the verdict if that feature is removed and whether it collapses back into the standard worry, where a plain Humean constant-conjunction theorist lands on the same case (I predict in between, and if she does I want to know whether that means my case is doing less work than I think), and the strongest line of attack available to whichever side comes out worse. The case is for the book group, where the goal is to show that this disagreement has teeth for people who have to predict something, so anything that merely restates each side's metaphysics, or that gets the result by stipulating it, counts as cheating.
