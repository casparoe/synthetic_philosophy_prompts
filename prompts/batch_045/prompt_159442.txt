So I've been chewing on something for a few weeks now and I figured I'd just ask here, because everyone I know in real life has stopped taking me seriously about it.

Earlier this year my younger sister got engaged, and I spent about four thousand dollars I'd been saving up to fly home, help out with the wedding, and generally be around for her — she's had a really rough couple of years and I wanted to be there. Around the same time, a friend of mine who is very into that whole "give money where it does the most good per dollar" thing pointed out that the same four thousand, sent to one of the cheap health programs he likes, would probably prevent a serious illness or maybe even save a life. I don't really dispute his numbers, and honestly it did sting. But I also don't feel like I did anything wrong, and I can't figure out how to say why. When I say "she's my sister and I love her," he says that's just a feeling wearing a reason's clothing, that everybody has feelings like that about their own people, and that a feeling everybody has can't be the thing that makes an action right. My mom thinks I'm overthinking and my roommate just shrugs.

Then it got wierder. He proposed a rule for me to adopt from now on: before I spend more than about a thousand dollars on myself or on someone close to me, I have to write a short note giving a reason that would convince a neutral stranger who didn't know any of us, and if I can't produce one that holds up, the money gets sent to the charity instead. I tried it once on something smaller, and two things happened that I can't make sense of. The first is that sitting down to write the note made me feel like a slightly worse person — like I was being asked to step outside my own life and check whether my sister measured up against everybody else's sister, and that doing that was itself a small betrayal of her. The second is that when I did manage to write a note that passed his test — something like, keeping my family close makes me stable and happy, which makes me far more productive and generous over the long run — handing it over felt gross, and not because it was false. It felt like I'd already had the right reason and then went and had one extra thought that spoiled it. He says both reactions just show I haven't thought clearly enough yet, and that I should keep using the rule until the squeamishness wears off.

Is he right? I've got two things tangled together and I'd like both addressed: whether his original point (that the money should have gone to the charity) is actually a good argument, and whether the note-writing rule is a good way to run a life. Please don't just tell me it's a personal decision or that I should split the difference — actually take a side on whether that rule is damaging something real in me or whether my discomfort is just irrationality I should get over. Write it in American English, in continuous prose only: no headings, bullet points, bold text, tables or numbered lists, just paragraphs the way you'd write them in an email.
