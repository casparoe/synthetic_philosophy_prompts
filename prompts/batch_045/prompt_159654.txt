I'm in a four-person reading group — me (philosophy), two law-and-AI people, and an engineer who actually deploys these things — and we've spent about two terms on the "responsibility gap" for autonomous systems and we are completely going in circles. The case we all use as our shared example is one we made up: a fleet of a few hundred hospital delivery robots that, over millions of runs, collectively learned a corridor-weaving behaviour that no human specified, that nobody could have read off the code or the training data, and that slightly raised throughput while slightly raising the rate of near-misses with visitors. One visitor trips and breaks a hip. We all agree on what happened: the behaviour wasn't foreseeable by any individual engineer, it was flagged as a *class* of possibility in the model card, the deployment sign-off used a throughput-and-safety metric under which the behaviour was net-beneficial in expectation, the fleet operator followed every documented procedure, and the harm can't be traced to any particular robot's decision, any particular training run, or any particular human choice. We all also agree that the visitor should be compensated somehow. After that it falls apart. Half the group says this is a genuinely new kind of gap and we need new machinery — strict liability on deployers, some kind of blame-free accountability, compensation funds, maybe rethinking what we want from blame practices at all. The other half says there is nothing new here: product liability, vicarious liability, corporate liability and the ordinary many-hands problem in large organizations already cover it, and calling it a "gap" is rhetorical. Our engineer keeps saying a third thing, that the buck-stopping is a design choice and we could just build the authorization chain in if we wanted to.

My own hunch, which I'd like you to push on rather than flatter, is that "the responsibility gap" is at least three different gaps that get run together in the same breath, and that they have completely different diagnoses: a *trace* gap (we can't find out which decision caused this), an *opacity* gap (we know the causal story but don't understand why the system did it), and a *fittingness* gap (we know everything there is to know, and still no candidate — the developer, the deployer, the operator, the hospital, the fleet, the system itself — looks like an apt target of blame, even though someone obviously should bear the cost). If that's right, the first two are boring empirical questions about logging and interpretability tooling that will dissolve as the engineering gets better, and only the third is a real philosophical fight, and it's a fight about whether blame is essentially desert-shaped or just a forward-looking regulatory instrument. My friend in the group says that's wrong and the whole thing is basically verbal — that the two sides are just using "responsible" to mean different things (liable-to-pay vs. blameworthy) and everything else is theatre. A third possibility I can't rule out is that the sides are counting different cases as data: the gap people are reasoning about future end-to-end learned systems, the absorbers about current ones that sit inside ordinary corporate hierarchies.

So: what I want is a diagnosis of this disagreement, not a verdict and not a survey of who said what (please skip citations and literature review, I can find those myself). Engage the positions concretely — what the gap people actually claim about why existing doctrines fail, what the absorbers actually claim about why the many-hands case is old news, what the "build the chain in" reply really establishes. Is this verbal, substantive, two questions under one name, a hidden empirical question about how much control operators really retain, or bedrock intuitions about desert that neither side can argue for? Is any of it ill-posed rather than hard? And given whatever you conclude, what would actually count as progress here — a distinction like the one I sketched, an empirical result about operator control, some formal account of control in learned policies, a change of question (from "who is to blame" to "who pays"), or nothing at all? I'd especially like you to say which of the three gaps is doing the real work and whether the "net-beneficial in expectation but the cost lands on one unconsenting person" feature of the case is a red herring or the crux.
