For a graduate seminar on nineteenth-century social critique I want a short piece of in-universe satire, and I would like it cast as an internal document.

The setting: a new periodical, founded by people completely convinced by Kierkegaard's 1840s polemic against the press and the public, has begun soliciting contributions. The piece is its managing editor's numbered "Contributors' Guide and House Rules," about 600–800 words, twelve clauses or so, preceded by a short header. The editor is entirely sincere, writes in a flat bureaucratic register, and is working through ordinary housekeeping questions: bylines and anonymity, circulation figures, whether letters from readers can be printed, whether two subscribers reading the same issue in the same room constitute a public, what the office should do when a satirical paper requests an interview about our opposition to the press.

The comedy has to come out of the doctrine rather than from any added absurdity. Each clause should follow faithfully, and to its logical end, some genuine feature of the critique: the public as an abstraction that can never assemble and that has power precisely because no one is present; leveling as the work of numerical comparison and of envy; anonymity as the machinery that lets an opinion be held without being owned; an age of publicity in which every event is converted into material for talk and decision is dissolved; the single individual as the only possible addressee of a truth; pseudonymity and indirect communication as the devices by which one warns against a readership without forming one. Exaggerate these; do not supply him with invented doctrines he would not accept.

Two constraints. First, he is never named in the document — call the founding essay "the Diagnosis" if a label is needed — and nothing real should be quoted; a reader who has never heard of Kierkegaard should still enjoy it as workplace absurdity, while a Kierkegaardian should judge it fair. Second, the editor must never become self-aware. Where the contradictions surface — a printed periodical that cannot acknowledge a readership; a position so comfortable that everyone can hold it, and which therefore levels better than any other — they must surface as administrative problems to be solved by a subcommittee, a form, or an office, say a Public-Hood Audit, an Office of Non-Assembly, or a rule requiring each contributor to adopt a fresh pseudonym per issue and to have that pseudonym disavow what it signs. No winking, no footnotes to the reader, no stated moral.

Let the reductio land by itself in the final clauses — the print run, the masthead, the possibility of anyone's ever having an opinion about the journal — and leave the memo unsigned.
