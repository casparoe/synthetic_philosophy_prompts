I teach a graduate seminar on self-knowledge, and this term I have been trying to get my students (and myself) to stop arguing about luminosity and the KK principle in the abstract and instead to work from verdicts on cases. So let me lay out mine, since I have found that my own pattern is less tidy than I assumed. Say that a condition is luminous when being in it puts you in a position to know that you are in it, and read KK as: if you know that p, you are in a position to know that you know that p. Now: (1) Anya has an abscessed tooth, is in considerable pain, and is asked whether she is in pain — she says yes at once; I want to say she is in pain, knows she is, and knows that she knows. (2) Ben is walking off a fell on an autumn evening; at 8:03 pm he is, as a matter of fact, cold, but if asked he would say "chilly, not cold yet"; I want to say he is cold and does not know that he is, and a fortiori does not know that he knows. (3) Cara's colleague was promoted over her; she is jealous — her partner sees it, her remarks at dinner have an edge that everyone notices — yet she denies it sincerely and with confidence, and I am inclined to say not merely that she fails to know but that a moment of silent attention would not fix it: she is not in a position to know. (4) Dev, a surgical resident in hour thirty of a shift, is too tired to operate safely; he says he is tired but fine. Again I say he is too tired and does not know it, though here the explanation feels different from Ben's: Dev is not near a boundary, his self-assessment itself is degraded by the very state he is assessing. (5) Elena, an editor, reads a manuscript and judges that the statistics are botched; she is right, and her judgment is a good one. Later the same day she learns that a technique she was taught is systematically unreliable and that this affected a class of judgments of exactly her kind, with no way for her to tell her case apart from the others; asked now whether she knows, she says she no longer does. My verdict is the opposite of hers: she still knows the statistics are botched, but she does not know that she knows — K fails to iterate while holding. (6) Farid glances at the departures board, forms the belief that the train leaves at 10:07, and it does; he knows, and he knows that he knows.

The trouble, as I see it, is that my verdicts pull in two directions at once. Cases 1 and 3 are both clear, non-marginal occurrent mental states — Cara is no more borderline than Anya — yet I give luminosity in one and withhold it in the other; and cases 5 and 6 are both clear, well-grounded true beliefs, yet I withhold iteration in one and grant it in the other. The principle I have been toying with, to be tested rather than trusted, is a double margin-for-error view: for any subject x and condition C, x is in a position to know that C obtains only if (a) C is not marginal for x — x would still be in C under small perturbations of the underlying conditions; (b) x's capacity for self-ascription of C is unimpaired at the time, where "impaired" covers both impairment by the state itself, as with Dev, and motivated distortion, as with Cara; and (c) for iteration, the margin must itself be non-marginal — x must be able to tell that her grounds discriminate C from the nearest cases in which C does not obtain, which is what Elena loses and Farid has. I suspect this is both too strong and too weak, and I would rather see it repaired than defended. There is also a case I cannot settle: (7) Mira has been told by her therapist, repeatedly, that she is angry with her mother; she has come to believe it sincerely, and she is angry. Does she know that she is angry? My instinct says yes, but that it is not knowledge "from the inside", and I cannot decide whether a clause about the source of the self-ascription belongs in the principle or whether that is a separate question entirely.

What I would like from you is reflective equilibrium done on request. State one or two candidate principles precisely enough to be applied mechanically, and for each clause name the case that motivates it; apply the result to Mira and to at least one further case of your own construction that I have not listed; and tell me honestly which of my verdicts your best candidate cannot accommodate — where I should expect to choose between a principle complex enough to save all seven and a simpler one that forces me to overturn one, say which pair is genuinely unreconcilable and which side you would take. I am not asking for literature: I can find that myself, and I would rather you reasoned from the cases. Please also flag how confident you are in each substantive claim and single out the point you are least sure of, since I plan to put your answer in front of the seminar and would like them to see where the pressure really lies. Kindly write in British English.
