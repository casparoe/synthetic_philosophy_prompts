In three weeks I am presenting a paper on the Identity of Indiscernibles to a philosophy-of-physics seminar, and what I want from you is a preparation sheet for the question period, not a defense of the view.

The thesis. The standard way of staging this debate — hunting for some property or relation that discerns the two spheres in Black's otherwise empty, perfectly symmetrical universe — mislocates the issue. What makes a counterexample possible is not qualitative similarity but symmetry. Say that a world is symmetric when its total qualitative structure admits a nontrivial automorphism: a permutation of its domain, other than the identity, that preserves every natural property and relation. My claim is that a world refutes the Identity of Indiscernibles exactly when it is symmetric in this sense, and I defend two corollaries of that diagnosis.

First, the fixed-point corollary. The offending automorphism must actually swap the objects at issue and must not be blocked by anything else in the world. That is why Black's original arrangement works while the obvious variants do not: put a third, asymmetrically placed body into the universe and the spheres are discerned by their relations to it; there is no longer an automorphism that moves them while holding everything else fixed.

Second, the embedded-observer corollary. Any world containing a situated observer whose own position and internal state are not invariant under the swap is ipso facto not a counterexample, because her situation supplies an indexical discerner — she can always mean the sphere on her left. So a genuine counterexample must be a perspectiveless world: one in which every possible inhabitant either sits on the symmetry plane or is doubled into a mirror twin whose every state is likewise reflected.

The payoff, which is the part I expect to be fought over: on this diagnosis the Identity of Indiscernibles is a principle whose failure is in principle unobservable from inside any world in which it fails. If it fails in ours, no measurement, no observation, and no indexical thought could reveal that it does. So I conclude that the principle is not a necessary metaphysical grounding claim that has finally been refuted; it is idle — its truth and its falsity alike outrun anything that could make a difference — and the search for ever more exotic discerners is ad hoc repair work on a principle that should simply be dropped.

The audience. About twelve people and roughly forty minutes of questions after a twenty-five minute paper. Three are philosophers of physics who work on quantum indistinguishability and symmetry: they know permutation invariance, the symmetrization postulate, occupation-number descriptions of many-particle states, and the absolute/relative/weak discernibility hierarchy, and they will be impatient with anything that looks like that hierarchy restated in new notation. Two are metaphysicians, anti-haecceitist and broadly Lewisian about recombination, who take the two-sphere case to be settled and think the live question is whether cardinality supervenes on qualitative character. Four are working physicists — condensed matter and AMO — who treat identical particles as a matter of untrackability and label-free bookkeeping, and whose first question about any distinction will be what observable difference it makes. The rest are graduate students who will ask whatever the senior people signal is safe to ask.

What I want: eight or so objections, ordered by how much damage they would do rather than by how likely they are, each tagged with who raises it and phrased the way that person would phrase it — in the physicists' idiom where it belongs to them, in the supervenience idiom where it belongs to the metaphysicians. For each, give me a reply I could deliver in about two minutes, a note on where that reply is soft, and a verdict on whether I should concede the point outright, concede it partially, or hold. Then tell me what I should concede in the first two slides, before anyone can take it from me; which single reply has to land for the paper to survive at all; and the one question I am praying nobody asks. My own suspicion is that it is the objection from the empirical content of permutation symmetry — that indistinguishability is not the unobservable, merely metaphysical affair I make it out to be, since symmetrizing states of identical particles changes the counting of states, fixes the Gibbs paradox, and puts interference terms into scattering cross-sections. Tell me whether that really is the worst question in the room, or whether I am overrating it and something in the metaphysicians' corner cuts deeper.
