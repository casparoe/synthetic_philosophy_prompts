I teach a graduate seminar on medieval political theory, and last term a colleague from the law school sat in on the session on tyranny and afterward argued that the whole tyrannicide material is anachronistic costume drama: that Aquinas's treatment of sedition and his treatise on kingship give us no purchase on anything anyone now actually has to decide. I think he is half right, and I want to test the other half by trying to make the material do real work. The occasion is a practical one. I sit on the advisory panel of a small foundation that funds civic-resistance and human-rights organizations, and its board — two lawyers, a former ambassador, a retired aid worker, and two donors, no philosophers among them — has asked me for a written screening procedure it can run on funding requests that involve support for the killing of a head of state or of a ruler's inner circle. They meet for half a day once a month, they see dozens of requests, they cannot read Latin or Aquinas, and they need something they can apply in a single afternoon without summoning me: an ordered sequence of questions, a specification of what counts as a sufficient answer to each, an explicit statement of where the procedure terminates in a no and where it hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts, and a note at each step about whether that step is a genuine test or a judgment call wearing procedural clothing.

What I want from you is that procedure, built as an operationalization of the Thomistic account rather than a summary of it. Please be explicit about three interpretive decisions you have to make in order to build it at all. First: whether the familiar cautions — that the community should not act rashly, that the remedy may turn out worse than the disease — are constraints on permissibility or only on prudence; the entire shape of the checklist depends on whether a grim expected outcome blocks the action or merely counts against it in deliberation. Second: who may authorize. Aquinas's requirements appear to presuppose a public authority competent to speak for the political community, and the cases my board sees are precisely ones in which that authority has been hollowed out, so the procedure needs a test for what I have started calling the proxy-authority problem: when a rump parliament sitting abroad, a federation of trade unions, a traditional council, or a broad-based popular assembly may stand in for a functioning public authority, together with an epistemic threshold, given that in most of these cases the ruler still commands the genuine allegiance of a substantial minority of the population. Third: the account seems to presuppose an individual ruler, and I want to know what the procedure does with depersonalized or distributed tyranny — a junta, a standing committee, a coercive apparatus administered partly through automated surveillance and fronted by a figurehead — including whether killing a subordinate who actually wields the coercive power is the same kind of act, for these purposes, as killing the titular ruler. I would also like the procedure to take a position on a title-forfeiture question my seminar got stuck on: how a ruler who came to office with a genuine title but has since gutted the institutions that conferred it is to be classified, whether the standard contrast between defect of title and defect in the exercise of rule has anything useful to say about that decay over time, and whether there is any threshold at which a once-lawful ruler becomes, for the purposes of the procedure, a usurper.

Then run the procedure on three cases, and do not soften the third. (1) A president elected ten years ago in an internationally credited election who has since packed the courts, abolished term limits through a compliant legislature, and presides over security services credibly implicated in roughly a thousand killings; a coalition that includes the surviving caucus of the pre-purge parliament, two national unions, and a religious council has publicly declared him a tyrant and has asked for support for an operation to kill him. (2) A six-month-old junta that dissolved parliament and is shooting peaceful protesters, where a rump of the previous parliament sitting abroad claims to speak for the country and has requested a strike against the junta's leader. (3) A long-entrenched and genuinely atrocious ruler — the facts about the mass atrocities are not in doubt — where the only feasible means of killing him is a strike carried out by the intelligence service of a self-interested regional power, the predictable sequel is a succession war between two militias, and the ruler's own minority community is likely to be massacred in the reprisals that follow; my board will want to say yes to this one, and I suspect that a procedure honestly derived from the material should say no. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: where Aquinas's commitments — about the common good as ordered rule rather than as a set of individual rights, about the moral significance of killing itself, about who may licitly kill for a public cause — come apart from the human-rights baseline my board actually holds, and which of those gaps would have to be closed by an act of decision rather than by further argument.
