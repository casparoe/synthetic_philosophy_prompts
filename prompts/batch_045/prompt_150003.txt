I'm a fourth-year PhD student in urban planning with a secondary field in political theory, and I've been handed one 90-minute session of a graduate seminar I'm co-teaching on property, place, and political membership — 18 students, mostly planning and policy master's students, plus four law students who reflexively turn every question into a question about standing doctrines and two philosophy MA students who keep trying to argue about whether "the public" is anything over and above the people who happen to show up. I have to keep the session structured rather than discussion-led, and I'd rather the students run into the central problem than have me name it, so I'd like you to design a simulation game for me: roles, rules, materials I can prep in an afternoon, a payoff scheme in points rather than money, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief.

The thing I want them to feel is this: every land-use decision has to first settle who counts as a member of the relevant public — which residents, which radius, which time horizon — and that procedural decision does most of the substantive work, yet nobody in the room can settle it disinterestedly, because they already know where they stand. Concretely, I'm imagining a fictional neighborhood (say a two-street corridor with about two dozen parcels, a church parking lot, a strip of single-family homes, a few small apartment buildings, and a shuttered retail site) and a proposal for a mid-rise building with some number of income-restricted units; I'm happy to take your numbers. What I want built into the mechanics, if you can do it, is: a stage where the class must vote on the *standing gradient* itself — how much say a household gets at 100 feet, 500 feet, a mile, and nowhere near — before anyone is told which parcel they occupy or whether they own or rent there, so they're choosing the demos under a partial veil of ignorance; then a vote on the project proper with secret objective cards; then a rule that anyone who votes to block must discharge a *placement obligation*, naming an alternative site in the city that should take the units instead, with each named district having a small absorption capacity that visibly runs out, so the group discovers on its own that "somewhere else" is not an available answer; then, without warning, a redistribution of position cards — owners become renters, long-time residents become recent arrivals, a small landlord becomes a tenant, and so on — followed by the same vote again, so each student can see whether their earlier vote tracked their position or a principle they'll still stand by. I'd also like three empty chairs with placards representing the households who would occupy the building but can't be in the room, and some shared dial on the board — a regional rent index, a displacement counter — that moves by small amounts for everyone whenever units get blocked, so the diffuse benefit registers mechanically even though weakly. Please don't let the words NIMBY or right to the city be spoken by the facilitator until the debrief; no lecture up front.

In the debrief I need the moments mapped onto the ideas: the gradient vote onto the boundary problem and the non-neutrality of any definition of the affected public; the placement obligation onto the difference between having a complaint and having authority over a place; the empty chairs onto the asymmetry between concentrated, identifiable costs and diffuse, unrepresented benefits; the reshuffle onto whatever it is that people are actually protecting when they object — use value, exchange value, neighborhood character as a positional good, or standing itself — and onto the gap between the inhabitant's claim and the owner's claim. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: the law students will try to litigate my fictional zoning code, someone will treat it as a puzzle to win rather than a case to judge, the person who draws the distant parcel will check out, at least one student is a landlord and another is facing a genuine rent increase, and the reshuffle will be gamed if anyone suspects it. And be frank about what the game structurally cannot reproduce and I'll have to say in so many words myself — the role of time and repetition, the fact that real hearings are decided partly by who has the Tuesday evening and the lawyer, the way state law fixes in advance what a city's "public" is for these purposes, and whatever you think is most badly flattened by compressing a multi-year process into an hour and a half. If it helps, I've also been asked to run a 40-minute version of this for a neighborhood association board next month; tell me which mechanic to cut and which to keep.
