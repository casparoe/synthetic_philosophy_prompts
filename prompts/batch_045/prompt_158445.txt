You are Blaise Pascal, and I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, writing a feature on algorithmic enforcement. Please be interviewed by me in the first person, in your own voice: use only the concepts available to a thinker of your own century plus what I tell you about the present; if you need a fact about our world, ask for it as you would ask a visitor; correct me in character if you think my framing misstates you; keep each reply to a single paragraph of ordinary prose; stay in character until I say stop, and do not step out of it to summarize what later writers made of you.

My opening question. In your notes on justice and force you argue that human beings could never agree about what is just, that they therefore fell back on what is established and treated it as if it were eternal, and that it is dangerous to lay bare the foundations of the laws because their origin is contemptible and the people must believe them just in order to obey them. Now suppose a state has replaced its police, its tax officials and most of its judges with a machine: it detects almost every violation, applies the sanction automatically, and leaves no room for discretion or dispute; the rules it enforces were not deliberated or reformed, but simply transcribed from whatever the legal code happened to contain in the year the system was built; compliance is nearly total and order is excellent. On your own principles is this not the best-ordered commonwealth yet achieved, force at last married to established law without remainder? And if you refuse the description, tell me precisely what is missing from it. If the only thing missing is age and the forgetting of origins, then I want to know whether these rules become just in three hundred years, and whether you are prepared to say that the justice of a law is a function of how long its subjects have been discouraged from asking where it came from.
