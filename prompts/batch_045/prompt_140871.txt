In both the Nicomachean Ethics and the Politics Aristotle discusses money and exchange. (a) Explain why, on his account, money comes to exist and what functions it performs. (b) Using the example of a builder and a shoemaker, explain how money makes justice in exchange possible: is the just return supposed to be an equal amount of product, or is it something else? (c) Aristotle criticizes certain ways of acquiring wealth. Distinguish the natural acquisition of goods for the household from the unlimited wealth-getting he calls chrematistics, and explain why he thinks retail trade and especially lending at interest (usury) are unnatural. (d) Finally, does Aristotle hold that the value of a product is determined by the amount of labor that went into producing it? Say briefly why or why not.

And here is the student's answer:

Money comes into being because barter is inconvenient: in a small household you can swap goods you need, but once the community gets bigger you cannot easily carry your surplus around and wait for someone who happens to want exactly what you have. So people agree by convention (not by nature) to use a single thing, usually a metal like silver, as a medium and measure. This is money. Its main job is to make goods commensurable: a house and a pair of shoes are very different, but money lets us assign them a common measure so they can be compared. Because of this, money lets builders and shoemakers do proportionate reciprocity: the builder and the shoemaker exchange their products through the medium of money, and justice in exchange means they get back an equal value in return — that is, the house is worth, say, ten shoes, so if the builder gives a house he must get back ten shoes (or their money equivalent) in order for the return to be equal, which for Aristotle is a matter of arithmetic equality of value. Money also has to be a stable measure, so Aristotle thinks the value of goods is really fixed by the labor that produced them, with money only expressing that underlying substance; this is why Marx later praised Aristotle for coming close to a labor theory of value, even though Greek society's reliance on slavery stopped him from seeing it clearly.

On wealth: Aristotle distinguishes household management (oikonomia), which is the natural acquisition of the goods the household and city need for a good life, from chrematistics, the art of getting wealth. Natural acquisition is limited: you stop when you have enough to live well. Chrematistics is unnatural because it aims at limitless wealth and treats money as an end rather than as a means to use. Retail trade is the clearest example: the shopkeeper buys and sells not to use the goods but to make a profit from the exchange itself, so Aristotle censures it as unnatural. The worst form is usury, lending money at interest, because money was meant to be used in exchange and is barren — it cannot produce offspring — so making money breed money is the most unnatural of all. Still, Aristotle concedes that some exchange is necessary for the self-sufficiency of the city, so money itself has a legitimate role as a medium of exchange, even if the limitless pursuit of wealth is corrupting.
