Write a satirical white paper, 600–750 words, in the deadpan voice of a senior civil servant: an internal consultation document issued by a fictional government body, the Directorate of Moral Cohesion, charged with identifying and enforcing society's shared morality. Format it as numbered sections, with a dry summary of consultation responses and one footnote dissent from an invented staff sociologist.

The target is legal moralism in the form it takes in the Hart–Devlin exchange: the claim that a society is held together by a common moral code, that a real shift in that code risks social disintegration, and that a legislature may therefore criminalize conduct the shared morality condemns even where no one is harmed. The comedy must come from taking that apparatus seriously as an administrative program rather than from sneering at it. Devlin's disintegration claim is an empirical prediction about what holds a society together, so the Directorate has to operationalize and measure it, and the measurement is where the machinery seizes.

Build the piece around four administrative headaches. First, the identification problem: turning a common morality into survey data requires deciding whose conviction counts, how intensely it must be felt, and how to tell a shared morality from a bare majority or from the loudest organized minority. Second, the benchmark standard: the Directorate still calibrates intensity of moral feeling against its panel of reasonable persons, recruited for decades from one South London bus route that was rerouted years ago, leaving a self-selected rump of residents near the old stops. Third, the feedback loop: annual attitude surveys, public information campaigns, and prosecutions are themselves interventions in the moral culture, so the Directorate enforces a consensus its own procedures helped manufacture, and cannot say what the code would have been without them. Fourth, the reductio, which should arrive as a routine recommendation: the latest wave of the Disintegration Index shows a large majority now endorsing live-and-let-live, which under the Directorate's founding statute obliges it to criminalize tolerance in order to preserve the shared morality, and it proposes to do so.

Keep the narrator entirely straight: no winking, no editorializing, no breaking character, no acknowledgment that anything has gone wrong. Do not attribute invented claims to real thinkers, and do not quote from any real text; the humor should be internal to the logic, so that a reader who knows the debate recognizes the objections to legal moralism being taken seriously and run to their conclusion.
