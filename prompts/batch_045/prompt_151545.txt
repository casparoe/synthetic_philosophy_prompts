I write on behalf of a small reading group of retired scientists — mostly physicists, with one botanist and myself — which meets monthly to worry at philosophical questions we encountered too late in life to study properly. My own training was in X-ray crystallography: thirty-odd years of inferring structure from diffraction patterns, and, latterly, of watching order emerge from nothing but local interactions. The group has asked for an essay, and I should be grateful if you would write it.

The topic is the MU puzzle. I restate it so that nothing hangs upon the accuracy of my description. Take the alphabet consisting of M, I and U; take the initial string MI; and permit four rules: (i) a string ending in I may have U appended to it; (ii) everything following the initial M may be duplicated; (iii) any occurrence of III may be replaced by U; (iv) any occurrence of UU may be deleted. The question is whether MU is derivable. It is not. The customary way of seeing this is not to search for a derivation but to notice an invariant: let n be the number of I's in a string; rules (i) and (iv) leave n unchanged, rule (iii) reduces it by three, and rule (ii) doubles it. Since n begins at one, and neither doubling nor subtracting three can turn a number that is not a multiple of three into one that is, n is never a multiple of three. But MU contains no I's at all, and zero is a multiple of three; so MU is unreachable. The moral usually drawn — the jumping out of the system that Hofstadter made much of — is that the decisive reasoning was about the system rather than within it.

My question is whether that moral bears any metaphysical weight, and in particular whether it supports the anti-mechanist argument familiar from Lucas and, more recently, Penrose. I should like the argument put in a sharpened form of my own devising, since the versions in circulation lean on Gödel sentences and, I think, conceal what is doing the work. Fix any formal system S, sound for the modest arithmetic needed below. Because string-rewriting can simulate any mechanical procedure, one can write down — tediously but explicitly, given a description of S — a finite puzzle of just the MU kind, with its own alphabet, initial string and handful of rules, arranged so that the target string is derivable if and only if S proves a contradiction. Then the correct answer to the puzzle is no precisely when S is consistent. If we can see that S is sound, we can see that the answer is no; but S itself, being sound, cannot establish its own consistency, so S cannot settle the puzzle. And the construction iterates: given any system, there is a harder puzzle. The conclusion drawn is that no machine captures the human mind, since for every machine there is a puzzle we can answer and it cannot.

Three lines of reply occur to me, and I want them adjudicated rather than merely catalogued. First, the deflationary one: jumping out is simply the ordinary practice of reasoning at the metalevel, the outside of one system being the inside of another; the search for invariants is itself mechanical in character, and the iterative climbing can in principle be mechanised too, by iterating consistency extensions and even continuing that iteration along constructive ordinal notations. On this view the gap is between a fixed system and an open-ended procedure, which is no argument against mechanism at all. Secondly, the objection from self-ascertainment: the argument requires me to know which system I am, yet if I am a machine there is no reason to expect that I can produce a correct description of my own workings, still less certify their soundness; so the argument may at best establish an incapacity for self-knowledge. Thirdly, the objection from idealisation: we can see that S is sound only for toy systems, and for any serious candidate formalisation of a working mathematician the soundness claim is itself a conjecture about which mathematicians err; and the we that supposedly outruns every machine may be not a mind but an open-ended historical process.

Please write an essay of some twelve hundred to fifteen hundred words, in a register suitable for my reading group — no jargon without explanation — which does the following. Say plainly whether the parametrised puzzle argument gives any support to anti-mechanism, and if it does, state exactly which premise carries the load and defend that premise against the strongest objection. Take a position on whether jumping out of the system names one cognitive feat or several: I suspect the phrase conflates at least noticing an invariant of an object-system, reflecting upon one's own procedure, and altering the rules of the game, and that these differ in how threatening they are to mechanism. Then address, in the form in which a defender of the opposing view would actually press it, the objection you take to be strongest. Reach a verdict and hold to it; do not close by remarking that both sides have a point or that the matter awaits further work.

Two requests of housekeeping. I shall not be checking references, and the group has no interest in who said what; argue from the structure of the case and let no claim rest on a citation. And you may take my own prejudice as a target: a career spent watching collective order arise from local interactions has left me with the instinct that levels of description carry no metaphysical weight, so that talk of the mind's transcending mechanism is either a platitude about levels or an unexplained mystery. I should be glad to have that instinct vindicated; I should be gladder to have it dislodged.
