yo, I'm trying to write up the chapter on naturalizing mental content and I keep losing the thread, so I want the spec before I read any more of it. Give me the list of requirements that any naturalistic theory of mental content has to meet to count as adequate — where naturalistic means it has to build content out of materials that aren't themselves intentional or semantic (causation, function, selection history, information, whatever it wants to use), and where adequate means adequate for one specific job: grounding actual content attributions in empirical cognitive science, i.e. being the thing a working scientist could lean on when she says what some mechanism in some organism represents. Not adequate for winning a metaphysics seminar.

For each requirement I want the case or consideration behind it — the concrete situation a theory that ignored that requirement would get wrong. At minimum grapple with: how a state can be about something and be wrong about it; how content manages to be about the distal thing rather than the proximal stimulation or some gerrymandered disjunction of causes; whether content has to come with correctness conditions at all and where a naturalistic story is supposed to get normativity from; how a creature represents something it has never encountered, and things that don't exist; what to say about a state that tracks something perfectly but that nothing downstream consumes and that nothing ever selected for; and whether content has to make a difference to what the system does or can just sit there doing no work.

Two wrinkles I basically never see handled, so don't just hand me the standard list:

1. Systems with no evolutionary history but a training history. Think of a model whose internal units get shaped by repeated rounds of a proxy objective, with a selection-ish filter on top — variants kept when the score improves, thrown away when it doesn't — but no reproduction, no lineage, no consequences for anything that could be called an organism. If a theory of content is going to be usable on those at all, what does it have to say, and what would have to be true of the training setup for one of those units to count as meaning something rather than merely being correlated? Turn that into a requirement.

2. The reuse case. A mechanism that got whatever function it has from one kind of historical pressure and is now, reliably and usefully, doing something quite different. Does the content follow the old history or the current job? Whatever you think, state the requirement that your answer forces onto the theory — I care more about the requirement than about the verdict.

Then: mark each requirement as non-negotiable versus a preference of one particular camp. Flag any pair you suspect can't be jointly satisfied, with the case that shows the clash — that's the part I'm most interested in, since if the list is internally inconsistent then that itself is the finding. And tell me which single requirement excludes the most candidate theories, and why it has that much force even though it sounds modest.

A short scorecard at the end is fine — the two or three live families of approach (covariation/information-based, history-and-function-based, use or role-based), a line or two each on where they crack, no names and no citations, I've got the literature labels. Skip it if you think it'd be a distraction; the requirements and their motivating cases are the thing I actually want.
