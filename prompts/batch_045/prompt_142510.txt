A first-year recieved this from a popular science newsletter he subscribes to and forwarded it to me after our lecture on scientific realism:

"Quantum mechanics only ever hands us probabilities, so for the better part of a century physicists argued about whether the wavefunction describes reality or only our ignorance of it. Einstein took the second view: he thought the wavefunction was a statistical description of an ensemble, more or less like a probability distribution over coin flips, and that some deeper theory would eventually tell us what the particles are actually doing. Then in the 1960s Bell showed that no hidden-variable theory can reproduce the quantum predictions, which killed that hope. The final nail arrived more recently: Pusey, Barrett and Rudolph proved that the wavefunction cannot merely be a summary of what an observer knows — provided two systems can be prepared independently of one another, the mathematics forces the quantum state to be a real physical thing. So the epistemic view of the wavefunction is dead. What this really means is that the wavefunction is a physical field, spread out over a space of enormously many dimensions, and that every possible outcome of a measurement actually happens somewhere. Copenhagen-style talk of the wavefunction collapsing on measurement has to be read as a genuine physical process, not just a change in what we know."
