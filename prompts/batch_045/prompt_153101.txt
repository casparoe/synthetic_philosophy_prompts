The view I have converged on about the badness of pain is this: what makes an episode of pain bad for the person who has it is not its sensory character — the intensity, the location, the stabbing-versus-burning profile — but its aversiveness: the subject's being, in the midst of the experience, braced against it, wanting it to stop, recoil-ready. Strip away the aversiveness and you are left not with a milder pain but with a state that is axiologically inert, however vivid and informative its sensory profile remains.

My principal support is pain asymbolia, on the standard clinical picture: patients who localize and discriminate noxious stimuli, rate intensity, distinguish sharp from dull, and yet report no unpleasantness, show no distress, and neither withdraw nor complain. If the badness of pain were seated in the sensory-discriminative character, or in what the experience represents about the body, the asymbolic subject's state should still be bad for her; it seems not to be. I hold a subsidiary claim less confidently: that aversiveness is itself a conative state rather than a further sensory quality, so that the badness of pain is not discovered in the experience but conferred by the subject's stance toward it.

What I want is an unsentimental map of how much this package risks. Please press hardest on the following, and on anything I have not thought of.

First, whether my reading of asymbolia does work it cannot bear: the patients may retain an aversive dimension they are unable to report, or the lesion may disable the very capacity whose absence I am treating as evidence — in which case the case shows only that a measuring instrument is missing, not that the measured quantity is gone. Say how I would tell these apart.

Second, the dilemma I may be caught in: if the asymbolic subject is genuinely still in pain, then badness may be internal to pain after all; if she is not, then my central case is not a case of pain and supports nothing. Is there a third option?

Third, the mirror cases, where aversion is absent or mixed with attraction while pain seems present and bad — pain sought out or savored, pain undergone for a goal, itch-scratching, the burn of exertion. Does my view have to deny badness there, and is that a cost or a result?

Fourth, a case I invented to test the view: suppose a reversible intervention in a healthy subject selectively blocks aversiveness while leaving sensory discrimination intact, and that subject, fully informed and with the intervention available, declines to have it lifted. Does my account entail that her pain is not bad for her, and should I accept that or treat it as a reductio?

Fifth, the third-personal question: if badness tracks the subject's actual stance, does the view still underwrite reasons for others to prevent her pain, or does it make those reasons hostage to her adaptation and acquiescence?

For each line of pressure, tell me whether it is something I should concede as a real cost, something a defender is entitled to dismiss, or something I should answer by refining the view. I especially want to know whether the appeal to a conative stance has insulated the account from correction — whether any possible pattern of report and behavior could now count against it — and whether that insulation, if it exists, is a defect here or an acceptable feature of a view about the constitutive basis of value. Conclude by telling me which single consideration you would press if you wanted me to abandon the view, and why.
