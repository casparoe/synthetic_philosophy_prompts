Grading a batch of exam answers and I'd like a second read on this one.

The question:

Q. A Buddhist consequentialist holds that an act is right iff its total consequences are at least as good as those of the alternatives, where the consequences include both (i) outward consequences, i.e. effects on the welfare of sentient beings, and (ii) inward consequences, i.e. the act's karmic fruit: its effects on the agent's own future dispositions, whether greed, hatred and delusion get strengthened or weakened, which then cause further effects downstream. Assume, with the tradition, that karma is a matter of intention: the fruit an act bears is fixed by the intention it is done with. Call a theory self-effacing when the best consequences are produced by agents who do not act on it, who neither calculate totals nor aim at their own karmic fruit (aiming at the fruit, plausibly, corrupts the intention and so worsens it).

(a) Objection: if the fruit is fixed by the intention, then ranking acts by their fruit is just ranking them by intention, so adding karmic fruit to the consequence set collapses Buddhist consequentialism into a pure ethics of motives. Is the objection right?
(b) If Buddhist consequentialism is self-effacing, is that a problem for the theory, and if so what kind of problem?

The student's answer:

(a) The objection is right, and it shows that calling this view consequentialist is a mistake. If the karmic fruit of an act is fixed by the intention it is done with, then the act with the best consequences just is the act done with the best intention, so consequences do no independent work and rightness is settled by the motive. And where the two come apart the tradition sides with the motive anyway: an act done from genuine compassion that happens to have poor outward effects is still karmically good. So this is an ethics of motives, and the consequentialist apparatus is decorative.

(b) Self-effacement is worse than it looks. If trying to produce the best consequences itself makes the consequences worse, the theory is self-defeating: it tells you to maximize and then tells you that maximizing fails. A theory that instructs you not to use it cannot be a theory of right action; at best it is advice about what not to think about. Buddhist consequentialism therefore collapses into a kind of quietism, and we would do better to read the tradition as a virtue ethics concerned with character rather than with consequences.

No-self points the same way. If there is no persisting agent, then whoever receives the karmic fruit is not the person who acted, so inward consequences are really just outward consequences for someone else and the distinction between (i) and (ii) does no work. Buddhist consequentialism must therefore be strictly agent-neutral: no special weight on your own cultivation, and no reason to work on your own character rather than a stranger's.
