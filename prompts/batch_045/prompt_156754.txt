I convene a small fortnightly reading group on decision theory — mostly graduate students, plus two philosophers who disagree with each other professionally — and we keep stalling on Newcomb's problem for the same reason: people arrive with tribal self-descriptions (they call themselves one-boxers or two-boxers) and then talk past each other, when what actually separates them is a handful of prior commitments they have never stated. What I want is a diagnostic instrument rather than a literature survey: a decision tree that starts from the bare case (two boxes, a predictor with a long track record who has already filled the opaque one, take the opaque box alone or both) and sorts a person by what they say about cases and counterfactuals, telling them which position they hold only at the leaf.

Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of -isms: first the questions, then the combinations they permit, then the actual positions located inside that space. I want six or seven branch questions, each phrased so that a smart newcomer could answer it without knowing what it leads to, and none of which may simply ask whether you would take one box or two. At least three of the nodes must be variant cases rather than abstract claims, and I would like these three included: the simulation variant, in which the prediction is produced by running an exact simulation of you in this very situation and recording its answer; the copy variant, otherwise the same, except that you are told you do not know whether you are the original or the simulation — if you are the simulation, your answer is recorded, the real opaque box is filled to match, and you are then shut down with no payout; and the open-prediction variant, in which the prediction is shown to you before you choose. Ten to fourteen leaves, and the following must appear among them: dominance-style causal two-boxing; evidential one-boxing; ratifiability-style or otherwise sophisticated causal one-boxing; algorithmic or timeless one-boxing, on which your decision procedure and the prediction are two instantiations of the same computation; the resolute-choice or pre-commitment view, on which you should have paid in advance to become a one-boxer; and an exit leaf for the thought that the case as stated is under-specified or metaphysically impossible and so yields no verdict. I also want at least three cells of the space left explicitly empty, each with a one-line diagnosis of why nobody occupies it — incoherent, merely unattractive, or underexplored — and I want you to flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot in principle separate rather than pretending the branch that was supposed to separate them does real work.

Then run me through it. My own answers, phrased as commitments: I deny that the match between the simulation's answer and mine is a causal chain in either direction, and prefer to say it is the same computation run twice; I take the object of evaluation to be the procedure I am running rather than this token act; in the copy variant I would one-box whether I turned out to be the original or the simulation, because I do not think which room I am in changes what I should do; in the open-prediction variant I would take both boxes; I accept that I have no causal influence at all on the contents of the opaque box and do not think that settles the question; I think it would be rationally required, not merely lucky, to have paid in advance to be made a one-boxer; and I do not take the under-specification exit, since I see no incompatibility between a perfect predictor and my genuinely deliberating. Where your nodes do not line up with these, interpolate as best you can. Tell me which leaf I land on, and if my combination is unoccupied or has no settled name, say so and coin a label for it. Please mark how confident you are in the overall classification and single out the one judgment you are least sure of — a node whose placement seems to you most likely wrong, or a leaf you suspect does not really belong where you have put it. End with a single final line of exactly the form \answer{name of my leaf} and nothing after it.
