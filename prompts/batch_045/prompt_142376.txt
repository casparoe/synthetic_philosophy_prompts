I have been reading, as an amateur, a little of what philosophers say about our emotional responses to stories, and I keep running into something I cannot find discussed. I am after a name for it if there is one, but more than a name: I want to know what exactly it is, what it is not, and where its edges are.

It is not the puzzle people usually raise about fiction — the one about how we can be frightened or grieved by characters we know were invented, given that fear and grief seem to require belief that their objects exist. I am entirely convinced that the objects do not exist; that is not my difficulty. My difficulty is about values, not existence.

Three cases.

First: I watch a revenge film. What the protagonist is doing is something I would condemn without hesitation if I read about it in a newspaper, and I know while watching that it is wrong. Yet I want him to succeed. I notice myself leaning forward, attending to whether he will arrive in time, feeling the tightening that goes with wanting an outcome. The wanting is not make-believe wanting; it takes my attention the way real wanting does. And afterwards I do not feel that I have compromised myself, or that I have done anything I should apologise for. It is as if my emotions were being run on a set of values I do not hold.

Second: I come across effectively the same sequence of events in two works, presented in one as a triumph and in the other as a catastrophe. My response reverses completely between them, and the only thing that has changed is the presentation. I take that presentation to be no reason whatsoever for any belief about the events — it is merely a way of showing them — and yet my response tracks it exactly, and would track it again if I reread.

Third: a scene that a work plainly wants me to find moving leaves me cold. Here I am inclined to speak of a failure, but the failure does not seem to be located in any fixed place: sometimes I say I have missed something or have read badly, sometimes I say the work has not earned it. Notice that this case has a normative structure the first two lack — there is blame going round — and I cannot tell whether that makes it the same phenomenon seen from the other side or a different one.

What I would like to know: is there an established concept here? I have encountered the phrase *suspension of disbelief*, but as I understand it that concerns whether one takes the events to be real, and I have no trouble not taking them to be real. I also have a vague impression that there is a literature about cases where a reader cannot go along with a story, where the story asks them to regard as fine something they regard as monstrous and they balk; but my first case is the opposite of balking, and my second involves no balking at all, so I suspect I am looking at something else, or at a different part of the same territory.

So, more precisely: are the first two cases one phenomenon? Is the third the same phenomenon inverted, or a distinct one? What is the object of the wanting in the first case — the character, the events, or the way they are being shown? Does it matter whether I endorse the values in question, or is endorsing them simply one further instance of the same mechanism? Does the phenomenon extend to non-fictional framings — a friend telling an anecdote with an unmistakable editorial spin, an advertisement, a stylised hypothetical in an ethics seminar, where I feel outrage I would not defend? And does it extend to framings I have produced myself?

A label alone would not settle any of this for me. I would rather have a definition with boundary cases, and if no established term covers the thing I have described, then a coined one together with an account of what it excludes.
