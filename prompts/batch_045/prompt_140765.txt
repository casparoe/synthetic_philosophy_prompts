I convene a fortnightly reading group for graduate students in economics and philosophy on formal epistemology, and next week's session is on Aumann's agreement theorem: agents who start from a common prior and have common knowledge of each other's posteriors cannot agree to disagree. I want the second half of the session to be a single real case rather than another toy urn example, and the case I keep returning to is the reaction of British academic economists to the budget Geoffrey Howe delivered on 10 March 1981 — a budget that raised net taxes by roughly £4 billion at 1981 prices, at a moment when inflation was running near 15 per cent and unemployment had risen by almost a million over the previous year. At the end of that month, 364 academic economists put their names to a statement sent to the government and to the press saying there was "no basis in economic theory or supporting evidence" for what the budget was doing and that it threatened Britain's "social and political stability". (The usual description of this as a letter to The Times is, I'm told, inaccurate: it was a statement circulated to academics and journalists rather than a published letter, a detail I take to matter a great deal here.) Only a handful of economists publicly backed the government — Patrick Minford and Alan Walters were the two Thatcher named in the Commons, and Minford called the statement a "dangerous and dishonest game" — while one signatory, later a member of the Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee, said he signed because it was "the only game in town". Within a year or so output was recovering, inflation was coming down, and unemployment was still climbing; the episode was re-litigated on its twenty-fifth anniversary in a 2006 Institute of Economic Affairs volume edited by Philip Booth. If any of those details are off, correct them quietly and carry on, but treat the broad shape as fixed.

So: work that episode through the theorem in both directions, and be disciplined about the mapping rather than impressionistic about it. I want you to say who the agents are before you say anything about what they should have done — is it the 364 individually, or the 364 as a corporate body whose "posterior" is the statement, or Howe and the Treasury, or Minford — and to say what the event is supposed to be, since the statement bundles at least four different kinds of claim: a forecast about output and jobs, a causal claim about fiscal contraction in a slump, a claim about the state of economic theory ("no basis in... theory"), and a claim about social and political stability. Ask honestly whether the theorem applies to each of those, whether agreement is even well defined across the bundle, and whether the conclusion would change if you re-described the event more coarsely or more finely. Then give me an explicit account of what each side's information partition plausibly contained and of what would have counted as a posterior on each side, given that almost nobody on either side published a number.

What I most want, though, is the diagnosis, and I already know the lazy version of it, so don't give me a list of the theorem's assumptions with "these probably failed" attached. Rank the candidate explanations — genuinely different priors, a shared prior but different model spaces so that the partitions aren't over the same states, no honest posterior report being made at all (a signature is cheap talk, and the "only game in town" remark is evidence of exactly that), motivated or non-Bayesian updating, or the statement being an expression of preference and political advocacy rather than of belief — by how much of the episode each actually explains, and tell me what would have to have been true for the antecedents to hold. Then run a counterfactual I've been toying with: suppose that instead of collecting signatures, someone had required each participant, on the government side as well as the academic side, to post a probability for a small set of pre-specified, publicly checkable outcomes over a fixed horizon, under a proper scoring rule with a modest financial stake attached. Would that mechanism restore common knowledge of posteriors, would it do anything about priors or about shared state spaces, and what would you expect to observe if it were run — convergence of the reported numbers, or merely an honest measurement of a gap that the theorem was never in the business of closing? Does the no-trade-flavoured corollary have any real work to do here, given that the two sides were in effect taking opposite sides of a reputational bet on the economy, or is that a false analogy? Finally, tell me what the episode shows about the theorem itself: whether a consistency constraint of this kind has any predictive bite about persistent real-world disagreement, whether the fact that the signatories were, crudely, on the wrong side of the forecast is evidence about priors or merely about luck, and where the theorem is simply silent — about how fast convergence would come, about which consensus you would converge to, about what to do when you discover you cannot afford to be honest. Aim for something in the region of 1,200 words, keep it tethered to the case rather than drifting into a general essay on Aumann or on 1980s British macroeconomics, and skip literature reviews and suggestions for further reading: I have no web access and don't want them.
