I teach an undergraduate seminar on democracy and collective choice in a politics department: 18 students, one 75-minute session, mixed preparation (roughly a fifth have had a little game theory, the rest none), a whiteboard and index cards but no projector. I want a runnable activity in which the students discover for themselves that they cannot design an aggregation rule under which nobody ever has an incentive to misreport their ranking. No opening lecture and no mention of the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem or its statement until the debrief.

Here is the setup I have in mind, which you should use or improve on. Round zero: each student privately ranks four genuinely live alternatives (say, four ways to spend a small leftover departmental budget for the class — a real, if trivial, stake), seals that ballot in an envelope as their sincere ranking, and the facilitator keeps it. Then three or four rounds in which the class proposes any rule they can state in under two minutes, votes under it, and sees the result. Between rounds they may rewrite the rule entirely. Each voter sees only the previous round's published tally of ballots, never who submitted what, and may vote however they like. The facilitator then computes, from the sealed sincere ballots, what the same rule would have produced had everyone voted sincerely, and the class sees who gained by misreporting and who lost by staying honest.

Three things matter to me. First, the last twenty minutes should be spent testing escape routes rather than describing them: cut the alternatives to two, or make preferences single-peaked along an ordering the class imposes, or select a ballot at random and take its top choice, or let people report intensities and compensate losers with side payments. Each should be tried on the spot so the class feels what it costs. Second, I want at least one round arranged so that sincere voting under the students' own rule picks an outcome the group agrees is worse than what misreporting would have produced, so they have to confront whether strategy-proofness is even a desideratum rather than assuming it is. Third, the debrief has to map moments onto the theorem's conditions — at least three alternatives, a single deterministic winner, respect for unanimity, no dictator, and an unrestricted domain of rankings — and be frank about what the activity cannot show: a handful of profiles is not a quantifier over all profiles, and success was measured against each student's own sincere ranking rather than against cardinal utility, so the proof itself has to be supplied in words.

Give me the activity as I would run it: materials, the exact scripts and tally sheets, the round-by-round timeline, the debrief, and an honest note on what will predictably go wrong (students treating round zero strategically, the real stake being too small to care about, someone proposing a rule nobody can compute by hand). Finally, tell me where you are least confident: flag any formal claim you are unsure of, particularly what strategy-proofness amounts to once the rule is allowed to randomize and how far the class of strategy-proof randomized rules extends beyond random dictatorship, which claim you would want a social choice theorist to check before I run this, and which part of the session you would pilot first.
