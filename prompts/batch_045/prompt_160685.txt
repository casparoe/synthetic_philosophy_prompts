I wanted a clean argument that rule-following contributes nothing at all to moral worth, using the banality-of-evil case as the lever. I got the conclusion I wanted and then noticed I'd also proved something I don't believe. Here it is.

Two gadgets, both my own definitions, so there's nothing to look up:

- **Deputy Test (D):** an action done in an official or otherwise rule-governed role has positive moral worth only if the agent would still perform that action — and would still refrain from the forbidden alternative — if the rule were repealed and no one could ever find out what she did. Rules are worth-neutral: they transmit no credit.
- **Inheritance (I):** any moral worth a compliant action has must be inherited from reasons the agent already had independently of the rule. Rules never *generate* reasons; they only point at reasons you antecedently had.

The argument:

1. D.
2. In the paradigm case — the mid-level functionary who books the trains, signs the form, applies the statute to the listed category — everything the agent offers as her reason is exhausted by "it is my assignment," "it is the rule," "it is lawful." Nothing survives the Deputy Test.
3. So the functionary's compliance has zero moral worth; the rule contributes nothing in her favour; her office and its procedures add no credit at all. (They also add no excuse, but that's the part everyone already grants.)
4. D and I say nothing about the content of the rule, the character of the regime, or the kind of role. Whatever makes the functionary's compliance worthless makes every compliant action's worth come from somewhere other than the rule too. So: no action's moral worth ever derives even in part from the fact that it accords with a rule, an order, a law, a promise, or a role. Call this Rule-Independence (RI).
5. But RI is false. There is something morally good in the judge who sentences the defendant she privately loathes *because* the statute requires it, in the anaesthetist who runs the pre-op checklist she'd quietly skip if left to her own judgement, in the person who keeps a promise she now has every self-interested reason to break precisely *because* she promised. And there is something morally bad in the caseworker who administers the statute selectively by her own lights and calls it conscience.
6. So D, or I, is false — and my argument for the conscience side collapses into "rules can be sources of moral reasons after all," which is more or less my interlocutor's position plus a footnote.
