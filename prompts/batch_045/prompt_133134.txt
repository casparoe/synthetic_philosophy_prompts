I sit on the program committee of a regional art prize, and a vendor is pitching us an automated pre-screening tool: a model trained on several hundred thousand ratings that a panel of museum curators and critics assigned to works in our categories. The pitch ends with an appeal to philosophy. One of the vendor's people sent me a transcript of a podcast episode on whether beauty is objective, in which the host summarizes Hume's essay on the standard of taste roughly as follows:

"I'll put it like this. Beauty is not a property of objects at all. It is a sentiment in us, and sentiments are not the sort of thing you can refute by argument: if a man tells you his coffee is bitter, there is nothing further to say to him. Asking what is really beautiful is no better a question than asking what is really sweet. But that does not mean every sincere reaction is self-certifying, and this is the part people forget. Hume gives us the figure of the true judge: someone with delicacy of taste, by which he means someone who feels aesthetic qualities more strongly than the rest of us; someone schooled by long practice and by comparing many works of the same kind; someone free of prejudice, meaning not swayed by who made the work or what it is fashionable to say about it; and someone with good sense. Where such critics agree, their joint verdict is the true standard of taste and beauty, so beauty turns out to be objective after all — objectively whatever the qualified feel. Where they disagree, the disagreement is noise introduced by defective judges, and the way to settle it is to poll enough qualified critics and take the majority: for every work there is a determinate correct verdict, and that is how you recover it. Note too that beauty is tied to our constitution; a creature with different sense organs would find different things beautiful. None of this is any longer impractical, and here is the interesting consequence. A model trained on the ratings of a large panel of curators meets Hume's conditions better than any human being can: it has been practiced on more works, it compares more of them, it has no prejudices, and its delicacy can be measured directly rather than taken on trust. On Hume's own principles, then, its output just is the joint verdict of the true judges, and we should defer to it."

I read philosophy for pleasure but not professionally, and I cannot tell what to trust here. The opening sounds like the Hume I half-remember; the closing is exactly what the vendor wants me to accept. I am suspicious of the middle and of the last step, but I cannot locate the precise mistake, and I would rather not wave the thing through because I lack the vocabulary to say no.

Please audit the passage claim by claim, numbered, in the order the host makes them. For each, tell me whether it is something Hume actually holds, a simplification a charitable commentator would tolerate, or a distortion he would reject — and where it is a distortion, say what he says instead and what a defender of the standard reading would object to. Three things I especially want separated out: whether delicacy of taste is being used in Hume's sense or in an everyday one; whether the joint verdict is the sort of thing you can recover by polling qualified critics alive today and taking the majority, and whether on Hume's account there is a determinate right answer for every work or only for some; and whether good sense is being left empty in a way that matters to the argument. Then take the last step on its own. The host himself grants that on Hume's view beauty is relative to the constitution of the creature doing the contemplating, so that a being with different sense organs would have a different taste. Say whether that concession undercuts the extension, whether a system that has no sentiments and only predicts the ratings of trained humans can satisfy a criterion built around delicacy, and whether such a model would be better described as a true judge or as a very efficient device for aggregating the verdicts of the judges in its training set.

Finally, tell me what the corrected Hume gives my committee: any reason to defer to such a tool, any reason to refuse it, and the one distinction I should press the vendor on if they keep using this argument. Argue it from the essay; I do not want a bibliography or a reading list, because I cannot act on either.

End with a single machine-readable line, and nothing after it:
