I work on policy at a mid-sized social platform and I've been given the delightful task of rewriting our internal guidance on slurs. Not a banned-word list — we have one of those and it's useless — but an actual explanation of why one flagged post is a violation and another that contains the exact same word isn't, written so that a reviewer with no philosophy training and thirty seconds per case can apply it. I keep reading papers in the semantics and pragmatics literature and coming away feeling like they were built for a totally different purpose than mine (explaining intuitions about reported speech in a seminar room, mostly), so before I read any more of them I want the spec. Please write me the desiderata: what an account of slurs would have to deliver to be adequate *for this job*, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, so I can hold the literature up against it instead of the other way round.

The phenomena it has to handle — and please use the messy versions, because these are the ones that actually show up in our queue: a slur used by a member of the targeted group in a song or a comment thread, and the same token surfaced by our recommender to an out-group listener; a slur inside negation, a question, the antecedent of a conditional, or an indirect report ('she said he called him the n-word'), where the reviewer has to decide whether derogation survived; a slur aimed at someone who isn't a member of the targeted group, used as a generic insult; a slur quoted for educational or documentary purposes, or appearing as a screenshot of a dictionary entry; a word that's a slur in one dialect or language and an ordinary term in another, or a borrowed word whose speakers genuinely don't know its valence; a respelled, emoji-substituted, or initialism version that most users read as the slur anyway; and — the one that broke my brain — a slur invented for a fictional group in a novel or game (a slur for androids, for a made-up fantasy race) that gets used in-character, plus the newer case where a bot or language model emits a real slur with nobody's intentions behind it at all. I'd also want requirements about the distinctions it can't collapse: slur versus plain pejorative or insult, slur versus taboo word, derogation versus offensiveness versus harmfulness, targeting a group versus targeting an individual, and being a slurring word versus being used as one. For each, say what breaks if a theory collapses it.

Then the part I actually care about: mark which requirements are non-negotiable for my purpose and which are just the prejudices of one camp (I don't need the theory to be elegant, or to keep slurs out of semantic content, or to preserve compositionality in any strong sense — but I do need it to be usable by someone who cannot know what the speaker was thinking, and to give stable answers across time as reclamation happens). Flag any pair of requirements you suspect can't be jointly satisfied — my guess is that 'explain why derogation tracks speaker endorsement or group membership' and 'be applicable without access to intentions or identity' pull apart, and I want to know if there are others. Tell me which single requirement does the most work in ruling out candidate theories. And only after all that, give me a short scorecard — a couple of sentences each, not a table of essays — for the main contenders as you'd characterize them by content: the expressivist view that slurs add a separate expressive or attitudinal component, the prohibition view that the ban itself does the work, conventional-implicature and presuppositional treatments, and any contextualist or reclamation-friendly view. Please write the whole thing in American English, and feel free to tell me if the honest conclusion is that no existing account is adequate for what I'm asking.
