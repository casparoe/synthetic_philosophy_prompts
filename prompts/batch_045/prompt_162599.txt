So — I'm a novelist, and I've accidently written a book about free will without meaning to. My protagonist Mira spends most of it trying to become someone who doesn't flinch, and by the end she mostly is: she acts well, and she doesn't have to fight herself anymore. My writting group won't shut up about whether she's 'free' at the end, and arguing with them has made me realise I'm confused about something fairly specific, and I'd like it explained rather than surveyed.

Here's the shape of it. There seem to be two different things I might want when I want free will for Mira, and they come apart in her case. One is a snapshot thing: right now, at the moment she acts, is the action suitably hers —coming out of her own machinery in the right way, responsive to reasons, not the product of a shove or a tumour or a stage hypnotist? By that standard, end-of-book Mira is a poster child. The other is historical: is she the author of the machinery itself — did she make herself into the person who doesn't flinch? And there the worry is obvious: everything that shaped her (a humiliation at nineteen, a book she happened to pick up, a friend who was kind at exactly the wrong moment) was itself unchosen, so her self-shaping looks like just one more link in a causal chain, and I've started calling that the residue problem.

What I can't get straight is this. The historical variety looks unsatisfiable in principle — any self-shaping bottoms out in something you didn't choose — but the snapshot variety looks too cheap. Imagine someone whose values have been quietly and gradually reshaped over years by a manipulator (a cult, a feed, whatever) in a way that leaves her perfectly coherent and perfectly reasons-responsive at every single step. She sails through the snapshot test and I still want to say she's been robbed of something. So: what is the historical variety actually asking for, if total sourcehood is off the table? Is it a degreed thing with a threshold, and what would set the threshold — or is it instead a demand about the *kind* of process, wich is a different animal entirely? And is the historical demand really a separate demand, or can the snapshot theorist just build the right sort of history into their condition and thereby swallow it — in which case does my original distinction evaporate?

One more, and it's the bit I actually care about as a writer. I get the sense that 'the free will worth wanting' is a slippery phrase, because sometimes it seems to mean 'the property we actually have and actually value' and sometimes 'the property that would justify praise, blame, resentment, the whole apparatus', and those feel like different questions with possibly different answers. If that's right, say so, and say why the slide between them is so tempting. If I'm wrong and it's really one question, tell me which one it is and why the other reading was an illusion.

Please don't give me a map of the literature — no positions-with-names, no who-argued-what, I can't follow up on any of that anyway. Just the conceptual mechanics, in plain language, a page or so. And no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables — continuous prose, the way you'd write to a friend.
