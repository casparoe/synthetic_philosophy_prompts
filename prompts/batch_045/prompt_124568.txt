I'm on my city's charter review commission — five of us, we meet in a basement room at the public library, and we owe the council ONE recommendation by the 14th. I've been voluntold to draft it and I'd rather start from something decent than from my own notes.

Here's the mess. There's a package of three changes on the table, for municipal elections only (not state, not federal):

1. Lower the voting age to 16.
2. Extend the franchise to all resident non-citizens who've lived in the city at least six months.
3. Make voting compulsory, enforced by a $25 fine waivable for "good cause."

Some facts about us. About 90,000 people. Turnout in the last municipal election was 11%; of the ballots cast, roughly two-thirds came from homeowners over 60, and that electorate went on to pass a 30-year stormwater bond and rezone the land by the community college. Non-citizen residents are maybe 6,000, around half of them undocumented, lots of them working at the food processing plant or with kids in district schools. There are also ~1,800 out-of-state landlords who own rental housing here and pay property tax, several thousand commuters who work here and use our roads but sleep in the county, and a state that funds something like 40% of our budget. Nobody has proposed enfranchising any of those.

A couple of things I can't get past and want the memo to actually deal with:

- If the argument for #2 is "these people are governed by this city and have a say nowhere else," then the commuters and the landlords have the same shape of claim, and so does the state. If the argument is something narrower — they live here, they're part of the place, they can't just pick up and leave — then say that, and say why residency is the line instead of affectedness, and what that implies about the commuters.
- Enforcement of #3: our clerk has no reliable address data for homeless residents (900-ish people), there's no same-day registration, and registration closes 21 days out. So the fine lands on whoever we can find and mail to. Also, if #1 and #3 both pass, a 16-year-old who doesn't vote has committed an offense — who gets the fine, the kid or the parent?
- A local group, Neighbors for a Real Mandate, has floated something they call "conscientious abstention": you must show up, but you can sign a public statement explaining why you're not voting for anyone, and that discharges the duty — no fine, and your reasons get printed in the city newsletter. Half of us think that's compulsion with the teeth pulled out. The other half think it's the only version that isn't a tax on being poor, sick, or working two jobs.
- We have about $40k we could spend on turnout work instead of on fines, and our high school teacher member wants any 16-year-old vote paired with the required 11th-grade civics course (she'd run a parallel mock election). Our other member thinks that's the state conscripting kids into a political act their parents might object to.

What I need is a memo I can read aloud at the meeting, 900 words tops. It should:

- pick one of these and say so in the first two sentences — (A) all three; (B) #1 and #2, no compulsion, spend the $40k on turnout; (C) #1 only, #2 to a separate ballot, no compulsion; (D) #3 with conscientious abstention but no franchise expansion — or a fifth option you think is better;
- argue in terms of actual people in this city, not Democracy with a capital D;
- give the strongest version of the losing view, stated well enough that the member who holds it would say "yes, that's my point" — in particular, if you're against #2, the strongest version of "governed here, voiced nowhere," and if you're for #2, the strongest version of "a demos has to be a bounded identifiable group and being-affected doesn't give you one";
- propose a staged timeline: what happens in the spring election, what gets piloted, and what we measure besides raw turnout to decide whether to keep going;
- tell us plainly which pieces to drop because the city attorney will kill them.

No citations or reading lists — I can't check them and nobody on this commission cares what a professor said in 1997. There's a typo or two above and I don't care about that either, just don't copy my formatting. End the whole thing with a single line: \answer{X — short phrase}, where X is the letter of the option you're recommending.
