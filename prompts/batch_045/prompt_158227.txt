Last night I had a long, unresolved argument with a friend about public reason and religious arguments in politics, and I would like an independent ruling on one part of it.

The familiar filters — the requirement that a citizen be able to offer a secular rationale for a coercive law, the proviso that religious reasons may be introduced into public debate provided suitably political reasons are given in due course, the translation requirement, and the broader accessibility/shareability tests — all inspect a reason's content: does the conclusion follow from premises any reasonable citizen could accept? My friend thinks this is the wrong target. She proposes what she calls the crux test. On her view, what matters is not whether religious vocabulary appears in someone's political advocacy, but where it sits in the load-bearing structure of the argument. A religious consideration is admissible if, at the point where the argument actually does its work — the crux — the speaker relies on a claim she is prepared to defend with reasons addressed to anyone. It is inadmissible if at the crux she relies on the bare authority of a revelation, a text, or a religious community, i.e. on a premise whose force just consists in its having come from that source. Religious premises are perfectly fine in the periphery: as heuristics, as evidence about consequences, as motivation, as a statement of what is at stake. They are objectionable only when they carry the weight.

Please apply the crux test, on its own terms, to the following six ways of supporting a coercive law, sorting them into tiers from most to least admissible and giving one sentence of justification per item:

1. A bare scriptural citation offered to an audience not assumed to grant the text's authority, with no further support.
2. An appeal to a tradition's long-standing social teaching, presented as a heuristic about consequences — the historical prohibition is named, and the mechanism it was tracking is spelled out in secular terms.
3. A claim about what God requires, where the speaker adds that the requirement was arrived at through her community's institutional procedures of discernment and that those procedures are reliable.
4. A natural-law argument whose premises concern human flourishing and whose conclusion is independently derivable, but which the speaker says she would abandon if it ever conflicted with revelation.
5. An appeal to a law's expressive or pedagogical effect: the law teaches what the community already believes, where the belief is religious but the claim about the law's teaching effect is empirical.
6. A report of personal religious experience offered as a reason for a specific policy, accompanied by a secular backup argument the speaker herself regards as weaker.

Two further things I want. First, tell me where the crux test's ordering diverges from what the secular-rationale or translation requirements would give, and whether each divergence is a point for or against my friend's test — I do not want a summary of the standard views restated, I want the disagreements. Second, be explicit about where your own ranking is most fragile: which item you would most readily move, which consideration would move it, and whether anything you know of would lead you to drop the crux test in favour of one of the standard filters.

Conclude with a single machine-readable line giving your tiering, ordered from most to least admissible, with tiers separated by a vertical bar — for example \answer{2 | 5, 3 | 6 | 4 | 1}.
