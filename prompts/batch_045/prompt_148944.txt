The reason I think this is worth doing is that I've started to suspect people are using one phrase for three different claims, and I've given them my own names:

1. The leverage reading: put your effort where effort actually pays — you can't make the river card come, so spend your energy on the decision, not on the outcome. That's just expected-value thinking as far as I can tell.
2. The accounting reading: it's about what can be credited to you or blamed on you. You don't get credit for a cooler and you don't deserve blame for one either; the only line item that's really yours is how you played the hand.
3. The authorship reading: the actual ancient-sounding one, where the "up to me" part is meant to carve out a piece of the process that is *you* — the assent, the judgment — and everything else, including your own body and your own first reaction, is just stuff happening to you.

My hunch is that the modern revival is running on reading 1 while talking like it's running on reading 3, and that reading 1 is just poker's "don't be results-oriented" wearing a toga. But I also can't shake the feeling that 2 and 3 are doing work I can't see, and I'd like to know whether that's real or whether I'm being sold something.

One more thing that's been bugging me and that I want you to deal with rather than dodge. In poker, how much of a result is "up to you" is a function of sample size. Over one hand, almost none of it is — you can play it perfectly and lose. Over a hundred thousand hands, your win rate is basically yours. So control isn't a fixed boundary in my world, it's a thing that sharpens as the sample grows. Does the Stoic line move with the sample size or not? If it doesn't, is that a flaw in the Stoic picture, or is refusing to wait for the long run exactly the point?
