So I've been arguing with a friend about responsibility gaps for autonomous systems and I want to use the May 6 2010 flash crash as the test case. Facts as I have them (correct me if I've got something wrong, but please don't go off and look things up): sometime after 2:30 pm the market started falling very fast, the Dow lost roughly 600 points in about five minutes and close to 1000 on the day, around 9 percent, then gave most of it back within twenty minutes or so; liquidity vanished, a bunch of big-name stocks and ETFs printed absurd prices for a few minutes (pennies in some cases, near 100k in others), and the mechanism was automated execution algorithms interacting with high-frequency traders rather than anybody deciding to dump. Structural fixes came later — circuit breakers and that sort of thing — and five years on the DOJ charged a single trader, Navinder Sarao, with spoofing, which plenty of commentators thought was a bit like blaming lightning for starting a fire.

Here's the wrinkle I actually care about. The responsibility-gap argument I know best is built around learning machines: the designer can't predict what the system will do, so the usual conditions for blame don't attach to anyone. But nothing in the flash crash was a learning system. These were hand-coded rules, individually pretty transparent, doing what they were written to do. So either there's no gap here at all and I'm wasting my time, or there's a gap of a different shape than the literature talks about — and I think it's the second, but I can't say cleanly how.

So analyse the episode using the four-way distinction below, which I'm stipulating rather than quoting from anywhere. Use it first, then tell me if it's a bad taxonomy. (1) foresight gap: nobody could have known. (2) intervention gap: someone could have known but couldn't act in time. (3) authorship gap: no single agent's conduct produced the outcome, because each party did something locally reasonable. (4) uptake gap: there is a decent candidate to blame, but blaming them can't do the work blame is supposed to do — deterring, expressing, restoring.

Write it in these sections, in this order:

The setup I'm handing you — about 100 words. Restate the four gaps and say whether the taxonomy is gerrymandered or is secretly two distinctions wearing a trenchcoat.

Mapping the case — about 300 words. Who the candidate bearers are (the trader who launched the sell program and could have switched it off, whoever wrote the volume-targeting rule, the high-frequency firms, the venues whose routing rules let orders fall into a void, the regulators), what each one's real options were in that window, and what counts as foreseeing anything when the interaction itself is the hazard.

Which gaps open and which don't — about 250 words. Go through all four and say, for each, whether the flash crash instantiates it, kind of instantiates it, or doesn't, and what would have to be different for it to.

Where the theory misdescribes the case — about 250 words. My own suspicion is that gap talk quietly assumes a victim and an individual bearer, and here the harm was diffuse and partly self-inflicted by whoever sold at the bottom, so the framework may not even have the right shape. Also say whether the intervention gap here is really about time or about nobody having the standing to stop the whole market, given that each firm pulling its own algorithms makes things worse collectively.

What the case does to the theory — about 250 words. The bit I'm most interested in: if emergent unpredictability among individually transparent components opens a gap, does the responsibility-gap literature collapse into the older problem of many hands in organizations, so that its claim to be about machine autonomy in particular fails? Or is the combination of emergence, speed and no-standing genuinely new? And read the Sarao prosecution as a response to an uptake gap: legitimate gap-closing or scapegoating?

Confidence — about 120 words. Say how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the one you're least sure of and why.
