I convene a small graduate reading group on the long-term safety of artificial agents, and one member — sympathetic to the project but sceptical of its foundations — has settled on a single analogy to keep the orthogonality thesis and the instrumental convergence thesis in what he takes to be their proper place. A mind's intelligence, he says, is like a car engine. An engine has a horsepower rating and no opinion about where it is driven; give it a destination and it will take you there, and making the engine more powerful neither changes nor improves the destination. That, he maintains, is the whole content of orthogonality: a conceptual truism about the independence of a capacity from the end it serves. It follows that whatever substance the risk argument has must be carried entirely by instrumental convergence, and that thesis, on his reading, is only the unremarkable observation that some means — fuel, roads, remaining in running order — are useful for reaching a great many destinations. Since nobody ever expected more horsepower to steer you toward a better place, and since noticing that most destinations require roads is not a discovery about engines, he concludes that the pair of theses has been inflated into a danger they do not contain.

I do not want a verdict on AI risk. I want an audit of the analogy he is using to reach that verdict.

1. Set out the correspondence explicitly, as a table: what plays the role of the engine, of horsepower, of the destination, of the driver, of the road network, of fuel, of the map or navigation system, and of the mechanic. Then note which elements of the target domain have no counterpart in the picture at all, since a mapping that leaves out a load-bearing element is itself part of what needs auditing.

2. Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the use he is making of the picture from those that do not, and for each one that matters say whether it cuts against his deflation or against the argument he is deflating. Candidate places to look: an engine has no model of the world and no beliefs about it; it cannot select its own destination, redesign itself, invest in the road network, or act so as to avoid being scrapped; its driver is external to it and persists across the trip; the roads are given rather than something to be acquired, closed off to others, or reshaped.

3. I have come to suspect that three things are being imported by the picture rather than argued for, and I would like each tested rather than granted.

(a) Satiability. The destination framing makes every objective something that is arrived at, after which the engine idles. The objectives that drive the standard convergence arguments are directions rather than destinations: maximize X, where X is unbounded. Does instrumental convergence lose its force, or change its shape materially, once the final goal is satiable or otherwise bounded? If it does, is that a defect in the analogy, or a substantive limitation on the argument that the analogy happens to expose?

(b) Corrigibility. In the picture the driver stands outside the car, is continuous with it over time, and can re-aim it at will: the destination is revisable from without. In the target domain there is a familiar line of argument that a capable agent has instrumental reason to resist having its own goals altered, so that capability and amenability to correction may fail to be independent even if capability and goal-content are. Call the first independence claim synchronic orthogonality and the second diachronic orthogonality. Is the diachronic claim the one the risk argument actually needs? Is it supported by the same considerations as the synchronic one, or is it a separate and stronger claim? And does the engine picture conceal that separation?

(c) Modality versus distribution. The picture can express a modal claim — any destination with any horsepower — but instrumental convergence is not modal. It says that across a reference class of final goals and of environments, certain means are instrumentally useful for a large share of them, and that the share is large enough to matter for prediction. Can any variant of the engine picture carry a claim of that shape, or does the analogy fall silent precisely where instrumental convergence begins?

4. Finally, take up the charge of triviality directly. Orthogonality is sometimes read as denying something substantive: that greater cognitive capacity tends to bring a better grip on value, on the grounds that evaluative or moral cognition is itself a species of intelligence and improves along with the rest. Is that denial a truism, a contested conjecture, or something the analogy settles by stipulation? If orthogonality has that content, does my colleague's deflation survive, or was the engine carrying it the whole time?

If there is a better analogy for the two theses — one that keeps whatever is true in the engine picture while making the distributional and diachronic claims visible — say what it is and what it would show.
