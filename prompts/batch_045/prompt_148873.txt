I've been reading about futarchy — the idea that you vote on values but bet on beliefs, i.e. you settle on some welfare metric by ordinary voting and then let prediction markets pick whichever policy actually scores best on it — and I want to try running it over something real: the 2016 Brexit referendum.

I'll stipulate the facts so you don't have to go looking. Cameron called an in/out referendum mainly to settle a decades-long fight inside the Conservative party and to see off UKIP. The ballot was one binary question. Leave won, roughly 52-48, on a turnout in the low 70s. Sterling dropped hard overnight, Cameron resigned within hours, and then came about three years of parliamentary paralysis — a big part of which was that nobody had ever agreed what "Leave" meant: Norway/EEA-style membership, a WTO-terms hard exit, or something in between. Gently correct anything I've mangled, but please don't let the analysis hang on a detail I've misremembered.

What I want is the two-way case analysis, done carefully rather than impressionistically:

1. The mapping. Who the players actually are in futarchy's terms (the electorate? MPs? the betting public? the EU, which also gets to move after the UK does?), what the option space is, and — the hard part — what the metric is. I'd rather propose one than dodge it, so here's mine: real household disposable income per head five years out, scored against a synthetic-control UK built from a basket of similar economies, so we're comparing Britain to what it plausibly would have looked like had it stayed rather than to its own past. Is that metric even workable? Say specifically what breaks about it.

2. What futarchy says the actors faced and should have done — what the conditional market would have looked like in 2016, who'd have been in it, and whether its price deserved any authority.

3. Where the model explains the episode and where it misdescribes it. The bit I keep circling: futarchy assumes the options are specified well enough to be priced, and "Leave" wasn't. Is that a case the theory simply never anticipated, or a badly run vote that futarchy would have fixed by forcing specification up front?

4. The reverse direction — what the episode teaches about futarchy. My suspicion is the real lesson isn't about market accuracy at all but about legitimacy: a lot of what the referendum was doing was expressive, or was a fight about who gets to decide, and a price can't deliver that. Give me the strongest version of the opposite view too.

Also, and this matters: keep separated what's genuinely settled among people who work on prediction markets and mechanism design from what's contested — whether these markets are actually well calibrated, whether a welfare metric is politically agreeable at all, whether futarchy has any shot outside a narrow domain. Don't dress up a contested position as the consensus, and flag where you're extrapolating past what the literature really covers.
