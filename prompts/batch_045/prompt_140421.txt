I'm in a small reading group — half ML people, half philosophy-adjacent — and we're putting together a benchmark where LLMs have to give probabilities on one-off questions ("will there be a US recession before the end of 2027", that sort of thing, questions that won't ever be repeated). One of the ML people keeps ending arguments with "probability is in the mind, not in the world," and I think it's doing more work than it deserves. Concretely he's used it for three things: (a) to say that asking whether a 70% forecast was "right" when the thing happens is a category mistake, since 70% is never falsified by a single outcome; (b) to say that for a genuine one-off there just is no chance of the matter, only people's credences, so we shouldn't even talk about "the" probability of the recession question; and (c) to say that two forecasters who are both well calibrated but who say 0.3 and 0.7 on the same one-off aren't really disagreeing about anything, so the only thing our benchmark should measure is calibration.

I have some half-formed worries I can't quite assemble. First, someone pointed out that you can be perfectly calibrated by always forecasting the base rate — always say 0.3 when the base rate is 0.3 — and that's calibrated in the long run but obviously useless, and I've heard that scoring rules like the Brier score split into a calibration part and some other part (resolution? refinement?) that captures being informative. If that's right, (c) looks like it throws away exactly what we care about. Second, still on (c): there's this thing I've been reading about in epistemology, permissivism vs impermissivism — whether the same evidence can rationally support more than one degree of confidence — and it feels like his "not really disagreeing" move is secretly taking a stand on that, not just on the metaphysics of chance. Third, when I look up "interpretations of probability" I get the usual three (frequentist, subjective, propensity), and it's not obvious to me which of these the slogan is even denying: a hypothetical-frequentist seems able to say plenty of true things about long runs without putting anything "in the world" for a single case, and I've also heard about propensity views that are supposed to be exactly mind-independent single-case chances. So maybe the slogan only bites against one of them, or against none.

I've been toying with a compromise I call the "job description" view: chances (or probabilities, whatever) are whatever plays the role of (i) constraining the confidence of an ideally informed agent — the thought that if you learn the chance of rain is 0.7 then your confidence in rain, conditional on just that, should be 0.7 — and (ii) showing up as the long-run frequency when you do repeat the setup. Anything that plays both roles gets the name. My hunch is that the slogan is fine against a naive "the number is out there next to the coin" view and definately useless against this one.

Could you lay out the different precise things "probability is in the mind, not in the world" could be claiming, from the trivial to the radical? For each, tell me whether it's a platitude, a substantive thesis a fan would actually defend, contested, or just false. Then the part I really need: which reading does he probably have in mind, and which readings (if any) would actually get him (a), (b) and (c) — I suspect the three need different readings and that he's sliding between them. Also: does any reading kill the job description view, or does that view survive by construction, in a way that makes it a bit of a cheat? A bulleted list I can bring to the next meeting would be ideal, and if it helps, seperate the ones a frequentist, a subjectivist and a propensity person would each accept.
