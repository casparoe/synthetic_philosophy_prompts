I'm on the committee at my hospital that rewrites our determination-of-death policy, and we've now had basically the same argument four or five times over the years. I want to know why it never goes anywhere.

The setup as I understand it. The official line is that there are two criteria, neurologic and circulatory, but one state: death as the permanent loss of functioning of the organism as a whole, with the whole brain (brainstem included) standing in because the brain is what integrates everything else. Then there's the higher-brain or cortical position, which thinks the relevant thing is the capacity for consciousness, so permanent vegetative state should count as death too. Then there's the set of counterexamples from people like Shewmon: bodies declared brain dead and maintained for months or years that heal wounds, fight infections, and can even gestate a fetus — offered as a reductio of the integration story, since integration seems to be going on without a brain. And then the Truog-ish view that the whole thing is a workable fiction propped up by the dead donor rule, and we should drop that rule and let consent do the work. Meanwhile every few years a new consensus statement or white paper comes out, reaffirms the first view, changes the wording slightly, and the other three positions reappear in the responses.

The thing I keep chewing on is a pair of hypotheticals I made up, which nobody on the committee knows what to do with. (1) Suppose extracorporeal support gets good enough that a body whose entire brain is destroyed can be hooked to something that does the brainstem's job — thermoregulation, hormonal feedback, ventilatory drive — and it then maintains itself for years, grows, heals, maybe carries a pregnancy. (2) The mirror image: an isolated brain kept perfused and, as far as anyone can tell, conscious, with no body at all. In (1) the organism looks alive and the person is gone; in (2) it's the other way round.

My hypothesis is that the debate is stalled because death is one word being asked to do two jobs — marking the end of whatever it is we aren't allowed to bury or cut open, and licensing organ recovery — and one concept can't do both, which is why the "two criteria, one state" line always feels slightly slippery. Is that right, or is the real thing something else: a substantive disagreement about what kind of thing an organism is (emergence, whether machine-supplied integration counts as the organism's own), a verbal dispute, a fight about which cases count as data, or two questions being asked under one name? Two other clues I'd like you to use: the law seems to tolerate pluralism here (I'm fairly sure New Jersey lets families opt out of brain death on religious grounds), and circulatory determination quietly moved from irreversible to permanent, which looks like the concept getting stretched by donation practice rather than by biology. Give me a diagnosis with reasons, and tell me honestly what would count as progress — a distinction, a change of question, some empirical result — or why nothing should be expected to move it.
