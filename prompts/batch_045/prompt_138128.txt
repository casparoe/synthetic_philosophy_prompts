I've been trying to put a name to something for a while now, and I can't find the word for it — which may mean there isn't one. I'd like the carving, not just a label, so let me describe what I keep seeing.

The background distinction I'm working from (correct me if I have it garbled): for a given attitude there seem to be two kinds of reason. One kind bears on whether the attitude is *correct* — whether the proposition is true, whether the thing really is fearsome, whether the person really is blameworthy, whether the loss really is worth grieving. The other kind merely makes it good, useful, decent, or required of you to *have* the attitude: someone pays you to believe it, someone threatens you unless you're frightened, keeping up the resentment is politically necessary. Reasons of the second sort are real reasons, but they don't answer the attitude's own question. I'm not interested in that distinction as such. I'm interested in what happens to it when the second sort of reason doesn't stay where it was put.

Three cases.

1. A friend of mine was told, accurately, that surgical patients who expect to recover do measurably better. Grant that she had no evidential reason at all to expect it; the statistics for her case were grim. What she had was a reason to *want* to expect it. She couldn't simply decide to believe it, but she could steer: she read the survivorship literature and not the mortality literature, spent time with the recovered patients in the support group and not with the ones doing badly, asked her surgeon for the optimistic end of the range rather than the middle. Six months later she expected to recover, and she could cite chapter and verse. As far as I can tell the belief now rests on genuine evidence, and the evidence supports it: take away the whole episode of steering and what she currently has still supports what she currently believes. What I can't say is whether the belief is defective. It was produced by a route nobody thinks is the right way to produce a belief, but the route isn't doing any current work. Is there a notion of an attitude's inheriting a defect from its ancestry? Or is every complaint here a complaint about what she did, none of it about what she now believes?

2. A widower I know was told, truly, that his grieving was frightening his children. So he had a reason to want the grief to be smaller — and that reason plainly had nothing to do with whether grief was called for, since his wife had died and if anything calls for grief that does. He did the ordinary things: went back to work early, kept the photographs up but stopped the evening ritual of looking at them, took up a demanding sport. A year on, the grief is much smaller. Here's the part I can't get straight. Grief comes in degrees, and a degree can be wrong: too much for what happened, too little. Say his is now too little for what he lost. The thing that made it too little was a consideration of the second kind. So is his state defective in the way it would *not* be defective if the same reduction had been produced by, say, a head injury or by the ordinary blunting of time? Is there a term for a complaint about an attitude that is really a complaint about its *size* combined with a story about how it came to have that size — and does the second ingredient ever add anything to the first?

3. Two senior people in a department I know about have been told that the place will destroy itself unless they trust each other. Their colleague's unreliability is a matter of record; neither of them thinks it will change. So the reason they have for trusting her is not an answer to the question trust asks, which is whether she's trustworthy. But trusting someone isn't a single inner item — it's partly what you do (rely on her, don't check up, read the memo in the better light, give the second chance) and partly what you're disposed to do and expect. Each of those they can take up, and for each of them, taken on its own, they have perfectly respectable reasons: the department, the students, the work. So there's a reading on which they have reasons of the right kind for the *components*, the components add up to trust, and the trust itself answers a question to which they have no good answer. Now compare someone paid to love, and a parent who acts lovingly toward a child for the child's sake and finds the feeling arrives later. Same shape, or a different one?

What the three have in common, as I see it: a reason that everyone agrees is of the second kind for the attitude in question gets presented as, or converted into, a reason of the first kind for something adjacent — the wanting of the attitude, the getting oneself into it, one of the dispositions that partly constitute it, or its intensity — and then, because the adjacent thing is connected to the attitude, the attitude itself is either treated as being in good standing or condemned as compromised, and no one says what principle decides which. Sometimes the transfer looks like laundering and sometimes it looks like the only thing a responsible person could do.

What I think it isn't, so you can tell me where I'm wrong. It isn't just the point that you can have a reason to bring an attitude about without having a reason to have it; my cases start there rather than end there. It isn't the worry that a belief fails to rest on its evidence — in case 1 the belief does rest on evidence, and the complaint, if there is one, is about ancestry rather than present support. And it isn't, I think, the familiar structure in which a reason is fine for the first step of a chain and goes missing by the last: in those cases the reason fails to arrive, whereas in mine the reason arrives, or appears to, and the question is whether it arrives in the right currency.

So: does this have a name? Not a name for the general distinction — that one I have — but a name for the transfer, for the family of transfers, or for the defect they produce. If the three cases are really three different things, I want to know that too, and which of them is the genuine article. If there's no established term, coin one and define it, and then give me the boundary: what feature of the relation between the adjacent item and the attitude — causal versus constitutive, a matter of degree versus a matter of existence, whether the subject avows the route or hides it from herself — decides whether the transfer goes through? Give me one case just inside the line and one just outside. And tell me whether anything I've described is a problem for the standard ways of handling the general distinction, or only a new application of them.

I'm not asking for a reading list or citations; I can't chase references from where I'm writing, and invented ones are worse than none. Names of concepts are very welcome, sources aren't necessary.

Last thing: please close by saying how confident you are in the overall account you've given — especially in your verdict on whether these are one phenomenon or three — and by singling out the single claim in your answer you'd be least surprised to turn out wrong about, and saying why.
