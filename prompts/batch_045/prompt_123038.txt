I run a short "foundations" module every spring for third-year mathematics and computer-science undergraduates — mostly formal-methods and proof-assistant people, with a couple of physicists sitting in — and the same argument breaks out every year and goes nowhere. Half the room holds that a theorem is true because we have a derivation of it and that this is all "true" could intelligibly mean here; the other half finds that intolerable, because there are sentences that (assuming the axioms are consistent) those axioms can neither prove nor refute, and it seems absurd that such a sentence should thereby be neither true nor false. Someone then points out that one can encode zero as the empty set and each successor as n ∪ {n}, or instead as {n}, that both encodings support proofs of exactly the same theorems about the naturals, and that nobody thinks one of them is a discovery about which objects the numbers "really" are — and at that point the discussion usually splits into team names and stops being about anything. I've decided the real problem is that my students don't see that the view they are defending is a bundle of separable claims whose joints they have never had to press on, so I want an instrument that sorts them by what they will say about cases, built from the logical space of the question rather than from a list of -isms.

What I'd like from you is a ten-to-twelve-question diagnostic, with six to eight leaves, that I can hand out cold in a seminar — no philosophy prerequisites, twenty minutes to complete. Constraints on the questions themselves: none may use a label, none may ask outright whether numbers exist or whether mathematics is about abstract objects, and none should be answerable by knowing which tradition one already identifies with; every question should be a concrete case or a blunt claim that a student can react to without knowing where it leads. Please separate at least these axes rather than collapsing them: whether undecided sentences have determinate truth values; whether the truth of a mathematical statement is explained by objects and structures or by proof and practice; whether there is a fact of the matter about which encoding of the naturals is the real one; whether the "how could we possibly know?" worry is a separate burden from the "what makes it true?" one; and whether the whole dispute is substantive at all. Cases I want used include the two encodings above, an undecidable-in-the-axioms sentence, whether there is a size of infinity strictly between that of the naturals and that of the reals, a purported proof that is machine-checked but far too long for any human to survey, a student who computes 7×8=54 and what exactly makes that a mistake, a civilization whose arithmetic is modular or lacks multiplication altogether, and a claim about the outcome of a computation that in principle terminates but that no one will ever actually run — does it have a truth value now? At most one question may turn on applications to natural science; half my room is pure and does not care. And I want one question specifically designed to separate the student who thinks the dispute is merely verbal from the one who thinks it is genuine but unresolvable, because those two nod along with each other all term and I suspect they agree about nothing.

For the instrument itself: do not make the existence question the root — pick something else (determinacy, or the individuation of the numbers, or the normativity of mathematical correctness) and say in a sentence what you chose and what that choice hides. Give me a scoring key from answer patterns to leaves, where each leaf is described by the commitments that define it and carries at most a mnemonic name attached afterwards, plus one sentence on the awkward commitment that holders of that position usually discover only under pressure. Then do three things that matter more to me than the tree itself. First, mark the regions of the logical space that no position occupies, and for each say whether it is empty because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Second, flag any pair of leaves your questions cannot actually separate rather than pretending they diverge. Third, identify two or three answer patterns that look jointly unstable — for instance someone who grants determinate truth values to undecided sentences while also saying truth just is provability — and for each tell me whether it is genuinely incoherent or merely surprising, and, if the latter, which leaf it should still count as.
