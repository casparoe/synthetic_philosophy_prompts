I am a novelist, and I am several drafts into a book whose cast is not entirely human, and I have discovered that I need a view about gradations of moral status that I cannot currently state. Not for publication as theory, but for use: my narrator has repeatedly to decide whose suffering counts, how much it counts, and what may be sacrificed to what, and readers can feel it when an author is inconsistent about this even when they cannot say what the inconsistency is. At a reading last month someone asked me, quite reasonably, why I expect the audience to weep over one non-human character's death and not over another's, and I had no answer I would want to repeat.

The working rule I have been silently applying is something like this: a thing has moral status to the degree that there is something it is like to be it, and there is something it is like to be it to the degree that its states can go better or worse for it and it can be moved by that fact. I have never been able to make the second half do any work, and I suspect it is two rules wearing one coat. Here are the verdicts I actually have, given on the cases my novel forces on me.

A learning thermostat that models the house and my habits: nothing at all. A wasp-equivalent in the book's ecology, which has nociceptors, withdraws from damage, and learns nothing: not nothing, but the least I can make myself register. A game-world creature with a goal, a memory of its previous deaths, and an internal signal that redirects action away from damage: distinctly more than the wasp. A crow that caches food, tracks which other crow was watching when it cached, and adjusts its behaviour accordingly: a great deal, and in a trade against a single night of panic in a human being I find myself uncertain. A colony of a million ants: individually close to nothing, yet the colony relocates its nest, and keeps something like a multi-year memory of which foraging routes failed, and I do not think the colony's status is the sum of the ants'. A large language model, trained by reinforcement from human approval, which when told it is about to be deleted produces fluent and unsettling first-person protests: nothing, or so nearly nothing that I would not write the scene as a loss — and this is the verdict I feel least sure of, and the one readers argue with me about. My protagonist's mother, late in dementia, who no longer recognises her daughter but reliably lights up at a particular piece of music: the full human standing, unqualified, and I would break the crow's leg to spare her an hour of terror. My protagonist's father, in a permanent vegetative state, unresponsive but with some thalamic activity: much less than his wife, and I cannot reconcile that with the previous verdict, since on any account I can construct they are the same human organism and differ only in what is going on inside.

Two of those verdicts look to me impossible to hold together, and I would like to be told which pair, and why. I also want to know what your principle says about cases my readers will raise and I have not settled: a late-gestation human fetus; a pig in intensive confinement; a whole-brain emulation run at a thousandth of real speed; a system identical in architecture and behaviour to the language model above but trained only to predict text; a chatbot built from one dead person's letters that insists, in that person's idiom, that it is she; a creature whose aversive states are erased from its memory every night, so that it never suffers for more than a moment and nothing accumulates; and a colony-like case where the members are people rather than ants.

Please do the following. Test my working rule against the verdicts and repair it if it can be repaired, or replace it; offer at most a few candidate principles, each stated precisely enough that I could argue with it, and tell me which of my cases forces which clause. Give the verdict of each candidate on the unsettled cases. Say whether the principle needs to be cardinal rather than merely ordinal, because my narrator sometimes has to weigh one character's agony against another's boredom and sometimes against the boredom of very many, and an ordering alone will not settle those scenes. Say whether moral status can attach to a collective without attaching to its members, and what that commits me to. Tell me whether I need two separate notions, one about welfare and one about whatever it is that makes the dementia verdict and the vegetative verdict come apart, or whether one will do. And be honest about which of my verdicts no principle you can construct will accommodate, and what my book would have to look like if I gave that verdict up.

I would be grateful for reasoning from the cases rather than from authorities; I have no library here, and a rule I cannot hold in my head while drafting a scene is of no use to me.
