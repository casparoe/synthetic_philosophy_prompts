I am a professor of philosophy at a large public research university, and I have been asked to chair a four-person committee that will spend a one-time bequest of about three and a half million dollars over five years. The donor's letter of intent is unusually directive. It does not ask us to support excellent philosophy; it asks us to support work that can demonstrate, by the end of the term, that philosophy makes progress of a kind that skeptical colleagues in other departments would recognize. The money can pay for released time, postdoctoral fellows, graduate fellowships, and workshops. It cannot pay for large-scale empirical work, and we have no in-house strength in formal methods beyond what two of our faculty already do. The committee must report to the donor's board at the midpoint and at the end, and whatever we choose has to be defensible both to that board and, more importantly, to the rest of our department, which is split three ways: one group thinks demonstrable progress requires importing something empirical, one thinks it requires formalization, and one thinks the real obstacle is that we have never gotten clear about which of our questions are genuine and which are artifacts of how they were originally posed.

I do not want a survey of the debate about whether philosophy makes progress, and I do not want names and titles. I want an agenda, ordered and defended.

Some distinctions I have been using privately, and which I would like you to adopt, revise, or discard. First, a philosophical question can have several fates: it can be answered in a way that later practitioners accept; it can dissolve, in the sense that the vocabulary in which it was posed stops being usable; and it can go dormant and then return, sometimes in new terms, after having been declared closed. Call the last of these recidivism, and note that if a field's settled questions recidivate at a high rate, then counting settlements at a single moment badly overstates what it has achieved. Second, some subfields seem to progress not because their questions are easier but because they possess what I will call tribunals: shared standards, or shared argumentative moves, whose verdict participants actually treat as closing a line of inquiry, at least for a while. Where tribunals are absent, questions stay open across generations regardless of the talent applied to them. Third, a great deal of what philosophy produces is neither answers nor dissolutions but scaffolding: transfer principles, impossibility and dilemma arguments, distinctions that shrink the space of coherent positions without selecting one.

Given all that, here is what I am asking. Suppose the center can commit to at most four lines of work, and must pick among (a) a retrospective audit of debates that were once live and are now treated as closed, designed to estimate a recidivism rate rather than to celebrate settlements; (b) deliberate tribunal construction, i.e., work aimed at giving some currently tribunal-less area a standard of closure; (c) a standing forecast registry in which contributors log, in advance, their expectations about which live disputes will still be live in fifteen years and why, to be scored later; and (d) direct funding of scaffolding work in areas chosen for how much of the space of positions they might collapse per unit of effort. Rank these, or replace them. Tell me which line is upstream of the others, which is tractable by people with our training and time horizon, which is neglected relative to its importance, and which would produce impressive-looking output that changes nothing downstream. Say explicitly what you are weighing against what when you order them, and what evidence at the midpoint should move a line up or down or shut it down.

Two further things. One: if part of the honest answer is that nothing we can fund will deliver what the donor's letter asks within five years, tell me that plainly, and tell me what reframe of the deliverable the committee should put to the board instead. Two: our department's third faction wants to spend real money on the question of which of our questions are pseudo-questions. I am unsure whether that is the highest-leverage item on the list or an excuse for philosophers to study philosophers instead of the world. Please take a position, and tell me which considerations would move you off it.
