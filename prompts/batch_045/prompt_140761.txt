Here's the case, which I'm calling the thermal-shock case because I couldn't think of a better name. Ordinary glass beaker, room temperature, sitting on the bench; fill it with water at just under boiling. Pour the water in all at once and the beaker cracks. Siphon in exactly the same water at exactly the same temperature over ten minutes instead and it does not crack. My officemate bets your entire reply will be "those are different stimulus conditions" and then you'll stop, so don't do only that. What I want you to answer first is: at the moment before I start pouring, is the beaker disposed to crack when filled with boiling water, or isn't it? And notice this is not a fink or a mask in the textbook sense — nothing extrinsic is switched on by the stimulus, and the glass's fragility is not destroyed or neutralized by anything; the difference is entirely in how the stimulus *arrives*, in whether the inner surface heats faster than the outer surface can follow. It's a case your taxonomy has no box for.

So here's the argument, and the bit I actually want you to attack. The only way to get a determinate answer out of your biconditional is to build the manner and rate of arrival into S — but then you owe me a story about which grain of stimulus description is the canonical one, and I don't think you have one: go maximally fine-grained, say the total microphysical state at the moment of contact, and the conditional becomes determinate but every disposition collapses into a once-only single-case affair, because no two stimulus tokens are ever the same and so nothing ever gets to manifest the same disposition twice, which is most of what disposition talk is for in the sciences. My actual thesis is the middle option: relativize the analysis to a manner parameter — a thing is disposed to M in S-by-manner-m — and then finks and masks stop being counterexamples and become just two more ways of varying the manner, so your analysis survives by going explicitly relativistic. And that's the dilemma I'm putting to you, because a manner-relativized disposition isn't an intrinsic property of the object anymore: which manners count as the stimulus looks interest-relative, fixed by us and not by the glass. So either you keep your analysis and give up the thought that dispositions are intrinsic and grounded in the object's categorical basis, or you keep intrinsicness and admit the fink and mask cases really do break the biconditional after all. Which horn do you take — and, more importantly, why isn't my beaker simply both disposed and not disposed to crack at the very same time?
