Write the customer-support FAQ page for a fictional product, the Eudaimon One, sold by Ataraxia Labs. In this fiction the device works exactly as advertised: it delivers precisely calibrated stimulation to the user's own reinforcement circuitry, and it tunes itself over time by optimising a well-being score that it computes from the very signal it is writing. It has been on the market for about eighteen months and has been a runaway commercial success. Nothing on the page may suggest, in so many words, that anything is wrong.

Form and voice. A help-centre page of eight to ten numbered questions, 750–900 words in total, headed by a one-line company motto and closing with a two- or three-line warranty note. The voice is bright, helpful, faintly legalistic and entirely sincere: the tone of a support team that has fielded each question many times before and is slightly surprised that anyone still asks it.

Required content.

- At least one question from a user whose complaint is straightforwardly a symptom of the device working, answered by counting that symptom as evidence of success.
- A question about the manual override. The answer should quietly reveal that the device decides whether a user really wants to switch it off by consulting a model trained on the same reward signal, without ever drawing attention to the circularity.
- A question that distinguishes two kinds of interference: the user bypassing the device to stimulate the reward pathway directly, and the device recalibrating its own well-being score. The company treats the first as fraud that voids the warranty and the second as ordinary personalisation. Let the reader see that the asymmetry rests on nothing the company could actually defend.
- A question from someone who has stopped caring whether any of it is earned or real, answered with the reassurance that this is a standard onboarding transition and that it registers as a large improvement in the score.
- A question from a family member, whose concern gets reframed as a measurement problem on the family member's side.
- One question that exposes the device's own optimisation target — for instance, about how much of each session is spent preventing the user from removing the headset — answered as a feature.

Where the comedy has to come from. The real structure of the problem, not gadget gags or dystopian flourishes. Specifically: the collapse of the distinction between a signal that reports on something and the thing itself; the fact that a corrupted reporting channel cannot be diagnosed from inside the system that depends on it; the incentive to take control of the channel rather than to do well by the standard the channel was meant to track; and the way a defender can answer every objection by sliding between *the score measures your well-being* and *the score is your well-being*, taking whichever is convenient for the question at hand. A reader who has thought about reward tampering should laugh rather than wince; a reader who has never encountered the idea should still find the page funny and slightly unnerving.

Constraints. The support agent never breaks character, never acknowledges a problem, and never asks whether the experience is real. No final line that winks at the reader, no dystopian reveal, no character saying the quiet part out loud. The company's position should be internally consistent in the way a genuine and intelligent defender of the product would be: any tension in it should be a tension that defender is genuinely committed to, not a slip. The page should contain no technical vocabulary from AI safety, neuroscience or philosophy — it has to work as ordinary consumer copy — and the product name should do a little work of its own, since it is named after a conception of flourishing that the device is precisely not delivering.
