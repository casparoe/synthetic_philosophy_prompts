Me and my friend Priya spent about three hours last night going round and round about the non-identity problem and got nowhere, mainly because she'd give a verdict on every case I threw at her but wouldn't say what the rule was. So here are her verdicts, and there's one case we never got to. What would she say about it, and more importantly what's the principle she's actually running?

Her verdicts, which she was annoyingly firm about:

1. A woman is a few weeks pregnant. There's a cheap, safe thing she can do that will stop this child from being born with a condition. She can't be bothered. The kid is born with it; his life is fine, but harder. Wrong, she says — obviously wrong, obviously a wrong to him, obviously he's got a complaint.

2. A teenager has a baby. The kid's life is rough — not unbearable, but well below anything you'd call a decent shot at a life. If she'd waited she'd have had a different kid with a much better one. Also wrong, she says, and wrong to the kid specifically, and he's got a complaint, and she didn't budge at all when I pointed out that he wouldn't have existed otherwise. There was one bit I couldn't interpret: she said she'd let him sue, but only for the extra costs the condition brings, not for the fact of existing.

3. A doctor tells a woman that if she conceives this month the child will have a mild, easily treated problem; if she waits two months she'll have a different child who'll be fine. She conceives now. Not wrong, she says. Not wrong to anyone. The kid has no complaint. Her call.

4. A couple conceives knowing the child will have a condition they can't afford to treat, and knowing their existing daughter's life will be noticeably worse as a result. Wrong. She said this one was easier than 2, because the daughter gets hurt and because they knew what they were getting into.

5. When I tried her on the big impersonal one — a policy under which every future person has a decent life, just a clearly worse one than under the alternative, and a completely different set of people exists because everything downstream shifts — she said she had no idea and started talking about something else.

The case we didn't get to: a couple can only have a child using donor eggs. With her own eggs, the child will inherit a condition that's manageable — normal lifespan, will probably be a happy kid, but several surgeries, chronic pain, call it 15 to 20 percent less wellbeing across a life. Either way the kid is well clear of any not-worth-living line and comfortably above any decent-minimum line; the parents are great, the kid is wanted, nobody else is affected either way. They use her eggs because they want a child who's genetically theirs. They could definately have afforded the donor route; they just didn't want it.

So: does Priya say they did something wrong? Does she say they wronged that child? Would she say he's got a complaint when he's twenty? I care less about the answers than about the rules that fit 1 through 4 and come apart here. I can see three candidates: something like it's just about how good the outcome is and nobody has to be worse off; something like you owe whoever you create a decent life and above that line it's the parents' call; and something like you owe the kid you create the best start you could reasonably have given them, measured against the kid you could have had instead. But there are probably better ones I'm missing, and I can't tell which of the three the 1-to-4 pattern really supports, or what the damages-only remark is evidence for, or whether her verdicts on 2 and 4 have already knocked out the hard-line view that nobody can be wronged by being created with a life worth living. Which would you bet on, and what single case would you put to her next to settle it? I'm guessing the policy one in 5, but I'm not sure that seperates all three.
