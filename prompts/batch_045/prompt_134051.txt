Here's the question as it was set:

**Question 3.** CERBERUS is a planning system used by a regional health authority. The authority covers three districts, A, B and C, with 200, 400 and 600 patients respectively (1,200 in total). CERBERUS must recommend one of two ways of spending a fixed one-off grant. The outcomes are known with certainty:

- **Plan X**: fund a mobile surgical unit in District A only. The 200 patients in A each gain 6 units of well-being; nobody else gains anything.
- **Plan Y**: fund an outreach programme across all three districts. Each of the 1,200 patients gains 2 units.

The team has specified three candidate decision procedures:

- **(A)** maximise total well-being;
- **(B)** maximise total well-being subject to the side-constraint that no patient may be left below a fixed floor;
- **(C)** recommend the plan that would be chosen by a contractor behind a "role veil": she knows the structure of the choice but not which patient she will turn out to be, and she ranks plans by the outcome for the worst-off position (maximin).

**(a)** Show how (A), (B) and (C) can come apart in this case — they need not all come apart in the same way — and say which, if any, CERBERUS should use.
**(b)** A member of the authority objects: "CERBERUS will never be one of the patients, so the role-veil standpoint isn't available to it. If it nevertheless pretends to have an equal chance of being each of the people it benefits, it should simply maximise expected benefit per person — and since total and average track each other in a fixed-population case, (C) collapses into (A) after all." Assess this objection.
**(c)** Does it make any moral difference that the chooser is a machine rather than a human committee? Say why or why not.

And here's the student's answer:

**(a)** (A) is plain maximising consequentialism, and it has the familiar problem that it treats distinct patients as if they were time-slices of a single life, adding gains and losses across a boundary that the separateness of persons is supposed to make morally significant. On these numbers (A) picks Y: 1,200 × 2 beats 200 × 6, even though every individual under Y gets a third of what the 200 under X would get. That doesn't refute (A) — a maximiser will just say each person's gain counts the same and that more of them count for more — but it's where the theories bite. (B) differs in form rather than just in outcome: the floor is a side-constraint, not something to be traded off, so (B) is best read as a hybrid view and not as (A) with a tweak. Its weakness here is that everything hangs on where the floor sits and on whether it is a floor on what people end up with or on what we do to them; the question doesn't say, and I think it should have. I'd also note that the machine status is a red herring for the criterion of rightness: a computer can apply whichever rule we like more consistently than a committee can, and it can be audited afterwards, so being a machine matters for implementation and for how much confidence we have in the output, but not for which plan is the right one.

On (C): the device needs a reference class — whose positions are we imagining occupying? — and that choice is itself a moral one the question skates over. If the class is all 1,200 patients in the authority's area, then X leaves 1,000 of them at zero and Y leaves none at zero, so maximin picks Y. If the class is only those who gain under at least one plan, the ranking can flip. So the output of (C) is hostage to a parameter nobody fixed, and I'd call (C) underspecified rather than wrong.

**(b)** The canonical formal treatments of the veil go back to Harsanyi, whose contractors, not knowing who they will turn out to be, rank options by the outcome for the worst-off position — that maximin rule is really the formal core of what Rawls later called the difference principle. Rawls's own version is the more permissive one: he adds the assumption that you have an equal chance of being anybody, which turns the choice into a straightforward expected-benefit calculation. So (C), as the team has written it, is the Harsanyi rule, and the objection seems to me basically right, so we should accept its conclusion. Once CERBERUS weights each person it benefits equally it is maximising expected benefit per person — that is, the average — and in a case like this the relevant population is the same whichever plan is chosen, so maximising the average just is maximising the total. (C) therefore collapses back into (A) and the role veil buys nothing that (A) didn't already give us; the point that CERBERUS will never itself be a patient adds nothing, since the veil was always a device for representing impartiality rather than a literal reincarnation story.

**(c)** I do think it matters that the chooser is a machine, though not because machines can't be moral. CERBERUS's recommendation is the joint product of the modellers who wrote the objective function, the clinicians who set the parameters, the auditors and the procurement rules. No single person's contribution was necessary or sufficient for the output, so responsibility is genuinely diffused — in the end nobody is responsible for it. And a distribution that nobody is responsible for cannot be unjust, since injustice, like blame, has to land on an agent. So the machine doesn't pick the wrong plan so much as dissolve the moral question. With a human committee there is at least someone to whom the patients can address a demand for justification, and being able to demand a justification is partly what makes the choice a moral one at all. If I had to decide, I'd run (B), with the floor set at whatever the worst-off patient could reasonably reject.
