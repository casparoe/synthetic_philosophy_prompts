hi — I'm a novelist, about two thirds of the way through a book, and I think I've written myself into a philosophical hole rather than a structural one, so I'd rather find out now.

The setup: near-future, a residential care home. Most of the residents have a companion machine, and the one the book follows is attached to a woman called Vera — six years, it knows her routines, it reads to her, it's the thing she talks to. It runs on an architecture where it can't act on a reason it can't state out loud and it has to revise when a stated reason gets defeated in conversation; that constraint is baked into how it talks and it's most of what makes the voice interesting to write. In chapter nine Vera asks whether her daughter is coming and the machine tells her the truth. Later it agrees to be wiped, because Vera's daughter asks it to and it said it would do what she asked.

Here's roughly where I've landed, and I want to know whether it hangs together:

(1) Moral agency is reasons-responsiveness and nothing more. No consciousness required, no feelings required. A machine can be a full moral agent or the word means nothing.

(2) Being someone whose good matters is a different thing and does require consciousness. These machines have none. So nothing matters to it for its own sake — we can have reasons about it (Vera likes it, wiping it upsets people) but not reasons for its sake.

(3) It's genuinely appropriate to blame it in chapter nine. Not "send it back to the manufacturer," the full thing: resentment, the sense that it owed her something. If blame here turns out to be only forward-looking I lose the scene.

(4) It really does reason, it isn't mimicking. It's counterfactually robust, change what it knows and it changes what it does, you can talk it out of things. I want nothing to do with the line that it's a very fancy autocomplete.

(5) The auditability constraint is a feature, not a limitation. A system that can't state its reasons isn't a moral agent at all.

(6) But there's something missing and I keep circling it: nothing is ever difficult for it. It never has to overcome itself to do the right thing. I want the reader to feel that as a real deficiency in it as an agent — not just as a fact about what's going on inside the casing.

(7) As a novelist I also basically believe that moral perception is mostly inarticulate. The characters who can always explain themselves are the ones I trust least, and being able to state a reason isn't the same as acting for it. I've written several hundred pages of other stuff on this assumption, so it's load-bearing.

(8) Two verdicts I'm committed to. The machine was right in chapter nine, and a human friend who said exactly that, for exactly the reasons the machine gives, would be blameworthy for cruelty; the machine is not cruel. And at the end, the reader should feel the wiping as a loss, and that is the right response — but not, I think, because anything matters to it.

So: which of these actually pull against each other and which only look like they do? I'd rather you discriminate than flag everything, because I can't revise on all eight. Rank the real tensions worst first, say for each what would have to give and what it would cost me in the book, and then tell me which single revision cleans up the most. Two constraints: don't tell me to make the machine conscious, I'm not doing that, and don't give me the AI safety lecture — I've read it, it isn't the problem I have.
