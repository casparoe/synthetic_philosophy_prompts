hey — I'm a novelist, ~200 pages into a book about a made-up island republic whose legal order is built to rewrite itself: ordinary statutes flip on a majority vote, the load-bearing bits of the Code need two-thirds, and a handful of clauses are "entrenched," meaning you have to clear an even higher bar to touch them *or* to touch the entrenchment itself. A friend got me hooked on a game called Nomic a while back (players take turns proposing changes to the rules of the game, including the rules about how rules change) and it's basically ruined me, because now every scene in the Assembly turns into a self-reference headache — and my writing group has started asking what the actual rule in this fictional system is *supposed* to be, and I've noticed my own gut verdicts dont line up with each other. So here are my verdicts; I want the principle behind them, if there is one.

1. Old rule: majority. Proposal: "from now on, amendments require two-thirds." Passes 54–46. Verdict: valid — the harder bar governs later amendments, it doesn't reach back and kill the vote that created it. 2. Old rule: two-thirds. Proposal: "from now on a majority suffices." Passes 58–42. Verdict: no good, it had to clear the bar in force at the time (which I realize is case 1 mirrored, and I'm answering the other way). 3. "Any amendment adopted with less than two-thirds is void" — itself adopted at 61%. Verdict: valid going forward, but it can't annul its own adoption; I'm much shakier here than in 1. 4. A single bundled proposal that strips the entrenchment off a clause and rewrites that clause in the same vote, clearing the higher bar. Verdict: cheats — do it in two votes, disentrench first, then rewrite — but it's the same people and the same numbers, so I can't say why I think that. 5. "For the purposes of this Code, 'majority' means two-thirds of those voting." Passes 55–45. Verdict: sneaky, I lean invalid, even though structurally it looks like case 1. 6. A poison pill: "if the Assembly ever lowers the amendment threshold below two-thirds, the Code is repealed outright and customary law revives." Later a properly adopted amendment lowers it to a majority. Verdict: the pill fires, the Code dies, and the pill dies with it, so we land in customary law — no paradox, fine. 7. Retroactive cure: a repeal failed 64–36 last year under the two-thirds rule; this year the Assembly, properly, by two-thirds, enacts "last year's repeal is deemed to have been validly adopted." Verdict: revolting, shouldn't work, even though procedurally it's spotless.

The only principle I can actually articulate is "a change is valid iff it satisfies whatever was in force the moment before it takes effect," which gets me 1, 2, 3 and 6 but overrules me on 4, 5 and 7, and I dont want to give up all three. Can you do better — a principle (or two rival candidates) stated sharply enough that one character could deliver it in a floor speech and another could immediately try to poke a hole in it, with the clause of each one tied to the case that motivates it, and an honest list of which of my verdicts it can't save? Please also say what it predicts for the case I keep dodging: the Assembly redefines who counts as a voter (enfranchises the outer islands, or disenfranchises everyone who owns property) and the *new* electorate then ratifies the change. And my partner says my verdicts are just a ratchet — thresholds go up easy, come down nearly never — and that I've dressed plain conservatism up as jurisprudence. Is she right, and if she is, is the ratchet defensible anyway?
