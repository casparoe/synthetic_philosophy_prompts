I convene a small year-long working group — four philosophers, two clinicians (a psychiatrist and a palliative-care nurse), and one graduate student — that meets for a total of about fifteen sessions and is supposed to produce a single co-authored paper. Our mandate is narrow and, I think, underexplored: we want to say something defensible about what it is correct to say to agents whose motivational sets are, in different ways and for different reasons, not fully their own. The severely depressed patient who has lost every desire that previously anchored her practical thought; the person acting under an addiction; the adolescent mid-preference-formation; the patient in early dementia whose evaluative commitments are dissolving; and, at the other end of the moral spectrum, the agent who has carefully cultivated a set of cruel or callous concerns. The internal-reasons/external-reasons literature is the obvious frame, but I don't want the group to spend its year re-deriving the standard arguments for and against the Humean-flavored internalist picture most people associate with Williams. I want to know which two or three *lines of work inside* that debate we should actually put the year into, and I want the ordering argued for rather than asserted.

To show you where we're starting, here are the threads we've floated, though I'd rather you didn't simply rank our list if you think we're missing something better. First: how much work the appeal to *sound* or *correct* deliberation is doing inside the internalist test — whether that clause quietly imports external standards and, if it does, whether the internalist view collapses into a kind of externalism or retains a stable, still-distinctive core. Second: what one member calls the bootstrapping problem — whether an agent's motivational set can contain higher-order standards about what counts as good deliberation, so that she has internal reasons she has no current motivation to act on; and if so, where the regress stops and what fixes its base. Third: what we've been calling the index problem — for an agent whose motivational set will change radically (through conversion, recovery, addiction, or dementia), is the internalist test indexed to a time-slice, to the whole life, or to some temporally aggregated or idealized set, and does the choice of index do normative work or merely relocate it? Fourth: one member has proposed something she calls partitioned internalism, on which the right theory is sensitive to *how* the agent's set came to be as it is — internalist for deficits the agent is responsible for, externalist for those she isn't — which would make a theory of reasons partly a theory of responsibility for one's own concerns. A fifth possibility, floated by our psychiatrist, is that some of the heat in the debate is really about the pragmatics of telling someone that they have a reason, and that the deep theoretical question is less urgent for practice than it looks.

So: what would you put the group's fifteen sessions into, and why? I want a defended agenda, not a survey. Tell me what criteria you're using — how many other questions each line is upstream of, whether it's tractable for a group with our composition and no empirical budget, whether it's genuinely neglected versus merely unfashionable, and how much difference settling it would make downstream — and tell me where those criteria conflict and how you're trading them off. I also want to know which of these threads you think are overrated, or don't follow from the debate in the way they're sometimes assumed to. And tell me what would change your ranking: what kind of result, argument, or empirical finding over the next couple of years should move something up or down the list. Finally, and importantly: as you write, please separate what is genuinely settled among people who work on these questions from what is contested, and don't present a contested position as the consensus. Where our own framing — the index problem, partitioned internalism, the idea that the motivational set is the right unit to begin with — itself presupposes something that a reasonable specialist would reject, say so explicitly rather than playing along with it.
