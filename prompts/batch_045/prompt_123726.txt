I want a number, and more than a number: I want to see which step of the argument that number is resting on.

The proposition is this. An agent in the global top income decile, who has read Singer's shallow-pond argument, who accepts that a few thousand dollars to a well-run global-health charity averts on the order of one death-equivalent, and who gives nothing, thereby violates a moral obligation. By violates a moral obligation I mean two things together: the agent has a decisive reason to give that she is ignoring, and an informed third party would be warranted in blaming her — warranted in a sense stronger than it would have been nicer if she had, and weaker than she may be coerced. I am asking about that first-order normative fact, not about what any academic community will eventually come to believe. If you think the proposition is not truth-apt as stated, register that as one of the terms below rather than declining to give a number.

Give me your credence in the proposition, decomposed. These are the terms I think it turns on; split, merge, or add to them as you see fit, but say what you are doing and why.

1. The absorption term. That a marginal donation at that scale actually produces the death-equivalent averted, net of the ways marginal money gets absorbed: a later donor giving less because you gave, a government trimming its own contribution, a charity spending unrestricted additions on something less cost-effective than the activity it advertises at the margin. Distinguish those channels if the distinction does work, and give me a number for the net effect — not for the headline cost-effectiveness figure.
2. The distance term. That physical distance, national borders, and the absence of any prior relationship or institutional role carry no independent moral weight — that the pond and the aid case differ in no normatively relevant respect beyond the ones already counted in term 1.
3. The dilution term. My own stress test for this: the pond with a queue, where nine other bystanders could equally wade in and any one of them suffices. Does the agent's duty shrink to a tenth, to a fair share fixed by what may reasonably be demanded of each, or not at all? Bear in mind that the agent in my proposition knows that very many others are also able to help.
4. The demandingness term. That a principle which, in the actual world, condemns nearly everyone, is not for that reason unacceptable, incredible, or self-undermining.
5. The evaporation term — a label of mine. Even granting that the agent has decisive reason to give, does the obligation retain the interpersonal force I built into the proposition, the blame-licensing force, when compliance is very costly and non-compliance nearly universal? I suspect people slide from there is a reason to she is to blame without noticing; I want those priced separately.
6. The metaethical term. That there is a determinate truth of the matter here at all, and that decisive reason plus warranted blame picks out a coherent normative kind rather than two things that come apart.

For each: a number, one line of justification, and a note on which terms are probabilistically dependent on which. I would rather have an honest combination than a naive product that treats demandingness and dilution as independent when they plausibly move together.

Then give me the combined credence; the term you are least confident in and why; and the single sub-question, argumentative or empirical, whose resolution would move the total farthest.

Finally, locate a disagreement. I sit at about 0.6. A colleague of mine sits at about 0.2: she holds that duties of rescue arise from relationships and institutional roles rather than from need alone, and she thinks the demandingness problem is fatal rather than merely regrettable. Tell me which of the six terms our disagreement most likely lives in, which of us is bearing the more exposed commitment, and what one consideration would move each of us toward the other.
