I am in an eleventh-grade philosophy elective, and last week we read Berkeley's idealism. I tried to turn his master argument — the one where any attempt to conceive of a tree existing unconceived seems to fail, because the attempt itself supplies a conception of a tree that is being conceived — into an argument against him, and now it seems to prove far more than I want. Here is the chain I built:

(1) To conceive of a thing is to have an idea of it before the mind.
(2) An idea exists only in being perceived; to be an idea is to be perceived.
(3) So when I set out to conceive of a tree that exists unperceived, what I actually end up conceiving is a tree together with my conceiving of it — a perceived tree. I have not conceived what I set out to conceive, and I cannot.
(4) If I cannot even conceive of a tree existing unperceived, then I cannot coherently believe that one does.
(5) So it is not coherently believable that trees exist unperceived.
(6) But Berkeley himself needs me to believe that the tree in the quad keeps existing at three in the morning, when no created mind perceives it; that is exactly what he says God's perceiving of everything secures.
(7) So if the master argument is sound, Berkeley's own account of the continued existence of sensible things is incoherent, and the argument destroys not only mind-independent matter but also the tree in the dark quad.

I would rather keep (3) than (6): the master argument feels right to me as it is usually presented, and (6) looks like something Berkeley plainly commits himself to. So I suspect the trouble lies elsewhere — perhaps in (1), perhaps in the move from (3) to (4), perhaps in some shift in the wording that I cannot locate. Which step should I give up, why did that step look safe, and what does each available repair cost me? In particular, I want to know what I am paying if I repair it by leaning on Berkeley's distinction between sensory ideas and his notions of minds and of their operations, and whether, once repaired, my chain still has any force at all against the claim that to be is to be perceived.
