I've got a works-in-progress talk next week (30 minutes plus about 25 of Q&A) in front of a criminal-law theory group: mostly legal philosophers who work on omission liability, complicity and the act/omission distinction, plus two metaphysicians who think omissions aren't events and so can't stand in causal relations at all. The thesis I'm defending is that the duty requirement the law bolts onto omission liability isn't tracking causation — what actually drives causal judgements about omissions is whether the omitted action was the normal or default thing for someone in that agent's role to be doing, and the duty talk is really a liability-limiting device that got mistaken for a causal constraint. The case I build it around is a lifeguard and a random sunbather, both scrolling their phones while someone drowns ten metres out. I also want to lean on a distinction of my own between first-order omissions (not doing X at the time) and what I'm calling second-order or meta-omissions (not arranging things so that you'd end up doing X — no alarm set, no note, no routine, no backup), and claim that a lot of negligence cases are really meta-omission cases that the standard difference-making accounts mishandle, because the thing that's a difference-maker and the thing that explains the harm come apart.
