A friend in a graduate seminar built the following argument against agent-relative constraints. Neither of us accepts the conclusion, and I cannot find the break.

The case is the familiar paradox-of-deontology setup, with the mechanics made explicit. Five other agents hold one innocent person each and will kill their hostage if and only if I do not kill mine; if I kill mine, all five refrain. Their threats are credible, causally independent of anything else I do, and my killing is the only way to prevent the five deaths. Assume also what is true in any realistic version of this situation: whether the practice of not killing innocents exists, persists, or collapses does not depend on my act.

(1) If an agent-relative constraint against doing X is justified at all, it is justified collectively: what justifies it is the security, trust, and predictability that agents produce when they generally accept and act on it. Call this the collective defense.
(2) So the strength of my reason to comply on a given occasion is fixed by what my compliance does on that occasion, given what others are in fact doing: the goods it produces or sustains.
(3) In the paradox case my compliance prevents nothing and produces none of those goods, while my violating the constraint prevents five killings. So there the constraint supplies either no reason at all or one outweighed by the five deaths.
(4) Scale the case up: fifty killers, five thousand, five million. If the reason is outcome-shaped, it simply grows with the number. So the constraint either never binds, or binds only up to some threshold. Neither is what an agent-relative constraint is supposed to be: a bar that holds whatever the numbers.
(5) Now the reverse horn. In the actual world the existence and stability of the practice does not depend on me: my compliance makes a negligible causal contribution to it whether the violation rate is one in a billion or one in three. So by (2) my reason to comply is negligible in every actual case, and by continuity it is negligible even in the ideal case where everyone else complies perfectly.
(6) The collective defense therefore cannot deliver what the deontologist needs from it. It yields a reason of roughly zero strength exactly where she thinks the constraint binds hardest, including the case in which she is the last complier left.
(7) Run the same collective reasoning one level up, about which norms agents should internalize and teach. Assessing acts by their consequences is fully compatible with holding that agents who deliberate by strict constraints rather than by that assessment both act better and bring about better outcomes.
(8) So the collective defense first dissolves every agent-relative constraint and then requires their reinstatement. Constraints are both unjustified and mandatory, which is incoherent.

Name the step where this goes wrong and explain why it looked as though it followed. If I must choose, I would rather abandon the collective defense than abandon the claim that the constraint holds even when I am the only complier, but I want the price of each option: what dropping (1) costs, what dropping (2) costs, whether anything weaker than either blocks both horns at once, and whether the two horns really stand or fall together.
