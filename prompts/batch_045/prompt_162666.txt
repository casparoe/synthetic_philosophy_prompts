I've just been put on the committee that will choose the handful of headline numbers my organisation's funding and bonuses will be tied to next year, so I've been reading up on Goodhart's law, and I'd rather test the framework against a case than collect warnings. Take Volkswagen's diesel scandal: as I understand it, roughly eleven million cars were programmed to run full emissions controls only under laboratory test conditions, so they passed the regulatory cycle while emitting many times the permitted NOx in real driving, and the gap surfaced only after an independent clean-transport group commissioned its own on-road testing, years in.

The taxonomy I've been working with — use it, but tell me where it breaks — distinguishes proxy decay (the measure stops tracking the goal because the world moved and the correlation was never structural), proxy gaming (agents change what they do so the number improves while the goal does not), and proxy falsification (nothing whatever improves; the reported number is simply made false). My instinct is that only the middle one is really Goodhart, that the third is ordinary fraud, and that the first is nobody's fault.

So: map the case for me — who was optimising, over what proxy, against what goal, under what competitive pressure — and say which box it falls into, or whether the boxes collapse into one. Then run it the other way: what the law predicts here that actually happened; what it stays silent about or misdescribes, such as the years before detection, the fact that outsiders rather than the regulator found it, and the usual remedies of multiple metrics and unpublished targets; and whether the episode supports reading Goodhart as an epistemic claim about proxies losing evidential value under optimisation or as a claim about what incentives do to agents.
