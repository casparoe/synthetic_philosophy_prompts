I co-chair the working group rewriting the code of conduct for the company formed by our merger last year. We have offices in Copenhagen and Singapore, and our mandate is to say whether we keep one global rulebook or let each site set its own. At our last meeting a colleague closed the discussion by repeating the proverb that the nail that sticks out gets hammered down, and took it to settle two things at once: that we should not try to carry Copenhagen's relaxed habits about working hours and after-hours messaging into the Singapore office, and that there is no further argument to be had about whether the stricter office ought to relax them. The room nodded. I want to know what, if anything, that sentence actually established.

Please treat the sentence itself as the object of analysis rather than as advice. Lay out, as a list I can take back to the group, the distinct precise claims it could be making — the descriptive ones about how norm enforcement works, the prudential ones, the coordination-theoretic ones, the ones about what a community may demand of newcomers or a guest of a host, and anything stronger still. For each reading, say whether it is close to a platitude, whether it is substantive and defensible, whether it is genuinely contested, or whether it is false or undermines itself when pressed. Then say which reading or readings would actually deliver my colleague's two conclusions, and what else would have to be true for them to do so.

Two complications I would like handled rather than smoothed over.

First, I do not trust "tight" and "loose" as single dimensions. I have been separating: how much conduct is regulated at all; how severe the sanction for breach is; how certain it is that a breach is noticed and sanctioned; and who is licensed to notice and punish, peers or designated managers. For each reading, say which of those variables it is really about. My suspicion is that several of the plausible readings concern only certainty of enforcement and the licensing of enforcers, and say nothing about how long the rulebook should be — which matters here, because the proposal actually on the table is to replace our long, sporadically enforced code with a short list that is enforced every single time it is breached. Say which readings recommend the short-and-certain rulebook, which recommend the long-and-flexible one, and which give us no guidance at all.

Second, the proverb pictures enforcement as something that simply happens to the nail, as if noticing deviance were costless. I would like to know which readings depend on that picture, how they change once monitoring is expensive and informal and selective, and whether any of them quietly assume an environment where tolerating deviance is unusually costly. I also want the asymmetry addressed: if the sentence supports leaving the Singapore office alone, does any reading of it equally support leaving the Copenhagen office alone, or is it doing work in one direction only?

On confidence: please keep separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on social norms and cultural variation, and do not present a contested empirical claim — for instance that tighter norm enforcement is an adaptation to ecological threat, or that it reliably buys coordination, order, or productivity at acceptable cost — as if it were established. Mark explicitly which parts of your answer I should not repeat in the meeting without hedging, and where you are extrapolating.
