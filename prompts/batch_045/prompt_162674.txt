I'm giving a 20-minute talk next month to the people who actually run citizens' assemblies for governments — about thirty deliberative-democracy practitioners — plus two councillors who've commissioned them, and one political theorist who works on epistemic arguments for democracy. The practitioners care about recuitment, representativeness and the conditions for good talk; the councillors care about mandate and cost; the theorist will want to know whether my case is epistemic or procedural. My thesis: the trouble with assemblies isn't that they're advisory, it's that they're advisory *and* one-off, so I'm proposing a standing chamber drawn by stratified lot with exactly one power — it can force the elected chamber to hold a recorded vote on any motion it passes, within 60 days, and can do nothing else. My three reasons: (i) the actual failure mode of assemblies is framing capture, since civil servants write the brief and the options; (ii) a permanent body with a real power would pull in a different, more confident kind of member; (iii) a compelled vote costs politicians almost nothing, so it's winnable. I'm also floating a second feature — a fresh random sample that votes the same motion having never deliberated, the motion carrying only if both pass — but I don't know that it survives this room.

What should I concede in the first two minutes, before someone else says it? And include the objection I'm hoping nobody asks.
