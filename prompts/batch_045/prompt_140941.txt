My thesis, sketch version:

1. Higher-order evidence never gives you a *new* reason to change your mind. It doesn't add anything to the scales. All it does is delete reasons you already had — specifically, the first-order reasons whose force depended on the reliability of the very capacity or process that the higher-order evidence calls into question. Call the survivors the "residue."
2. Your credence should be set by the residue alone. If the residue still supports P, then believing P while also believing that your belief that P was unreliably formed is not irrational at all. It's just what rationality looks like when the unreliable bit wasn't doing any load-bearing work.
3. So the famous cases — the exhausted driver, the third glass of wine, the one where someone creeps in and tells you your reasoning has been impaired — only *feel* like a special norm against akrasia because in those cases the residue is empty or nearly empty. People mistake "no case left" for "special prohibition."
4. Corollary: there's no rationally required *degree* of deference to higher-order evidence, and no synchronic coherence requirement between first-order and higher-order attitudes that's doing any independent work. There's just the arithmetic of what's left after deletion.
5. Where I think this earns its keep: it explains why my mum's combination isn't a logical or rational failure on her part, and why the thing I actually have to do isn't argue her out of it.
