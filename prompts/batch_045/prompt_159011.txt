I'm in the final semester of an MPP program, and I'm also sitting in on a graduate seminar in population ethics where last week we spent the whole session on the procreation asymmetry: the package of claims that a person's pleasure is good and their pain is bad, that the absence of pain is good even when there is no one who enjoys that absence, and that the absence of pleasure is not bad unless there is someone who is thereby deprived of it. The seminar split roughly in two. One group treats the asymmetry as simply what a person-affecting morality looks like: harm requires a bearer, benefits to be counted require someone who gets them. The other treats it as an unstable compromise, and thinks the honest move is a total or impersonal view that just sums welfare across whoever ends up existing, asymmetry be damned. I'm the weakest philosopher in that room and probably the strongest economist, so I want to try to settle the question for myself by forcing it through the only vocabulary I am actually fluent in, which is the vocabulary of applied cost-benefit analysis.

So here is what I'd like you to produce. Restate the asymmetry, and then each of those two rival treatments of it, as faithfully as you can in the working vocabulary I use every day: willingness to pay and compensating variation, consumer and producer surplus, Kaldor-Hicks compensation tests, distributional weights, the social discount rate and discounting across generations, and from the environmental-economics side, existence value, option value and bequest value, including how those are actually elicited in practice. Then run the translated machinery over two cases my group invented, which I think are harder than the standard one. First, the delayed-creation case: you can press a button that postpones someone's conception by ten years without changing the length or quality of the life they would otherwise have had. The asymmetry seems to imply that nothing is lost, since no one is deprived, and yet almost everyone in the seminar thought ten good years had plainly been lost. Second, the risky-creation case: you can either create a child whose life is a coin flip between quite good and quite bad, or create no one, where the asymmetry appears to license counting the downside but not the upside. Say what a CBA of each case would actually look like on the page, what numbers it would need and where they would come from, and whether the translation yields anything different from what the philosophers said in English.

What I care about most is the residue report, and I'd rather you reason it out than tell me who to read. Tell me which of the asymmetry's commitments translate cleanly into CBA, which translate only with distortion or after quietly changing the subject, and which cannot be expressed in that vocabulary at all. In particular I want to know whether a benefit that accrues to no one, or a harm whose subject never exists, has any possible expression as a line in a benefit-cost table, or whether the currency of CBA is constitutively defined over existing preference-holders so that such a line is not merely missing but unwritable. Please flag honestly anywhere your translation has smuggled in CBA's own assumptions and called them translation. Then give me your verdict on what the residue means: does it point to a real limitation of cost-benefit analysis as a decision procedure for policies like climate discounting, prenatal screening or pronatalist transfers, where the affected parties do not yet exist, or does it point instead to the philosophers having dressed up a bookkeeping convention as a substantive moral disagreement? I'd like something structured enough that I could hand it to my seminar instructor without embarrassment.
