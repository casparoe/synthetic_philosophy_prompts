ok so the guy who runs our Sunday meetup (read the Sequences twice, has opinions about subagents, owns more than one whiteboard) wants to add what he calls courage rounds to the monthly thing: after a couple of drinks we go round the room and everyone says the thing they'd normlly be too polite to say about someone else in the group. His pitch is straight in vino veritas — alchohol doesn't install beliefs, it just lifts the censors, so whatever comes out was already in there. Cheap epistemic upgrade, and because we'd all agree in advance that nothing said counts, there's no downside.

What I can't get past is that the same slogan gets run backwards whenever it's convenient. Drunk words are sober thoughts when it's an accusation about somebody else. But the moment it's a promise, an apology, a confession, or a decision you'd rather not own, suddenly that wasn't me, I was drunk, nothing that happens at the party is binding. Same analogy, opposite conclusions, chosen per case.

Meanwhile the one classics person in the group insists this all goes back to Plato on drunkenness. As he tells it, wine gets defended in the Laws as a way of testing and training character rather than as a truth serum, and it comes with conditions: a sober person in charge, nothing for people who are on duty, nothing for the young, and the point being to practise self-command rather than to extract confessions. He also says Plato wouldn't grant the premise at all, because on his picture the soul isn't one agent with a censor bolted onto it, it's competing pulls that get weighted differently, and wine just reweights them. I haven't read the Laws, I can't check any of that, and I'm not going to.

Our house style is basically the subagents thing anyway: no single real me, just subsystems whose vote shifts with blood alcohol, mood, audience, and who's sitting opposite you. On that view is that the real you? is just a badly posed question.

So please audit the analogy. I want the correspondence table first — what plays the censor, what plays the true self, what plays the confession, what plays the sober observer — then where it breaks: state-dependent recall, dose-response, the way drink raises confidence and mood rather than merely removing brakes, the fact that drunk speech is addressed to a room and shaped by what that room rewards, and the asymmetry in whose favour we deploy it. Then say which of those breaks actually bite for what he's proposing and which are noise. And give me a verdict: does either conclusion survive — that courage rounds hand you real information about what people believe, and that nothing said in them binds anyone? If Plato's test-and-train version is the better analogy, say what it would license (a sober chair, a dose cap, feedback only and no promises, debrief the next morning rather than in the room) and whether that rescues the protocol or quietly swaps it for something nobody proposed. British English, and don't send me to the text — my unread copy of the Laws can stay unread.
