I'm a junior at a public high school, and I've somehow ended up as the student representative on a small committee that has to make a recommendation to our school board. The issue is a large mural painted in the main hallway in the late 1930s, paid for at the time by a local immigrant fraternal society, which shows Columbus stepping ashore with soldiers and friars behind him and a kneeling Taíno man in the foreground. One group of students has circulated a petition asking the board to take it down, or at least to move it out of a hallway that every student walks through every day; a second petition, mostly signed by parents and alumni, says that we are erasing history and that whatever Columbus did, he was a man of his time. I've been asked to write the part of our one-page recommendation that responds to that second argument, mainly because I'm the person who said out loud in class that I didn't think it settled anything. Now that I actually have to produce the paragraph, I'm much less confident about why it doesn't settle it.

Two things people have said to me in the last week keep circling in my head, and I'd like help sorting out which of them is doing real work. My history teacher pointed out that we also credit people for seeing further than their own time: her example was Jefferson, who gets praised for writing the sentence about all men being created equal and, in the very same breath, gets some kind of allowance made for the fact that he held people in slavery, and she thinks we cannot have it both ways. My friend on the debate team put it differently — he says that if you and I had been born in Columbus's place, with his upbringing and his options, we would very likely have done what he did, and that if that's true we have no business condemning him. Both of these strike me as pointing at something genuine, but neither one feels like it answers the question the board actually has to vote on, which isn't about whether Columbus was a bad man so much as about whether our school should keep honoring him on a wall, and I can't explain the difference in a way that would survive being read out loud at a board meeting.

So I'd be grateful for your help with the shape of the argument rather than with a verdict. What exactly does the man-of-his-time defence establish, and what does it leave untouched? Is my friend's point — that we would probably have acted the same way — relevant to the question we're deciding, or does it answer a different question? And is there any principled way to tell the cases apart: to say when a historical figure's worst actions were really the sort of thing nobody in his position could have recognized as wrong, and when they were the sort of thing that plenty of people at the time, including some of the people who were there and saw it happen, did object to? I'd especially appreciate it if you could also flag the mistakes I'm most likely to make in writing this up, since I'd rather not hand the board something that sounds fair but quietly begs the question.
