I do comms and fundraising for a tiny harm-reduction nonprofit (syringe exchange plus a naloxone-by-mail program), and I also somehow got appointed the family member who is supposed to get my partner's dad to stop smoking. Those two things collided last weekend: my partner said some of what I do for work is manipulative and that I do the same thing to her dad, I said no it isn't, and then I couldn't explain the difference. I've been going through it case by case, going with my gut, and my gut is not consistent, so I'd like you to find the rule I'm implicitly using or tell me I don't have one. Here are the cases and where I land:

1. I told a donor that our matching gift ended at midnight when it actually runs to the end of the month. Money goes to the same place either way. Verdict: clearly not okay.
2. On the donate page, monthly giving is preselected and the one-time option is smaller and greyed out. Nothing is hidden, one thing is just easier. Verdict: fine, not manipulative at all.
3. We text lapsed donors one named person's story and his photo instead of the program's outcome statistics, which are more accurate about what we actually do. The story is his and the photo is real. Verdict: fine, but it's the one I'd least want to defend out loud, and I notice we shelved the stats version because it tested worse.
4. I'm paying my fifteen-year-old brother twenty bucks a book to read this summer. I payed him for the first three last week. Verdict: fine, though my partner says I'm buying him and I kind of see it.
5. Her dad smokes. For a while I kept his granddaughter's asthma action plan on the fridge next to the cigarettes, and whenever he lit up I'd bring up the kids. He never asked me to. Verdict: not okay — I stopped — but I can't say why it's worse than 3.
6. Except that once he said, don't let me smoke in the house, say whatever you have to. When I do it now it feels completely different: fine, even good. But that's odd — same guilt, same soft spot, just permitted.
7. Me, on myself: I deleted the food delivery apps, I put my running shoes in front of the door, and I tell myself the gym is basically on the way when it's a ten-minute detour. Verdict: all fine, arguably the sanest thing on this list. But by my own rule below I'm manipulating myself, which sounds like it should be worse than doing it to someone else, not better.
8. The thing that actually started the fight: our donation software now scores each supporter on stuff like loss aversion and how strongly they respond to a single identified person, then picks the subject line and photo for them automatically. I can't see which version anybody got; two neighbors could get different emails about the same program and never know. Verdict: not okay, or at least I want to switch it off. But I can't say why it's different in kind from 3, which I defend. Maybe it isn't, and I should stop both.

My tentative rule, made up on a walk: something is manipulative when it works by getting a grip on something in you that thinking clearly wouldn't touch — a habit, a fear, a soft spot, a number you can't really evaluate — rather than by giving you a reason you could still stand behind after thinking about it. It seems to get 1 and 8 right and 3 partly, but it also seems to say 2 and 7 are manipulative, which I don't think they are, and it has nothing to say about why 6 feels different from 5. My partner also uses "that's manipulative" as a conversation-stopper, and I suspect manipulative and wrong aren't even the same question — 4 feels a bit manipulative to me and also completely fine.

Three cases I haven't decided. (A) A nurse at our exchange tells a client, "a lot of people your age find it easier to cut down than to quit." True, and she picked it because she knows he'll hear "people like me manage this" when he's dead average on every measure we have. She's his nurse, he's her client, and she wants him to stay in the program. (B) My sister never argues with me when I'm dithering; she just asks, "what would you tell me if I was the one deciding?", then waits. The reasons I come up with are mine and I mostly believe them — but she chose the frame. (C) Same software, different setting: no psychological scoring, it just tries headlines at random and keeps whichever one pulls the biggest gifts, and after a few weeks it's using some sentence nobody at the office wrote and no one can explain, it just works. Nobody's soft spot is being modeled there, it's just optimization that's blind to reasons.

Please don't just hand me the names of theories — I want the rule stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case you haven't seen, with each clause traceable to the case that forces it, plus an honest note about which of my verdicts it can't save. Sections, in this order:

- "The three undecided cases" (~250 words)
- "What I'd have to give up" (~100 words)
