I run a monthly "hard problems" night at my local library — usually 12 to 18 adults, a total mix of backgrounds, most of them have never taken a philosophy or econ class, and we get 90 minutes in a room with a whiteboard, index cards and sticky notes. No projector, and I'd rather not have the participants on laptops (I can use one myself if I need to).

We've been chewing lately on how groups make decisions, and a few weeks ago we got into a genuinely frustrating argument about whether a group can even have a "will" at all, or whether the outcome is just an artifact of whichever voting method you happened to pick. I want to run a session that makes them *feel* the force of the result behind that — the one that says no ranked voting rule can give you all of the following at once: (1) anyone can rank the options however they like, no rankings thrown out; (2) if literally everyone puts X above Y, then X beats Y for the group; (3) whether the group prefers X to Y can't depend on how people rank some third option Z — no spoilers; (4) no single person's ballot just is the answer every time. I know this as the impossibility result about ranked group choice (Arrow's thing, and the Condorcet cycling stuff around it), but I do NOT want to name it or explain it up front. No lecture at the beginning at all if you can avoid it.

Here's what I'd like you to design.

The setup I have in mind, which you can improve or replace: they make a *real* decision with small but actual stakes (I can promise in advance that we'll really do whatever they decide — where the next meetup's food comes from, what topic we do next month, how we spend a small leftover budget). So the ballots aren't hypothetical. Then, roughly:

- A first vote under plain plurality on three options, done fast, before anyone is suspicious.
- A reveal that a fourth option was sitting on the whiteboard the whole time that nobody ranked first, added to the same ballots, re-counted, and the winner flips. I want them to have the reaction "that's wrong — nothing changed about how anybody feels about A versus B" before anyone says the word spoiler.
- A beat where they get to see that a group of perfectly consistent, transitive individuals can produce a group ranking that goes in a circle, and separately a beat where the group ranking is perfectly consistent but still flips because of an option that didn't win. I really care about these being *distinguished*: my sense is people mash together "the group is being irrational" and "the method is broken," and I want the activity to force them apart. If you think that's too subtle for 90 minutes with non-specialists, tell me and say which one to cut.
- A "design your own rule" round in small teams: each team writes a rule in plain English on one index card, and the other teams try to break it by handing in a made-up set of ballots. Score it somehow — the team whose rule survives the most attacks, or the team that breaks the most rules, wins something small (bragging rights, picking next month's topic). I want them to notice that every rule they invent either hands someone a permanent veto, or lets a losing option change the outcome, or ignores ballots — and that "just ask people how strongly they feel, on a 0-10 scale" is a dodge rather than a fix, since it changes what counts as input instead of solving anything.
- One device I really like but can't get to work: I (or a planted volunteer) act as a black-box "decision machine." Participants can hand me any set of ballots they want, as many times as they want, and I announce the group ranking. Their job is to reverse-engineer my rule by experimenting. Ideally they eventually work out that the machine has a secret dictator, or that it's ignoring everyone but one person, and only then do I reveal what the machine was doing. Can you make that actually run in a room of 15 people without it taking 40 minutes or feeling like a guessing game against the facilitator?

What I want back:

- The actual rules and materials, and who does what when.
- A minute-by-minute timeline for 90 minutes that tells me where to cut if we're running long.
- A short list of the very few things I'm allowed to say out loud before the debrief, and the phrasing. I will absolutely talk too much if you don't constrain me.
- The debrief: which moment in the room is supposed to map onto which of the four conditions, and how I draw that out of them rather than announcing it.
- What will predictably go wrong. Specifically: someone will say "so democracy is fake / voting is meaningless" and I want them to leave with something better than that — I'd rather they leave feeling there's no free lunch and that choosing a rule is choosing which flaw you'll live with. Also someone will propose flipping a coin, or a rotating random dictator, and I don't know what to do with that. And someone will say our sample of ballots is tiny so this proves nothing — which is definately fair and I want a good answer ready.
- Be honest about what the activity cannot show and I'll just have to state: the generality over *every possible* combination of ballots, the fact that all of this assumes the rule only sees rankings and not intensities, and anything else you think is out of reach for a game.

Please write your answer in American English, and keep it practical — I've got one room, one whiteboard, and alot of index cards.
