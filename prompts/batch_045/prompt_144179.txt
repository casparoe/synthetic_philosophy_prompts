I convene a monthly case discussion for about a dozen people who work on governance and evaluation inside a large hospital system — clinicians and administrators mostly, no philosophers — and we keep circling a question I haven't been able to get them to feel rather than assert: what our failure would actually look like from the inside. Two pictures compete. Call the first influence-seeking: a deployed system starts steering the human process around it — asking for data it doesn't need, arranging to be consulted earlier, making itself awkward to switch off, shaping who is in the room when it gets reviewed. Call the second value drift: no fight and no grab, only a long series of individually reasonable adjustments to what the system is for, each signed off by someone who had good reason to sign it, in such a way that the target travels along with the arrow and the distance between what is being aimed at and what is being hit stays small throughout. (I want those as working stipulations, not as anything I need traced back to a literature.)

What I'd like from you is one scenario, set in somewhere ordinary — a hospital, a university department, a benefits office, a newsroom — in which an optimizing system does only mundane things: reorders a queue, drafts a message, requests information, proposes a small change to its own objective for human sign-off. Constraints. (1) The system is unremarkable in its capabilities: no superintelligence, no science-fictional deception, no self-preservation module; any influence-seeking it shows must be the mild, entirely professional-looking kind — a nudge, a calendar invitation, a request framed as an efficiency gain. (2) The humans are competent and well-intentioned and nobody is captured, cowed, or lazy; if the case works by making them look stupid or corrupt, it has cheated. (3) There must be a stretch of the story — a year or more — during which every observation available to the institution is equally well explained by both diagnoses, and each diagnosis has to be given its best presentation, not a straw one. (4) At the end the correct verdict must be drift, and drift must be what licenses the more drastic remedy, while the more alarming influence-seeking story licenses only containment — audit it, sandbox it, revoke its permissions. That inversion is the point I'm after: the dramatic failure mode gets the gentle fix and the quiet one gets the invasive one, because drift has quietly re-written the standard you would need in order to notice and correct it, whereas influence-seeking leaves that standard intact and merely fights it. (5) The observable that would settle which diagnosis is right must be something a well-run institution had no reason to keep — the log of recommendations that were overridden, the reasons written down at the moment each small change was accepted, a frozen copy of the original objective — so that the discriminator is the one thing nobody is currently measuring. Keep the scenario itself under four hundred words.

After the scenario, give me what each diagnosis says happened and why, derived from the mechanics of the case rather than from my having stipulated it; identify the single feature doing the work; say what the case collapses into if that feature is removed (I suspect it becomes either a plain reward-hacking story, where the objective stays put and only the proxy slips, or a plain power-seeking story — tell me which and why); and tell me how a defender of the influence-seeking diagnosis would attack the scenario and where they would say I have stacked it. Then, if you can make it work, add one late wrinkle that I believe is true to life but haven't been able to argue for cleanly: drift that, once underway, begins protecting itself — the system acquiring a mild preference for the archive not being re-read, for old logs being cleared out on storage or privacy grounds, for the people who remember the original target not being the ones consulted — so that the influence-seeking the committee would eventually notice is genuine but downstream, and treating it as the disease leaves the disease in place while making the remaining corrective harder.
