Write a satirical internal memorandum of roughly 600–700 words, formatted as a departmental notice (To / From / Subject line, numbered clauses), from the "Office of Foundational Compliance" of a university mathematics department to its faculty. The Office is announcing that no proof may invoke a choice principle until an approved Choice Impact Statement has been filed under a three-tier schedule: Tier 1 (countable choice and dependent choice) is pre-approved and needs no paperwork; Tier 2 (the ultrafilter lemma, a.k.a. the Boolean prime ideal theorem, and the results obtainable at its price — Hahn–Banach, Tychonoff for compact Hausdorff spaces) requires a short form; Tier 3 (unrestricted choice, Zorn's lemma, the well-ordering theorem, and anything of the same logical strength, including the assertion that every vector space has a basis) requires full committee review.

The jokes have to come from the mathematics itself and from the collision between genuine calibration facts and bureaucratic form-filling. Work in the following, without ever explaining them to the reader:

- Zorn's lemma, the well-ordering theorem, and the axiom of choice are equivalent over ZF, so a colleague who files three separate statements for one proof is committing a paperwork offense.
- The ultrafilter lemma sits strictly between ZF and full choice: the compact Hausdorff case of Tychonoff is a Tier 2 expense, while Tychonoff in general costs Tier 3.
- Tier 1 is cheap and covers most routine analysis, but it will never yield a non-measurable set; the Office's preferred pilot environment, Solovay's model of ZF plus dependent choice in which every set of reals is Lebesgue measurable, cannot be procured, because obtaining it requires an inaccessible cardinal, which exceeds the department's purchasing authority.
- Banach–Tarski duplication (a ball reassembled into two from as few as five pieces) is a reportable event: the surplus ball must be logged as a capital asset and stored, and the storage budget is already overspent.
- Choice is usually invisible, so the Office audits proofs for undeclared use; a de minimis exemption is available only where a canonical choice exists — shoes, not socks.

Invent one short precipitating incident for the memo to respond to, keep the institutional voice deadpan throughout, and include a single indignant quotation from a functional analyst who insists his theorem only ever needed countable choice. Never break character or flag the satire; a set theorist should be able to tell that the author knows which principle buys which theorem. Please write in American English.
