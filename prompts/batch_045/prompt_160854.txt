I'm a retired groundwater hydrologist — thirty-odd years of contaminant transport modelling, mostly — and since I packed it in I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the university. We wandered into population ethics a few weeks ago and I've been chewing on it ever since.

Here's what I want help with. I made what I thought was a boring point: with the same technology and the same consumption per head, a much bigger population means much less room for everything else — more of the landscape paved or ploughed, more pressure on the sinks we dump into, aquifers and the nitrogen cycle and the atmosphere. One of the group, a cheerful pronatalist, answered with the old Julian Simon line, people are the ultimate resource, and said it like that closed the matter. It doesn't close it for me, but I also can't say exactly why.

So: what are the different precise claims that one sentence could be making, with verdicts on each? My own stab, which you should feel free to scrap or add to:

1. A causal claim about ideas: more people means more minds, more minds means more innovations, and the gains eventually outweigh the extra mouths, so growth makes the people already there better off too, not just the total bigger.
2. A substitutability claim: there's no fixed natural constraint that actually binds, because ingenuity substitutes for whatever runs short — so resource-limit worries are always wrong, not merely sometimes overstated.
3. An axiological claim: a world with more people in it is, other things equal, a better world. This is where total versus average versus person-affecting views and the different-people problem come in.
4. A threshold claim, which is my own hobbyhorse and the one I never hear anyone state out loud: the worry isn't about maximizing at all, it's about falling below some minimum scale needed to keep a complex civilization running. How many people does it take to sustain a semiconductor supply chain, a research university system, a language with living native speakers, a power grid that somebody still knows how to repair? Below some number things may not degrade smoothly — they become unmaintainable, and you get a one-way ratchet. Note that this reading has nothing to do with bigger is better: it's about a floor, not a ceiling.

For each reading: is it a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or something false or near enough false? And the bit I care most about — which of them would actually do the work he was making it do? Which reading, if true, would genuinely defuse my point about sinks and landscape? My suspicion is that none of them do, and that he was using a claim about ceilings and about total output to rebut a claim about floors and about per-head impact. But I want that spelled out rather than assumed.

Two things to watch, and they're the reason I'm asking instead of just reading:

- Stock versus flow. People in this discussion slide constantly between population size and population growth rate, and between fertility is below replacement and the population is shrinking. As I remember it, a population can keep growing for decades after fertility drops below replacement, because of age-structure momentum, and a big stationary population is a completely different animal from a growing one of the same size. So for each reading say whether it's about a level, a rate, or a rate of change of a rate — and if you're unsure about the demographic facts, say so rather than inventing numbers.
- No citations and no reading lists, please. I'm not writing anything up and I couldn't check them anyway. Formal notation is fine — sums, integrals, discounting, I can follow that — but I'd rather you didn't guess at empirical magnitudes.

And the thing that irritates me most in this literature: for each claim, keep clearly seperate what's settled, what's an open empirical question where you'd need data you can't see (tell me what data would settle it), and what's contested among people who actually work on population ethics or growth economics. In particular, don't quietly install total utilitarianism as the default framework or treat the repugnant conclusion as anything like a consensus view, and be willing to say outright if a lot of people in the field think the whole question is a larger population better is ill-posed to begin with. I can tell when something is being handed to me as settled when it isn't, and I stop reading.
