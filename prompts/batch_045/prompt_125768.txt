I run a fortnightly reading group of about fifteen machine-learning researchers — fluent in probability, almost no philosophy — and I have one ninety-minute session, whiteboard only and no pre-reading, to get them from zero to the result that an evidential decision theorist, in a world large enough to contain many agents relevantly similar to itself, can be rationally required to cooperate in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma against a stranger. I don't need the lecture written; I need the plan for it.

Give me the order in which the components have to arrive and why each depends on the ones before it: the gap between an act's evidential bearing on an outcome and its causal influence on it; the one-shot dilemma and why plain dominance reasoning says defect; the population picture, in which your decision is a draw from a distribution over agent-types and so tells you something about the other draws; and the threshold inequality, in closed form, relating how strong the similarity-based correlation must be to the ratios in the payoff matrix. Then give me the one example that should carry the whole session, together with the constraints it has to satisfy and what each buys: no predictor or Newcomb-style apparatus, no repetition and no shadow of the future, no literal duplicates — similarity must be graded and statistical — and the correlation must be something the audience can read off the setup rather than take on stipulation.

I expect a specific set of misconceptions and want each defused at a stated point in the sequence, not at the end: that this is the iterated dilemma under another name; that the agent is treating its own action as bringing about the other's; that exact copies make the answer trivial because there is really only one agent; that the agent can manufacture the correlation by deciding; and that the result either vindicates evidential decision theory generally or is explained by the world being large, rather than by the correlation structure it happens to contain.

Tell me where to place the case I take to be the real test: a population that is half mimics, who do what you do, and half contrarians, who do the opposite, with you unsure which you face, so that the correlation nets out to zero. What is that case for, and what should the group be able to say about it when they leave? Finally, give me one closing question whose answer separates someone who has the mechanism from someone who has only the slogan that your action is evidence about what similar agents do, and say what I should openly decline to settle in ninety minutes — including which decision theory is correct, and what an agent who is already certain what it will do is even supposed to condition on.
