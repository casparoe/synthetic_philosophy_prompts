I maintain the backend for an open history reference project — volunteer work, on the side of a normal engineering job — and I've inherited the job of rewriting the rule our editors use to decide whether a claim gets tagged as fringe. The tag matters more than it sounds: it changes where the article ranks in search, and our corpus gets pulled into retrieval and fine-tuning pipelines, so a bad rule doesn't just mislabel a page, it launders nonsense into other people's systems. I have no philosophy training, and the arguments on our mailing list have convinced me that the disagreements are almost never about the evidence for Atlantis or ancient-astronaut explanations of monumental architecture or Fomenko's rebuild of the whole chronology; they are about what sort of thing a demarcation rule is even supposed to be. So before I let the list argue further I want a specification, and I want it written for a fixed purpose: the rule has to be applicable by a generalist editor, working from an article and whatever is linked from it, without resolving the underlying historical question and without specialist consultation. Both errors cost something real — tagging a responsible-but-heterodox hypothesis suppresses work that might be right, and failing to tag a confident fabrication gives it our house style — so please keep that purpose in view when you decide what counts as a requirement.

What I want is the requirements list: the cases any adequate rule has to get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, the results from how historical inquiry actually works that it can't contradict, and the reason behind each. The distinctions I keep tripping over, to get you started but please don't limit yourself to them: false-but-ordinary-error versus pseudohistory; a heterodox hypothesis argued inside the discipline versus one argued from outside it; a bad method that landed on a true date versus a good method that landed on a false one; a claim that is protected by cheap auxiliary hypotheses every time it is pressed versus one that is genuinely untestable in principle; and tagging a whole research program versus saying whether anything inside it is still worth crediting — the salvage case, where someone chasing a lost continent nonetheless produces the only careful survey of a site anyone has. Flag for each requirement whether it is non-negotiable or a prejudice of one camp; I strongly suspect some of the things people demand are just preferences wearing argumentative clothing, and in particular I want your view on three candidates: that the rule must be decidable by a non-specialist in an afternoon, that it must track evidence rather than who believes what, and that political or nation-building utility counts as a marker at all. If some subset of the requirements can't be jointly satisfied — say, decidability by a generalist, sensitivity to real evidence, and symmetry between insiders and outsiders — say so explicitly and show me the conflict; that is the part I most want to see. And tell me which single requirement knocks out the most candidate rules.

Three rules have been proposed on the list and I'd rather you judged them as candidate requirements than as theories, since I can't tell which is which. Priya's Double-Standard Test: build the mirror case, with the same evidence structure but believed by the right people, and if your rule stops firing it was only ever sociology. Marcus's Vindication Condition: there must be a specifiable set of observations that would get a tagged claim untagged, and it must not be empty. Sam's Replacement Rule: a claim counts as fringe only when it discards an established finding while offering no account of why the accepted evidence looks the way it does. Test them, and the requirements generally, against a case I made up for the purpose: a set of dressed stone terraces on an inland hillside, dated by mainstream archaeology to the first millennium BCE from pottery in the upper layers, which a group instead attributes to a seafaring culture two thousand years earlier, on the grounds that the tool marks on the lower courses look like metal tools; that the deep radiocarbon samples came back too old and were discarded as groundwater contamination; that a local flood epic mentions stone steps to the water at that spot; that no settlement of the right date exists nearby, which they read as evidence the builders came from elsewhere; and that the heritage ministry has refused permission to re-date, which they present as suppression. Every rule I try either flags it for reasons that would also flag a contested ordinary dig, or bogs down on the contamination question, which neither I nor the rule is competent to settle. Please don't give me citations, a reading list, or a survey of the literature — I can't chase references and neither can the system I'll run this on, so argue from the cases themselves. If one of these three is just a restatement of something with a name in the philosophy of science, tell me in one line and move on; keep any scoring of existing proposals to a few lines, because the criteria are the deliverable.
