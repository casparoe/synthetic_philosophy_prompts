I've been reading on my own (no classes, just stuff I find online and in the odd popular book) about Ulysses contracts and the idea of constitutional precommitment — the bit where a person or a government deliberately ties its own hands now so that its later self can't do the thing it knows it'll be tempted to do. I think I get the basic picture, but I keep worrying the whole thing is held together by an analogy that's doing more work than it should, so I want to try it on a real case.

The case I have in mind is Argentina's convertibility plan, 1991 to 2002. My understanding of the facts, and please correct me if I have any of them wrong: after years of runaway inflation (thousands of percent at the peak around 1989, if I remember right, and repeated currency collapses before that), the government passed a law in 1991 that fixed the peso one-to-one to the US dollar and made the central bank hold dollar reserves backing the money it issued, so it couldn't just print. Inflation more or less collapsed. The peg held for about a decade through a bunch of shocks, then things got worse and worse in the late 90s, there was a freeze on bank deposits at the end of 2001, and it ended with default, devaluation, and the peg abandoned in 2002. I don't need the economics explained to me as such; I want the philosophical read.

So: analyze this as a Ulysses contract, and then tell me where the model breaks. I'd like an actual mapping rather than a general vibe. Who exactly is Ulysses here, and who are the sirens — is the temptation the political temptation to print money, or the electorate's demand for spending, or something else? What is the mast and what are the ropes, concretely? Who are the rowers with wax in their ears, i.e. who got bound without having consented to anything? And what would count as straining against the ropes versus actually getting untied, since a real rope either holds or doesn't but this one seems to have held in some sense right up until the moment it didn't. Then say what the theory predicts the actors should have done at the points where it mattered — in 1991, in the mid-90s when the peg was already painful, and in 2001 when everyone could see where it was heading — and what it explains about the episode versus what it totally misdescribes.

Two things I've been chewing on that I'd like you to take seriously rather than wave away. First: in the original story Ulysses binds *himself*, for the sake of his own long-term good. But the Menem-era government that tied the knot in 1991 wasn't the government or the electorate stuck with it in 2001, and a constitution or a currency law mostly binds people who never agreed to it and may not share the aims of the people who wrote it. That seems like a completely different moral situation — closer to one person handcuffing a stranger than to self-binding — and I suspect the precommitment literature slides between the two whenever it's convenient. Is that fair? Does the analogy still do any work once you insist on the distinction, and if so, which of the theory's conclusions survive?

Second: I take it the standard justification for a Ulysses contract is weakness of will — your future self will *want* the wrong thing for bad reasons, so you stop it now. But there's a different thing that looks identical from outside, which is that your future self has learned something you didn't know and reasonably changed its mind. A central bank that wants to devalue in 2001 might be akratic, or might just have figured out that the peg was a terrible idea, and the theory as I've met it doesn't seem to have a clean way to tell those apart — it mostly just says the rope is there precisely so that wanting out doesn't count. Relatedly, Ulysses's arrangement has no escape hatch, and that's the point; but this case makes me think every sane precommitment needs a release valve built in advance by the cool-headed self, and I can't tell whether adding that makes the theory better or just dissolves it into ordinary judgement. Also, the peg didn't only change what Argentina's future governments could do, it changed what lenders and investors did — apparently they lent much more freely because they thought the hands were tied. That feedback isn't in the simple picture at all.

Be honest about where precommitment theory is silent here instead of forcing it to have an opinion, and tell me plainly whether this episode is a win for the Ulysses framing, an anomaly, or a case it was never built for.
