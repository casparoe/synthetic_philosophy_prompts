I run a platform infrastructure group at a mid-sized software company, and for the past two years my side project has been an internal forecasting market for our own roadmap questions — whether the data-centre migration will be finished by the end of Q3, whether the new storage layer will hold up under ten times current load by November, whether we will fill at least four backend roles before the hiring freeze. Participants stake a small amount of play money, the company acts as counterparty and absorbs the net losses, downside is capped, and we sometimes feed the resulting prices into the planning decisions those prices are about. I have never studied philosophy; I picked up the forecasting and market-design material on my own, so please write for someone who is comfortable with probability and Bayes' rule but who does not know the literature's vocabulary, and don't spend time re-explaining the standard results I already lean on — the no-trade results for traders who share a prior, the point that agents with a common prior cannot agree to disagree, and the point that a market which fully revealed everyone's private information would remove anyone's incentive to acquire it. No citations, no reading lists, no bibliography: I only want the structure of the problem, not the sources.

I'm writing up the rationale for an internal engineering-wide doc, and a friend who did a philosophy degree read a draft and said my story is a pile of commitments that can't all be true at once. He won't tell me which part he'd attack, and I'd rather not be fobbed off with "these are all basically compatible." So here is where I think I've landed, as precisely as I can put it. (a) When I have no private information of my own on a question, I take the market price as my own credence — full deference, not partial: market says seventy per cent, I say seventy per cent. (b) When I do have a private signal — I actually know the state of the migration code — I keep my own number rather than moving toward the price, even after I've looked at the price, on the grounds that the price aggregates everyone else's information and mine isn't in it. (c) I read the no-trade results as telling us something substantive about real markets: the volume we observe is far larger than rational traders with genuinely different private information could generate, so most of it has to be explained by overconfidence or by people simply having different priors, and that's a claim about actual traders, not a remark about a model. (d) I also think the shared-prior assumption those results use is best read as a modelling convention — a stipulation that all differences in belief are to be traced to differences in information — rather than as an empirical or psychological claim that traders really do start from the same prior. (e) I think it's worth paying people to go and gather information, and that careful forecasters modestly beat the market over time, even though I also think the market price is very hard to beat. (f) I think the errors our own traders make are correlated rather than independent: they share managers, incentives and culture, they are systematically optimistic about their own projects, and insiders are reluctant to place a public bet against their own manager's deliverable. (g) I don't think our payoff structure biases the price, since the subsidy and the capped downside are the same for everyone. (h) I think it's fine to include questions whose answers our own decisions partly determine, and fine for the price on such a question to be one input into the very decision it's about. And one concrete case: last quarter, on whether the migration would finish on time, I had no private information, the market settled around thirty per cent, and I still told my team to plan as if it would finish — my reasoning being that the price was being dragged down by participants who enjoy betting on failure and by optimistic insiders declining to bet against their own project. I want to keep both that verdict and (a).

What I want from you is an audit rather than an essay. Go through the set and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look that way on a first pass — I'd guess at least one of my "tensions" is spurious, and I want you to say so rather than flagging everything — then rank the real ones by severity, and for each say precisely what would have to be given up and what that would cost me elsewhere in the set. Finish by naming the single revision that would clean up the most at the least cost, and by telling me whether my migration verdict is one my own principles can actually deliver or whether I was quietly relying on something I haven't stated.
