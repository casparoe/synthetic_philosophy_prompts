I'm on the oversight board for my county's pretrial services, and we're rewriting the policy that says how our risk instrument gets validated, and I keep running into the same shape and I want somebody to say out loud what we're committed to.

The instrument spits out a number, roughly a probability that someone released before trial will fail to appear. Two requirements do most of the work in the policy. The first is blindness: race isn't an input, and neither are the obvious stand-ins, so no zip code and no tract-level demographics, and the vendor certifies that. The second is calibration within group: among all the people the instrument scores at, say, 0.3, about thirty percent of them actually fail to appear, and that has to hold separately within each racial group and not just in the aggregate. On top of that our auditors publish false positive and false negative rates by group and treat any gap in either one as evidence something has gone wrong.

Three things about this bug me. One, we forbid the model from looking at race and then judge it almost entirely by race-conditional statistics. I get that those aren't literally inconsistent, but it seems to presuppose something about what fairness is supposed to be a property of. Two, we run the same instrument a second time to hand out slots in a diversion program, which is a benefit rather than a burden, so a false positive now means denying someone help instead of detaining them, and the policy applies exactly the same metrics and the same thresholds in both uses, as if an instrument could be certified fair in the abstract before anyone says what it's for. Three, and this is the one nobody on the board wants to touch, within-group calibration needs a group variable and we have four of them: self-identification at booking, the officer's classification on the arrest report, whatever scheme the original validation study used, and the census categories our thresholds were set against. Which one you pick changes what a given person's score would have to be for the instrument to count as calibrated, and nothing in our policy says which is the real one; in our own data the choice moves some people's required score by more than the entire disparity we've spent two years arguing about.

There's also a standard result I've run into a couple of times, that short of a perfect predictor or identical base rates you can't have within-group calibration and equal false positive and equal false negative rates across groups at the same time, which I take to mean our policy is either asking for something impossible or asking for a choice we never admit to making.

So what does this package actually presuppose? Does it presuppose that fairness is a property of a number rather than of an allocation, that groups are found rather than made by the booking process, that blindness at the input and awareness at the audit are aimed at the same target? Which of those is the practice genuinely stuck with and which is it merely comfortable with, and where is it just underdetermined, so that several different views would license exactly what we already do? And if we dropped the blindness requirement, or took seriously that fairness depends on what the score is used for, what would concretely have to change in how this policy gets written? I'd much rather you commit to a position on where the real tension lies and on what we should do about it, and tell me how confident you are and what would change your mind, than get handed a menu of options. And please write it as plain continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just something email-length I can forward to the board.
