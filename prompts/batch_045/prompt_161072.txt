Build me a pair of cases about advance directives that flip the usual verdict in the direction people find hard to construct.

The default intuition is that the more carefully the earlier self anticipated her later change of heart and tried to bind herself against it, the stronger the directive's claim on us. I want the opposite. In Case A, the naive directive: the earlier self writes something like no aggressive treatment if I can no longer recognise my family, without really thinking about how she'll adapt; she does adapt, and the later self is contented and wants to live. Verdict wanted: honor the directive. In Case B, the evidentially sealed directive: the earlier self explicitly foresees that after the onset she will adapt, will report being happy, and will ask to be kept alive, and she writes the clause precisely to discount that future testimony — don't listen to her, that's just adaptation, that's not me. That is the directive everyone takes to be most authoritative, and I want it engineered so that it is the one we should override.

The mechanism I have in mind, fix it if you think it's wrong: B's clause is written so that no possible future evidence could count against it, which makes it less like a considered judgement about her own good and more like an akratic bit of self-binding, a refusal to ever learn anything; A's directive rests on a prediction that turned out false, but at least its content stays answerable to how things actually turn out.

Constraints: keep both to one paragraph each, realistic — hospital, dementia, family at the bedside, nothing science-fictional. The later self in both must be genuinely doing well, not confused, not depressed, stable preferences that really do contradict the directive. The earlier self in both is competent, uncoerced, and deciding for her own sake rather than out of worry about being a burden. Hold identity metaphysics fixed across the two cases: same degree of psychological continuity in each, so nothing can turn on she's a different person now.

After the scenarios, run each of three views over each case: the whole-life authority view, on which a person gets to govern her life as a narrative whole and precedent autonomy beats the later self's current wants; the time-relative interests view, on which what matters is the welfare of the person who exists at the time of treatment; and a psychological-continuity view, on which authority tracks how much continuity and connectedness there is between the writer and the patient. Show that the verdicts fall out of the views rather than being stipulated, and it's fine if a view has to bite a bullet somewhere.

Then finish with three short things: which single feature of the design is doing the work; what happens to both cases if I strip that feature out; and how a defender of whichever view comes out worst would attack the pair.
