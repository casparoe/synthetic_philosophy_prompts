**Cheap worlds: modal realism's missing measure**

Quick recap of the target so we're on the same page. Lewis: worlds are concrete, actuality is indexical (my world is actual for me the way here is here for me), and the principle of recombination says, roughly, that you can patch together parts of different possible worlds and get another possible world — nothing runs out, there's no cost to a rearrangement. The defense is openly cost-benefit: you pay in ontology, and you get paid back in theoretical utility — modality reduced to plain quantification over worlds, a semantics for counterfactuals in terms of similarity, and no need to fake up possibilities out of actual-world materials, which is what leaves the ersatz views unable to represent alien properties or anything our world's stock of universals and predicates can't model.

Here's the bit I haven't seen made much of. Recombination being free means the plurality is absolutely lousy with what I'll call cheap worlds: for any world W, and any qualitatively specified region you like, there's a world that matches W bit-for-bit throughout our region and differs only somewhere far off. (Pedantic note: Lewis thinks a world is a maximal spatiotemporally interrelated whole, so a region with no spatiotemporal ties to ours is a *different world*, not an extra island bolted onto ours — which is fine, because separate worlds are exactly what I need, but flag it if something downstream quietly needs them to be islands in one world.)

Two consequences.

First, counterfactuals. The semantics picks the closest antecedent-worlds. Cheap worlds let you satisfy any antecedent at nearly no cost in overall similarity: hold our region fixed exactly, make the antecedent true out in the boondocks. A world where the antecedent is true *here* costs you a local divergence and probably a small violation of law; the cheap candidate costs you a comparably tiny region of mismatch somewhere else. By Lewis's own criteria — maximize the spatiotemporal region of exact match, minimize violations — the cheap worlds win or tie. And the whole teeming infinity of them is available for every antecedent, with what happens out there entirely unconstrained by what happens here. So the consequents stop tracking anything local. Lewis is happy to say similarity is vague and that's why counterfactuals are vague and context-dependent; maybe that absorbs this. But the cheap-world ties aren't a localized bit of helpful context-sensitivity, they're degenerate and everywhere.

Second, measure. Everything anyone wants to do with the plurality *after* the reduction — chance and the Principal Principle stuff, expected utility over ways things could go, decision theory, the anthropic/cosmology measure stuff people here have done to death — needs a measure over worlds, not merely a domain to quantify over. Recombination hands you a domain with no measure on it, and as far as I can tell no interesting cardinality fact either. Quantification works fine; counting, weighting and expecting do not. The application layer is unfunded.

So propose a fix: take the quotient. Count indiscernible duplicate worlds once — let the measure live over qualitative characters rather than over concreta. Call it the quotient measure. I want to argue that this is a *problem* for Lewis rather than a friendly amendment, for two reasons.

One: it doesn't deliver. Quotient a continuum-sized plurality of worlds that differ along continuous parameters and you haven't got a counting measure, you've got a set of equivalence classes that still needs a measure defined on it. The problem moves up a floor instead of out of the building.

Two, and this is the part I actually care about: weighting worlds by their qualitative character is precisely what ersatz worlds were *for*. Lewis wanted concrete worlds because of abundance — possibilia you couldn't model out of the materials this world happens to supply. But once your measure only sees qualitative types, the concreteness does no work at all. You paid for a warehouse full of concrete duplicates and you're reading the catalog. Which means the ledger has to be redone: the feature that buys the benefit (abundance, free recombination, no upper bound) is literally the same feature that writes the bill (no measure, ties everywhere). It's not that modal realism is expensive; it's that the purchase and the price are the same object.

What I'm not saying: not that modal realism is crazy, not the familiar "how could we ever know modal facts" complaint, not skepticism about alien properties. I'm saying the theory's post-reduction applications require structure the theory doesn't supply, and the natural place to shop for that structure is somewhere Lewis can't follow.
