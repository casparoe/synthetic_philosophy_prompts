In analytic number theory and its philosophy, a debate about the epistemic status of heuristic arguments for the distribution of the primes has continued for decades without resolution, and I would like a diagnosis of why it has stalled rather than a verdict on which side is right.

On the heuristic side, the starting point is a probabilistic model: one treats the indicator that an integer n is prime as if it were a random event of probability about 1/log n, and one presumes a certain independence across different integers or across the values of several polynomials, subject to a correction. The naive Cramér model implements this for a single sequence; the Bateman-Horn conjecture (Paul T. Bateman and Roger A. Horn, 1962) implements it for systems of polynomials, predicting that the number of simultaneous prime values up to x is asymptotic to a product of local densities, the singular series, times an integral that reflects the naive independence heuristic. The classical prime k-tuple conjecture is the linear case. These heuristics have been strikingly successful: they generate the main conjectures about prime patterns, and several of their consequences, such as the existence of infinitely many bounded gaps between primes, were later proved by rigorous methods that did not simply formalize the heuristic.

On the rigorist or formalist side, the complaint is that the "probabilities" involved are not probabilities over any genuine random process: the primes are a fixed deterministic sequence, and the naive independence assumption is not merely unproven but false in detectable ways. Maier's theorem (Helmut Maier, 1985) is the canonical counterexample: for intervals of length about (log x)^A with A > 1 fixed, the normalized count of primes has limit superior greater than 1 and limit inferior less than 1, so the Cramér model's prediction of a limiting value fails. The standard reply is to calibrate the heuristic by inserting the singular series, which encodes local divisibility correlations; but this calibration is itself conjectural, and no general derivation of Bateman-Horn from a precise mathematical principle is known.

Meanwhile, a partial formalization exists in a few cases. The Green–Tao theorem replaces the presumption of independence with a surrogate: the primes are shown to be a dense subset of a weighted pseudorandom majorant, and a transference principle then imports Szemerédi's theorem. As one summary puts it, "The Green–Tao theorem, proven by Ben Green and Terence Tao in 2004, states that the sequence of prime numbers contains arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions." The rigorous replacement for independence is not a naive product measure but a demand that the majorant satisfy a linear forms condition and a correlation condition, with the relevant uniformity measured by Gowers norms; later Green–Tao and Green–Tao–Ziegler work, including an inverse theorem for the Gowers norm and orthogonality of the Möbius function to nilsequences, extends this machinery. Crucially, this is a formalization for specific linear patterns, not a general license for the calibrated-independence heuristic.

To make the diagnosis precise, I want to introduce a distinction between two things that I think the debate often runs together. Call the first calibrated local independence: the practice of taking a naive independence heuristic and correcting it by explicit Euler products or singular series that encode correlations modulo small primes. Call the second majorant pseudorandomness: the Green–Tao-style requirement that a dominant weight for the primes satisfy higher-order conditions such as the linear forms condition, the correlation condition, and Gowers uniformity, so that a transference theorem can be applied. My hypothesis is that the decades-long impasse is not a single disagreement but a tangle of three separable issues: (i) a verbal or methodological difference between using "probability" in the context of discovery, to generate trustworthy conjectures, and demanding a context of justification, a formal license for the calibration; (ii) a substantive but hidden formal question, namely whether every calibrated local independence heuristic for polynomial patterns can be underwritten by some majorant pseudorandomness theorem, perhaps conditionally on standard conjectures such as the Elliott–Halberstam or generalized Riemann hypotheses; and (iii) an empirical or structural question about whether Maier-type irregularities have higher-order analogues that prevent any fixed majorant from satisfying the needed conditions, in which case the search for a general formalization would be misguided.

Please diagnose the actual structure of this dispute. Is it primarily verbal, a fight about the word "probability" or "independence"; substantive, a real disagreement about mathematics that could be settled by a theorem; a difference about standards of evidence for what may be conjectured versus what may be believed; a clash of bedrock intuitions about randomness in deterministic sequences; or two research programs answering different questions under one name — for example, calibrated local independence as a heuristic engine versus majorant pseudorandomness as a rigorous surrogate? In your diagnosis, be fair to what each side actually says: heuristicists do not deny Maier's theorem or the utility of singular series, and rigorists do not deny that the heuristics produce conjectures that later turn out to be true. Then, given your diagnosis, say what would count as genuine progress: a general transference theorem deriving the Bateman-Horn conjecture from a Gowers-uniformity or linear-forms/correlation-condition hypothesis; a classification of the patterns for which calibrated local independence can be thus underwritten; a proof that a higher-order Maier obstruction blocks such a derivation in general; or a convincing argument that the question is ill-posed because the heuristic and rigorous projects optimize different epistemic aims and no bridge should be expected. If you think no progress is possible or desirable, explain why, and say what the two communities should instead agree to disagree about. I am not asking for a bibliography or a survey of the literature; I want the structure of the disagreement and the shape of a way out, or an argument that there is no way out.
