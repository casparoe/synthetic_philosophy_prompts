I write about consciousness for a science desk, and nearly every piece I do now runs into the same wall. One camp says an animal, a patient under anaesthesia, or a large model is conscious, and points at what the subject does or says. The other camp says the behaviour and the reports would be word for word the same if nothing at all were felt, so nothing follows. What I never see stated is the standard: what a story about how felt states stand to judgments and reports about them would have to get right before it could tell me whether a self-report is evidence of anything.

That relation is what I want the specification for. Not a tour of the positions, and no history of the debate: a requirements list, written for a stated purpose. The purpose is this — I need the theory good enough to license or refuse to license an inference from self-report to consciousness in subjects very unlike us. Good enough to tell a reporter what she may conclude from a press release about an octopus or a language model; not good enough to settle the metaphysics.

So: give me the desiderata. For each one, the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and a verdict on whether it is non-negotiable or the preference of one camp. Three cases I want forced into the list, because I do not think they are standard and I suspect a theory's treatment of them separates the ones that are doing work from the ones that are gesturing.

The first is slow drift. Suppose the felt character that accompanies a given neural state type rotates gradually over a subject's lifetime, the way a colour wheel might slowly turn, while everything causally downstream stays locked to the neural state: the reports, the discriminatory abilities, the wincing, the memory of how it felt last year. Nothing changes behaviourally, nothing is available to a third person, and the subject's own judgments track the neural state rather than the felt one, so that at sixty she says the wine tastes exactly as it did at twenty and means it. Is this a coherent possibility that a theory must explain away, or a confusion it must dissolve, by showing that felt character cannot come apart from the state's causal or nomological role in that manner? Either answer is acceptable. What is not acceptable is silence about what the correlation between felt character and report is supposed to rest on, if the first answer is right.

The second is the unhurried judge. Consider a being that feels pain but forms the judgment that it is in pain only when prompted, and forms it by noticing its own guarding behaviour and its incipient inclination to say ouch — by self-observation, that is, rather than by being struck. Its judgments are perfectly reliable. Does an adequate theory owe us an account of the difference between this case and the ordinary one, or is the difference theoretically inert, in which case judgment is doing less work in the standard arguments than everyone assumes? I want to know which of the usual desiderata collapse if the answer is inert.

The third is borrowed authority. Take subjects who are perfectly reliable about their own states but for whom the reports carry no more felt weight than a readout on a monitor: no sense of privileged access, no sense that they could not be wrong. Is explaining the felt authority of first-person report a genuine requirement on a theory, or is it a prejudice — a datum that only looks like a datum because of what we already believe about causes?

Against those cases, adjudicate the demands actually made in this neighbourhood, and tell me which are real and which are camp loyalties: that the theory respect the causal closure of the physical; that it not render consciousness nomologically idle; that it be compatible with consciousness having been shaped by natural selection, or else explain why that is not required of it; that it make room for the double dissociation of judgment from felt state without stipulating the dissociation away; that it not quietly collapse into eliminativism, or into identity claims asserted rather than argued; that it yield verdicts in the hard cases — anaesthesia, non-human animals, artificial systems — without a fresh stipulation for each; and that it be statable without invoking phenomenal properties inside causal explanations, which I cannot tell whether to count as a discipline or a confession.

Two closing things, and they are the parts I most want. First, if some subset of the requirements you endorse cannot be jointly satisfied, say so and show where the contradiction bites: a list that turns out to be internally inconsistent is itself the finding I would run. Second, name the single requirement that does the most work in excluding candidates, and say what it excludes. A short scorecard — three columns, no more — for the view that the judgment is constitutively rather than causally tied to the state it concerns, the view that self-knowledge is always inference from one's own behaviour, and the view that there is nothing phenomenal to be reported, is welcome; keep it brief, since the criteria are what I am paying for.
