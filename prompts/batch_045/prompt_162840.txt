My sister teaches high-school history and I've been helping her rewrite a unit on why things happen — her students keep turning in "causes of X" essays that are just five bullet points in a row with no structure, so we started arguing about what a good answer even looks like, and I've ended up somewhere I didn't expect. I now think that any historical outcome big enough to be worth explaining is overdetermined. The version I actually defend goes like this: for any event E a historian singles out, if you run what we started calling a redundancy audit — list the candidate causes, then for each one ask what happens if you remove it while holding the background conditions fixed and check whether E still turns up under a description you'd count as roughly the same thing happening — you'll find at least two independently sufficient routes to E, not just a pile of necessary conditions that bolt together into one route. "Independently" matters to me: two routes only count if cutting one doesn't also cut the other, because they often share a hidden enabling condition, and routes that would have produced E but were never actualized ("shadow routes" in our notes) count too. So monocausal stories aren't merely incomplete, they're structurally wrong, and my sister has started telling her students that asking for the cause is a category mistake. She's worried this makes the whole unit unteachable, and honestly so am I.

So I want the case against it, and I want it blunt. I've noticed I can win any argument by loosening the description of E until a second route appears — if a regime falls ten years later, under a different general, by a different mechanism, is that "the same event"? — and a friend says that means I've defined the view into unfalsifiability. Tell me what would genuinely count against the overdetermination thesis, sorted from fatal to merely awkward, including things that have arguably already happened (the quantitative turn in economic history, with research designs that try to isolate one factor and hold the rest fixed and sometimes report large single-factor effects, is the one that actually nags at me). I want you to separate what a committed overdetermination theorist should concede as a real defeater from what they'd rightly wave off, and I especially want the ones I can't see: places where the audit's own machinery — the "hold the others fixed" clause, the independence requirement, the reliance on counterfactual routes nobody observed — does all the work and might be doing it badly. Also say plainly whether the view collapses into something its rivals already accept (plain multi-causal explanation, necessary-conditions-plus-background-conditions pluralism, or just "be careful"), because if it does that's worse for me than being wrong. And if you think it has been qualified into unfalsifiability, say whether that's actually a problem for a thesis of this kind, or whether a methodological rule like "find a second route before you publish a one-cause story" can be insulated and still be worth teaching fourteen-year-olds. I'd personally be most moved by a worked audit on a very well-documented case where only one route survives even at a generous grain, or by an argument that the procedure is biased toward finding redundancy.
