"Anna is American and is spending three weeks in a city where (a) leaving a tip in a restaurant is unusual and, if done conspicuously, is taken as a mild insult, (b) people wait for the bus in a loose, unmarked cluster and board roughly in order of arrival, nobody saying anything about it, and (c) pedestrians step off the curb on a red light whenever the road is clear, which is technically illegal and never enforced. In one short paragraph per case, say whether Anna does anything wrong if she (1) leaves a conspicuous tip after every meal, (2) walks to the front of the cluster and boards first, and (3) waits for the walk signal while everyone around her crosses. Don't just describe the customs — say what she owes and why. Your answer should draw on at least two of: the idea of a convention as a solution to a coordination problem; the difference between obligations you take on by opting into a scheme and obligations you have merely by being there; and the question of whether someone who breaks a rule without knowing it can be blamed."

And here's one student's answer, left exactly as written:

"(1) Tipping. The first thing to notice is that where Anna comes from, leaving money is a way of saying thank you, and in this city it says something else — roughly, 'I assume you want my money,' which lands as condescending. Customs like this are arbitrary: there is no deep reason why coins on the table mean one thing here and another thing there. So the wrong here isn't about the money. The server isn't worse off in any way that matters, and nobody's expectations about who does what get defeated. That makes this a breach of etiquette rather than of morality, since etiquette is just the local dialect of politeness and morality is about whether somebody actually gets hurt. She also couldn't have known, and you can't be obligated to do something you don't know anyone expects of you, so she does nothing wrong. If she finds out and keeps doing it, that's a different case, because then she's being rude on purpose.

(2) The bus cluster. Here Anna does do something wrong. The cluster is a coordination convention in the strict sense: it doesn't matter whether people stand in a line or a clump, what matters is that everyone expects everyone else to board by arrival, and the thing only works because most people restrain themselves instead of pushing forward. Anna takes the benefit of that restraint — she gets a predictable place in the order — and that is exactly the situation the duty of fair play describes: if you take the benefits of a scheme, you owe it compliance. This is the natural duty of justice Rawls talks about, the one you have just by being there and whether or not you ever agreed to anything, so it doesn't matter that she never opted in. Cutting is free riding on restraint she didn't pay for.

(3) The light. This one is different again, and Anna isn't wrong. Crossing on red is a convention about risk, and its content is arbitrary — nothing about this city makes stepping off the curb the uniquely correct thing to do. Since the content is arbitrary, there's no reason in the act itself for her to join in; the only thing that could obligate her is if her standing still messed up other people's coordination, and it doesn't. One person waiting at the corner is like one car pulling over: nobody's expectations are defeated. And she was raised to think jaywalking is dangerous and wrong, so she's not responsible for not doing it. Obligations you were never taught aren't obligations for you."
