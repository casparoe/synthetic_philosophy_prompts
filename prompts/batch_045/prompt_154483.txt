I'm a novelist, not an academic, and I'm working on a book partly set in the 1920s Black arts world, so I keep bumping into this Du Bois slogan—you know the one, roughly that all art is propaganda, or his notorious line about not caring a damn for any art that isn't used for propaganda. I'm fuzzy on the exact title/date/venue (I think it was some 1926 talk or essay, maybe in The Crisis?), but I don't need a citation; I just need the conceptual lay of the land.

Here's where I met it: in my writing group, alot of the political writers throw the slogan around, and its starting to feel like a conversation-stopper. A fellow writer—she writes very on-the-nose activist plays—used it to defend herself when I said her new play felt too preachy. She said, more or less, that Du Bois already showed all art is propaganda, so there's no neutral aesthetic high ground; every novel or painting is pushing some line, and the only real question is which side it's on. So the slogan was being used to shut down my criticism that her work is too didactic, by collapsing the difference between propaganda and non-propaganda art and making propagandistic into just a description, not a flaw.

Meanwhile, in my own outline I've been playing with a distinction I partly made up (so tell me if it's silly): propaganda of content versus propaganda of address. Propaganda of content is the explicit message the work states—vote for X, believe Y. Propaganda of address is who the work treats as its imagined audience, who it thinks is in the room and worth addressing; a work could be formally open, not stating any slogan, but still flatter or ignore or constitute a certain public. My novelist brain wants Du Bois's slogan to be about propaganda of address, not just content, because that's the reading that would make my character's quiet, ambivalent illustrations political without being preachy. But I'm not sure the slogan can bear that, and I'm definitely not sure it's what my writing-group friend meant.

My half-baked theory is that she's sliding between a very weak reading—something like every artwork has political effects or can be read as taking sides—and a much stronger reading—something like the only legitimate art is art that self-consciously serves a political movement, or that aesthetic quality just is propaganda value. If that's right, the slogan feels deep because the weak reading is true and the strong reading is the one doing the argumentative work.
