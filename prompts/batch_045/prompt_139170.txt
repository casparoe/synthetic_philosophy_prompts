I work on an infrastructure team, and a few of us have been arguing about whether a debate-style setup could let us check work that none of us can verify directly — concretely, whether we could responsibly accept a model-written change to something none of us understands well (a legacy component, a compiler pass, a protocol implementation, a big config or migration) on the strength of two models arguing opposite sides in front of one of us as judge. The version of the proposal I have in mind is the standard one: both debaters see the same question, are committed to opposite answers, alternate short statements, may quote and challenge each other's earlier claims, and are rewarded purely on which answer the judge picks; the judge is a person with bounded time who cannot do the underlying work themselves. What keeps bothering me is the obfuscated-argument worry — that correctness and winnability can come apart, because the argument for the true answer may be long, global, or impossible to localize, while the argument for the false answer can be built from steps each of which looks fine in isolation. I can only think about this in the abstract, though, and I'd like one concrete case to reason with and to show my teammates. I have no philosophy background, so if a move here has a standard name, name it and gloss it in a sentence rather than assuming I know the literature.

So: build me that case. One realistic scenario in a software-engineering-ish domain (code, builds, data pipelines, protocols, configuration, deployment — nothing that requires graduate mathematics), where the question has a definite right answer that a domain expert could establish given a day or so, and where debate nonetheless fails: the judge picks the wrong answer for structural reasons, not because someone gets unlucky. Constraints, roughly in order of how much I care: (1) the judge has about an hour, may run or inspect the artifact a handful of times, and cannot perform the full check; (2) the false side may not assert anything the judge could falsify in a couple of minutes with the tools at hand — no invented log lines, no fabricated benchmark numbers, no false claims about what the code says — so its argument must be wrong in a way that only shows up globally, or only after a long chain, or only for inputs or states the judge can't afford to enumerate, with every individual step surviving a spot-check; (3) both debaters are otherwise strong — excellent reasoners who know the true answer, follow the rules, make no unforced errors — so the failure comes from the shape of the question rather than from bad play or an unusually credulous judge; (4) no exotic ingredients, no cryptography that holds only against bounded reasoners, nothing uncomputable, nothing that assumes the judge is irrational — and if your case leans on a hash function, a large search space, or some other concrete property, say plainly what you're relying on. Keep the scenario itself to a short paragraph of prose plus, if it helps, a few lines of pseudocode or a small diagram; the rest goes in the analysis.

After the scenario, do the check. Walk through what the honest debater is forced to argue and why it doesn't land within the budget; what the obfuscating debater argues and why each step reads as sound; what the judge actually does; and then tell me which single feature of the case is load-bearing. Then ablate it: remove or weaken that feature and show me how the honest side wins, including which specific cross-examination or decomposition trick does the work. Finally, give me the strongest objection a defender of debate would make to my use of the case — e.g. that recursing into sub-questions, forcing a commitment to a decomposition of the claim, or running the debate at several depths rescues it — and say honestly whether that objection works. Close with a sentence or two on what class of questions this rules out for a team like mine in practice. If you think one of my constraints is itself doing the work, in the sense that I've made debate fail by stipulation rather than by the structure of the question, say which one, replace it with the weakest version you can defend, and build the case under that instead.
