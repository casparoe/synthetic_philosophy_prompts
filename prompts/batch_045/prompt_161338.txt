In our constitutional reform group we are drafting a proposal for a second chamber chosen by lot: 300 members drawn from the electoral roll, serving staggered three-year terms with a third replaced each year, supported by a permanent secretariat of officials, and free to decide for itself which government bills it examines. Whenever I press worries about competence or accountability, one member answers with the same move: it is only a jury. We already entrust the gravest decisions about a person — guilt, imprisonment, in some jurisdictions life — to twelve people taken essentially at random, with no mandate, no expertise and no electoral check, and nothing about that seems absurd to us; so, he concludes, the standard objection to sortition collapses, and rotation is all the safeguard against a political class that we need.

I would like the analogy audited rather than either waved through or waved away. Please give me the correspondence table — what plays the judge, the charge, the evidence, the verdict, the appeal, the twelve — and then the places the mapping fails: juries answer a question someone else has framed for them, are instructed and supervised by professionals, are corrected by higher courts, are deliberately selected for ignorance of the case, and disperse once they have answered; the chamber we are proposing chooses its own business, sits continuously, is stratified so as to mirror the population rather than to be impartial towards a single defendant, and is corrected by nothing but the next draw. Say which of those failures matter for the use he is making of the analogy and which do not, and say plainly what the jury picture is still able to carry — that ordinary people can deliberate competently on a bounded question, that no electoral mandate is required, that no term limit is required — and what it cannot. If a jury is the wrong picture for a standing, agenda-setting chamber, suggest a better one and say what it makes visible that the jury does not. Please answer in British English.
