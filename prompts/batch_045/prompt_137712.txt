Hi — I've been trying to work out what I actually think about weighing pleasures off against pains, and I've ended up with a pile of verdicts and no principle. Can you have a go at pulling one out of them?

Ground rules so we don't talk past each other: I'm only asking how good or bad outcomes are, comparing outcomes with the same number of people in them, ignoring rights, consent, desert, who caused what. I'm not a full-on negative utilitarian — I do think pleasures count for something — but I keep coming back to the thought that past a certain line, pain is the only thing that matters. And I've tried saying the comparisons just aren't well defined; that only relocates the problem, so I don't want it as an answer.

My verdicts, going on gut. Pain intensity on a 0–10 scale, 10 being the worst pain there is.

1. Mild toothache for one afternoon, for one person, vs a thousand people each getting one tiny static shock off a doorhandle — noticeable, faintly unpleasant, gone in a second. The toothache is worse. Below the line, stuff just adds up.

2. Mild toothache for an afternoon vs one really good slice of lemon meringue pie. The pie wins. At low intensities pleasures and pains trade off fine.

3. A wrist being set without anaesthetic — call it three minutes at 9 or 10, the worst pain this person has ever had — vs any finite number of people each getting one mild momentary itch. The wrist is worse, and I'll stick to that at a thousand, a million, a trillion. I'll admit the trillion version makes me grip the table.

4. Same wrist, but the person gets a genuinely lovely cup of tea afterwards, vs the wrist and no tea. The second is better — but here's the thing, the tea feels like it counts for almost nothing, much less than it would on a normal day. Which I think contradicts what I said in 1 and 2.

5. One minute at 10/10 vs two minutes at 9.9/10. The two minutes is worse.

6. Two minutes at 9.9 vs four minutes at 9.8. The four minutes is worse. And I'll keep going: eight minutes at 9.7, and so on down. Which means that, chaining it, something like eight hours of a dull 5/10 ache comes out worse than one minute at 10/10 — but that's exactly what 3 denies, because on my view anything above the line is supposed to beat any amount of anything below it. 5 and 6 are my two irreconcilable ones, I think.

7. The single most intensely pleasurable minute available to a human being vs one mild stomach ache. Obviously take the bliss, the ache is nothing. But I notice I have no urge at all to say the bliss beats any amount of mild ache — it doesn't feel like there's a line on the pleasure side doing the same work. Maybe that's just me being inconsistent.

8. Same 4/10 ache in two people: one has lived with chronic pain for twenty years and barely registers it, the other has never had worse than a headache and finds it frightening and all-consuming. I want to say the second one is worse off, but I also want to say that what matters is the pain itself, not how much the person minds it. Not sure.

9. A patient under anaesthetic shows every neural signature of a 9/10 pain but never experiences it; someone else, absorbed in a video game, has a 9/10 pain and doesn't notice until the match ends. I say the first doesn't count at all and the second counts fully. Which makes me wonder whether the thing I'm tracking is really intensity, or something more like how much of a person's attention or mental bandwidth the pain takes up.

The principle I've been toying with, to be tested and repaired rather than admired: there's a threshold, and a pain crosses it just when the person having it would, right at that moment, hand over any finite quantity of future mild pleasure to make it stop now — so the line is fixed by the shape of the sufferer's own trade-offs, not by a number on a scale; above it, pains are lexically prior to everything below; below it, pleasures and pains are commensurable and aggregate; and where two outcomes tie on above-threshold pain, everything below the line breaks the tie at its full normal rate.

What I want: the principle stated properly, clause by clause, with the case that forces each clause; an honest list of which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and which one you'd overturn; and its verdicts on three cases I haven't given you — an enormous number of extremely small pains, a pain that ramps from 3 to 9 over an hour versus a steady 6 for the same hour, and whether the same line is doing any work for non-pain bads like grief, humiliation or boredom. Don't bother with references or a reading list, I can't look anything up and I don't want them anyway. And please don't just hand me a calculus with a number in it; I want the structure.
