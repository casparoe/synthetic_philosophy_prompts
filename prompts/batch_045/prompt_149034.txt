Please engage with two distinctions I've been playing with, and tell me straight if you think either one is bad:

1. The scramble test. Take the amino-acid → base binding-preference table (the thing that says cop goes for a particular base, that lpu/rpu hunt out the leftmost/rightmost purine, that mvr and mvl just move the enzyme along the strand) and permute it — randomly, or adversarially. Then ask what survives: do self-copying strands still turn up, does the shape of the reachable-strand set change, does anything worth calling the isomorphism still go through? My worry is that everything anyone has found is an artifact of one hand-tuned table and would evaporate under scrambling, in which case a lot of what looks like a discovery is really a report about a single random draw. But I cant tell how expensive that question is to actually answer.

2. Shallow quines vs lineage-stable replicators. A strand whose translation yields an enzyme that copies that very strand — once, under ideal conditions, alone in the soup — is one thing. A strand that keeps getting copied while mutations accumulate and while other junk strands compete for the same enzymes is quite another. I want to know which of those the isomorphism is really about. If it's only the shallow one, then the self-reference story looks much thinner than people let on: it's the fixed-point construction, and all the biology-flavored vocabulary is decoration. If it's the second, the analogy might be saying something about robustness that the bare Gödelian version doesn't.
