I am a senior at a large public high school, and I run a philosophy club that meets once a week for a 55-minute lunch period. Attendance is usually between fourteen and eighteen students, mostly 16 to 18, and the group is uneven: a few of them read ahead, several are there because it looks good on a transcript, and at least two are there for the snacks. I cannot assign homework for a club meeting, and I cannot rely on anyone having thought about the topic beforehand.

The topic I want to do next week is why promises bind. I got into it through an ordinary irritation: during group projects, people say "I'll have it by Thursday" all the time, and it seems to matter a great deal whether they also said "I promise" — but I cannot say clearly what the extra words are supposed to have done. It is not obvious to me that promising adds anything at all, since I already ought not to let people down, and it is also not obvious why saying certain words should be able to give another person a kind of hold on me.

What I would like from you is a runnable activity for that session: a game, structured simulation, or staged sequence of real choices in which the students actually make promises that cost them something small, and then run into the difficulty themselves rather than hearing me describe it.

Constraints on the design:

No lecture up front, and no theory vocabulary until the debrief. I do not want the words "convention," "expectation," "authority," "normative power," "practice," or the names of any philosophers or views in the first forty minutes. The point should arrive through what the rules make happen.

The stakes have to be real but tiny, deliverable inside the session or within the following week, and free of both money and embarrassment: things like giving up a seat, running a two-minute errand, forfeiting a turn, or bringing something small on Friday. I need a concrete way to make the cost bite without anyone being shamed if they break.

The rules, roles, or incentives should generate at least three of the following four difficulties as things the students actually encounter, not as questions I put to them afterward:

1. The redundancy worry: what, if anything, does saying "I promise" add to "I fully intend to" or "you can count on me"? I want them to feel the gap between someone merely expecting something of you and someone having a special standing to demand it of you.

2. The release asymmetry: the person you promised can let you off, but asking to be let off is itself awkward, and being released does not feel the same as never having promised. Both the power and its limits should show up in play.

3. Conflicting promises: promising the same thing to two people, or promising one person something you have already promised another. I want the group to discover that it has no settled rule here — not "first in time wins," not "the more important one wins."

4. The contentless promise: "I promise I'll do whatever you ask me to this week, whatever it turns out to be." At least one promise should be made before its content is known, and should then be met with a request that is genuinely unwelcome but not harmful or degrading.

Two further cases I would like built into the structure somewhere, because I think they separate views that otherwise look identical: the assignment test, where the person you promised says "don't do it for me, do it for Sam instead," and we ask whether redirecting a promise is the promisee's to decide in the way that redirecting a debt would be; and the unowned release, where someone promises the whole club something and no single member can cancel it. I have tried to make both work as a "promise board" with cards and as a simple release round, and in both versions the students just shrug, so I need a better mechanic.

Please also give me: a minute-by-minute timeline for the 55 minutes; the full rules and materials; a debrief of ten to fifteen minutes that maps specific moments in the activity onto the "it is just a useful social convention" answer, the "it is about expectations and not letting people down" answer, and the "promising hands someone a limited authority over you" answer, saying for each which moment is its best evidence and which moment it handles worst; an honest account of what will predictably go wrong — I am especially worried about ironic or joke promising, about someone refusing to participate, about promises made that cannot in fact be kept, and about the deflationary move that it is all just feelings — with a way of handling each; and a frank statement of what the activity cannot show and I will have to say myself, for instance whether the bindingness depends on the practice actually existing in a community or could have been otherwise.

I do not need reading suggestions or sources. I want the session plan itself.
