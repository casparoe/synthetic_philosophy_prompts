me and two friends have been running a tiny translation reading group for about a year now -- two poets, one retired latin teacher, me (i just like this stuff) -- and we just finished working our way throught Hofstadter's book on translation, the 1997 one built around dozens of re-translations of that short Renaissance-French poem by Clément Marot addressed to a sick young woman, where he keeps trying to hang onto rhyme and meter while changing everything else. Great book, but every single session ended the same way: someone says that version isn't faithful, someone else says faithful to what, and then we argue from vibes for an hour and go home. So I want a spec instead of vibes.

What I want the theory for, so adequacy has something to be relative to: given a source text and a candidate translation, it has to be able to tell us what got carried over, what got dropped, and what got smuggled in -- and it has to be able to do that *without* collapsing it into one overall verdict, i.e. no single fidelity score, no "translation B is better than translation A" as the basic output. Multidimensional accounting or bust. It also needs to be able to refuse: to distinguish this text is untranslatable in a way that matters from you guys just haven't tried hard enough yet.

So: write me the desiderata list. What does an adequate account of translation fidelity actually have to deliver? For each requirement give the case or consideration behind it -- the thing that made people demand it -- and mark it non-negotiable or this is a prejudice of one camp. Here's the pile of cases I want covered, plus whatever I'm obviously missing:

- a nonsense poem where the invented words do most of the work, so there's barely any propositional content to be faithful to
- a poem where the form *is* the content: say a poem about memory fading whose lines get shorter and shorter and whose rhyme words lose a syllable each stanza, so the shape carries the meaning
- a text where one single ambiguity is the whole point, i.e. a pun that a translation must either resolve (destroying it) or replace with a different pun
- a contract, a dosage instruction, an aircraft checklist -- where getting the spirit right and the letter wrong kills somebody, and where free adaptation is malpractice
- dialect and register: a novel where a character's ungrammatical speech is doing class-marking work, and the target language's repertoire of class-marked ways of talking just isn't isomorphic to the source's
- subtitling and dubbing, where there's a hard external budget (syllables, reading speed, lip movements) that forces loss whether you like it or not. Does a theory that can't even represent a budget count as defective, or is that a separate engineering problem?
- intralingual and non-verbal cases: the same poem rendered into 1590s English, or into a conlang someone invented, or turned into a piece of music. Must a theory cover those, or is restricting to interlingual translation a legitimate scoping choice? I genuinely don't know which way this one goes.
- texts with no settled source: a folk ballad with forty variants, a collaboratively edited manual, a text that came out of a language model. What is fidelity to, when there's no single authorial intention and no single fixed original?
- a sacred text, where the community's practice treats certain words as non-substitutable and part of the reader's experience just *is* that these are the same words again

And here's the bit I have no vocabulary for and care about most, so give it a real requirement and not a shrug. Call it the compensation-licensing condition: sometimes the rhyme won't survive, so a translator saves the line-endings with heavy alliteration instead, or keeps the meter but drops a metaphor and puts a different image of similar emotional weight elsewhere in the stanza. Some of those moves feel like genuine trades -- you lost something and you *paid* for it -- and some feel like a bribe, an unrelated nice thing nobody asked for. The theory has to say which substitutions count as legitimate compensation and which are just free goodies, and I want the principle behind the distinction, not a list of examples. Two riders on this: can a translation be better than its original and still be a translation, and if so what is better cashed out as? And can the theory represent two translations as incommensurable -- neither worse, just different -- as opposed to one being straightforwardly worse?

One more I want ruled on: does the claim that no language is a cruder or less subtle version of any other belong on the list as a hard constraint any theory must respect, or is it just professional etiquette?

Then, and this is the part I most want: mark which subsets of the desiderata can't be jointly satisfied. I have a suspicion that preserve the sense, preserve the form, and read like something a native speaker would actually write form a trilemma, and that be faithful to what the author was up to and be faithful to what the text did to its first readers come apart in a way no theory can paper over -- but do it properly and find the ones I haven't thought of. And tell me which single requirement on your list eliminates the most candidate theories.

Short scorecard at the end, and I mean short -- a few sentences each, no table, I'll make my own: the letter-fidelity camp (Nabokov-ish, literal to the point of perversity, footnote rather than adapt), the reader-experience camp (Nida-ish dynamic equivalence, the target reader should be moved the way the source reader was), and the form-fidelity camp (Hofstadter-ish, the music is the thing, rewrite as freely as you need to keep it). The list is the deliverable; the scoring is just so I can see whether the list discriminates at all.
