I have been working through a chapter on divine providence in a popular introduction to philosophy of religion, and it presents Molinism in a way I half recognize from other things I have read but cannot fully evaluate. The chapter summarizes the position like this:

Molinism holds that God has three kinds of knowledge, ordered not in time but in logic. First, natural knowledge: knowledge of everything that could possibly happen. Second, middle knowledge: knowledge of what every possible free creature would freely choose in any situation it might be placed in — true counterfactuals of freedom, such as the claim that if a certain man were offered a certain bribe in a certain frame of mind, he would freely take it. Third, free knowledge: knowledge of what will actually happen, which God acquires only once he has decided which world to create. The point of the middle stage is that these counterfactuals are true before God decides anything and not because he decides anything; he consults them rather than fixing them. This is supposed to let God be fully sovereign over history without determining anyone's will: by arranging circumstances whose outcomes he already knows, he can bring about whatever overall result he wants while every human choice remains genuinely free. Molinism thus solves the old puzzle of how God can know the future in advance and we can still be free, and it steers a middle course between Calvinism, on which God simply decides who is saved, and views on which he merely foresees what free creatures happen to do.

The fatal problem, the chapter continues, is the grounding objection. Before a person makes a libertarian free choice, there is nothing about her character, nothing about her circumstances, and nothing about God that makes it true that she would go one way rather than the other — and if it were God's will that made it true, we would be back to determinism. So there is no fact of the matter about these counterfactuals, the middle tier collapses into the first or the third, and Molinism is for this reason rejected by most philosophers who work on the topic.

I am attracted to the middle-course framing and I want to know how much of it survives, but the grounding objection as stated here sounds to me close to decisive, and I cannot tell whether it has been set up fairly. I would be grateful for an audit.

Please go through the passage more or less sentence by sentence and tell me: what a Molinist would be happy to endorse as stated; what is a tolerable simplification for a popular audience; and what is a genuine misrepresentation that a defender would object to, with a note on why it matters. I am particularly unsure about three things. First, whether the claim that God can bring about whatever overall result he wants is something Molinism actually delivers, or whether the doctrine constrains God in a way the chapter never mentions. Second, whether it is right to present Molinism as a solution to the problem of foreknowledge and freedom at all, or whether that is a different problem that the doctrine simply inherits. Third, whether the grounding objection has been presented in its strongest form or in a form that is easier to answer than the real one: I would like to know what the standard Molinist replies are, whether any of them is a genuine reply rather than a standoff, and whether the objection, if it works, is equally available against views the chapter treats as unproblematic.

Finally, one downstream question. A commenter on a forum claimed that the corrected view commits its holder to something like this: that everyone who ends up damned is someone whom God could not have brought to salvation by any arrangement of circumstances compatible with their remaining free, so that their damnation reflects no defect in God's will but only in what was available to him. I have seen this labelled with a phrase involving different possible worlds. Is that a real consequence of Molinism, a live but optional implication, or does it depend on the parts of the chapter's summary that are wrong? If it is a real consequence, does it count against the view or is it something Molinists embrace?
