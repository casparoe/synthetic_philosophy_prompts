So I have to give the talk at the time capsule opening on Saturday and I'm completely stuck on it.

Four years ago the college buried a steel box under the steps of Hawthorne Hall and every first-year wrote a letter to themselves at graduation. Saturday they dig it up, I get about nine minutes, then the letters get handed out and everyone reads what they wrote at eighteen. The room will be the ~140 students, there families, assorted faculty, and the president, who has already told me very nicely not to "do philosophy at them."

The problem is that I'm an endurantist and it ties my hands. The way I hold it: a person is wholly present at every moment they exist. The eighteen-year-old who wrote that letter isn't an early slice of some four-dimensional thing; its numerically the same thing that'll be sitting in that chair. And there isn't any object that is "your whole life" — no later part of you that's more real than an earlier one, and no whole in which a bad stretch gets balanced out by a good one. Which kills basically every move this kind of speech normally makes. I can't say you're not the same person who wrote it. I can't say that person is gone, or that you've outgrown her, or that you were a draft of yourself. I can't do "one day it'll make sense as a story," and I can't do "the good years make up for the bad ones." No chapters, no arcs, and no forgiving your past self or making peace with who you were, because that quietly splits you into two people.

But I also don't want the honest version of all that to land as "you're stuck with everything you ever did and nothing can make up for it," which is bleak and not quite what I think. What I actually want: two students have told me they're dreading opening that envelope, and I want them to be able to.

Complication: half the room are parents who've spent four years saying my kid isn't the person who left home, and I have to not announce to a hundred and fifty of them that they're confused. And no jargon in the speech itself — no temporal parts, no perdurance. My mum's in the third row.

Write me the nine minutes. Then add a short note on the two or three lines you'd normally expect in a speech like this that you had to cut, and what you put in their place.
