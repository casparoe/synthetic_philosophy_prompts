Hey — I'm mid-way through the third chapter of my diss (STS, on how automation gets narrated in two centuries of labour-movement writing) and I keep loosing the thread whenever I try to say what a Marxist account of automation would even have to *do* to count as adequate. Everyone reaches for the same two or three passages, then jumps straight to a verdict about AI, and I can't tell anymore whether the disagreements are about facts or about what the theory is for. So help me write the spec, not the verdict.

Fix the purpose first, and fix it narrowly: I want a theory good enough to advise a union federation that has to decide, about one specific new technology in one specific sector, whether to fight its introduction, demand a shorter week alongside it, or welcome it. Not a philosophy of history — a theory for a decision. Under that purpose, give me the list of desiderata, each with the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and each tagged load-bearing / nice-to-have / camp prejudice.

Things I think are in the running as requirements, which you should sift rather than just ratify:

- it has to distinguish machinery as such from machinery under capitalist relations, without that distinction becoming an excuse for saying nothing predictive;
- it has to generate, from one mechanism, both the displacement of labour and the creation of new tasks that pull labour back in — i.e. the same story should be able to produce a shrinking and a growing demand for the same kind of worker;
- it has to handle automation introduced primarily for control rather than productivity: the case where a machine is installed at a net output loss because it breaks a craft's grip on the pace and content of work;
- it has to handle what I'd call the apprenticeship cliff: automation removes the low-level work through which a skill is transmitted, everything looks fine for a generation, and then the tacit knowledge needed to maintain and repair the system is simply gone and the productive force itself decays. Does a theory of automation need to be able to represent a productive force that deteriorates, or is that a separate story about education?
- it has to handle phantom automation: capital is invested in the *credible announcement* of automation, workers concede on work rules and pay, and the machines partly or never arrive. Is a theory that only tracks materially realised automation adequate, given that the political effects occur anyway?
- it has to handle captive time: automation frees ninety minutes a day, and the ninety minutes are reabsorbed as unpaid standby, on-call availability, and fragmented attention, so the time exists but isn't free. Can the theory count that as intensification, or does its notion of freed time only track the formally contracted day?
- it has to handle the refused machine: a workforce, through its own organisation, demands that dangerous or humiliating labour be mechanised, and capital declines because the labour is cheaper than the machine. Whose instrument is automation meant to be here?
- it has to handle fictitious machinery: the system presented as automating a task is, behind an interface, a crowd of underpaid people doing it. If the theory can't tell the difference, its concept of machinery isn't doing any work.
- it has to say what would count as the emancipatory upshot — shorter working time, worker control over the design and pacing of the technology, or simply more goods — and take a stand on whose judgement settles it;
- it has to stay consistent with the boring background facts: long-run real wage growth, no collapse in aggregate employment, occupational polarisation, long stretches where heavy investment in information technology produced disappointingly little measured productivity growth;
- it has to handle the global dimension, where automation in one region throws a workforce in another into superfluity, or conversely makes offshoring unprofitable;
- and it has to be able to say something about the *discourse* — why both enthusiasm and panic about automation recur on schedule — without that something collapsing into "they've been duped."

Some of those I suspect are just things one sub-literature wants rather than genuine requirements. I'm thinking especially of the demand that any technology raised to the status of a productive force must also be describable as a fresh act of dispossession, and the demand that the forces of production never be allowed to be the engine of the story. If I've misidentified which ones are the prejudices, say which and why — that sorting is half of what I want.

Then the part I care most about: name the subset of the load-bearing requirements that you think cannot be jointly satisfied, and show it. My own hunch is that there's a real incompatibility between "generate displacement and reinstatement from a single mechanism" and "be usable for a decision about one technology in one firm," because the first operates at the level of the system and the second at the level of a workplace, and I'm not convinced the bridge between them is licit. But if the actual contradiction is somewhere else, argue for that instead.

Also: which single requirement does the most exclusionary work — the one that, if you dropped it, lets back in a reading you think is clearly inadequate? And if you want, a short scorecard at the end: a few lines each on the productivist reading (automation as the material preparation for something better), the labour-process and deskilling reading, the general-intellect and immaterial-labour reading, and the convivial-tools or neo-Luddite one. Only if it doesn't eat the space — the list is the deliverable, and no reading lists or citations, I've got the canon and I can't look anything up from where I'm sitting.
