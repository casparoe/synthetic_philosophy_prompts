I teach an upper-level seminar on philosophy of biology and animal behaviour: eighteen students, one eighty-minute session, no budget beyond index cards, envelopes, and a projector. I want a runnable game in which they feel, rather than are told, what the handicap principle actually requires of a signal. The error I want them to walk into is the slogan version — that a signal is trustworthy because it is expensive — and the correction I want them to reach themselves is the differential-cost condition: what does the work is not that the signal costs something, but that it costs a signaller of a given quality less at the margin than it would cost one of lower quality, so that mimicry stops paying.

Design it concretely. Give each student a hidden quality drawn from three types, let them choose a signal level, and let an anonymous, randomly re-matched partner decide whether to accept, with payoffs that depend on the signaller's true type. Specify the type distribution, the payoff table, the matching protocol, and the number of rounds per condition. Then run the same machinery through a sequence of conditions: signals free; signals costly but at an identical price for every type; price falling in quality; an unfakeable index made available alongside the signal; and finally a noisy public cue correlated with type, which should produce the case where it is the middle types who signal hardest and the top and bottom stay quiet. If you think a round in which the signaller may either burn the cost or hand the same value to the receiver as a gift earns its place, include it and say what it settles.

Two constraints matter to me. First, reputation is the confound that will make the whole thing trivial, since repeated play with known partners sustains honesty without any cost at all: tell me how the matching protocol blocks it and what to do when students try to build a reputation anyway. Second, they should not hear the terms handicap principle, separating equilibrium, or counter-signalling until the debrief. Give me that debrief mapping observed behaviour onto the conditions for honest signalling, flag which round is most likely not to produce the textbook pattern and how to recover, and be frank about what a card game cannot show about the biological claim.
