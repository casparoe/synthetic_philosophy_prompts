I'm a backend engineer (Go, Postgres, way too much SQL) with no philosophy training whatsoever, and I've somehow agreed to run my team's brown bag next week, so I need something I can actually stand up and present. Here's how I got into this. I was complaining at lunch about our natural-language query feature: the user types something like show me the invoice for order 8812 and internally that's a lookup that has to match exactly one row, and if it matches zero rows or more than one we're honestly not sure what to do. Someone said that's literally what philosophers fight about with the word the, and then the whole table got into it: one person says a sentence whose lookup matches nothing is just plain false, another says truth and falsity don't even get a grip and the sentence is neither, a third says it depends on whether you were using the description to pick out a specific person you had in mind versus saying whoever fits this. They all sound about the same in ordinary conversation, which is exactly my problem: I can't tell the views apart by asking people to state their view, and neither will my team.

So please build me a diagnostic, part quiz and part decision tree, that sorts someone into the actual position by what they say about concrete sentences, never by which label they like. Ten to twelve questions, each a tiny plain-English scenario a coworker could answer in a couple of seconds with two or three options, phrased so nobody needs to have heard of any of these theories to answer. Please work in cases like these: there is no king of France (the classic bald one); someone says something about the dog when there are two dogs in the room; a description that picks out a person you're currently looking at but doesn't really fit them (the guy with the martini, who is actually holding water); a description buried in a belief report where the described thing doesn't exist (Dana thinks the CTO is lying, and there is no CTO); a role that's vacant right now or whose holder is disputed; and a future one, like the first person to walk on Mars.

Leaves I need covered, plus any you think I'm missing: the Russell-style reading where the is an existence-and-uniqueness claim, so a failed lookup makes the sentence false; the Strawson-style view where it's a presupposition failure and the question of truth doesn't arise; the Donnellan-style distinction between using a description referentially and using it attributively; and, if it deserves its own leaf, whatever the semantics people do when they treat uniqueness as a presupposition that projects out of negation, questions and conditionals. Name names only where you're confident and give every leaf a plain descriptive label too, because I will not remember who wrote what. If there are live options I've never heard of, like treatments of incomplete descriptions or the behaving more like a pronoun or a context-bound variable, make those leaves as well.

Two structural things I care about, since I think that's where the interesting part is. First: keep the two failure modes separate, nothing matches versus more than one thing matches, and be honest about which positions' machinery can't actually distinguish them, because I suspect a few just lump them together. Second: at least three or four questions where the description sits inside something bigger, like negation, a belief report, a question or a conditional, since that's apparently where the views that agree on the simple case come apart. I also want at least one fork built on a scope ambiguity, like whether a negation takes the whole sentence or just part of it, because my team will hit that instantly and it's basically a SQL grouping bug, so make it a real branch rather than a footnote. Alongside the questions I want a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto leaves and says which answers are the signature tells and which are just noise, and a taxonomy view where every branch is labeled with the claim that splits it. For any combination of answers the logical space allows but nobody actually defends, list it with one line on why: incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. And flag any pair of leaves the tree genuinely can't separate instead of pretending it can; I'd rather have an honest these two collapse here note on the slide than a quiz that lies. No bibliographies, citations or further reading, I can't look anything up from the podium and my team definately won't.
