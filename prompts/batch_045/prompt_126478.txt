I'm in a reading group that is working through Sen's entitlement approach to famines — the 1981 book, the Bengal and Ireland material, the Great Leap Forward comparison — and my housemate, who has spent years in humanitarian logistics and has watched these systems from the inside, takes a much harder line than Sen's own careful wording suggests. We have found that we never get anywhere arguing about theory directly, but we do have strong and surprisingly settled gut reactions to individual cases, and we disagree about which of those reactions go together. So I want to try it from that direction. Below are my verdicts. I'd like you to find the principle, if there is one, that they add up to; to tell me plainly which of them it cannot accommodate and which therefore have to go; and to say what the principle predicts about the two or three cases at the end that I haven't made up my mind about.

(1) Bengal, 1943. The rice crop was only modestly below a normal year, and aggregate food availability was not the problem; wartime spending and speculative panic drove prices far beyond what landless labourers and rural service castes could pay, while the government removed boats and rice stocks from vulnerable districts and kept procuring rice to supply the war effort. My verdict: a political crime, and plainly not a natural disaster — there wasn't really a food shortage to be a disaster. (2) Ireland from 1845. Here the trigger genuinely was natural: a blight destroyed the potato, which was the sole staple of the rural poor. But grain and livestock kept leaving the country, relief was conditioned on work and on not disturbing property and market order, and the administrative response was shaped by ideas about dependency that were doing independent work. My verdict: the blight was natural, the famine was a crime. (3) The Great Leap Forward, early 1960s. Some provinces really did have bad harvests, and much of it really was administrative chaos; but procurement quotas were set on wildly inflated local production reports, central stocks were not released, grain continued to be exported, and the shortfall was publicly denied, so the chaos was partly manufactured and then protected. My verdict: the clearest and worst of the three, worse than Bengal, and I want the principle to say why chaos plus lying is worse than Bengal's wartime triage. (4) A remote valley. An earthquake-triggered landslide destroys the only road into a subsistence-farming valley in a very poor mountainous country; district officials learn of it six weeks later; the state has no aircraft, no stockpile within three hundred kilometres, and no surplus anywhere. Several thousand die before food arrives. My verdict: a natural disaster, full stop — so the mere fact that a state failed to prevent mass starvation is not enough to make it a crime. (5) The forecasting model. A state grain agency sets procurement on the basis of a national harvest-forecast model trained on district reports that local cadres have an incentive to inflate; the model predicts record yields; the headline number is legally the official estimate and the only figure the centre will act on; procurement proceeds for four months after local market prices and mortality data in the districts would have exposed the error to anyone permitted to look. My verdict: still a crime, even though I cannot point to a person who chose starvation or even knew about it. (6) An embargo. An international coalition imposes a comprehensive economic embargo to halt a weapons programme, having estimated in advance that child mortality will roughly double; the sanctioned government then diverts what food remains to the army and to its client cities and refuses to reallocate. My verdict: both are guilty, but in different ways, and I would not put them in the same box. (7) The fishery. No shortfall in national food supply at all: a state-licensed industrial fleet collapses the inshore fishery that a coastal population both eats from and earns its cash in; supermarkets two hundred miles away are full of cheap rice; the fishing families cannot pay for it, cannot legally move, and cannot sell their boats for anything like their value. My verdict: this is a famine even though it is not a food-availability decline, and it is a crime if the licensing decision was made knowing roughly this would happen and for the benefit of exporters. (8) The fertilizer gamble. A poor, newly independent country in a drought year continues exporting grain, because the hard currency buys the fuel and fertilizer that will avert a far bigger famine the following year — and in fact it does: roughly a quarter of a million die this year, and about two million do not die next year. My verdict: I do not want to call this a crime, and I feel slightly dishonest about it, because the mechanism is the same as in (2): food leaving a country while its people starve, decided by officials who foresaw the deaths. My verdicts on (2) and (8) are the pair I most suspect cannot both survive. (9) The uncounted. A wealthy functioning democracy with a free press and ample grain; in an export-agriculture district, undocumented migrant farmworkers — not citizens, not on any voter roll, not sampled in the national food-security survey, not read by anybody — suffer famine-scale mortality after a crop failure and a collapse in piece rates. No official wanted them dead; no official thought about them at all; the state had every capacity to act; the press never covered it. This is the one I go back and forth on: Sen's own claim about democracies suggests it shouldn't be possible, my reaction to (4) says no one is guilty, and my reaction to (7) says someone is.

My own stab at a principle, offered to be tested rather than defended: a famine is a political crime just in case (i) the starvation was a foreseeable outcome of an allocative decision, or of a rule governing allocations, made by an institutional agent that had the capacity to have decided otherwise, and (ii) the claims of those who starved were given less weight than the claims of others in a way that is itself open to criticism — where the relevant sense of "weight" is not the agent's own preferences but something the principle has to supply. I can see immediately that (5) strains clause (i)'s talk of a decision, that (6) needs an account of responsibility for causing rather than for failing to prevent, and that (2) and (8) pull apart only if clause (ii) can distinguish ends from side-effects or beneficiaries from victims. Please test it against all nine cases, repair it or replace it with a better one, state the repaired version precisely enough that it generates verdicts, and then tell me what it says about: a famine in which most of the deaths are from measles and cholera in relief camps rather than from lack of calories; a famine produced by commodity derivatives trading that governments did not create and could not have stopped; and a state that refuses donated food from a geopolitical rival because accepting it would require admitting the shortfall and allowing foreign monitors, in a year when its own rationing is already doing about as well as it can. I'd also like to know whether "natural disaster" and "political crime" are, on the resulting account, jointly exhaustive, or whether (5) belongs in some third category.
