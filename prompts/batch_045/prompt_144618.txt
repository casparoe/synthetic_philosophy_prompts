I'm working my way through a popular history of nineteenth-century political thought, and its chapter on Mill ends with an account of his late turn toward socialism that I can't quite trust. The closing page reads like this:

"By the end of his life Mill had become a socialist. The late manuscript published after his death as Chapters on Socialism completes a conversion that had been under way since the Principles of Political Economy, where he insists that while the production of wealth is governed by necessary laws of nature, its distribution is purely an affair of human institution. Having said that, he had in effect concluded that nothing in economics stands in the way of our distributing wealth as we please, and that the only question left was how quickly private property could be done away with. His own answer, in practice, was that it need not be quick at all, because socialism is nothing more than redistribution carried out gradually through the tax system; the workers' cooperatives and profit-sharing schemes he praises are simply a polite Victorian name for the welfare state. What separates Mill from Marx is only that Mill wanted the change to be peaceful. And the nervousness about state power, and about a society of conformists crushing individuality, that runs through On Liberty had by this point been quietly set aside: Mill had come to think that once people are properly educated to identify their own good with the good of the community, the machinery of competition becomes unnecessary."

The Mill I had a picture of from On Liberty and Utilitarianism — deeply suspicious of the tyranny of the majority, attached to individuality and to experiments in living, hostile to anything that would hand public opinion the direction of people's lives — doesn't look much like the Mill in that paragraph. But I may simply be over-identifying him with his most famous book, and the popularizer sounds very sure of himself. In the other direction, I've heard that in his Autobiography Mill says that he and Harriet Taylor counted, in some qualified sense, as socialists: that they looked forward to a society no longer divided into an idle and an industrious class, in which the produce of labour would be shared on an acknowledged principle of justice rather than left to the accident of birth, and in which people would exert themselves strenuously for benefits shared with the community rather than exclusively their own — all while rejecting the tyranny of society over the individual that most socialist systems were thought to involve. That sounds closer to the chapter's Mill than to mine, which is why I'm not confident I know where the misrepresentation lies.

Could you go through the passage claim by claim and tell me what a Mill scholar would accept, what they would wince at as a simplification that still points at something real, and what is straightforwardly wrong? I'd especially like to know:

- what Mill's distinction between the laws of production and the laws of distribution actually licenses him to conclude, and whether the inference to distributing wealth however we please is one he can make;
- what is gained or lost when his cooperative and associationist proposals are translated into tax-and-transfer redistribution, and whether the wage relation itself, and not merely the level of a worker's income, was what he wanted to change;
- what role is played by the open, empirically unsettled question of whether people will work as hard for a shared benefit as for their own, and by his insistence that schemes of association should be tried experimentally and allowed to prove themselves against private enterprise;
- and whether any of this can be reconciled with the arguments of On Liberty, or whether the late sympathy for socialism really is a repudiation of them.

Finally, the chapter goes on to conclude that had Mill lived another generation he would have been a straightforward social democrat, and would have recognised the twentieth-century welfare state as essentially what he had in mind. Does that follow once the passage has been corrected, or is it a further step his actual commitments would resist?
