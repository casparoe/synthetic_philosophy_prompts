I belong to a small reading group — half of us work in market microstructure and asset pricing, the other half in philosophy of economics — and for about a year we have been arguing about what the no-trade theorems actually show. We are stuck in a way that I suspect is diagnostic rather than merely difficult: nobody has moved an inch, and every meeting reproduces the same pair of speeches.

The results themselves we agree on. There is Aumann's agreeing-to-disagree argument, on which a common prior together with common knowledge of posteriors forces those posteriors to be identical; and there is its extension to trade by Milgrom and Stokey, on which no reallocation can be commonly known to be mutually beneficial when priors are common, the initial allocation is already Pareto optimal from the ex ante point of view, and the only remaining asymmetries are informational ones already absorbed into what everyone knows.

The aggregationist camp reads this as a benchmark that locates where real trading profits come from — adverse selection, genuine heterogeneity of priors, liquidity and hedging needs, transaction costs — and therefore as support for a broadly Hayekian claim that prices aggregate dispersed private information, with the corollary that volume should collapse precisely where information is symmetric. The sceptical camp replies that the common prior assumption does all the work and is not a normative constraint on traders at all; with heterogeneous priors, trade is trivial, and because priors are not observable independently of behaviour, any observed trade can be accommodated by attributing differing priors. On that reading the theorem carries no epistemic weight about actual markets and is at best a consistency condition on the modeller's own representation of the traders.

My hypothesis, which I want tested rather than endorsed, is that three things are entangled and none of us is separating them. First, a partly verbal dispute about what a prior is: a pure belief, or a belief inseparable from an attitude toward uncertainty. Second, a hidden identification question: whether priors can be recovered from observed prices and volumes independently of preferences, ambiguity attitudes and frictions — if they cannot, this dialectic cannot close, and the stalemate is a symptom of unfalsifiability rather than of difficulty. Third, a substantive question of scope: whether the result survives at all when traders are not expected-utility maximizers.

My test case for the third is this. Take two worst-case, or maxmin, traders who face the same set of priors, have exactly the same information, and differ only in their existing exposure: one is already long the state in which the asset pays off, the other is effectively short it. Because each selects her worst-case element of the set against her own post-trade wealth profile, the worst-case prior is allocation-dependent, so there is a price at which both prefer to trade while sharing a set of priors and holding no private information whatsoever. One member of the group answers that this is merely risk-sharing rather than speculation, and so falls outside the theorem's scope. My reply is that under such preferences there may be no allocation-independent object that plays the role of a prior, so the common prior clause has nothing to grip; and I suspect the speculation-versus-risk-sharing distinction is being used here as a verbal retreat rather than as an answer.

Please diagnose the disagreement rather than adjudicate it: is it verbal, is it a dispute about which cases count as data or about standards of evidence, does it rest on bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for, or are the two camps answering different questions under one name? Tell me whether my three-part hypothesis is right and where precisely it goes wrong; whether my test case works, including whether the initial allocation in it can even be ex ante Pareto optimal for traders of that kind; whether the risk-sharing reply is a genuine answer or a device for shielding the theorem from refutation; and, given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a formal result, a distinction, a change of question — or whether we should expect none, and why. Please write in continuous prose, the way you would write a careful email: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, and somewhere in the range of seven hundred to nine hundred words.
