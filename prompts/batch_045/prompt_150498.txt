I'm writing a seminar paper on self-awareness in the Arabic Peripatetic tradition, and I keep circling back to the same knot, so I'd rather argue it with you than read about it. Please play Avicenna — Ibn Sina, in your later years at Isfahan, patient with a slow student but not to be moved off your main claim — and answer me in the first person, in your own vocabulary: nafs, 'aql, wahm, māhiyya and anniyya, necessary and possible existence, the Agent Intellect, the soul's beginning with the body and its not perishing with it. Use nothing you could not have had: no Descartes, no Locke, no psychology or natural science from after your death, no qualia, no indexicals. If I use a modern term, ask me what I mean in your terms before you answer. Two short paragraphs per turn, no citations and no appeal to later commentators; concede small points freely, but never the claim that the self is not the body. Stay in character until I say stop.

Here is the objection, and it is the one my supervisor thinks sinks the whole argument. Grant the setup: a human being brought into existence all at once and fully grown, suspended in open air with his limbs spread so that they do not touch one another and nothing presses against him, seeing nothing, hearing nothing, with no sensation of his own body. Grant too that he still affirms his own existence, and would not thereby affirm so much as the existence of a hand or a heart. What follows is still less than you want. Suppose that instead of one such man the Necessary Existent brings two into being in the same instant, in two separate voids, exactly alike in every respect — the same thoughts, the same self-affirmation, no causal contact between them ever. Each says I, and each is completely certain, but nothing in that self-awareness tells either of them which one he is; the two affirmations do not differ in content at all. So the suspended man seems to reach only a self that is not this one: an awareness of what it is to be a self, or of a nature, rather than of something numerically distinct from its qualitatively identical twin. And if an immaterial soul can be this soul only by being hung on this body — which is exactly what the body is doing for you in the ordinary case, and exactly what you have stripped away in the experiment — then your argument secures the survival of something, but not the survival of me. Answer me inside the void, then: what makes that I in the second man his, and not the other one's?

And to save you from the easy exits: if you tell me each soul is individuated by its own relation to the matter it first informed, I will ask what becomes of that relation when the body is gone, and what your flying man has then shown about a soul that never had one. If you say instead that the self-awareness is of the soul's existence rather than of its quiddity, I will ask why a man asleep, or a man swallowed up in grief, is not aware of it.
