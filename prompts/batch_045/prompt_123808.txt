hey — me and two other people have had a philosophy of physics reading group going for about a year and a half now, and we keep circling back to the Born rule problem in Everettian quantum mechanics and we havent moved at all. I want a diagnosis of why, not a survey of the positions, and I want an honest answer about whether anything could ever end it.

The state of play as I see it. One camp runs decision-theoretic derivations in the Deutsch–Wallace style: take branching as given, describe an agent choosing between acts whose outcomes are sets of branches, impose axioms that are supposed to be pure constraints of rationality, run a representation theorem, and get out that her credences and utilities have to be amplitude-weighted. Another camp goes for derivations from symmetry and decoherence — Zurek's envariance sort of thing — and pulls the Born weights out of invariance properties of entangled states. A third treats it as self-locating uncertainty before branching: you don't know which branch you'll wake up as, and then some principle to the effect that your credence shouldn't depend on far-away or irrelevant parts of the state is meant to pin the weights down. And then there are the sceptics — Kent, Maudlin, Price and co — who say this is all circular: either the rationality axioms already smuggle in amplitude-weighting, or the branching structure is quietly doing the work, or, worse, in a theory where every outcome actually happens there are branches where the statistics come out wildly deviant, and to dismiss them as negligible is to use the Born measure to justify itself. Plus a background grumble that maybe probability just isn't the right thing to want from a deterministic branching theory in the first place.

My hypothesis, which might be junk: it isn't one disagreement. There are at least three questions under one name — what an agent who knows she is about to branch should bet; what, if anything, makes it objectively the case that one outcome is likelier than another; and what statistics a typical observer should expect to see — and different people are answering different ones and then talking past each other. And underneath that there's a hidden formal question: whether branching structure plus non-probabilistic constraints really forces the Hilbert space norm as the unique measure, or whether Gleason-style uniqueness results only pin down probabilities over outcomes in a single measurement and give you nothing at all about self-location among branches.

I also made up something that I want you to tell me is worthless if it is. Call it branch inflation. Set up two experiments with identical amplitudes but wildly different numbers of branches: in one, a result gets recorded on a single spin; in the other, the same result gets amplified into a pointer, a wall of air molecules, and a billion redundant records. If branch count mattered at all these should come apart, and if it doesn't, then only-amplitude-matters was built in at the start instead of derived. Does that do any work the existing circularity objections dont already do, or am i just reinventing the quantitative problem with a new label?

So: is my three-questions diagnosis right, or is the real fault line somewhere else — different standards for what a derivation is allowed to assume, or bedrock intuitions about what probability has to be that nobody can argue for? Where does each side think the burden of proof actually lies? And given whatever you think is going on, what would count as progress: a theorem about uniqueness of the measure, a decision-theoretic axiom nobody can refuse, an experiment, or just giving up and taking the weight as a primitive caring measure? Or should we expect nothing to move, and why?
