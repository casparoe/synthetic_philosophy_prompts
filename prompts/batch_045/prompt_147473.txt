I'm retired now — thirty-odd years in infectious disease surveillance, the last decade of it as the person who had to sign off on the numbers before they went anywhere — and a couple of years ago I started going to a philosophy reading group at the local university. We got onto causation a few weeks back and somebody brought up al-Ghazālī's occasionalism: the view, as it was explained to me, that there isn't really any necessary connection between what we call a cause and what we call an effect, that what we actually observe is one thing following another, again and again, and that the "connection" is something we bring to it rather than something in the world. Apparently Hume arrives at something similar a few centuries later minus the theological bit.

And I keep thinking about the fact that my entire working life consisted of naming causes.

Three things I personally did thousands of times:

1. Death certificates. You write a chain of events and then you have to pick a single "underlying cause of death" for the statistics — the thing that set the train of events going. Everyone filling in the form knows the person had six things wrong with them. One gets the slot, and the rest get listed as "other significant conditions."

2. Outbreak investigation. We'd say "the cause of the outbreak was the contaminated borehole." Nobody says the cause was the existence of the bacterium, or that the people were unvaccinated, or that it was the rainy season. Those are background. We pick the thing we could most cheaply have removed.

3. And then, in the same job, in the same week, we'd compute attributable fractions and be perfectly happy writing "this exposure accounted for roughly 30% of cases in this population." Fractional, aggregate, quantitative. And the moment a clinician asked about one particular patient with one particular cancer, the fraction vanished and we were back to a yes-or-no answer, delivered with great confidence.

Meanwhile in the epidemiology we were taught there's this picture of a "sufficient cause" as a pie made of component causes, where almost nothing is a cause on its own and almost nothing is necessary — which sits oddly next to a form that makes me nominate one.

So: I've started calling 1 and 2 together the "bookmark theory" — the cause is just the name of the cheapest place to have intervened, and calling it *the* cause is an administrative act rather than a discovery about nature. My question is what this whole package of practices actually presupposes, and whether the occasionalist should object to it.

Concretely, I want to know:

- Does picking out one underlying cause, and giving it that elevated status, presuppose that there is some real connective glue in nature — something the occasionalist denies — or is selecting on cheapness, abnormality, or intervenability enough on its own? Which parts of my practice would the occasionalist have to call a mistake, and which would survive unchanged?
- Someone in the group said the practices only presuppose that causes are "difference-makers," and that the occasionalist gets difference-making for free, because the regularities are still regular whether or not there's a necessary connection behind them. Is that right, or does a counterfactual view of causation smuggle back in exactly what the occasionalist threw out? What do the counterfactuals bottom out in, for someone who thinks there are no real connections?
- Is the trio I described even consistent — one cause on a certificate, fractions in a population, hard yes-or-no for an individual? Does that force me into some kind of pluralism about causation, or is there a single account that licenses all three and makes the switches in granularity look principled rather than ad hoc?
- And the practical one: if the occasionalist picture is true, what would actually have to change in how we write death certificates, run outbreak investigations, or report attributable fractions? If the honest answer is "nothing at all," I'd like to know why a practice built entirely around cause-talk can be completely indifferent to whether causation is real.

I don't know the proper vocabulary for any of this, so please don't assume I do — but do tell me where the practice is genuinely underdetermined, i.e. where several different theories of causation would all let me go on filling in the forms exactly as I did.
