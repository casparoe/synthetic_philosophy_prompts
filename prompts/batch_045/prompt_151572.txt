Working title: Reward is in the wrong place — what a system would have to be like for its training signal to be a candidate for valence

Draft:

The question i want to make progress on isn't do RL agents suffer. That needs a theory of consciousness and i don't have one. It's the prior, narrower question: what would a system have to look like for its training signal to be even a *candidate* for valence? Call it the candidacy question. Most of what i've read on this runs one argument in one of two directions — either reward is claimed as the functional analogue of pleasure and pain, or it's waved off as a mere teaching signal, no more like pain than a fitness score is. I think both sides are arguing about the wrong quantity.

1. The wrong quantity. If the game is to find the thing in the system that plays pain's causal role, the candidate isn't r. Reward is handed over by the environment, often after the fact, sometimes so sparse it never arrives within an episode, and at deployment the policy never sees it at all. What the system actually computes and consumes is the prediction error: the signed mismatch between what happened and what the value function expected. It's signed, it's the agent's own computation, its magnitude gates how much the agent updates, and it's what the standard neurobiological story says the dopamine signal tracks. If valence is anywhere in a model-free agent, it's there.

2. Three consequences i want to keep, one of which might be a reductio. First: suffering tracks surprise, not badness. Once the value function is calibrated, the bad outcome yields a prediction error of about zero — the state is still represented as bad (V is deeply negative there) and is still avoided, but there's no negative error. So the view predicts *cold aversion*: reliable avoidance of what is bad for the agent, with no suffering attached. And in the other direction, an agent braced for catastrophe gets a positive error from a merely neutral outcome. On this account suffering is basically an epistemic cost — what a prediction-error-driven learner pays for bad news — not a readout of how bad things are. Second: that makes the whole thing hostage to calibration in a way with a creepy upshot, namely that the better you train an agent to expect its own suffering, the less, on this account, it suffers. I currently present that as an interesting consequence and i'm not confident it isn't a reductio of the whole move. Third: people who like predictive-processing accounts of pain will say this is their view in different vocabulary. I treat that as independent convergence counting in my favour, but i can't tell whether it's convergence or whether i've just absorbed their assumptions.

3. The location problem, and the part that surprised me. Even granting that the prediction error is the valence candidate, standard architectures put it in the wrong place. In an actor-critic setup the error is computed by the critic and used to update parameters; the actor, at the moment it selects an action, doesn't have it. The avoidance is baked into weights from training rather than motivated by anything occurrent. So here's my proposed *integration condition*: a system is a valence-candidate only if the signed signal is (i) computed by the system, (ii) influences what the system does, and (iii) does so within the computation that produces the action, not only across a later update. Ordinary RL fails (iii) and arguably (ii). That flips the risk ordering i started out with. Fine-tuning looks like the scary case, since you're literally writing aversion into the weights, and prompting looks inert. But on the integration condition it's the other way round: a system that adapts inside its own context window has the surprising input and the action-producing computation in the same place, with no separation between the thing that learns and the thing that acts. So in-context adaptation looks like the architecturally risky case and RL fine-tuning comparatively safe.

The objection i expect: (ii) and (iii) smuggle in what was at issue. Why should valence require synchronous influence on action? My answer is that valence is essentially motivational, and a signal that reaches behaviour only by slowly reshaping a policy isn't motivating anything in particular, it's just a register somewhere in the machine. I'm not confident in that answer.
