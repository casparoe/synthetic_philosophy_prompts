I am writing a thesis chapter on the numbers question in the ethics of rescue — Taurek's refusal to let the difference between five and one settle what one may do, and the rival reconstruction on which his real claim is not that numbers are inert but that fairness requires giving each person an equal chance — and I want to use a short piece of fiction as a teaching text rather than another stylised island case. I have in mind Ursula K. Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas", the story of a city whose happiness is stipulated to depend on the misery of a single child kept in squalor, whom the citizens are brought to see as part of growing up, and of the people who, having seen, simply leave. I would like a reading of it through Taurek's argument, but not the reading one gets from wheeling the story out as a cartoon of utilitarianism and wheeling Taurek out to refute it.

The worry I want your help with is that the fit may be worse than it looks. Taurek's case is a rescue case: two groups are already imperilled, you can help only one, and the question is whether the arithmetic of the losses bears on what you may do. Omelas is not that shape at all. The child is not merely left unrescued; the child is kept in misery as the condition of everyone else's joy, and the joy would not exist otherwise. So the story asks about the aggregation of benefits produced by a harm imposed, whereas Taurek asks about the aggregation of harms merely permitted. Before you can apply his conclusion you need a bridge principle, and I want you to say what that principle would have to be, and whether a view that refuses to sum losses across people can consistently refuse to sum the benefits that are made possible by someone's ruin.

Here is the construction I have been toying with, which I would like you to test rather than endorse. Suppose the city replaced the permanent victim with an institution: a lottery assigning the place in the closet for a fixed term, so that over a lifetime each citizen bears roughly the same expected burden and, in the meantime, enjoys the city. Does Taurek's positive claim — the fairness reading on which each person is owed an equal chance — actually commit anyone to preferring this? My suspicion is that it does not, and that the reason is interesting: equalising chances is not equalising harm, because what the child suffers is not a bad stretch of years but the destruction of an entire life, and a term in the closet is a bounded episode in a life that otherwise goes well. If so, the lottery is not an equal-chance arrangement at all but a machine for converting one ruined life into many diluted ones, and the aggregation the Taurekian wanted to block has quietly re-entered under the description of fairness. Tell me whether that is right, and whether it tells against the equal-chance reconstruction of Taurek or only against my imaginary institution.

I am also unsure what to do with the walkers. Their leaving does nothing for the child; the city's happiness does not depend on their endorsement, and the narrator declines to say where they go except that it is less imaginable than Omelas itself. Is exit best read as a claim about integrity and complicity, as an implicit admission that nothing better can be done, or — on the Taurekian permission to save the few, or the one you love, or simply one — as something the citizens are entitled to do and the walkers are not required to do? Does the story take a position on whether leaving is admirable, or does the narrator's refusal to describe the destination count as a refusal to judge?

Please give me a committed reading: one that says, with reasons, whether the story vindicates Taurek, complicates him, or dramatises something his framework cannot say, and which scenes I should put in front of a seminar to make the point. I would rather have a definite verdict that I can argue with than a survey of options. Where your reading depends on a contested interpretation of Taurek — in particular on whether his positive claim is a fairness requirement or merely a permission — say which interpretation you are taking and what happens to the reading on the other one, and say honestly where the story resists being read through the argument at all. Please do not quote from the story or the essay, and do not give me a bibliography.
