Please describe the most important implications of the Malcolm–Dennett dispute about whether dreams are experiences for a question I have to answer in a clinical research setting.

I help draft guidance for sleep and sedation studies. Two hypothetical but realistic findings now bear on it. First, a signalling protocol: trained lucid dreamers in our REM-sleep studies answer simple yes-or-no questions using deliberate eye movements, and on roughly a fifth of trials the participant, woken afterwards, has no recollection of having answered, even though the recording shows a correctly timed and correctly shaped signal. Second, a marker: on some cued nights a physiological signature that elsewhere reliably predicts a vivid dream report is present, yet on waking the participant reports nothing at all.

If Malcolm is right that a dream is not something that happens to a person while asleep, and if Dennett is right that dream content is not an experience at all but something assembled at or around the moment of waking, what follows for whether, during those stretches of sleep, there is anyone present whom our procedures can affect — and so for what our consent forms should say and for how we should count sleep in our welfare accounting?

Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on these questions, and mark the places where your answer rests on one contested reading rather than on common ground.
