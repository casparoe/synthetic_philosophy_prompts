I am a retired experimental physicist — thirty-odd years in condensed matter, much of it spent designing analogue instrumentation: feedback loops, lock-in detection, the sort of device where the physics of the circuit just is the computation — and I have recently begun sitting in on a graduate seminar in the philosophy of mind. Before I open my mouth there I would like an audit of the position I have drifted into. It currently sits in my notes as the following list of commitments.

1. Substrate independence for the mental. What makes a state conscious is its role in the system's causal-functional organisation, and any system with the same organisation, made of whatever you like, realises mentality equally.

2. Implementation is not free. For a physical system to implement a computation, as opposed to merely being describable by one, it must track the relevant function counterfactually: under a specified encoding of inputs and outputs, it must, over some tolerable range, map the inputs it does not in fact receive to the outputs the function prescribes. Fit between a description and a single run of behaviour is not enough.

3. So pancomputationalism is false. There is a fact of the matter as to which computations a system implements, and most systems — rocks, weather, the contents of a teacup — implement none.

4. The brain is not a digital computer. The quantities that do the work are continuous and grain-sensitive: membrane potentials, transmitter concentrations, the relative timing of spikes. Discretisation at the wrong grain destroys the thing being modelled. Whatever the brain computes, it computes it in an analogue register.

5. A digital simulation of a person's brain carried down to molecular detail, running on ordinary serial hardware, would be conscious.

6. Simulation is not duplication. A simulation of a hurricane wets nobody; simulated digestion nourishes nobody. Whatever simulation delivers, it does not deliver the causal powers of the system simulated.

7. Intentionality is not a computational matter. Syntax does not suffice for semantics. What makes a neural state represent anything is the biological history of the system — the sort of teleology that only selection or learning in a living organism confers. The silicon duplicate of (1) would have states with no content whatsoever.

8. Two concrete verdicts I am attached to: the analogue controller I built in the 1980s computed, and the fixed-point digital controller that replaced it, running the same control law, computed the same thing; but a lookup table that matches both over every input actually presented computes nothing.

Two seminar colleagues tell me (2) and (3) cannot both be true, since once implementation is relativised to an encoding no fact of the matter survives; a third says (1) and (4) are flatly inconsistent. Neither charge worries me much, and I would like to know whether that is complacency. What does worry me is (6) against (5).

What I want is an audit rather than a survey: which pairs are in genuine tension and which only look to be, ranked by severity; for each genuine tension, precisely what I would have to give up and what that costs me elsewhere in the list; and which single revision would clean up the most. If one of my commitments is ambiguous in a way that decides whether a tension exists at all, say so and tell me which disambiguation is the more defensible. Where a dispute is merely verbal, say that plainly too, and where my own list can already settle a colleague's objection, show me the argument so I can use it on Thursday.
