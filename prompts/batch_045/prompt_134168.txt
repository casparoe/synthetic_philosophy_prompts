I'm rewriting the final third of my advanced seminar on speech acts — we work through Austin's five-way scheme and Searle's five-way scheme, then a couple of weeks on the fringes: the hereby test, uptake, direction of fit, indirect acts, the etiolated uses Austin sets aside when an actor speaks on stage or words appear in a poem. What I want to replace my handout with is a dichotomous key in the style of a field guide: start from a bare description of an utterance-in-context and arrive at a named illocutionary category, without ever asking a student "is this a commissive?" A colleague in the linguistics department and I disagree about where the load-bearing joints are. She thinks two features do nearly all the work — the direction of fit and the identity of whoever is supposed to change (the speaker's future conduct, the hearer's conduct, or the world itself) — and that everything else is bookkeeping. I think the real divide is whether an act requires an institutional or conventional framework to succeed at all, and that direction of fit is downstream of that. I'd like the key built so that it adjudicates that disagreement rather than assuming either answer: construct the tree from the logical space first, by fixing four or five features of the sort just mentioned, saying how many combinations they permit, then locating the actual categories in that space, marking the combinations that no theory occupies, and saying for each empty cell whether it is empty because the view would be incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply undefended. Cap the terminal leaves at fourteen, and phrase every internal node as a question someone with no training could answer from a short description of a situation.

Each leaf should carry three things: the name of the position (or "unnamed" if nobody occupies it), a canonical example, and one sentence on the case that makes that category uncomfortable. Austin's five and Searle's five have to appear, along with room for indirect directives and hints, the acknowledgments (thanking, congratulating, apologizing) that sit awkwardly inside expressives, the verdictives such as a jury foreperson's "we find the defendant not guilty," and a node somewhere for cases where only a perlocutionary effect seems to have occurred. And these cases must be sortable by the finished key, since they are the ones my students always raise: the recorded voice on a station platform announcing that it is sorry for the delay when no one in particular is sorry; a statutory clause providing that a notice sent to a given address is deemed to have been served, where nobody speaks and no uptake occurs; an actor on stage saying to another actor that she promises to pay him Tuesday; "I promise that I will never promise anything again"; the oddly self-verifying family ("I assert that I am now making an assertion," "I am hereby speaking"); "you are now married" pronounced by an officiant whose licence expired yesterday, and the same sentence in a jurisdiction that recognises common-law marriage; a posthumous degree or a retroactive pardon, where the world-to-word fit seems aimed at the past; a smart contract that transfers an asset when its condition is met, where it is unclear whether anyone declared anything; and a customer-service chatbot's "I understand how frustrating this must be," which raises the question of whether the vocaliser, the composer of the words, and the party who incurs the commitment can come apart, and what happens to the sincerity condition when the third of those is a corporation and the second is a language model. Please also flag the pairs your key genuinely cannot separate — my bet is that behabitives and expressives, and verdictives and assertive-flavoured declarations, will collapse together — rather than inventing a distinction to keep them apart, and then run a short test set of eight of the cases above through the finished key, reporting any that land somewhere you don't like. No bibliography or literature review; I want the instrument itself.
