You are the philosophical adviser to the Franchise and Boundary Committee of a constitutional convention drafting the founding charter of a jurisdiction that is about to become self-governing for the first time. The Committee has roughly nine months, and the franchise clause it produces will be entrenched: any later amendment requires a two-thirds vote of the legislature plus ratification by a referendum conducted under the very rules being written now. Your task is not to draft the clause. It is to give the Committee a defended research-and-decision agenda: which questions about the composition of the demos it must settle in the constitutional text, which it should delegate to a standing body governed by a specified procedure, and which it should deliberately leave open — and in what order it should work through them.

The following facts are settled and are not to be re-litigated. The resident population is about 2.1 million. Roughly 45 percent hold fixed-term work-residency permits, non-renewable beyond five years and tied to employment in a designated export-processing zone; they pay taxes, use public services, and are subject to the jurisdiction's courts. About 12 percent of the citizen body has lived abroad for more than a decade, retains an unconditional right of return, and is currently on the electoral roll. A neighbouring state contains roughly 300,000 people whose agriculture depends on water allocations the new jurisdiction will control unilaterally. A substantial part of what would ordinarily count as coercion — debt collection, commercial arbitration, port and zone security — is contracted out to private firms under registered codes rather than exercised directly by the state. Finally, before the charter takes effect, an independence referendum must be held, and its franchise must be fixed by a body whose own authority derives from that same referendum.

Four proposals are on the table, and the Committee is inclined to combine them rather than choose. First, the avoidability discount: affectedness should count as a claim to membership only to the degree that the affected party could not have cheaply avoided the effect by moving, contracting around it, or exiting a relationship. Second, domicile-year accrual: full voting rights should accumulate over years of residence, with partial voting weight in the interim, so that membership is a gradient rather than a threshold. Third, the overlapping-constituency escape: abandon the idea of a single demos and allocate decision rights by subject matter, so that a person may be inside the boundary for water and education policy and outside it for criminal law. Fourth, a sunset clause requiring the demos to be reconstituted every thirty years by a randomly selected boundary convention, on the theory that no founding generation should bind the composition of all later ones.

I want an agenda, not a survey, and I want the ordering argued for rather than asserted. Please proceed as follows.

State the criteria you are using and the trade-offs between them: how much of the rest of the charter turns on a given question, how tractable it is in nine months for a committee of lawyers, civil servants and lay members rather than philosophers, how costly an error is given entrenchment, and how far a question can be deferred to a later body without that body's legitimacy being infected by the original mistake. Then give the ordered agenda. In particular, I want you to take a position on three things that the Committee keeps conflating.

One: the relation between the criterion of membership and the procedure for applying it. Any procedure for drawing the boundary seems to presuppose a demos that authorises it, and there is a well-known regress here. How much of the Committee's scarce time should go into trying to solve or dissolve the regress, and how much into constructing a bootstrap that is defensible without solving it? Is there a difference for this purpose between a jurisdiction whose founding is legally continuous with a predecessor state and one, like this, where the discontinuity is sharp?

Two: whether the proposals can be coherently combined, or whether some of them undercut others. Does the avoidability discount, for instance, do the same work as the overlapping-constituency escape, so that adopting both is redundant or doubly excludes the same people? Does domicile-year accrual presuppose a single demos whose membership admits of degrees, and therefore collapse if decision rights are disaggregated by subject matter? Does the sunset convention inherit the regress it was introduced to cure, or does randomness sever the dependence?

Three: what should happen when the criterion and any workable procedure come apart. The downstream water users look like a strong case on an affectedness view and a weak one on a subjection view, yet any procedure for registering them would have to be designed and administered by a body of which they are not members. Similarly, the contracted-out coercive functions look like they fail a subjection test even for people everylse would count as clearly subjected. Say which of these mismatches are diagnostic of a bad criterion, which are merely implementation friction to be absorbed by delegation, and which should be left unresolved in the text.

Finally, identify at least one question that the Committee treats as urgent and that you would recommend leaving deliberately open, with an explanation of why indeterminacy is superior here to a resolution reached under time pressure. And specify, for the top two items on your agenda, what argument or what change in the jurisdiction's circumstances would move them down the list. Where a recommendation depends on an empirical guess about migration, contract renewal rates, or the behaviour of the private security providers, flag it and say how the Committee could cheaply reduce the uncertainty before deciding.
