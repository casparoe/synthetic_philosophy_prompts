I run a small contract-review shop — me and three others, mostly commercial leases and SaaS agreements — and for the past year I've also been doing paid work for a lab that does the scalable-oversight thing, where you produce "expert" judgments on long documents by splitting the job into little pieces that crowdworkers or models can each handle, and then distilling the whole pipeline into a single model that jumps straight to the answer in one pass. Doing both at once has made me realize I've got a pile of fairly strong gut verdicts about when decomposition actually preserves the thing you wanted and when it quietly destroys it, and I can't get them to line up under any one principle. Here they are, rough. Splitting a 60-page lease into per-clause summaries done by different juniors and then merging the summaries into a risk paragraph: fine, and I trust it more than one senior person doing a single pass. Pre-flight check on a plane, per-system, any single failure grounds it: fine, obviously. A medical diagnosis where each organ system gets assessed separately by a different person and the disposition is read off the merged notes, with no generalist ever seeing the patient: not fine, and it feels dangerous in a way I can't quite articulate. Hiring: separate 1–7 scores on each of eight criteria, mechanically weighted: not fine, I'd throw out the ranking even if I got to set the weights myself. But a restaurant review split into food / service / ambiance and averaged: fine, no complaint whatsoever. Peer review scored separately on novelty, rigor and clarity: fine as input to a decision, not fine if the editor just aggregates the numbers and skips the overall recommendation — I want a human to actually say accept or reject at the end. Fact-checking an article by extracting atomic claims and checking each in isolation: fine for literal truth, not fine at all for "is this piece misleading", which is usually about what got left out or juxtaposed. Translating a poem line by line: not fine, and not fixable by doing the line-by-line part better. And one that bugs me in a different way: a go/no-go product decision decomposed into factual sub-questions (market size, engineering readiness, legal exposure) with the instruction to stick to the facts and not weigh in on whether the thing is good for the world — that seems not fine, but unlike the others it's not that the decomposition loses competence; it seems to work fine as a competence trick and still launder out something that mattered. My friend says I'm overthinking this.

The closest I've gotten to a principle is: decomposition is OK when (1) each sub-judgment is genuinely easier to get right than the whole judgment, (2) the pieces get recombined by a rule fixed in advance rather than by whoever happens to be in the room, and (3) the target is a fact or a quantity rather than something that has to be *owned* by somebody. But (1) and (2) don't seem to separate the plane from the patient, or hiring from restaurants, and I can't tell whether (3) is doing real work or just restating my verdicts in fancier language.

So: is there a principle that gets most of these, which clause is forced by which case, and which of my verdicts do I have to give up to get it? I want it stated precisely enough that I could hand it to a junior, plus its verdicts on cases I haven't listed — grading an undergrad philosophy essay with a rubric of sub-scores, a jury asked to answer special interrogatories instead of returning a general verdict, a data safety monitoring board deciding whether to stop a trial early, a manager deciding a promotion purely by ticking off rubric bullets. And tell me what it says about the distillation step in particular, since that step seems like it can fail even when the decomposition itself is fine — the one-shot model has to guess which decomposition the amplifier would have chosen, and the right decomposition is often context-dependent — and might even succeed where the decomposition was already lossy. If the honest answer is that "preserves competence" and "preserves alignment" need two different principles, say that and say why.
