I need help predicting a verdict, and I want the reasoning more than the answer.

The leader of our reading group on reduction and emergence keeps a public running list of cases she has ruled on — emergent or not emergent — and she has agreed to rule on one more at the end of term, from a written description, sight unseen. A few of us are betting on the outcome, so what I am after is a prediction plus an account of what the prediction would show about the principle she is running.

Her verdicts so far:

1. The wetness of water: not emergent. Molecular cohesion plus surface energetics; nothing about the liquid does work that the molecular story does not.
2. The temperature of a gas: not emergent. It is mean molecular kinetic energy, and she does not think the fact that temperature is realized in gases, metals, radiation and spin systems tells against the reduction, because the reduction proceeds via a role rather than via a shared microstructure.
3. The folding of a protein into its native structure: not emergent. She concedes that it is brutally hard to predict and that in the cell it usually needs chaperones, and she still says no: hard is not the same as emergent, and a bigger box is still a box.
4. A glider in Conway's Game of Life: not emergent. She grants that the pattern is invisible in the rules and appears only when you run them, and denies that this is the kind of emergence she cares about. She has also said she will not accept "weakly emergent" as an answer to a case — it has to be yes or no.
5. Superconductivity: emergent. Her stated reason is that strictly speaking no finite collection of electrons and ions superconducts; the phenomenon is tied to a limit in which a symmetry breaks sharply, so there is no route to it by aggregating the physics of the parts.
6. The fractionally charged excitations in the quantum Hall effect: emergent. The excitations are collective, carry a fraction of the electron's charge, and are defined by what they do rather than by what they are made of; she says nothing in the micro-physics of electrons in a magnetic field prepares you for them.

Note that all six are physics or chemistry. She has never ruled on a biological case, and when I asked about a cell she said that was the one she keeps not answering.

The new case is hypothetical, so nobody can look it up. A minimal synthetic cell: a vesicle containing a reaction network of roughly two hundred molecular species in which every catalyst is itself produced by some reaction in the network, and the network also regenerates the molecules that make up the vesicle membrane. Fed two simple precursors it maintains itself indefinitely, every molecule in it turns over within minutes, and once it grows past a threshold it divides. The description she will be given stresses three things. (i) It is finite and fully simulable: integrating the kinetics on a laptop reproduces the maintenance and the division, and there is no infinite-system limit, no broken symmetry and no non-perturbative step anywhere — the simulation is ugly but unproblematically correct. (ii) The same functional organisation has now been implemented in three unrelated chemistries, one lipid-and-water, one peptide–nucleotide, one in a non-aqueous solvent, and the three share no molecular species. (iii) The organisational description appears to carry causal weight: deleting most single catalysts is buffered by rerouting through other pathways, but deleting one whose role is to close a loop in the network stops maintenance, and no inspection of the molecules one at a time marks that difference.

What I would like from you: your best guess at her verdict; the two or three candidate rules that fit all six precedents and what each of them predicts here (the obvious candidates seem to be a singular-limit or deductive-autonomy rule, an organisational or downward-constraint rule, and a multiple-realizability rule, but I want to know what each really comes to once it is made to fit verdicts 2 and 4, and whether they are genuinely three rules); which of the six is the closest analogue and why that is itself contested; what she would be committing to by answering either way; and the single further case you would put to her afterwards to separate whichever rules survive her answer. I would also like to know whether feature (iii) ought to matter to her given verdict 4, or whether verdict 4 has already settled that it does not.

My own money is on not emergent, on the strength of 3 and 4 and the fact that the simulation works. But (ii) is the detail I cannot fit, and it is the one that makes me suspect the precedents do not reach this case at all.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the prediction, in one sentence — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
