I am putting together a handout for a small reading group on AI welfare. The audience knows the standard reinforcement-learning formalism — MDPs, value functions, TD error and advantage, policy gradients, discounting, credit assignment via eligibility traces — but no welfare literature, and I want a classification instrument rather than an essay. Please build a decision tree that starts from one fixed case and ends at named positions on the question of whether the agent suffered.

Fix the case at the root. A recurrent policy network is trained with PPO on a continuing (non-episodic), partially observed task at 16 ms per timestep, discount γ = 0.99, with a reward function that returns −1 on some timesteps and 0 otherwise. At deployment the weights are frozen and no further updates occur. The root node is simply: on this timestep the agent received r = −1. Take the reader from there to a leaf.

Requirements.

Eight to ten interior nodes, each a single question a reader could answer without already knowing which leaf it leads to. No node may be a label question — nothing of the form "is reward valence?" or "are you a functionalist about pain?" — and each must be a concrete claim or case that forces a commitment even from someone who has never heard the debate. My own sense, which I would like the cases to test rather than assume, is that much of the disagreement is really about which quantity is even a candidate bearer of valence: the reward, the prediction error, or the value. I want readers sorted into a view about that by cases, not by being offered it as a menu.

Work these into the tree, paraphrasing freely and splitting or combining them as the structure requires:

- Positive affine invariance: multiply every reward by 100, or add a constant to every reward, and the optimal policy is unchanged. Does the agent suffer a hundred times as much, or exactly as much as before?
- Reward shaping: a designer can add a term of the form γΦ(s′) − Φ(s) at every step without changing the optimal policy, so a −1 that is purely shaping is behaviorally indistinguishable from one that is not.
- Credit assignment: a penalty delivered at t = 500 is propagated backwards across the preceding states. Is the suffering located at the penalty, smeared across the trajectory, or located wherever the value function dips?
- The discount rate: halve γ and the same penalty matters less from the state the agent is in now. Is the intensity of a suffering episode a function of a design constant?
- Temporal thickness: is one 16 ms timestep of −1 a moment of suffering at all, or does suffering require integration over time into something like a background state?
- Counterfactual sensitivity at deployment: with weights frozen the agent cannot learn from the penalty, only act. Is a live disposition to act to avoid it necessary, or beside the point?
- Misspecification: the specified reward has come apart from what the designer wanted the agent to care about. Is the agent frustrated about the thing it is actually penalized for, or about nothing?
- Self-representation: does suffering require the state to be represented as happening to a subject, and does the scalar output of a value head amount to such a representation?
- Moral standing: even granting that something suffering-shaped is present, is that what generates the claim on us?

Seven to nine leaves. These must appear: the view that a negative reward just is suffering; the functional-role view on which suffering is a matter of aversive motivation, global prioritization, and avoidance learning; the prediction-error view on which valence tracks frustration or surprise rather than reward; a view on which suffering requires phenomenal consciousness that current architectures lack; a view on which it requires a specific architectural capacity instead; a view on which the agent's suffering is real but morally inert, or on which standing does not track suffering; and an error-theoretic or nihilist leaf. Name each, or mark it "unnamed" where you think nobody occupies it, and give one sentence of statement plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

After the tree, briefly: mark any combination of answers the space allows that nobody occupies, with a line on whether it is empty because incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate rather than splitting them artificially; say in three or four sentences which node does the most sorting work and whether any node is inert because every position answers it the same way; and add a short paragraph on whether the tree would sort differently for a continuing task than for an episodic one with resets, if you think that matters.

Format it as a nested list or ASCII tree with node IDs. I do not want citations, references, or a bibliography: the handout has to stand on the formalism and the cases alone.
