Some background, so you know what kind of thing you are dealing with. I left school at eighteen and work in warehouse logistics; for a couple of years now I have been reading philosophy on my own with five other people, none of us academics, meeting upstairs at a pub and working through whatever we can find free online. A few months ago we became stuck on the question of how an ordinary object — a bicycle, a person — manages to be the same thing from one moment to the next. Half of us hold that the object is, at each instant it exists, wholly there: the very same thing, complete, present again and again as time passes, so that there is no sense in which you ever meet only a piece of it. The other half hold that a persisting object is a long, stretched-out entity built out of temporal parts, and that whatever you encounter at a single instant is strictly a part of the object rather than the whole of it. We have now spent four sessions going round in circles. What frustrates me is that this is not a shortage of facts: we all agree about what physically happens to the bicycle as it rusts, we all agree that it had a bell at noon and no bell at six, and nobody disagrees about any prediction anyone else would make. Nothing anybody says seems to touch the other side.

My own hypothesis — please treat it as a hypothesis and tell me plainly if it is muddled — is that the two positions are not rival descriptions of the furniture of the world at all, but rival bookkeeping systems, and that the disagreement dissolves once you say what a claim about persistence over time is supposed to be for: telling you what you will find when you go back to the shed, deciding whether a person can be held responsible for what they did thirty years ago, or making sense of grieving someone who has changed beyond all recognition. Two members of my group reject this for opposite reasons. One says the real quarrel is smuggled in from an earlier disagreement about whether past and future moments are as real as the present one, and that settling that would settle ours. The other says it is a bare clash of intuitions which neither side can argue for, and that the best we can do is notice whose intuitions are doing the work. I am sceptical of both, but I cannot say why.

Two further things that may help, one of them my own invention. The case that actually split our group was not a ship or an axe but a machine I made up: a device which, when you press it, causes the preceding five minutes to occur a second time with exactly one small difference, leaving no trace of the first run except in the memory of the person who pressed it. I designed it to sharpen a question about whether the earlier stretch of a life can be shared between two later stretches, but I cannot tell whether it does any work that plainer cases do not, or whether it merely imports the very assumption under dispute. Separately: when I try to say what I myself believe, the picture I keep reaching for is that a persisting object is like a running bank balance — nothing about the balance is wholly present on any single day, what makes it the same balance is the unbroken tally of its transactions, and a gap in the ledger would simply be the end of it. I have started calling this the ledger view, and I genuinely cannot tell whether it is a third position, one of the two standard ones wearing a metaphor, or something that falls apart the moment anyone pushes on it.
