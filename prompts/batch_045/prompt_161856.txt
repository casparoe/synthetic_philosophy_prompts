Here's the background as I understand it. The "wrong kind of reasons" problem starts from cases like: a demon will torture you unless you admire him, or a doctor has a big pragmatic stake in believing you'll recover. In both there's clearly *a* reason to have the attitude, but it seems to be the wrong kind — the demon's threat is a reason to admire, but not a reason that has anything to do with whether he's admirable, and the doctor's stake is a reason to believe but not evidence. The usual way of drawing the line: right-kind reasons bear on whether the attitude fits or is merited by its object; wrong-kind reasons bear on the costs and benefits of having it. And the usual moves in response are things like: analyze the attitudes by their fittingness conditions, or go buck-passing about value so that value just is the having of right-kind reasons, or treat the puzzle as showing that reasons-talk is ambiguous.

My wrinkle: every stock case in this debate involves attitudes whose fittingness looks fixed entirely by features of the object — admirableness, fearfulness, truth. But there's a large class of attitudes where fittingness also depends on facts about *you* and your relation to the object: you can't fittingly feel gratitude unless you're the beneficiary, resentment unless you were the one wronged, guilt unless you were the agent, regret unless the outcome is attributable to you in the right way, pride in an achievement unless it's yours. Call them standpoint-dependent.
