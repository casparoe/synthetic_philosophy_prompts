I'm putting together a problem set for a course I'm TAing on collective decision-making, and I keep failing to write one question I really want. The thing I want to make vivid is that letting each person watch everyone before her can make every single individual better informed while making the group as a whole worse informed — and not by some tiny amount, but by a lot. I had an argument about this with a friend who works in public health and she thinks the second half is impossible, so I want a case clean enough to settle it.

The setup is fixed and I don't want it changed: binary state of the world, uniform prior, a line of about 25 decision-makers choosing one at a time, each getting one private bit that matches the truth with probability 2/3, each seeing the full history of earlier choices before picking one of two options. No abstaining, no waiting, no talking, nobody ever finds out whether earlier choices turned out right, nobody is paid or forced, no private bit ever becomes public, and everyone just wants their own choice to be correct — no taste for agreeing with others, no reputation. When the public evidence and a person's own bit exactly cancel, they go with their own bit (that's the convention we use in the course, so please keep it). The world where nobody can see anybody is the same in every respect: same 25 bits, same accuracy, same everything — the only difference is whether the history is visible.

What I need from you:

1. A mundane version of this — a hospital group adopting a screening protocol, franchisees stocking one of two product lines, farmers picking a seed variety, whatever you like — in one short paragraph, where the group's judgment is something real that actually gets aggregated (say a regional director who tallies the 25 decisions and goes with the majority). No sci-fi, no markets-with-demons.

2. Show the two verdicts with actual numbers, not hand-waving. On the individual side: every agent from the third onward should be strictly better off ex ante than she'd be on her own bit alone. Check agent 2 separately — my own calculation says she is *exactly* indifferent, with accuracy 2/3 either way, and that agent 3 comes out at 20/27; verify or correct me. On the group side: the accuracy of the majority should be stuck near 80% with the history visible, versus mid-90s at N=25 without it, and the gap should widen as N grows rather than close.

3. Tell me which single structural feature is doing the work, and show what happens to the case if you delete it.

4. Two things I specifically want checked because I think they flip: (a) if the group defers to the *last* person instead of tallying everyone, I think the comparison reverses — herding gives about 80% and going it alone gives only 2/3 — work out whether that's right and say why the aggregator matters so much; (b) if you insert one much better-informed agent (accuracy 0.9, publicly known) halfway down the line, I think she can break a herd that has already formed and drag the group's accuracy up to roughly her own accuracy before a herd re-forms downstream — check that too.

5. Finally, the objection I can't answer myself: in the world where people can see each other, no individual is worse off, so whose welfare is the majority accuracy supposed to be? A defender of letting people watch will say I've quietly swapped in a planner's criterion that nobody in the story has, and that no agent would ever vote to blind herself. Say how much of that objection lands, how you'd answer it, and whether the case survives if what actually gets implemented for everybody is the group's tally.

If some part of my specification can't be met, say so and tell me which constraint has to give — I'd much rather have that than a scenario where the numbers are just asserted.
