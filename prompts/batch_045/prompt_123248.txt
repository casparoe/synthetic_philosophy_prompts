Hey — I'm a journalist; I write about science and ideas for a general-ish audience, mostly long features about how knowledge actually gets made (replication drama, expert disagreement, scientists who turn out to be wrong and won't admit it). I keep running into the same shape of situation in my reporting and I want to get the philosophy straight without turning the piece into a literature tour. I can't verify citations from a hotel room anyway, so please skip the reading list and don't name papers or philosophers — I want the argument itself, and I want you to actually take a side somewhere rather than doing the "some say X, others say Y" thing and leaving it floating.

Here's the shape of the case. An expert forms a view — say a genomics researcher concludes a variant is linked to a disease, partly on gut feel built from ten years at the bench — and then something arrives that in some sense tells her to trust that judgment less: a colleague of similar standing disagrees; or she learns the analysis was run on a pipeline that later turned out to have a bug; or she realises she did the key pass at 1am after two glasses of wine. Nothing has directly shown the conclusion is false. She's just been handed a reason to distrust the process that produced it. Sometimes she revises; sometimes she says, in effect, I've seen the disagreement and I still think I'm right, and the people around her treat that as either admirable stubbornness or as flat-out irrationality. My sense is that philosophers have a whole apparatus for this — evidence about your evidence, and a puzzle about whether you can coherently keep believing something while also thinking the thing that produced that belief was unreliable.

What I'd like you to do: restate that whole debate in the vocabulary I use all day. I mean sourcing, corroboration, credibility hierarchies, denials, off-the-record, corrections and retractions, standing by a story, what you're willing to put in print versus what you privately believe, and the fact that in a newsroom it's completely routine to run something you're 60% on and kill something you're 90% on for reasons that have nothing to do with likelihood. Don't just tell me it's "like checking your sources" — actually do the translation, including for the part where she's accused of being irrational for not moving. Then give me the residue report, which is the bit I care about: what translated cleanly, what only survived because you fudged it, and what has no expression at all in newsroom vocabulary — and whether that last pile is a real gap in how reporters think or a sign that the philosophical worry was never as deep as it sounds on the page. Two wrinkles I specifically want handled. First, the editor case: my editor has no expertise in genomics whatsoever but she's an expert on me, and she says you always get overconfident when you're excited about a story. That isn't evidence about the world, isn't quite evidence about my reasoning on this occasion, and isn't peer disagreement either — it's evidence about my dispositions from someone who knows the reporter and not the topic. Where does that sit, and does the framework need a category it doesn't currently have? Second, the print/believe split: journalism has thick norms about assertion and almost none about belief, so the state philosophers treat as a kind of incoherence might just be Tuesday for a reporter. Does that dissolve the puzzle, relocate it to norms of assertion, or just show that my vocabulary can't see the problem? I should come away with one sentence I could drop into a feature without embarrassing myself.
