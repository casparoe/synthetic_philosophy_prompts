I'm a science writer — mostly AI and cognitive science — and I've been asked to run a 45-minute breakout at a media conference in the fall: about 25 working reporters, mixed ages, no philosophy background, half of them checking email, budget basically zero. I can get paper, pens, index cards, maybe scrounge some dice or poker chips. No laptops, and I can't count on a projector.

The thing I want them to actually feel, not hear about, is Dennett's old careenium setup. As I remember it: a planet covered in some stuff called careenium, whose surface is a huge carpet of tiny cells, each one obeying one dumb local rule about its neighbors. Zoom out and you see lumps moving across the surface — simmballs — traveling in straight lines and knocking off each other roughly like billiard balls, and you can describe the whole history of the planet with a tiny fraction of the bits it would take to list what every cell did. The tease is the question he asks: do the simmballs push the careenium around, or is the careenium just pushing itself around and the simmballs are a story we tell on top? I care about this on my own beat, because every week I write sentences like "the model decided to lie" or "the chatbot believed the meeting was at three" and I honestly can't tell whether I'm reporting a cause or just using a shorthand that happens to work.

So design me an activity where the room runs into that themselves. What I'd like: they should first succeed wildly by using the simmball-level description — predicting where a lump will be twenty steps out, beating the people grinding through the cell rules — and then hit a case where the object-level story breaks or has to be patched, ideally at a collision, so they're forced back down into the substrate. I want them arguing about whether the lump "did" anything before anyone says the word real. Rules, what to hand out, any roles, a timeline with minute marks, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the actual claim, including the compressibility point, which I think is the real one and which I definately don't trust myself to explain cold. Tell me what will go wrong the first time I run it — I can guarantee one person will try to win by being clever, and one will say "its all just electrons, right" about five minutes in — and be honest about which part of the argument the game can't stage and I'll just have to say out loud myself. And no opening lecture from me; I'll want to, so build the thing so I can't.
