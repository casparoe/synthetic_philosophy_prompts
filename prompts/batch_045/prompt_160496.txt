I'm TA-ing (badly) a first-year epistemology course this term and we've got a week on aesthetic testimony coming up, and I want to do something less bloodless than the usual can-you-know-the-film-is-good-from-your-mate's-say-so example. My plan is to show Dead Poets Society instead and read it as an argument about exactly that, but I want a reading that's honest in both directions — not a film squeezed into a seminar problem, and not the problem bent until it fits the film.

Read it through the aesthetic testimony debate. The obvious hook is the opening stretch where Keating makes the boys rip out the introduction to their poetry textbook — the one by the fictional Dr Pritchard that (if I remember right) has you rate a poem's 'perfection' and its 'importance', plot those against each other and multiply to get its greatness. That looks like a clean dramatisation of the anti-testimony thesis: you can't be handed aesthetic value in a formula, you have to meet the poem yourself. But I don't think the film is that tidy. Keating is himself a huge source of aesthetic testimony — the boys defer to his enthusiasm constantly, the club basically runs on borrowed taste, and what he swaps Pritchard's scoring for isn't silent first-hand acquaintance, it's Whitman, romanticism and his own performance. So is he replacing one testimonial source with another, or is the film gesturing at something the debate hasn't named? I've been toying with a four-way distinction and want it stress-tested: (a) transmission — testimony hands you the verdict as knowledge; (b) filtering — testimony tells you what to bother meeting, without tell you what you'll find; (c) calibration — you check a critic's verdicts against the handful of cases you do know first-hand and extrapolate from there; (d) licensing — a trusted voice gives you permission to trust your own shaky response, or standing to say out loud that you like the thing. Pritchard's scale is aimed at (a); Keating, I think, is mostly (b) and (d) and maybe a bit of (c). Does that taxonomy actually carve up the space, and does the film bear it out?

Then there are the bits that make me unsure whether the film even likes its own view. The ending: Todd stands on his desk and says O Captain, my Captain — the boy who finds his own voice does it by quoting someone else's poem at his teacher. Is that a vindication of aesthetic testimony or the reductio of it? And Neil: the aesthetic testimony that gets a boy killed. Keating's romanticism about poetry is what pushes Neil onto that stage, and the film plainly adores Keating while also showing his influence isn't reliably good — so what should its attitude to aesthetic deference be, given that? Where does the reading just break down, i.e. what in the film isn't about testimony at all and shouldn't be forced into it? What, if anything, does it add to the debate that the arguments on their own don't? And which two or three scenes would you actually show in a 90-minute seminar, in what order?
