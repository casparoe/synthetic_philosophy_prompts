I'm a junior at a large public high school, and I edit the humor page of our student newspaper. For the spring issue I'm assembling a section called "Big Ideas, Badly Implemented," and I'd like one piece for it.

The piece should be an internal document of a school district that has voted to adopt illusionism about phenomenal consciousness as its official position on consciousness for policy purposes. Format it as a single administrative regulation, roughly 750–900 words, in four parts: (1) a memorandum from an Assistant Superintendent for Student Experience to building principals, (2) a numbered glossary of official reporting categories, (3) a student-facing FAQ, and (4) a detachable "Report of Introspective Discrepancy" form with signature blocks for the student and a parent or guardian. The voice throughout is deadpan bureaucratic prose: no narrator, no winking, no character ever states that the doctrine is false or true, and no one ever says that students are zombies or that pain isn't bad. If the author wants to editorialize, it must go in a footnote attributed to a subcommittee.

The jokes have to come from getting illusionism right. Specifically: deny phenomenal properties as they are standardly characterized (intrinsic, ineffable, private, immediately apprehensible) while retaining everything functional — pain keeps its sensory-discriminative and motivational roles, still grounds complaint, still matters morally — and let the district's insistence on that point get funnier each time it repeats it. Use the misrepresentation move rather than crude eliminativism: introspection represents a perfectly real, non-phenomenal state as having just the features the doctrine says nothing has, so the illusion is a misdescription of something real, not a hallucination of nothing. Have the district treat students' reports as data — record what they say, bracket whether it is true — which should produce forms that take reports extremely seriously while refusing their content, including a 1–10 intensity rating of a quantity the district denies exists. Let the self-undermining worry land through form rather than argument: the compliance office strikes every occurrence of seems, feels, appears, and what it is like, and the approved replacements need striking in turn, until the redlines have redlines.

Three more things I want worked in. First, an inverted-spectrum-style dispute between two students that the district resolves as a data-entry error, on the ground that it does not compare qualitative characters because there are none to compare. Second, a standing "Public Explanation Subcommittee" with a budget line and a deliverable, charged with explaining why students persist in reporting phenomenality at all. Third, my own invented twist, which I'd like you to build the piece around: the regulation applies only within the district's jurisdiction — on school grounds, on district transportation, and at school-sponsored events — so the FAQ has to address the walk home, the weekend, and whether the property reattaches at the property line.

Don't name any real philosophers, living or dead; invent plausible officials, titles, and case numbers. The exaggeration should be of the administrative application, not of the thesis: someone who actually holds illusionism should recognize their view in it and feel it was a fair hit. But my classmates, who have never heard the word qualia, should still be laughing.
