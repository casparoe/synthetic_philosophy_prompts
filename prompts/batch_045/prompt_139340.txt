I've been reading the rationalist blogs since the middle of the last decade, and for the past two years I've run a monthly reading group — a dozen people on a good night, mostly working software engineers, two with graduate training in philosophy, one biologist. We've already done evenings on the orthogonality thesis and on why value looks fragile under optimization pressure, so everyone arrives able to say what a goal-content for a powerful system would have to be and why you can't just hand it your current desires. Everyone will also have read the short original statement of coherent extrapolated volition before they come; I don't need it summarized, and I'm not asking for further reading.

Next month it's my turn again, and I have ninety minutes to get the group to understand CEV well enough to be dangerous with it — specifically, to understand how it differs from the older idealized-preference and informed-desire pictures it keeps getting conflated with, and where the genuine open problems sit. Please don't explain CEV to me. Plan how I should teach it, and structure your reply in exactly these five sections:

1. "Dependency map" (~200 words): the order the components have to go in and what depends on what. I especially want to know whether the coherence condition has to be introduced as a fixed-point requirement before or after the group has actually felt why a step-by-step improvement of the subject fails to deliver one.

2. "The carrying example" (~250 words): the single case I should build the evening around, what it has to contain, and what it has to lack. My candidate is a sequencing case: the subject, given more information first and then unlimited time to reflect, converges on wanting one thing; given the reflection first and then the information, converges on wanting something else — so that there is no order-independent answer to what he would want. Tell me whether that case carries the idea or whether it smuggles in something that will mislead the group, and what to use instead if so.

3. "Misconception ledger" (~350 words): a table with columns for the misconception, the point in the session where it will surface, and the move that defuses it. My guesses at the top three: treating CEV as a decision procedure for present-day choices rather than as a target for a system; collapsing the several improvements it stacks up — more knowledge, faster reasoning, greater convergence with the person we would want to be, more of a shared history — into one vague notion of greater intelligence; and reading the would-want-to-be clause as purely epistemic. Correct the guesses you think are wrong and add the ones I've missed.

"The one I can't place" (~150 words): whether the circularity worry — that the would-want-to-be clause needs a prior standard of the better self, which is just the value theory CEV was supposed to let us avoid — is a misconception I should defuse in sequence, or a real hole I should concede out loud, and where in the evening that concession should land if it's the latter.

5. "The check and the refusals" (~150 words): one thing I can have them do in the final ten minutes that distinguishes actual understanding from everyone nodding along, plus what I should openly decline to settle at this level and the sentence to use when someone pushes for it.

Two constraints. First, the interpersonal step — extrapolating a shared we rather than a single person, including the prior question of who is in that we to begin with — needs its own slot in the sequence. I don't want it riding along as a footnote to the individual case, and I want the group to see why it isn't simply the same problem twice.
