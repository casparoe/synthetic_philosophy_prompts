For the past few months I've been assembling a small benchmark of analogy problems for probing small language models, in the spirit of the letter-string puzzles that crop up in the cognitive-science literature on analogy-making — you know the sort: "abc becomes abd, so what does ijk become?" Except that I have lifted the alphabet off the line and onto a 5×5 grid:

a b c d e
f g h i j
k l m n o
p q r s t
u v w x y

(I drop z so that the grid comes out square; "east" means one column to the right, "south" means one row down.) Every item in the benchmark supplies the same single solved pair, "abc → abd" — read it as "move the rightmost letter one step east" — and then asks what some new string turns into. Most probes are trivial, but four of them have been the subject of an argument that has now run for several weeks on a mailing list I have been on since the early days of the rationalist blogs, and I would like an outsider's view. The four are: "mno", where the rightmost letter sits at the eastern edge of its row although it still has an ordinary alphabetical successor; "wxy", where the rightmost letter is in the corner and both east and south run off the grid; "aabbcc", where the string has an internal grouping that the solved pair says nothing about; and "yxw", a run that steps westward, so that taking the last letter one step east would duplicate the middle letter instead of continuing the run. One friend insists that "mnp" is obviously right in the first case precisely because two quite different descriptions — cyclic east, and plain alphabetical successor — converge on it, whereas in the second case every candidate ("wxa", "wxe", "wxt", "wxx", "wxya", or simply declining to answer) has to buy its plausibility from a single stipulation, and that this asymmetry is the whole story. Another friend denies that there is any story to tell about the strings at all.

Underneath the squabbling there is a real disagreement, and that is what I want the essay to be about. Call one camp the structuralists: they hold that the solved pair together with the geometry of the grid fixes, for each probe, a uniquely natural answer, because the candidate rules can be ranked by how well they bring base and probe under a single description — shortest encoding, fewest ad hoc stipulations, maximal preservation of whatever pattern made "abc → abd" feel right in the first place — and that what looks like a concept slipping is only the solver climbing to a more general description that was implicitly available all along. Call the other camp the fluidists: they hold that the solved pair underdetermines the answer in every non-trivial probe, that what actually selects among the candidates is which concepts happen to be available and how entrenched they are (the alphabetical order we all learned as children, the sheer greater frequency of "successor" over "predecessor", recency, the pressure of the higher-level pattern), and that there is therefore no domain-intrinsic ordering of the candidates at all — "naturalness" is a fact about populations of solvers, and the impression that one abstract rule had been there all along is a rationalisation produced after the fact.

So: write me an essay of roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words in which you weigh these two accounts against one another and then defend a verdict. I do not want a fence-sitting conclusion, though a split verdict is perfectly acceptable: if you think the structuralists are right about two of the probes and the fluidists about the other two, say so, and say what does the work in each case. As you go, commit yourself to concrete answers — for each of the four probes, name the continuation you would call natural, or say plainly that you would decline to answer, and say whether you take that choice to be forced by the structure of the domain or imposed by the solver's repertoire. Steelman whichever camp you end up rejecting before you reject it, and if you think both camps are misdescribing the situation, say what the right description would be instead. Please write in British English.
