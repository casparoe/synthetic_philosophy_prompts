hey — I'm a journalist, I mostly cover science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about races to the bottom: the AI capability race, but also college-admissions tutoring arms races, adtech and the attention economy, academic publishing, that whole family. Every time I write about this, people in my feed reach for the same picture: it's a multiplayer prisoner's dilemma, so (the story goes) the diagnosis is settled and the fix is obvious — lengthen the shadow of the future, build reputation, get the same actors meeting repeatedly, have some institution enforce cooperation, and the trap dissolves. But I cornered an economist at a small workshop last month — she wasn't famous enough for me to quote, so I'm not going to name her — who said most of these cases aren't prisoner's dilemmas at all. Her claim, as I understood it: in a PD there's a jointly produced good, everyone is better off if everyone cooperates, and the cooperative outcome is Pareto-better than the equilibrium, which is exactly why iteration and reputation have something to grab onto. Whereas what she called a "slot tournament" (I wrote the term down as that but I'm probably mangaling it) is a situation where there's a fixed number of prizes — seats, returns, citations, attention, users — and what you're actually competing over is ordinal rank rather than absolute payoff. On her account that's constant-sum in the thing that matters, so the standard menu of fixes doesn't apply: if you unilaterally stop spending on the arms race you don't get a better world, you just get a worse rank, and nobody else's cooperation helps you either. The only levers she'd admit were changing the metric people are ranked on, changing the number of slots, or making the spending itself legible and banned. She said this is why so many of these races feel unsolvable even among actors who all know each other, meet annually, and sign lovely communiqués.

So: can you audit the two pictures for me? I'd like the actual correspondence table — who the players are, what the payoff is, what the equilibrium looks like, where the Pareto frontier sits, and what plays the role of "cooperate" and "defect" — for (a) the N-player PD / public-goods framing my feed likes, and (b) her rank-tournament framing, and maybe (c) the "it's just the tragedy of the commons with extra steps" version someone else keeps insisting on. Then where does each break? Concretely, in the admissions-tutoring, AI-capability, and attention-economy cases: is the thing being competed for genuinely ordinal and fixed, or is there a real jointly produced pie in which case the PD people are right and their fixes really would work? Does the PD framing quietly import a Pareto-improving cooperative outcome that just isn't available in a tournament, and does the tournament framing quietly assume the number of slots and the ranking metric are exogenous when they're usually themselves products of the race? Does either picture handle the case where the race changes the thing being raced for — the metric degrades, the prize is worth less once everyone has paid for it? And most importantly for my purposes: if her picture is even partly right, what happens to the conclusion everyone draws from the PD framing, i.e. that iteration, norms, and reputation are the main levers?

Last thing: someone at the same workshop muttered "all-pay auction" at me and I didn't follow, and separately I keep seeing people describe these as "externalities" and argue for a Pigouvian tax. Is the externality diagnosis the same claim as either of the two pictures, or a third one, and does it land on a different fix? And is there anything to the Moloch framing — the bit where the trap is described as something that actively eats cooperation — beyond just giving a spooky name to a Nash equilibrium? I want to write one paragraph in the piece that says what kind of situation this actually is without hand-waving, and I'd defintely rather not publish something that gets me an email from a game theorist. If both analogies are broken, tell me what a better one would be.
