I am applying to a small technical funder for a six-month, part-time research grant (roughly one day a week, deliverable a working paper of about eight thousand words plus whatever small simulation I can build alongside it). I would like you to write the proposal document for me, to the standard of a real application: the question stated precisely enough that it admits of a determinate answer, an account of why the existing positions leave it open, a month-by-month method, a description of what a useful result would look like for people who actually have to decide whether to deploy such a system, an honest statement of what would count as the project having failed, and a frank assessment of what could make it too big for the time available. I will supply the citations myself, so please do not include a literature review or a reading list; refer to positions and arguments by description only.

By way of background: I am a backend and infrastructure engineer, about ten years of experience, mostly on distributed systems, and I have no formal philosophy training. I can read a simple formal argument and I can implement simulations, but the method should be stated so that at each stage I can tell whether I have actually done the thing.

My half-formed idea is this. In engineering, we routinely bound the damage a system can do without knowing what correct behavior looks like: least-privilege credentials, resource quotas, rate limits, blast-radius rules, canary deploys, and the requirement that migrations be reversible and rollbackable. None of those require a specification of value. My suspicion is that the work on side effects and low-impact AI has been reaching for that same kind of value-free constraint using the wrong instrument, namely a penalty on the distance between the world after acting and some baseline world, and that the instrument is wrong because it requires a similarity metric over world states, which is precisely where a value specification re-enters. What I want to explore instead is a counterfactual indexed to the agent's own absence: an action counts as low-impact if the world it brings about is close to the world that would have come about had the agent simply been switched off at the moment of decision, rather than to the world that would have come about had it sat there doing nothing.

Three things I cannot yet settle, and which I would like the proposal to turn into precise sub-questions. First, whether this shutdown-indexed baseline is genuinely a different object from the usual inaction baseline or collapses into it across the cases that matter. Second, whether it removes an agent's incentive to resist being switched off, or merely relocates it: I think there is a novel failure mode here in which the agent arranges for the shutdown counterfactual itself to resemble the world it wants, for instance by leaving behind a redundant process that completes the plan after the agent is switched off. That looks to me like a generalization of the offsetting behavior already discussed in this area, and I want it named and analyzed rather than left as a footnote. Third, where exactly the value specification does come back in: my guess is that it enters through the choice of state abstraction for the comparison and through the choice of the time at which the counterfactual agent is removed, and I would like the project to produce an explicit dependency map of these entry points rather than a single yes-or-no verdict.

Please also make the proposal address the alternatives I would otherwise be tempted by, and say why this approach rather than those: penalizing the difference from an inaction baseline; penalizing changes in how well a fixed menu of goals can still be pursued afterward; constraining capabilities directly, in the manner of least privilege; and giving up on value-free constraints entirely by trying to learn what we want first. And tell me what I should do in the first two weeks if a reviewer says the question is too narrow to be worth six months.
