The answer I got:

> Rules like these are conventions. Following David Lewis, a convention is a solution to a coordination problem: it doesn't really matter which solution we land on as long as we all land on the same one, which is why driving on the left and driving on the right are both fine but mixing them isn't. So the content of the rule is arbitrary — it could just as well have gone the other way. But once everyone else has coordinated on one option, you have a reason to go along with it: you benefit from the scheme, and you frustrate other people's expectations if you don't. So conventions do generate genuine obligations; they're not just manners. It also follows that the obligation only exists where the coordination is at stake, so if nobody else is affected, the obligation lapses.
>
> That's why Dev did nothing wrong. The road was empty, no driver had to brake, nobody was delayed or frightened, and no one in Marlow saw him and had their expectations disappointed. If a convention is just a device for getting us all to the same equilibrium, then a deviation that nobody notices and that upsets no one isn't the sort of thing the convention is there to prevent. He isn't like somebody who drives on the wrong side of the road in traffic. I'd add that he couldn't be blamed anyway, because he's only visiting: the rule he grew up with in Fenwick is the opposite, and you can't realistically expect visitors to relearn all this. When in Rome, do as the Romans do — but that's prudence, not morality. What's actually right and wrong for him is still settled by what people in Fenwick think, and they wouldn't think anything of it.
>
> Tipping is different, and there I do think the visitor did something wrong. The café staff in Marlow are paid a wage that assumes tips, so leaving nothing takes money out of somebody's pocket. That's a straightforward harm rather than a breach of etiquette. So conventions matter morally in two separate ways: some matter because they coordinate us, and some matter because breaking them makes a person worse off.
>
> Last thing, though: not every convention deserves to be followed. Some places have conventions about who goes through a door first or who speaks first in a meeting that are bound up with keeping a certain group in their place. Those you should break, because the point of a convention is to solve a problem for everyone, and a rule that only works for one group isn't doing that.
