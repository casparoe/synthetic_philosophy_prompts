I teach an upper-level jurisprudence seminar, and this term I want to stage a mock adversarial collaboration rather than another rehearsal of the familiar mid-century exchange about whether the criminal law may be used to enforce morality. As a teaching artifact — and, frankly, because I want to see whether the thing can be done at all — I need you to draft the joint statement that two contemporary parties could actually sign. Party A is a legal moralist who concedes that the empirical disintegration thesis is in poor shape and has therefore reformulated the view: call it constitutive moralism. On this view a political society's shared morality is partly constituted by being given legal expression, so that decriminalising a practice does not merely fail to prevent a subsequent decline in adherence to a norm, but changes which morality it is that the society shares; the criminal law is not a cause of social cohesion but one of the places where a society's moral identity is made and declared. Party B is a Hartian liberal of a concessive stripe: she grants that some substantial shared morality is a necessary condition of any society worth having, insists on the distinction between a society's positive morality and the critical assessment of it, and denies only that legal enforcement does any work in securing or sustaining that shared morality which is not better done by leaving the norm to non-legal transmission, or which is positively undone when the law takes it up.

Two stipulations, so that neither side is a straw man. First, both accept that the cases which prompted the original exchange are settled: neither is arguing that consensual homosexual acts or prostitution should be criminalised, and neither may rest anything on them. I want the substance tested on cases we design instead, and here are three I would like used rather than substituted: (i) the segregated spectacle — a commercial practice that nearly every member of the society finds viscerally disgusting, performed only in venues no one enters without consenting to enter, and which harms or sets back no one's interests on any account of harm the liberal is willing to employ; (ii) the seepage variant of that same case, in which good survey evidence shows that people who come to accept the practice are, over the following decade, measurably less likely to comply with unrelated norms of honesty and mutual aid, though the direction of causation is entirely unestablished; (iii) the minority test, which is my own addition and which both parties must address — a moral conviction is eligible for legal enforcement only if the society would continue to hold it, and continue to want it enforced, even were its adherents reduced to a small and politically weak minority. Please also have both parties address the assurance argument I have been sketching in seminar: much moral compliance is conditional on the belief that others also comply; open and legally tolerated flouting degrades that assurance; and a declaratory criminal law is a cheap, reliable device for restoring it. Both should say whether the expressive work this argument assigns to the criminal law could in principle be done by non-legal institutions, and at what cost.

What I want from you is the document itself, with no preamble, no framing remarks and no history of the debate. Open with a single sentence stating the headline result: the most substantial claim the two parties found they could sign and that neither expected to sign. Then give a numbered section of agreed claims — claims about the formalism of the disagreement, about what a successful moralist argument would have to establish, about what the liberal concedes, about the three cases, and about the assurance argument — each claim phrased so that it is not vacuous: at least one party should have been inclined to reject it before the collaboration began. Follow this with a table of residual disagreements, in which each disagreement is stated twice, once in the moralist's own preferred wording and once in the liberal's, and neither wording is a caricature. Then a section on cruxes: for each, the question such that settling it would move at least one party, the case or piece of evidence both agree would bear on it, and each side's prediction about that evidence written down in advance, including what they would count as a result neither of them predicted. If you conclude that some part of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive — for instance if the constitutive claim and the causal claim collapse into one another under pressure — say exactly which part and why. Do not smooth the disagreement over with balancing formulae, do not attribute views to any named author or work, and do not give me a verdict on who wins.
