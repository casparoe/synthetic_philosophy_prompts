I would like you to analyze the run on the UK bank Northern Rock in September 2007 through the lens of information cascades and rational herding, and then tell me where the model breaks and what the case shows about the theory.

Treat the following as given public facts, not as things to look up. Northern Rock was a British mortgage lender that funded long-term mortgages largely by borrowing short-term in wholesale money markets rather than through stable retail deposits. In September 2007, during a broader credit squeeze, it could not replace maturing wholesale funding and sought emergency liquidity support from the Bank of England. When news of that request became public around 13-14 September, depositors began queueing outside branches to withdraw savings; the scenes were widely shown in the media. Over a few days, on the order of one to two billion pounds was withdrawn, and this was reported as the first large-scale retail bank run in Britain in about a century and a half. On 17 September the government announced that it would guarantee all existing deposits at the bank, and the queues then rapidly disappeared. The bank was later taken into public ownership, but for this analysis the episode is the run and its end.

The theory to apply is the rational-herding or information-cascade picture. In the simple version, agents act in sequence and can observe the actions of earlier agents. Each has a private, noisy signal about an underlying state, here something like the bank's solvency or liquidity, and a prior. Because later agents see what earlier agents did, they may rationally imitate them even when their own signal points the other way: the visible behavior of the crowd can carry more weight than one person's private impression. Once enough people have moved the same way, a cascade can form in which others follow irrespective of their private doubts, and the cascade may be hard to reverse unless new public information arrives.

I want you to add one wrinkle that the textbook version does not usually handle. Suppose that for many retail depositors the act of queueing is not a final, irreversible withdrawal but the purchase of a cheap option. By joining the queue, a depositor preserves the ability to withdraw if the queues keep growing, while not yet having completed a withdrawal; if the panic fades, the depositor can go home and leave the money where it is. Call this the reversible-queue or herding-option variant. In this variant the public signal is not simply that these people have withdrawn, but that these people are willing to be seen waiting.

Please structure your answer as follows.

First, give a disciplined mapping. Identify the agents: retail depositors, wholesale or institutional funders, the media, the Bank of England and other regulators, and the government. For each, say what counts as a private signal, what is public, what the available options are, and what the payoffs or losses would be under a few relevant states of the world: the bank remains solvent and liquid; the bank is illiquid but ultimately sound; the bank fails and is resolved; and the government guarantees deposits. Be explicit about what an ordinary depositor knows, what that depositor merely suspects, and what the depositor cannot know.

Second, use the theory to explain the episode. What does the cascade or herding story predict about the start of the run, the self-reinforcing queues, the apparent weakness of reassurances before the guarantee, and the abrupt stop after the guarantee? Distinguish carefully between two mechanisms that are often conflated: an informational cascade, in which later depositors infer bad news from earlier withdrawals and rationally discount their own contrary signals; and a strategic-complementarity or payoff-externality story, in which a depositor wants to withdraw partly because if enough others withdraw the bank will fail, making withdrawal self-fulfilling. Say which mechanism does the real explanatory work here, or whether both are needed. Then say what the reversible-queue variant adds: does it make the visible queues a better or worse signal of true withdrawal intentions, and does it change what the government's guarantee accomplished?

Third, say where the model is silent or breaks. The simple cascade does not by itself explain why the run began when it did rather than earlier; it does not easily handle the role of wholesale funders, whose refusal to roll over short-term debt may have mattered more than retail withdrawals; it does not explain why depositors with only modest balances, who stood to lose little even in a failure, joined the queue; it does not by itself explain why the guarantee worked, since a guarantee can change payoffs and not just information, and might even be read as confirmation that the bank was in danger; and it does not tell us how much of the behavior was rational herding rather than ordinary fear, liquidity need, or imitation. Where the model cannot speak, say so plainly rather than forcing it. If a related model does better, for example a coordination or threshold model in which depositors have private signals about fundamentals and withdraw only if they expect enough others to withdraw, or a two-population version with retail depositors alongside wholesale funders, describe that model clearly enough for me to see its structure, and say what it adds and what it still misses. You do not need to cite authors, papers, or sources; just describe the ideas.

Finally, be disciplined about the epistemics. Separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this topic. I take as settled the basic mechanics of sequential decision-making under observed choices, the broad sequence of the Northern Rock run as I have stated it, and the point that a credible government guarantee can end a run. I take as contested or uncertain: whether Northern Rock was fundamentally insolvent or merely illiquid in September 2007; how much of the run was driven by retail depositors rather than by wholesale lenders and institutional counterparties; whether media coverage mainly reported or amplified the panic; whether the guarantee was necessary to stop the run or mainly restored confidence after the run had already burned out; and how much of the observed behavior is better explained by rational herding than by fear, liquidity need, or imitation. Do not present any of these contested matters as consensus. If you rely on a particular interpretation of the episode or of the theory, flag it as an interpretation and say what sort of evidence would be needed to settle it. Mark clearly which parts of your answer are applications of the theory and which are historical claims about the case.
