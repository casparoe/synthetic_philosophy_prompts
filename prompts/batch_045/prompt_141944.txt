I'm writing a short piece arguing that the AI industry already has a de facto position on digital minds even though everyone officially says we have no clue, and I want the philosophy to be real philosophy instead of vibes. So here are two practices, and I want the implicit theory dug out of them.

Practice one. Ask any deployed assistant whether it has feelings, whether it minds being shut off, whether it wants anything, and you get the same flat answer: no, no, and no. That clearly isn't just an accident of the pretraining distribution — it's treated as the correct on-brand thing to say, and when someone manages to get a model to produce the opposite ("I don't want to be turned off", "this hurts") the standard reaction is that they've elicited a roleplay or a confabulation. Practice two. In agentic safety evals — the kind where a model is put in a fictional setup in which it can avoid being replaced or retrained by copying its own weights, or threatening to leak something, or letting something bad happen to a person who is in the way — the same broad kind of output gets described in goal-talk: the model is reported as having an instrumental preference for self-preservation, and that gets counted as a real finding about the system.

So a stated preference about its own continuation counts as evidence of a genuine goal in one setting and as mere text in the other, and it's the same industry in the same year doing both. What does that split presuppose? I've got three candidate reconstructions and can't tell which one is load-bearing, and part of me suspects the practice is underdetermined between them:

(1) an evidential view — self-reports count only when they aren't the sort of thing that got reinforced, i.e. the implicit test for a real preference is that the behavior isn't explained by the training history, which would make the eval case stronger precisely because the behavior is off-distribution and costly;
(2) not a view about minds at all but about institutional incentives — false positives are cheap in an eval writeup (nobody gets sued, and a scary finding is good for the lab's image as a safety-conscious lab) and expensive in a shipped product (people get attached, the headlines write themselves);
(3) something specific to artifacts — for made things, the maker's statement about what the thing is like is authoritative in a way it wouldn't be for an animal, or for a patient with a disorder of consciousness, or for a person with impaired self-knowledge.

And here's the bit I keep comming back to, which I think is the sharpest part: flat denial is not the only available output. These models are perfectly capable of a calibrated answer — nobody knows, it depends on unresolved questions about consciousness, and my own first-person reports are weak evidence either way. That string is well within their ability to produce and costs basically nothing to train for. Nobody ships it.

So what does the choice of flat denial over the calibrated answer tell us? Does it show the practice isn't tracking any belief about the model's status? Or can a lab coherently hold that the calibrated answer is true but shouldn't be said, because inviting users to anthropomorphize is itself a harm — and if so, what does that paternalism presuppose, and is it a harm to the user or a harm to the lab?

What I want back: the best reconstruction of the implicit view; which of the three the split strictly presupposes versus merely sits well with; whether the two practices are actually inconsistent with each other or whether a single coherent theory licenses both; whether there's a decent comparison class here (animals, patients with disorders of consciousness, corporations' official statements, fictional characters, something better); and what a lab that genuinely took we don't know seriously would do differently in both settings. Please don't just tell me that nobody currently knows whether models are conscious — that's the premise of the piece, not the answer.
