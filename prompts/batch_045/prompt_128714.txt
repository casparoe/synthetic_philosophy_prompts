Bit of a strange ask, and I'm making it partly because I'm four months from turning in a novel and genuinely don't have the bandwith to think about this properly. I'm one of five people on the program committee of a small private foundation — I'll leave the name out — whose charter commits it to funding writing and scholarship that defends Western civilisation and its inheritance. The actual wording is worse and vaguer than that, it was written by the donor in the early nineties, he's dead, and his widow is on the committee, takes the wording seriously, is not a fool, and will read whatever we circulate. We have to pick among three finalists for a single grant this month and the discussion has completely stalled, so I've been asked to draft the memo that says what we should do and why.

The finalists, briefly. One is a hybrid memoir-and-essay book by a writer who left Iran as a teenager; it argues fiercely for secular law, individual conscience, freedom of speech and the abolition of the death penalty, and also says outright that these things belong to nobody, that calling them Western is a way of pretending people like her didn't have to fight for them at home, and that the phrase in practice gets used to keep people like her out. The second is a polemic by a well-known essayist who defends the West at full volume and whose positive program includes compulsory national service, tight restrictions on building mosques, and financial incentives for people to leave the country; two of us think funding him is exactly what the mandate is for, two think it's a reputational disaster and possibly a legal one, and I can see both sides. The third is a set of historical essays arguing that the phrase as a self-description is recent, that it was coined to do boundary work rather than to describe a doctrine, and that the items people list when asked — democracy, secular law, individual rights, free inquiry — were mostly articulated as universals and are claimed as universals by writers all over the place, so that the category is either vacuous because everyone is already for it or tribal because it turns out just to mean us. That last one is the argument I keep going back to, and it's the one the widow will hate. My own attempt at a way out is to say the phrase is quietly doing three different jobs at once — naming a list of norms, naming a historical lineage of descent, and marking who counts as outside — and that every time our committee tries to write the list it comes out as things all five of us already liked anyway, which suggests the list isn't doing the work and the lineage is, in which case we are funding ancestry rather than arguments.

So: write me the memo I should circulate on Thursday. It needs an actual recommendation — who gets the money, or whether we reopen the field, split it, or give nothing this round — and it needs to say how the committee should read the mandate from here on so that the next lot aren't back in the same room in eighteen months. It should give the strongest version of whatever it's rejecting, because the widow will supply it if I don't, and I'd rather it commit to something than split the difference. Aim it at a smart reader who knows nothing about moral or political philosophy, including the other four of us. Please don't give me reading suggestions, footnotes, or citations, and don't rest anything on a quotation or a date nobody can check — I'm not going to chase references and neither is anyone else here, and I don't want it to read like a syllabus. And no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables: continuous prose, the way you'd write a long email, because that's literally what it's going into. Seven or eight hundred words would be about right.
