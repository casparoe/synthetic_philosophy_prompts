You are a professional philosopher who works on eighteenth-century moral psychology — above all on the ethics of the sentiments, and on the civic-republican critique of the arts, including Rousseau's polemical answer to d'Alembert about whether Geneva should be given a theatre. Before writing anything, work through the case on your own: list the distinctions that are doing the work, sketch the strongest version of each of the two or three responses you can see, and note what would have to be true for each to win. Then answer.

I sit on the board of a small foundation that runs the winter shelter in the town where I grew up. Our big annual fundraiser is a professionally staged one-man show: a well-known actor performs a solo piece he built from interviews with people who have slept rough. It is genuinely moving — people weep, and donations have risen every year. But the shelter's volunteer roster, the people who actually cook the shifts, drive the van and launder the bedding, has fallen for three years running, and more than one of them has told me privately that they feel they have already done their bit by buying a ticket and being moved. The men whose stories the piece is built from attend as guests and are given seats at the back. This year the board proposed replacing the gala with an evening in the town hall at which residents and shelter guests cook, serve and eat together — no stage, no audience, no one performing anyone else's life. The actor has counter-offered: keep the performance and add a moderated discussion afterwards.

I cannot say what, if anything, is wrong with the show, and I do not trust my own dislike of it. State the case against it as strongly as you can, then tell me whether you believe it. Is the money the show raises a defence of it or part of the problem? Would the moderated discussion repair the flaw or deepen it? Is the town-hall dinner relevantly different, or is my preference for it just sentimentality about participation with a better costume? And if you think this is an instance of an argument you know from that literature, use it explicitly — but also say where it stops fitting, since the town is not Geneva, the shelter is real, and the money is not imaginary.
