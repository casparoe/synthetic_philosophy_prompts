I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy writing a chapter on weakness of will, and I share a reading group with two behavioral economists. One of them put an argument to me some weeks ago that I have not managed to get out of, and what I want from you is a diagnosis of the argument rather than a survey of positions.

Start from the standard definition: an agent acts akratically at t only if she acts freely and intentionally against her own all-things-considered better judgment about what she ought to do at t. The argument, as he gave it:

(1) An agent's all-things-considered judgment at t ranks option A above option B just in case her preference ordering at t ranks A above B. There is no daylight between what she judges best at a time and what she prefers at that time for judgment to slip into.
(2) Intentional action at t follows the agent's preference ordering at t: if at t she prefers A to B, believes herself able to A, and is not blocked, she A-s.
(3) For an agent with a hyperbolic — or quasi-hyperbolic, beta–delta — discount function, the ordering over a smaller-sooner option s and a larger-later option l is time-indexed: l is ranked above s while both are temporally distant, and s is ranked above l once s becomes imminent.
(4) So at every time t such an agent does what she judges best at t. No sooner does the ordering reverse than the judgment reverses with it, and the action follows both.
(5) Akrasia requires acting against one's better judgment, and by (4) that never happens. So no agent with a hyperbolic discount function ever acts akratically in virtue of the reversal. The stock case — the dieter who eats the cake — is not a case of acting against better judgment at all, and the discounting model, precisely insofar as it successfully predicts the reversal, fails to generate the phenomenon it was introduced to explain.
(6) Nothing in (1), (2) or (4) depends on the shape of the discount function. Generalize, and no intentional action is ever akratic; continence becomes trivially universal rather than an achievement, and the effortful, variable, sometimes exhausting character of self-control becomes unintelligible.

He takes (6) as a reductio of the orthodox picture and concludes that we should stop treating continence and incontinence as a distinction in the agent's will and treat it as a distinction in her circumstances. I cannot accept that, and I also cannot find the step.

Three wrinkle cases I have tried on him, which seem to me to make the problem worse rather than better.

First, the clear-eyed dieter. Suppose that at t her discount function has reversed, so her preference ranks s above l, but she also sincerely and reflectively judges at t that l is what she should do, and says so while reaching for the cake. If this counts as akrasia, then (1) is false and judgment and preference can come apart at a time. But then I do not know what work hyperbolic discounting is doing: either the preference reversal is the whole story, in which case clear-eyed akrasia is impossible and my case is misdescribed, or the reversal is not the whole story, in which case the model under-determines the phenomenon.

Second, the sophisticated agent who pre-commits. If (4) is right, then at t she cannot act otherwise than her t-indexed ordering dictates, so resisting temptation without a commitment device is impossible; yet people plainly do resist without one. This makes me want to reject (2) rather than (1), and to allow that an agent can act against her present preference ordering. But then I owe an account of how such an action is explained and caused at all, and I have the familiar worry that contra-motivational action starts to look like a lucky spasm rather than something she does.

Third, an agent with the reverse profile: impatient at a distance, patient up close, so that she defers a readily available good while judging at t that she should take it, and then judges at the later time that she should have taken it. If akrasia is symmetric — if it should be possible to act against one's judgment in either direction — then a model that generates only one direction of reversal cannot be the whole account of failures of self-governance.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Please tell me why the step I should reject looked compelling in the first place, and price the alternatives rather than just listing them. Two specific things I would like addressed. If I keep (2), is the right move to reject (1) and reconceive better judgment so that it is indexed to the agent as a temporally extended entity rather than to a time-slice — and if so, what fixes which slice's judgment is authoritative for her, and does the notion of a better judgment survive once every candidate slice is admitted to be her? Alternatively, should I give up the standard definition and reconceive akrasia in terms of self-governance, practical identity, or the failure to conform to one's own diachronic policies — and if I do, what becomes of the ordinary first-personal phenomenology of acting against one's better judgment, and of the intuition that the continent and the incontinent agent differ in something other than their environments? If I have to choose, I would rather give up the judgment–preference link than the claim about how intentional action relates to preference, since the quasi-hyperbolic modeling result is not something I want to relitigate here; but I want to know exactly what I am paying and whether the bill is larger than I think.
