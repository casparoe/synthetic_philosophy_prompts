We did a session on the raven paradox: "all ravens are black" is logically equivalent to "everything that isn't black isn't a raven", so if an instance of a generalization confirms it (Nicod's criterion, roughly: observing an A that is a B confirms "all A are B"), then a white shoe confirms that all ravens are black. Which is ridiculous, but apparently follows. The three moves our TA gave us — accept it and say the amount is negligibly tiny, deny Nicod's criterion, or deny that logically equivalent hypotheses must be confirmed by the same evidence — all felt like dodges to me, so I tried writing down my own principle and it keeps breaking on my own cases.

My tentative principle: an observation O confirms hypothesis H (relative to background knowledge K) to the degree that O was a severe test of H — i.e. to the degree that O is the kind of outcome that would probably *not* have come about if H were false, given the procedure that actually produced O. So on my view the confirming power lives in how the observation was generated, not in the logical shape of the sentence you write down afterwards. I like this because it seems to explain why the survey case feels better than the glance case. But I'm worried its not really a theory of confirmation anymore, just a theory of how good an experiment you ran.
