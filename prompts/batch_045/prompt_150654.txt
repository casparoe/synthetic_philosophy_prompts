I'm retired now — thirty years as a lab geophysicist, mostly borehole data — and I got into philosophy a couple of years ago; I sit in on the decision-theory reading group at the local university. We've stalled on the absent-minded driver, and I'd rather have a document than another evening of talk.

Setup: two indistinguishable exits off a highway, the driver can't remember passing one or tell them apart. Stopping at the first scores 0 (truck stop in the desert), stopping at the second scores 6 (the town with the hotel), missing both scores 1 (motel at the coast). A plan is one number: p, the probability of stopping at any exit he comes to.

Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps: the ex-ante camp (rationality is assessed over whole plans, from before you set off; what you'd think at the exit isn't a separate decision) and the node-level camp (rationality is assessed where the action is, given the evidence you actually have there; a plan you'd abandon on arrival is not one you can rationally adopt). I want numbered claims both could sign; then a table of the residual disagreements, each in the wording its own side would accept; then the cruxes; and for each crux one case or argument both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Say plainly which parts of the quarrel are only about words.

Two things I want dealt with, neither of which anyone in the group could point me to:

1. My own algebra. If the driver derives his belief about which exit he is at from the plan he is following (P(first) = 1/(2−p)), then the p that maximises the value computed before departure is exactly the p at which he is indifferent at the the exit between stopping now and driving on and following the plan thereafter: p = 2/5, worth 9/5 per trip ex ante and 9/4 either way once you condition on being at an exit. I tried a three-exit version and it held again. Is that general, or an artifact of my numbers?

2. A ring-road variant. Same amnesia, but the road is a ring: miss the second exit and you come round to the first again, payoffs alternating 0 and 6 forever, and there's no last exit; run out of fuel and score 0. Now the ex-ante value is 6(1−p)/(2−p), which is strictly decreasing, so the best plan is an p as small as you like but not zero — there is no best plan — while at every single exit driving on looks strictly better than stopping. What does each camp say about that?

American English please, and keep the notation kind to someone who reads equations slower than he used to.
