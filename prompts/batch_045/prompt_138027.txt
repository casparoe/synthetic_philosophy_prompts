I am drafting the specification section of a report on whether adversarial debate between AI systems can serve as a substitute for direct human verification of their answers, in the regime where the arguments presented are obfuscated. I want the criteria settled before any proposal is scored against them, so this request is for the standard itself rather than for a survey of positions.

A reminder of the arrangement and of the difficulty, so that we are fixing the same target. A question is put to two systems, each committed in advance to a different answer; they argue; a human arbiter, whose attention covers only a small fraction of what is said, awards the win. The arrangement is supposed to help even where the arbiter could not have answered the question unaided, because honesty is thought to enjoy a structural advantage: the defender of a false claim must keep an entire fabric of detail free of contradiction, whereas the defender of the true claim need only find a single thread to pull. Obfuscation is the name I give to the class of cases in which that advantage is supposed to evaporate, because the flaw is real but not reachable within the arbiter's budget.

The theory I want specified is deliberately narrow. It must be good enough for exactly one decision: for a given class of tasks, may the verdict of such a debate stand in for our own check of the answer, in the case where we could not check it ourselves? Please give me the requirements such a theory has to meet, in the following form.

First, the requirements themselves, each with the case that motivates it and a sentence on why that case has force. Among the cases I expect to see handled: a correct argument that is merely long, and that a naive measure would classify as obfuscated; a short and elegant argument whose whole defect is one equivocation buried early, far outside what the arbiter reads; a question that turns on a contingent empirical matter no participant can settle at the time, so that there is no witness to be produced in either direction; a contest in which one system is simply stronger than the other, is assigned the false side, and wins anyway; a debate that ends without a verdict because the participants have quietly substituted an adjacent question that both can answer; and an arbiter who responds to uncertainty by referring the contested sub-claim to a further debate, and the regress that threatens thereby.

Second, the distinctions the theory must respect rather than collapse. The four I keep tripping over are: obfuscation versus genuine complexity; the honest side failing to locate a flaw that exists (a failure of search) versus the arbiter failing to recognise a flaw once it has been exhibited (a failure of verification); the procedure selecting for truth versus selecting for skill at modelling this particular arbiter; and failure that is an artefact of a small attention budget versus failure that survives any amount of adjudication effort applied to the same transcript. Add any distinction you think I have missed, and say which of mine you would discard as spurious.

Third, the considerations the theory has to be consistent with, whether or not they are flattering to the proposal. Two in particular: that length as such costs a maximally capable dishonest debater nothing, so the honest side's advantage cannot merely consist in falsehoods being long; and that whatever advantage exists is an advantage in a game the participants are trained to play, and is therefore available as a target of optimisation rather than a fixed feature of the landscape.

Fourth, and this is the part I most want: flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and argue for the incompatibility. I have one candidate in mind, namely that any criterion for obfuscation readable off the transcript alone seems either to condemn every long sound argument or to miss every buried flaw, and I would like you to state it properly or to dissolve it. I would like another as well, ideally one connecting the demand that the honest side's winning move be findable by a bounded debater with the demand that no computable feature of the transcript settle the result in advance.

Fifth, mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as the preference of a particular camp, and tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding the usual remedies: strengthening the arbiter, adding a cross-examination phase, appealing to a second-order debate, or restricting the whole method to questions with short certificates.

Finally, close with one worked miniature of your own construction: a small invented task, small enough that the numbers can be stated, showing your criteria returning a verdict on whether debate may be trusted there, and where the verdict is sensitive to a parameter you would want measured rather than assumed. A one-line-per-proposal scorecard at the very end is welcome, but only as an appendix; the substance should be the criteria, their motivating cases, and the incompatibilities. Where you need new terminology, introduce it explicitly, and where you reject the terms I have used above, say so and give the replacement.

Please write the entire answer in British English.
