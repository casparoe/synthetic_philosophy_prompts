I teach philosophy at a small liberal arts college, and last month I sat in on a two-day workshop at a nearby medical school on assistive technology and surrogate decision-making. The case the discussion kept returning to was a real one. A rehabilitation clinic has begun using an inverse-reinforcement-learning system to guide the daily care of patients with severe motor impairment: the system watches the selections a patient makes over several weeks — from a meal menu, a music menu, a short list of possible activities — and fits to them a "reward function," a numeric summary of what the patient is treated as caring about and how much, which is then used to guess what he would want in the many cases where he can't conveniently be asked: which lunch to serve, whether to page a particular visitor, whether to put on the news or open the blinds. The patient at the center of the argument was a retired high-school biology teacher in his mid-sixties, four months after a brainstem stroke, able to make selections only by small joystick movements of his left hand and, in principle, to answer yes or no by eye gaze — though each such exchange is slow, exhausting, and, the clinicians suspect, unreliable when he is tired. Before his stroke he had spent forty years preaching plain food, long walks, and no television before dinner. The system, trained on his actual selections, confidently inferred a strong liking for the sweetest and softest items on the tray and for the television over the garden. Over coffee afterward, a member of the engineering team and one of the medical school's ethicists went at it, and I — who have no stake in the outcome and said so — was asked to referee and couldn't. The engineer opened by saying that behavior is the only hard evidence anyone has, that a man who reaches for the pudding every day has told you what he wants, and that the pre-stroke sermons are just talk, cheaply produced by someone who never had to pay for them. (1) She added that if you want to know what a person prefers you look at what he does, not at what he says he admires. (2) The ethicist replied that a choice is evidence only relative to a menu and to the costs of selecting within it, that the clinic's menu was drawn up by someone else, that tremor makes some options far more effortful to reach than others, and that the system therefore cannot tell whether the garden was unwanted or merely expensive to select. (3) He went on to say that the difficulty is deeper than noise: for any stretch of observed behavior there are indefinitely many reward functions that would make that behavior look optimal — including ones on which he loves the garden and simply cannot select it reliably — so the fit to his behavior cannot by itself have singled out the one the system reports. (4) The engineer conceded the point but said the method is designed for exactly that: they model him as an agent who is approximately optimal, aiming at what he wants and executing it imperfectly, and among all reward functions compatible with his behavior they choose the one that commits them to the least — the one under which the alternatives his behavior does not distinguish between are treated as equally likely — which she described as a principle of maximal ignorance rather than an importation of the team's own tastes. (5) The ethicist said that both of those devices are themselves substantive pictures of him: "approximately optimal" casts him as someone who knows what he wants and whose failures are mere noise, which in a patient with tremor means the model will read the tremor as intention and hand back the result as his preference; and the maximal-ignorance tie-break, being a tie-break over future behavior, yields at best a compact predictor of what he will select, not something anyone could cite as his authorization when acting on his behalf. (6) She answered that the alternative is worse: waiting on the pre-stroke declarations means taking orders from a man who never faced a tray while his hand shook, and any human caregiver does something similar anyway, noticing what he reaches for and steering by it — the system only keeps better books. (7) He said a human caregiver has a face to be frowned at and a question to be re-asked, and that the system has a loop in it the caregiver does not: once it predicts the pudding, the pudding arrives first and the walk gets scheduled less often, so the selections it learns from next week are made in a world it has already narrowed, and the preference it infers is partly one it has been imposing.

I need to give these people an answer, and I would be grateful for yours. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move — rate each of the seven numbered moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and say briefly what is right or wrong in it — and then say who is more right overall and what, if anything, both parties are missing. Two further things: I came away with the sense that "preference" was doing two quite different jobs in their exchange, one having to do with predicting what he will do and another with what may be cited when someone acts for him, but I cannot articulate the distinction well enough to know whether it dissolves their disagreement or merely relocates it, and I would like you to try. And there was one point neither of them touched: the clinic retrains the model weekly on the new week's selections, and I could not tell whether, given what each of them has argued, more data helps or makes things worse. Please write in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, boldface, or tables — I would rather read it the way one reads a letter.
