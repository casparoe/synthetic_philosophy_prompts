I'm trying to work out what I actually think about lying, and I want to do it from my own verdicts on cases rather than by adopting a theory and applying it. The background that keeps nagging at me is Augustine's position: that no lie is ever permissible, that a deliberate false assertion is sin even when it is the only way to save a life, that the right response to the killer at the door is silence rather than a lie, and that what makes an utterance a lie is the speaker's will to deceive rather than the falsity of what is said. I reject the first half of that and am unsure about the second half, and I can't articulate why the split lands where it does. Here are my verdicts.

1. The killer at the door, asking for the friend I'm hiding: lying to him is fine — and not merely excusable or venially wrong, but right. I feel no residue, and I don't think silence is morally required as the alternative.
2. Telling my aunt I love the sweater: fine, trivially.
3. Telling a hiring panel I left my last job voluntarily when I was fired: not fine.
4. Telling a dying patient who asks that his estranged son called and sends love: not fine, though I place it below 3 in badness.
5. An actor on stage saying he is the Prince of Denmark: fine, and not a lie at all. The same sentence, spoken by a con man to an elderly stranger on the phone: a bad lie. Same proposition, same falsity, opposite verdicts.
6. Telling a conversational AI something I know to be false, expecting it to store and repeat the claim to other people later: fine. I feel nothing about this, and I'm inclined to say it isn't a lie.
7. Anonymously editing a crowd-edited reference page to contain that same false claim: not fine, and much worse than 6.
8. Telling a friend who has explicitly asked to be deceived about her surprise party that nothing is planned: fine.
9. Saying something true that I believe false, intending to mislead — I tell a colleague the meeting is at three, which is true, but I believe it is at four and want her to miss it: this is a lie, and not fine. That means falsity of what is said isn't necessary for lying, which is the one place I think Augustine gets it right.
10. The pair I go back and forth on: telling my mother, who has advanced dementia and asks daily, that her dead husband is at the shop and will be back soon — I think this is fine; telling a grieving family that their father died peacefully when he did not — I think this is not fine. Both are comforting falsehoods about someone's death, and I can't say what separates them.

My tentative principle, which I suspect is too complicated: an utterance is a lie just when the speaker presents a proposition as true, believes it false, and does so to an audience the speaker takes to be relying on a shared practice of testimony, without that audience's consent. The wrong is the breach of that practice, and its magnitude tracks how much the audience was entitled to rely on the speaker within it. Deception that works without misusing testimony — forged footprints, disguises, strategic silence — is a different wrong, and I don't want my principle to reach it.

Please test that principle against my verdicts, repair it or replace it, and tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon and why. I'd also like its verdicts on cases I haven't listed: an undercover officer who says he is a dealer; telling a six-year-old that Santa is real; a doctor who describes a placebo as medication that will help; a whistleblower who lies to a journalist about her identity; and a lie told to someone who will never rely on it and never learn the truth — a traveler about to die alone, say. Say which clause of the account is doing the most work and whether it is doing it honestly or papering over a distinction I haven't drawn.

Finally, be explicit about the epistemic status of what you give me: where the account is most likely to break, which single case or argument would move you toward Augustine's absolutism or toward a straightforward consequentialism about truth-telling, and which of my verdicts your account simply cannot accommodate rather than accommodates with strain.
