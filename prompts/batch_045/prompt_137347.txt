I convene a fortnightly seminar on the ethics of end-of-life decision-making, attended by graduate students in philosophy and by clinicians from a palliative care unit, and we have now spent two terms going round in circles about the doing/allowing distinction — mostly about whether it marks anything morally real, or whether it is a convenient shorthand for differences in causation, evidence, and institutional manageability. Rather than have the argument a ninth time, I want the document we should have produced instead: the joint statement of a properly conducted adversarial collaboration between the two positions in the room. Call the first the Distinctionist: she holds that doing harm is harder to justify than allowing it, that this is not merely a proxy for causal, epistemic or consequential differences, and that it behaves more like a constraint than like a weight to be traded off. Call the second the Deflationist: he holds that no irreducible moral asymmetry survives scrutiny, and that whatever defensible residue there is can be explained by such things as the causal independence of the threat from the agent, what an agent's inaction indicates about her will or about what others will do, the costs and risks of intervening, and the demands of any workable public rule. Please do not attach either position to named authors or papers — half the seminar is away and nobody can look anything up — and do not report empirical findings as established; where an empirical question matters, record it as a pre-registered prediction, together with a note of what result would surprise each side.

Begin with a numbered section of eight to twelve agreed claims: things both could sign without wincing, each specific enough to bite and neutral enough that neither is conceding anything by signing. Both can presumably accept, for instance, that the extension of the distinction in clear cases is not what is in dispute, that it is not simply the intending/foreseeing distinction under another name, and that the intuitive gap narrows when cases are carefully matched; I want more claims of that quality, and none of the form 'further argument is needed'. Then give a disagreements table: one row per residual disagreement, the Distinctionist's own preferred formulation in one column and the Deflationist's in the other, each phrased so that the relevant side would accept it as her or his own view rather than as a caricature, with a third column marking whether, in your judgement, that disagreement is substantive or merely verbal.

Then the cruxes — four to six of them, each with (i) the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side, (ii) a sentence on why it moves that side, (iii) the case or argument both would accept in advance as bearing on it, and (iv) each side's prediction written down before the fact. I have candidates; please use them or replace them, but keep at least one that turns on a clinical case and at least one that is an argument rather than a case. First, the residue left after what I have been calling the Equalised-Pair Protocol: hold fixed, across a doing case and an allowing case, everything that can be specified without saying which of the two it is — the size and certainty of the outcome, the agent's counterfactual indispensability, the effort, cost, risk and skill she must deploy, her motives and foresight, the number and identity of those affected, and what she is permitted or required to do anyway — and ask whether any moral difference remains; one sub-crux here is whether the condition 'the threat is already underway independently of the agent' can itself be stated in non-moral terms, or whether it quietly assumes what is at issue. Second, timing: is a death's status as done or allowed settled by the agent's conduct at the time she acts, or by the configuration at the time of death? Take the clinician who sets an infusion rate that is unobjectionable when she sets it and which, hours later, while she is asleep and unreachable and the pump cannot be re-set, becomes lethal because of a change in the patient's condition — and set beside her a second clinician with no history with the pump who notices the same lethal trajectory at the later time and also does nothing. Third, whether the asymmetry, if there is one, is lexical or has an exchange rate: is there some number of deaths allowed that outweighs one death caused, and does the answer change when a million agents each allow a share of it? Fourth, where in the world the asymmetry would have to be located if it exists — in the metaphysics of agency and causation, in the structure of rights and claims, in the agent's relation to her own will, or in the epistemic situation of observers and institutions — and, connectedly, whether the doing/allowing distinction explains the intending/foreseeing asymmetry or is explained by it. Finish with a short section on what each of us would count as evidence against her own view, and one on which parts of the dispute, if any, were never about the world at all. Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words, and keep to the register of a text two people are prepared to put their names to: no footnotes, no bibliographies, no rhetoric.
