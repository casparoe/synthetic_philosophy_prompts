**Compliance as a quota: a ledger view of political obligation and civil disobedience**

**1. The puzzle.** Philosophy usually separates two questions: whether there is a general moral duty to obey the law of one's own political community, and when, if ever, it is permissible to break the law for conscientious political reasons. But they are connected by a tension. If there is no such duty — if the philosophical anarchist is right that no existing state binds its subjects generally — then civil disobedience seems to need no special moral defence at all: it is simply permissible action that happens to be illegal, and the elaborate apparatus of conditions (publicity, nonviolence, conscientiousness, a political rather than private aim, willingness to accept the legal consequences) that is standardly used to mark off civil disobedience from ordinary lawbreaking becomes a puzzle about why anyone bothers with it. If there is such a duty, then civil disobedience has to be an exception to a duty that the disobedient herself acknowledges, and that looks unstable: either the duty is defeated case by case by the agent's own judgment of injustice, in which case it does no work, or it is not so defeated, in which case the disobedient is simply in the wrong. Both horns are uncomfortable, and the literature has mostly split between them.

**2. The diagnosis.** Both horns share an assumption: that the duty is *act-indexed*. The law directs you to do A, and the duty is to do A. I want to propose that the duty is *quota-indexed*. What a subject owes her polity is not conformity with each directive but her fair share of the compliance on which the effective operation of the scheme depends. Call this the Ledger View. Compliance makes deposits. Noncompliance makes withdrawals. The governing principle is No Net Drawdown: over the relevant period, one must not leave the scheme operating less effectively than it would have operated had one contributed one's fair share.

**3. Three consequences.** First, the puzzle dissolves. Breaking a particular law is not by itself a violation of the duty, because the duty never attached to that act; there is no exception needing a special justification, only a bookkeeping question about whether the ledger balances. Second, the familiar conditions on civil disobedience become functional rather than constitutive. Publicity is not part of what civil disobedience *is*; it is the most reliable way of keeping the ledger legible to others. Nonviolence is not part of the definition either; it is a constraint on how large a single withdrawal may be, since violent costs cannot be deposited back. Third — and this is the part I care about — the requirement of willingness to accept punishment gets a new rationale and a new limit. If submitting to arrest is a *deposit*, a costly and highly legible payment whose function is to demonstrate that the withdrawal is not free-riding, then it should be dischargeable by other deposits that are equally costly and equally legible. Where a disobedient self-reports, pays the prescribed penalty, or makes an equivalent costly transfer, the duty to submit to custody is settled, and a demand for jail time specifically is moralizing about a payment method.

**4. Two cases.** *Alma* withholds the fraction of her tax that corresponds to a military programme she judges unjust, pays the identical sum into a court registry, and files a public declaration of what she has done and why. On the standard account she is a defective case: she has not really refused, and she has not accepted the legal consequences. On the Ledger View she is a paradigm — she has taken nothing from the scheme, she has made her withdrawal fully visible, and she has paid a premium in trouble and risk precisely in order to make it visible. *Cara* quietly ignores a registration requirement she believes unjust; no one finds out; she continues to pay taxes, vote, serve on juries, and she volunteers for the county emergency service. The standard account says she is not a civil disobedient at all, since she fails publicity. The Ledger View says she has done nothing wrong in the narrow sense — her ledger balances — but has done something defective in another way: she has rendered her ledger unauditable and so has destroyed the only public evidence that she is not free-riding. Publicity is therefore a genuine requirement of justification, but a derived and epistemic one, not a constitutive condition on the category.

**5. The reverse argument.** If the Ledger View is right, then the near-universal practice of submitting to arrest is *evidence for* political obligation, not merely a consequence of it. The philosophical anarchist must treat that ritual as prudence, or as strategic signalling, or as moral habit surviving from a mistaken belief. But the ritual is strikingly over-fitted to a ledger picture: the disobedient pays, in public, in the currency the scheme itself issues, and asks for no exemption. The best explanation of the practice is that participants take themselves to owe a quantity of compliance and to be settling up. Civil disobedience is thus a data point against philosophical anarchism, rather than a problem only for those who reject it.

**6. Objections I try to answer.**

(O1) *selfish selectivity*: the view lets me speed, litter, and ignore zoning rules so long as I vote and pay my taxes. Reply: the fair share is not a fixed total but is indexed to the scheme's needs at the point of withdrawal; withdrawals that degrade the scheme exactly where compliance is load-bearing cannot be offset by unrelated deposits.

(O2) *no exchange rate*: there is no unit in which to compare a day in custody, a fine, a year of service, and one statute obeyed. Reply: we need comparative judgment, not cardinal measurement, and the legal system itself publishes prices; the ledger is denominated in the scheme's own currency.

(O3) *wrongs to identifiable others*: some noncompliance injures a specific party, and a deposit into the general fund repairs nothing. Reply: distinguish withdrawals that injure identifiable parties from those that merely reduce collective provision; the former require earmarked repair directed at the party injured.

(O4) *changing the subject*: obligation is supposed to be content-independent and grounded in authority, and my view turns it into a fair-play contribution, losing what people mean by political obligation. Reply: this is a virtue; content-independence is exactly what generates the puzzle, and if I have changed the subject I have changed it away from a claim that cannot be stated coherently.

(O5) *the auditor problem*: who audits the ledger? If the state does, no disobedient ever balances; if the agent does, conscientiousness swallows the view. Reply: the auditor is the reasonable agent acting under conditions of publicity, which is what converts self-assessment into something checkable; publicity is doing the epistemic work.
