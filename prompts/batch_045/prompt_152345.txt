I'm a metaphysician by training, so this is well outside what I actually work on, and I keep running into a pattern I don't have a word for. I'd be grateful if you could tell me whether there's an established concept for it, or whether I'm describing three different things and not noticing.

Here's the shape. Someone fully grants the cosmic claim — nothing matters, the universe is indifferent, in two hundred years none of this will have left a trace — and at the same time is gripped by some local thing in a way that has nothing to do with enjoyment and is not presented as a choice. They don't say "I've decided this matters to me." They say it matters, full stop, and they get a bit stiff if you call it a hobby or a coping strategy.

Three cases, because I'm not convinced they're one case:

1. A PhD student of mine told me over coffee that the field is churn and nobody will read any of it. She said it calmly, she isn't depressed, and then she went straight back to a paper she's been grinding on for two years. She has no illusion that it's important. But she also can't just stop: stopping would be, in her words, "a thing that happened to me" rather than a decision. When I tried to argue her out of the cosmic bit she pushed back — she doesn't think the two levels touch each other.

2. A neighbour retired and now spends most of his week volunteering on a project to restore a stretch of canal. He says outright that it's pointless; the funding will lapse, it'll silt up again. It's also the part of his week he talks about with the most energy. He doesn't enjoy most of it — it's cold, it's muddy, the other volunteers annoy him.

3. A friend who lost her religion years ago still goes to the services and keeps the whole yearly round, and is cheerfully clear that she believes none of it. She won't describe it as meaning anything; she describes it as where she belongs. But when the building was shut for repairs for eight months she said that stretch felt "weightless" — the same word she uses for the years before her kids were born.

What I keep noticing is that none of them are doing what the pop version of the absurdist stuff tells you to do. They haven't stood on a hill and decided to make something theirs. The pull was already there (case 3) or arrived and then stopped being optional (cases 1 and 2), and if you asked what would happen if they picked some other thing instead, they'd say that isn't how it works — you can't swap it out at will. And they'd deny the thing has any value that would survive their not caring about it. So it isn't "I made my own meaning," which sounds like an act of will; it isn't "this is genuinely valuable," which they explicitly deny; and it isn't "I like doing it," because mostly they don't, and plenty of what they do enjoy they'd call trivial.

My colleague in value theory tells me meaning is a matter of being gripped by something independently worth being gripped by. That sounds like it can't handle these cases at all: the grip is there and the worth is denied. But the rival thing I keep hearing in the corridor is that these people are just describing attachment, habit, or identity in inflated language, and that the word "meaning" is doing no work here. Both replies feel too quick.

So: does this configuration have a name? Is there a literature on the non-optional-but-not-valuable pull, as opposed to the chosen-project line that the boulder essay usually gets read as recommending? Are my three cases one phenomenon or three — the inherited one feels different from the acquired one? And I don't want a label on its own; I want the carving. What would have to be true for this to count as a genuine species of meaningfulness rather than a lookalike, and what exactly are the lookalikes? One boundary case I care about most: what do we say about people who make the same cosmic concession, feel no pull at all, aren't unhappy, and aren't looking for anything? Same phenomenon minus a part, or a different thing entirely?

Could you give me:

- "Does it have a name" (~150 words) — straight answer on whether there's an established concept or literature, and if there isn't, coin one in a sentence and say why that coinage
- "Nearest concepts" (~300 words) — the closest established accounts of meaning in life, what each gets right about these cases and where each breaks
- "One or three" (~250 words) — whether the inherited and acquired variants are the same phenomenon, and what the core case is if so
- "Definition and edges" (~300 words) — a definition, plus what it rules out: chosen projects, enjoyment, mere habit, attachment, the no-pull case
- "What it costs" (~200 words) — which accounts of meaning are in trouble if this is really meaning, and which can absorb it without strain
