I'm a novelist — three books, none of them bestsellers — and I've talked myself into a speech I don't know how to write. My third novel came out in Japanese last year and in Dutch this spring, and the two publishers decided, in a moment of enthusiasm nobody now wants to own, to hold one joint reception in Amsterdam next month. So it's about sixty people in one room: half of them Dutch publishing people who've known me since the first book and who will hear any padded compliment as evasion, a Japanese delegation of fifteen or so — my translator, her editor, her publisher, two booksellers — and then my mother and one ex I'd rather not have flown in. There's consecutive interpretation into Japanese after each short paragraph, and the interpreter has asked for the text in advance, so nothing can depend on a wink or on a joke that dies in translation. Five minutes, call it 700 words.

Here's the actual problem, and I'll be honest with you since you're the one writing it. My real view is that the Dutch translation is bad — not incompetent, but the translator smoothed my sentences and filled in every silence in the book, and the silences are the only thing the book has going for it — while the Japanese translation kept them, and I think hers is a better book than mine in places. Meanwhile the Dutch translator is a friend of twenty years and will be in the front row, the Dutch publisher holds an option on my next novel, and my Japanese translator already stood up at the Tokyo launch and said her own translation was inadequate, in that modest way people do, in front of her whole profession. I've been telling interviewers for two years that the novel is about what politeness costs, so I can't get up there and do a masterclass in gracious vagueness; but I can't just say what I think either, because my late editor drilled into me that you may withhold and you may not invert — stay silent about a fault, never say the opposite of what you believe — and I've realised this rule quietly collapses in a toast, since in a room like this leaving someone out of the praise *is* the criticism, and the Dutch half will hear the omission instantly and the Japanese half louder. I'm not asking you to split the difference or to be diplomatic for diplomacy's sake, and I definately don't want a speech that's secretly a calculation about that option. Write me the thing: something I can stand up and say without asserting anything I don't believe, that doesn't humiliate my friend, that answers my translator's self-deprecation without costing her face, and that neither half of the room can file away as the usual hot air.
