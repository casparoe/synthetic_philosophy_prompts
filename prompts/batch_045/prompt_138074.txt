Hey — I keep going in circles on the heterogeneity problem for hedonism and I want you to argue me into, or out of, a position. No citations or reading lists please, I've read the usual suspects; I want your own argument.

Setup: hedonism about welfare says a life goes well for you to the extent that it contains pleasure (and lacks pain). The heterogeneity problem: the things we comfortably call pleasures look wildly unlike each other — the first cold drink on a hot day, finally seeing why a proof works, a warm bath, scratching an itch, a long-nursed grudge finally landing, the specific relief of putting down a heavy bag. If hedonism is more than a slogan there has to be something all and only these share, and that something has to be what makes them good for you.

Two standard-ish families of answer:

(1) Felt-quality. All pleasures contain a distinctive phenomenal ingredient, a felt positivity that is the same stuff in every case, varying only in intensity, duration, and what it's attached to — the way redness is the same stuff across very different red objects.

(2) Attitudinal. Pleasure isn't a feeling at all but a stance you take toward an experience you're having: an occurrent liking or enjoying, wanting it to continue, savoring, being drawn back to it. Heterogeneity then costs nothing, because what's shared is a relation between you and your experience rather than a property of the experience's sensory character.

Here's the third one I invented and can't shake, which I want you to take seriously and then try to break. Call it the recalibration view: what all pleasures have in common isn't a quality or an attitude but a dynamic role. A pleasure is any experience that nudges upward the subject's operative reference point for how good things are supposed to feel, so that the neutral state you were perfectly fine with two minutes ago now registers as mildly disappointing, and indifference has to be bought back at a higher price. On this view pleasures are unified as the events that move a comparator's baseline, and their felt and intentional diversity is irrelevant.

What I want:

1. Run the hard cases above through all three, plus any case you think is harder. Where does each account misclassify — what does it count as pleasure that isn't, what does it leave out?
2. Separate the taxonomy question (is there a property shared by all and only pleasures?) from the value question (is that property what makes pleasure good for you?). Which account wins which? Is it a real cost if a view wins the first and loses the second, and can one unifier do both jobs, or should hedonists split them?
3. Is the recalibration view actually a rival to (1) and (2), or is it one of them in disguise, or has it quietly changed the subject to a theory of hedonic adaptation? Notice the worry that a raised baseline is something a pleasure does, not what makes it good — and on some readings it makes your future worse. Say whether that's fatal or whether (1) and (2) have the same problem in another form.
4. I don't want "pleasure is just a family-resemblance concept" as an escape hatch unless you'll tell me what work the word still does inside the theory of welfare once you say that.
5. What would actually settle any of this? Is there an introspective, conceptual, or empirical finding that would move you, or is the disagreement upstream of evidence?
