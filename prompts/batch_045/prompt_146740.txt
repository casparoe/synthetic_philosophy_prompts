I'm a staff engineer on the team that builds our hospital system's clinical documentation assistant, and I sit (non-voting) on the system's Algorithmic Accountability Committee. The committee reviews incidents after the fact and returns one of three verdicts. No epistemic injustice. Epistemic injustice, credibility type: roughly, someone was accorded less standing as a knower than the evidence supported, and this tracked something about who they are. Epistemic injustice, interpretive type: roughly, someone had something to convey but the shared vocabulary for conveying it did not exist, because people like them were not in the room where the vocabulary was written. It has once before invented a fourth label for a case it did not want to force into the other three: a patient advisory panel whose explanations of community distrust were used in a grant application with no credit and no feedback, which the committee called extraction. I have no philosophy training, so the definitions above are my paraphrases of how the committee actually writes.

Five precedents, all involving software we or a vendor shipped.

1. A symptom-checker intake tool. A Somali patient describes her problem in terms of daily activities she can no longer do rather than in terms of body locations; the phrasing is out of distribution; the model returns low urgency; the clinician sees only the score and never the patient's own words. Verdict: credibility type. The committee's reasoning was that the model was functioning as the hearer, that its score tracked membership in a group absent from the training data, and that nothing let the patient learn her report had been discounted or contest it.

2. Dictation for physicians, with a higher word error rate for Indian-accented English. The physician hand-corrects the transcript, the signed note says exactly what she intended and carries her full authority, and the cost to her is time. Verdict: no epistemic injustice. A tool that degrades a person's words is not the same as a tool that discounts them; she could see the error, repair it, and be credited with the repair.

3. Nurses record deterioration as a free-text sense of unease. Nobody reads the field and it is not an input to the sepsis model. When a nurse escalates verbally, residents ask for the model's score. The hospital's escalation criteria have no slot for I cannot say why, but something has changed, and no nurses sat on the committee that wrote them. Verdict: interpretive type. The committee noted that no one judged the nurses' credibility; the wrong was that the institution had no way to register what they knew.

4. Patient portal messages are routed by a language-confidence classifier, and messages below a threshold go to a queue that no one reads. Nothing is ever assessed for credibility, because nothing is ever read. Verdict: credibility type anyway, on the grounds that routing a message to an unread queue is a credibility determination with extra steps, and a patient cannot appeal a queue.

5. A middle-aged English-speaking patient with no interpreter self-reports a fever two days after surgery. The note is recorded verbatim and read, but a model weights a single post-operative fever as low-information, correctly on the evidence, and no workup follows. Verdict: no epistemic injustice. The report was heard and recorded, the discount was auditable and warranted, and the pattern did not track any group the hospital marginalizes.

Here is the case in front of us next month. Last quarter we shipped a summarization layer: before a clinician opens a chart, a language model rewrites the free-text patient-reported history into canonical clinical phrasing. It strips hedges and maps idioms onto standard terms, so a hedged, circumstance-bound description of breathlessness on stairs becomes a flat assertion of exertional dyspnea, and a remark about nerves becomes a chronic anxiety entry. A panel of clinicians reviewing a sample judged the rewrites clinically equivalent to the originals. The incident: a Spanish-speaking patient speaking through a phone interpreter had a hedged, idiomatic account converted into a confident symptom assertion; the workup found nothing; at a follow-up visit with a different clinician, the earlier entry read as a report of a symptom she never made, that clinician treated her self-report as unreliable, and the correct diagnosis was delayed by several months. Our own audit found that hedge-stripping and idiom-substitution happen for everyone whose speech is non-canonical, at roughly three times the rate for interpreter-mediated and low-health-literacy patients, who are also the least likely to know that a summary exists or to ask for the original text. There is no affordance for a patient to review or correct a summary before it is filed, and the summary is what the next clinician reads.

I would like four things. First, a prediction: which verdict and which label, and what rule the committee is implicitly running. Second, at least two candidate rules that fit all five precedents and come apart on this one, with what each predicts here. Third, a recommendation: which verdict best coheres with the committee's own track record, which of the precedents is the closest analog, and where the precedents genuinely underdetermine the answer, what principle the committee would be adopting with each choice. Fourth, the variation on this incident, or the hypothetical incident, that would tell the competing rules apart.

One wrinkle I cannot resolve on my own and would like addressed under each rule rather than averaged away: this does not look like a credibility deficit at the moment of encoding. The model gave the patient more apparent credibility than her own hedged report warranted, and the deficit appears only later, in a clinician who seems to be reasoning correctly from the record in front of her. Whether that matters, and why, is most of what I am interested in. Please do not answer with citations or a reading list; I cannot act on one and the committee certainly will not read it.
