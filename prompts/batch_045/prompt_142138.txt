I am a PhD student in philosophy, writing the third chapter of a dissertation on the metaphysics of cardinality and quantity, and I have a cluster of cases I cannot get into reflective equilibrium. I am not asking whether actual infinities are possible. Grant me, for the sake of argument, a hotel with rooms 1, 2, 3, …, one guest in each room, no spare rooms and no unhoused guest. My question is which further operations on that hotel — and on setups near it — I can accept, and why my verdicts fall out the way they do.

(1) One new guest arrives; every occupant of room n moves to room n+1; the newcomer takes room 1. Verdict: fine. Not merely "no contradiction follows" — nothing at all has gone wrong.

(2) Countably many new guests arrive; occupant of room n moves to room 2n; newcomers take the odd rooms. Verdict: fine.

(3) All guests in even-numbered rooms check out. Verdict: fine, but I want to say that the hotel now has fewer guests than it had, even though every remaining guest has exactly one room and every room exactly one guest, and even though I will not say it has "half as many."

(4) A vase. At step n, balls numbered 10n−9 through 10n go in and exactly one ball comes out; the steps occur at times converging on noon. If the ball removed at step n is ball n, the vase is empty at noon. If instead the ball removed at step n is the highest-numbered ball then in the vase, infinitely many balls remain. Verdict: I accept both results, and I accept that the difference between them is settled entirely by which balls are removed — not by how many are removed, since at every finite stage the net change is the same in both.

(5) A lamp, switched on and off at noon minus 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, … minutes, alternating. Verdict: there is no determinate answer to "is it on at noon?" — not because I cannot find out, but because the description is defective or the question ill-posed. This sits badly with (4), where I was happy to accept a determinate limit state of a supertask with the same convergent time structure.

(6) Each guest holds one dollar. At the sound of a bell, every guest passes one dollar to the guest in the room one below; the guest in room 1 keeps the dollar rather than passing it on. Afterwards every guest still has exactly one dollar, and the guest in room 1 has two. Verdict: not fine — something has gone wrong here, in a way nothing goes wrong in (1).

(6′) The mirror image: every guest passes one dollar to the guest in the room one above; the guest in room 1 passes a dollar up and receives none. Afterwards every guest except the guest in room 1 has one dollar, that guest has none, and nobody anywhere has more than one. Verdict: fine. Unfortunate for one person, but not the kind of thing I object to in (6).

(7) Two hotels with the same rooms and the same occupants, differing only in how they got there: one has always existed, with no first room built; the other was built between eleven and noon by adding one room at each of a convergent sequence of times. Verdict: I accept the first and balk at the second, although the end states are the same. This is the case I keep reversing on.

My own attempt at a principle: an operation on an actually infinite system is admissible only if it changes the holdings of no individual unless every gain is matched by an opposite loss in some other individual — a pairwise-conservation condition, applied to quantities but not to places. This rules out (6) while leaving (1) and (2) untouched, since moving house changes no one's holdings. But I can see two problems and cannot repair either. First, a room looks like a thing the newcomer in (1) gains and nobody loses, so "quantities but not places" is doing all the work and I cannot say why. Second, if I rerun (6) so that the extra dollar ends up with a passerby who was not previously in the system, my condition permits it, and I do not think it is fine.

What I would like: a principle, or the smallest set of principles you can manage, stated precisely enough to be applied, with the case that motivates each clause; its verdicts on two cases I have not listed — whether there is a fact of the matter about the proportion of heads when each of the countably many guests tosses a fair coin once, and whether a full hotel whose rooms are indexed by the rational numbers can house a new guest if every guest must move to a room whose number differs from their own by less than one; and an honest account of which of my verdicts above has to go. If the choice is between a principle that saves all of them at the cost of considerable machinery and a simple one that overturns one, give me both and tell me what each costs.
