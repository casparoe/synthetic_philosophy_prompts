I sit on the ethics committee of a clinic that offers neural substitution therapy: a patient's neurons are exchanged, a few thousand a day, for synthetic units matched to the input-output behaviour of the cells they replace, so that nothing changes in the patient's speech, memory, temperament or reported experience. We are now being asked to say which variants of the procedure count as treatment and which count as something else, and I have discovered that my gut verdicts do not compose into anything coherent. Here they are. (1) The standard slow protocol, ten years, patient awake and conversing throughout: obviously me at the end, no question. (2) The same set of replacements performed in a single operation, mid-sentence, the biological tissue discarded: not me, that is a death followed by a very accomplished impersonation, even though the end state is the same as in (1) and no one, inside or outside, could afterwards tell the difference. (3) The same single-operation swap done under general anaesthetic, so that the patient goes to sleep biological and wakes up synthetic: me, or near enough, no more of a rupture than an ordinary night. I am aware that (2) and (3) are an odd pair: (2) involves no gap in experience while (3) involves one, and yet I judge (3) more kindly. (4) A copy is made while the biological brain is left intact: the copy is not me, I am the biological one. If instead the biological brain is destroyed and two copies are made: I do not survive, there are simply two strangers, neither of whom is me. (5) The removed tissue is vitrified and, thirty years later, grown into a new brain that wakes up claiming to be the patient: not me; the synthetic patient who has actually lived those thirty years is me. So the original matter seems to be doing no work at all in my verdicts. (6) The sparing protocol: the synthetic units are added alongside rather than instead, taking over the causal role cell by cell, while the original neurons are left alive, nourished, firing, and connected to nothing. The end state is a wholly biological brain that does not drive anything and a mental life running on the network wrapped around it. I say without hesitation that I am the network; and then I notice that if I am willing to say that, I cannot see what entitles me to refuse to say it in case (2). That is the pair I keep coming back to.

So: what principle is actually doing the work here? Please state one precisely, or two or three candidates if they come apart, and motivate each clause by the case that forces it. I have read the usual literature on this and do not need it rehearsed or cited; I want the principle and its costs. Three further things. First, tell me what your principle says about cases I have not settled: (a) two patients undergo the slow protocol, and their units are gradually made functionally identical to each other, so that at the end there is one psychology running on two networks, and neither patient is distinguishable from the other by any test you like; (b) the slow protocol is carried out correctly over ten years, but during those ten years the patient has no experiences at all, being in an unresponsive dreamless state throughout; (c) a complete functional description of the patient is taken and run on hardware for a year while the biological patient goes on living, and only then does the biological patient die in an accident and the running system is told about it. Second, tell me where the line between (1) and (2) falls, if there is one: how fast, or how coarsely graded, does the substitution have to be before it stops being treatment, and what do you say if there is no such line. Third, and most importantly: say plainly which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, which of them you would overturn, and what it would cost me to keep them.
