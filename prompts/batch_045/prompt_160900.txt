I'm teaching a graduate seminar this term on social choice theory and its critics, and next week we are spending two sessions on Sen's 1970 result about the impossibility of a Paretian liberal. I have set the reading, so please don't summarise the theorem or reconstruct its proof for me; assume I have both in front of me. Let me fix the example as we will use it in class, since everything below hangs on it. There are two readers, the prude and the libertine, and one copy of a novel. The three social alternatives are that nobody reads it, that the prude reads it, and that the libertine reads it. The prude's ranking is: nobody reads it, then he himself reads it, then the libertine reads it. The libertine's ranking is: the prude reads it, then he himself reads it, then nobody reads it. Each is decisive over his own reading: the prude between nobody-reads and prude-reads, the libertine between nobody-reads and libertine-reads. Minimal liberalism then gives nobody-reads above prude-reads and libertine-reads above nobody-reads, while the Pareto principle gives prude-reads above libertine-reads, since both of them prefer that outcome, and the result is a cycle.

Here is the paper I want from you: the position paper I would otherwise have to write myself, and which I would hand out as the reading for the second session. Argue that the correct response to the result is to qualify the Pareto principle and to keep minimal liberalism, and do it in one specific way rather than in general. Argue that the Pareto comparison the proof needs — prude-reads against libertine-reads — is a comparison between two states that differ in who performs a protected personal act, and that a Pareto principle ranging over such pairs has already taken a stand on the very question in dispute, because it counts each party's preference about the other's personal conduct as a reason bearing on whether that conduct should take place. Restrict the Pareto principle, then, to pairs of social states that differ only outside everyone's protected sphere; motivate the restriction independently of the theorem, by a claim about which preferences are reasons for whom and not by the wish to escape a result; and show what the restricted package delivers in the example. I take it to deliver an ordering on which libertine-reads is best, nobody-reads next, and prude-reads last — an ordering reached by each party exercising his own right and by no one overriding anyone else's — but I want you to show the steps and to defend that verdict as the right one, not merely as the one that avoids the cycle.

Then do three things I will certainly be asked about in the seminar. First, meet the objection I expect from the welfarists in the room: that this is a renaming rather than a resolution, that a Pareto principle restricted to differences outside the protected sphere is minimal liberalism smuggled in under the word Pareto, and that the real work is being done by a moral claim about meddlesome preferences which you have asserted rather than argued. Give the argument, not the assertion. Second, apply the restricted package to a case we have not read about, and where it does not look comfortable. Two people may each contribute or withhold contribution to a small joint project. Both prefer that both contribute to that neither does; each prefers most that the other contribute alone and least that he himself contribute alone. If each has a protected sphere over his own contributing, the rights-based ordering puts both-contribute at the bottom, even though both-contribute is preferred by both to both-withhold and differs from it only in the two protected dimensions. Say plainly whether you accept that verdict. If you accept it, explain why the cost is one the liberal can bear, and say what, if anything, remains of the appeal of the Pareto principle: what work it can still do in ordinary policy cases in which no protected sphere is in play. If you don't accept it, then you owe me a different formulation of the restriction, together with the reason the contribution case falls outside it. Third, and this is the part I care most about: commit. Choose one of those two courses — accept the verdict in the contribution case, or amend the restriction — and defend that choice with reasons, rather than laying out both and leaving me to pick. Then tell me, in a sentence or two, which single premise of your argument you would give up first if you had to give one up, and what kind of consideration would make you give it up.

Write it as continuous prose, in the manner of an email or a short letter to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables, since I am going to read it aloud in the seminar as though it were a position paper and I need sentences and paragraphs. Aim for something I could actually hand out, around a thousand to twelve hundred words. And don't cite anything or direct me to further reading; I want the argument rather than the literature, and I already know whom to assign.
