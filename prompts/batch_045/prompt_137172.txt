I write literary fiction, and I need help from someone who works in epistemology, because a chapter of my current novel has produced an argument I can no longer manage.

The book is a first-person confession. My narrator abandoned a philosophy doctorate twenty years ago and is now writing, from a hospital bed, an account of a decision she made long ago and of what she knew when she made it. The book's refrain is *I knew, and I knew that I knew it.* In the pivotal chapter she turns that refrain against herself, and I need her dismantling of it to be serious rather than decorative. I also need to know what the other character — the former supervisor to whom she writes, and who answers in a letter I have not yet drafted — could honestly say back to her, without my turning him into a straw man so she can win the scene.

Here is her argument as I have it. Call a condition C *luminous* for a subject S when necessarily, if C obtains for S, then S is in a position to know that C obtains — where being in a position to know means having available, in that very case, grounds such that were she to judge on them she would know. Let C be *being in pain*.

(1) C is luminous. (Assumed for reductio.)
(2) If S is in a position to know that C obtains in a case α, on grounds G, then in any case α* differing from α only in respects S cannot discriminate at the time, G is still hers and still puts her in a position to know that C obtains. Her grounds are unchanged, and a difference she cannot detect cannot be the difference between grounds that suffice for knowledge and grounds that do not.
(3) So in a position to know transfers from any case to every case indiscriminably close to it.
(4) There is a finite series of cases α0, α1, …, αn — and she invites us to take n as large as we please — such that she is in agony at α0, in no pain whatever at αn, and each adjacent pair differs only imperceptibly.
(5) By (1) applied at α0 and repeated application of (3) along the series, she is in a position to know that she is in pain at αn.
(6) Being in a position to know that p in a case requires that p hold in that case; no one is in a position to know a falsehood.
(7) So she is in pain at αn, which by construction she is not. Hence (1) is false: being in pain is not luminous. Nothing in the reasoning turned on pain, so no condition that sometimes obtains and sometimes fails is luminous.
(8) Now let C be *knowing that p*. The principle that if S knows p then S is in a position to know that S knows p says exactly that this condition is luminous. By (7) it is not, so that principle is false.
(9) So she may know something and yet not be in a position to know that she knows it; and, worse, she may be in agony and not be in a position to know that she is in agony. Her refrain is unavailable to her, and her testimony — even about her own pain — is at best confident true belief.

That last step is the one I cannot live with, and I suspect the argument proves too much: run on pain it seems to say that a woman in the worst hour of her life may not be in a position to know what is happening to her, which is not a novel I know how to write.

A friend who read the chapter says the argument begs the question at (2) and (3): the margin — how far a case may drift while knowledge still survives — need not be uniform across cases. It may vary with the case, and in particular may shrink as the series approaches the point at which C stops obtaining. If it shrinks fast enough, then for any finite series you name there will be a first step that outruns the local margin, and the induction in (5) stalls short of αn. He adds that for some conditions there may be no such path at all, though he concedes that escape is unavailable for pain.

My narrator has a rejoinder, and this is the part I most want checked. She says: fix the series in advance. At each case in it, the local margin is by hypothesis some positive amount; you cannot have a margin of zero and still be in a position to know. A finite collection of positive amounts has a positive minimum. So take the steps small enough to fall under that minimum, and the induction goes through after all.

Tell me where this really breaks, and be explicit about the price of each option. Specifically: which step would you reject, and why did that step look innocent enough to be smuggled in? What does it cost to give up (1), to give up (2) or (3), to give up (4), and to give up (6), and are any of those costs ones a working epistemologist has actually been willing to pay? Is the minimum-margin rejoinder a genuine answer to my friend, or does it assume the very uniformity he is denying? If the honest verdict is that the argument is sound and that the intuition about pain has to be explained away rather than honored, tell me what that explanation is, since the supervisor's letter has to contain something a real person in the field could sign.

I would most like to keep the luminosity of pain and the factivity step; if something must go, I would rather it were (2) or (4) than (1) or (6). But I would rather have the truth about the argument than the ending I planned.

Two more requests. First, I do not need names, dates, or citations — I am told the argument is not my narrator's invention and has a literature, but provenance is irrelevant to me, so please do not spend your answer on it. Second, separate clearly between what is settled among people who actually work on these questions and what is genuinely contested; if the choice among (1), (2), (3), (4) and (6) is one that the field has not resolved, say so plainly rather than presenting one side as the consensus, and tell me what the live positions are and what each is trying to protect.
