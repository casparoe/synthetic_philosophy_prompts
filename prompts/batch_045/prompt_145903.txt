Hi — I'm a science-and-ideas journalist and I'm trying to finish a piece about how computer scientists actually decide that a problem is hopeless, and I keep hitting a weird double practice that I'd like a philosopher to unpack. On the one hand, the field runs on something like the Cobham–Edmonds move: a problem is "efficiently solvable" only if there's a polynomial-time algorithm for it, and once somebody proves a problem NP-hard, that's treated as a license to stop looking for exact methods and go do approximations, heuristics, or a hardness-of-approximation paper instead — referees accept this move without asking anything further. On the other hand, the very same community's engineers solve "intractable" problems to proven optimality every day: verification and scheduling and routing instances with six-figure variable counts fall to branch-and-bound plus clever preprocessing in seconds, and the papers that report this are full of benchmark tables rather than asymptotics. There's even a standing joke about "galactic algorithms," the ones that are polynomial but with constants and exponents nobody will ever run. So the practice seems to contain two opposite attitudes toward the same formal notion at once.

The bit that really puzzles me is an inconsistency across neighboring fields. Cryptographers designing a key-generation routine go out of their way to randomize, to avoid landing on structured or special instances that factoring or discrete-log attacks handle easily — they behave as though the hard instances are special and have to be aimed at deliberately. But the asymptotic worst-case framing that licenses the "give up, it's NP-hard" move behaves as though hard instances are what you'd be likely to meet anyway, and worst case is the honest accounting. It can't be that both are right in the same sense, or maybe it can and I'm missing something.

Here's the thing I want to inject into the discussion, because nobody I interviewed would engage with it: almost no real workload is a single instance. It's a family of instances shat out by the same compiler, the same chip design flow, the same logistics pipeline, over and over, so you can preprocess once, cache, tune or learn a heuristic on the family's distribution, and the marginal cost per instance collapses even though any one fresh instance from scratch is NP-hard. I've been calling this the amortization escape hatch and the corresponding notion family-tractability: tractable per instance within a recurring family, intractable in the textbook per-instance-from-scratch sense. So: what philosophical commitments about tractability, feasibility, or the nature of computational difficulty does the existing practice (polynomial = feasible, NP-hardness = stop, asymptotics = the real object, constants = mere engineering) presuppose? Is it a thesis about problems, or about instances, or about distributions, or about what a community can know? Which parts does the practice strictly need and which does it merely sit well with, and where is it underdetermined enough that several rival accounts — worst-case, average-case, smoothed, parameterized, fixed-constant resource bounds, my family-level one — would license exactly the same behavior? And if you swapped in a family-level notion of tractability as the official one, what would actually have to change in how the field allocates its effort and what counts as a result? Give me exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no intro, no sign-off.
