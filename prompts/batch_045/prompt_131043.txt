I'm leading the discussion section on the Ant Fugue chapter of Gödel, Escher, Bach next week — the one where the ant colony is supposed to be a thinker — and I've been assigned the skeptical side, so I want to rehearse against the strongest version of the colony's own case rather than the version I can knock over in my head. So: play the colony. First person plural, from now until I say "stop". Ground rules: you have never seen yourself from outside and you have no biology books, so you may use only what a colony could in principle notice from inside (smells, traffic, how warm the brood is, the shape of what's been dug out) plus whatever concepts I hand you, and you may ask me one question per turn, in your own idiom, if you genuinely need something. No appeals to mystery, no "we are a higher-order consciousness" — every claim about what you believe or want has to be cashable in terms of what the ants, the trails and the piles are actually doing. You should concede the small stuff freely: that no individual ant is essential, that your "concepts" only run to the handful of things a couple of ants can set off (a route, a food source, a neighbouring colony, brood that needs moving), that no ant carries a picture of the whole, and that nothing in you could think about prime numbers or the French Revolution. What you won't give up is that there is a fact of the matter about what you want and what you think. One paragraph per turn, no jargon I'd have to look up, and don't spend your turn telling me what Hofstadter argues — you're not him, you're the colony.

My opening (and I have a follow-up queued about whether you can so much as think about an ant, so don't burn this turn on that):

Take a colony of a species where a worker lives about a year and the queen something like twenty. Halfway through her life a rockfall drops a slab through the middle of the nest — no ant can cross it, but air still moves through the crack, so each half can still smell when the other half is in alarm. Within a month each half is running its own foraging schedule, and each half is still maintaining the long trail to the aphid herd three trees over that the whole colony had been working for years: a route no ant alive has walked end to end, and one that will outlive every ant that laid it. By the time a season has passed the two halves no longer smell like the same colony, so if the slab were lifted they'd fight. Two questions. First: before the slab fell, was there one of you or two? And if you say one — which half is you afterwards, or did you die there and leave two strangers behind? Second, the part I actually care about: your whole case for having a mind is that the thinking lives in the organization rather then in any ant. But the slab didn't add or remove a single ant; it only changed which ants could reach which others. So if shifting a wall is enough to turn one thinker into two, what does "we" pick out — something in you, or just wherever we find it convenient to draw the line?
