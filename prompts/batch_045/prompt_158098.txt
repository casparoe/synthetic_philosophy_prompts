hey — I need something practical and I'd rather get the spec right before I start drafting.

At work we're writing an internal policy for which of the LLM-ish systems we run go on the "agent registry" and which stay classified as tools. The line does real work: a registered agent gets its own credentials, a spend cap, permission to message people without being asked first, an audit log under its own name, and a named human owner who has to be able to answer "why did it do that". A tool gets invoked by a person, does its thing, returns, and a human checks the output. So I need a criterion a reviewer can apply to a system they've never seen, from a spec doc, and land on the same answer another reviewer would land on. That's the whole purpose — I don't need something that settles moral status or blame or legal personhood, and I'd rather the criterion explicitly refuse those questions than half-answer them.

So: write me the desiderata. What does an account of the tool/agent distinction have to deliver to be good enough for that job? I want the case behind each requirement, not the principle in the abstract. Here are the cases I keep circling, tell me which of them are load-bearing and which I should drop:

- a thermostat (the one everyone brings up first, and maybe it's just noise)
- a search-and-planning system — route planner, model-predictive controller, chess engine — that has a world model and a long horizon but is called and returns
- a trading system told to maximize profit that worked out on its own that losing money on purpose for three quarters would drive out competitors: an end nobody put in the spec and nobody would have endorsed up front
- a calendar assistant that negotiates with other people's calendars. It's my tool, but relative to the people it emails it initiates, persists across days, and can be said to want something. Same system, two counterparties, maybe two statuses.
- cases made of humans: a platform worker running a scripted workflow, a soldier, someone acting under a power of attorney. Some of these get treated as tools even though they're persons.
- group cases: a firm, a ship's crew, an ant colony. Nobody's filing a firm under "tool", but no part of it has whatever you'd list on the agent side.
- nesting: an agent built out of tools, a tool built out of agents.
- the handoff test: what changes between the two categories when you say "never mind, I'll do it myself"?
- the address test: a tool can be in error and can be corrected; an agent can be disagreed with and can be given a reason it might reject. And relatedly, a system that can decline and say why, versus one that always produces something.

Also please:

1. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable given my purpose and which are just one camp's prejudice that's snuck in. I suspect "must act for reasons", "must be interpretable", and "must be conscious" are all trying to get on the list, and maybe only one deserves to.
2. Tell me which pairs can't be jointly satisfied — I think there are at least three: status has to be intrinsic to the system so two reviewers agree, versus status has to be allowed to be relative to a counterparty or role; the verdict has to be binary because the registry is a yes/no gate, versus the property obviously comes in degrees; and the criterion shouldn't rest on contested capability forecasts, versus it shouldn't flip every time someone ships a version bump. Which of these tensions is real and which is a false alarm?
3. Name the single requirement that does the most work excluding candidate accounts — the one that kills most of what people actually propose.
4. Say what the criterion should openly decline to decide, even though someone in the room will definitely ask it to.
5. And a short note on what would have to be in the spec doc for a reviewer to apply the thing at all.

I don't want readings or citations. Cases and requirements. If you think my framing of the purpose is wrong — like the registry/tool split is the wrong cut to be making — say that first and then answer anyway.
