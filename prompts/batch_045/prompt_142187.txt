I sit on the committee rewriting the intake protocol for a memory and palliative care clinic, and before we look at any particular theory of the self I want a specification of what we should be demanding of one. The protocol has to settle three questions about a patient:

(i) how much weight to give a set of values avowed earlier — an advance directive, a letter, a recorded interview — when the patient now, with advanced dementia, appears settled and content and no longer avows them;
(ii) whether a generative model trained on the patient's own corpus, which continues to produce fluent first-personal avowals in their idiom after they can no longer speak for themselves, may be consulted as evidence of what the patient wants or would have wanted — call this a narrative proxy;
(iii) how to arbitrate between the family's account of who the patient is and the patient's own earlier account when the two conflict, including cases where the family's account is shaped by scripts (about gender, about decline, about what a good death looks like) that the patient explicitly rejected while competent.

I do not want your verdict on any of those. I want the desiderata: what an account of the self would have to deliver in order to be adequate to that task, with the case or consideration behind each item, and each item marked either as non-negotiable or as the prejudice of one camp.

The list must be built to survive the following material, which I take to be the phenomena any such account has to get right or explain away: a patient with dense retrograde amnesia who retains trait dispositions and semantic self-knowledge but no episodic memory of the life in question; a confabulator whose fluent self-narrative is false in detail but is produced by whatever mechanisms the theory itself appeals to; someone who has never constructed an explicit self-narrative, whether by age, temperament, culture, or executive impairment; a person whose commitments at a single time pull in incompatible directions, or whose reflectively endorsed judgments diverge from what they actually do; a person who repudiates a large stretch of their own past and asks whether what they did then meant what it seemed to mean at the time; and a narrative that is co-authored under conditions of dependency or oppression, so that the question of whether it is theirs comes apart from the question of whether it is true.

It should also respect, or explicitly collapse and say why, the distinctions that keep doing work in this area: having a self versus how much of one; synchronic unity versus diachronic unity; a narrative as constitutive of the self versus a narrative as evidence of it; a person's authority over their own narrative versus that narrative's answerability to the facts of a life; the self as an abstract posit useful for prediction and explanation versus the self as a further constituent of the person. That last one is the crux for the narrative proxy, and I want the specification to say what follows from treating the self as a center of narrative gravity rather than as an extra thing: whether a proxy that sustains the same gravitational center has any claim to be a continuer, whether one human organism can host more than one such center or two organisms share one, and whether facts about what a community is willing to take up in the intentional stance are allowed to settle any of this.

Three further things. First, tell me which desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied; that is the part I most want, since if the list is internally unsatisfiable I would rather know before we build a protocol on it. My own suspicion is that the requirement that a self-narrative be answerable to the actual facts of a life, the claim that there is no deeper fact of the matter for it to be answerable to, and the requirement that revision of a self-narrative can be genuinely self-transformative cannot all be held together, but make the case in your own terms. Second, name the single desideratum that does the most to exclude otherwise live candidates, and say what it excludes. Third, mark separately whether a theory is required to yield a determinate verdict for a committee working from bounded evidence, or whether licensing a refusal to decide can itself count as adequacy.

Close with a short scorecard — a paragraph apiece, no more — on how four families of view do against the list: the abstractionist picture of the self as a center of narrative gravity; narrative self-constitution views of the kind that pair a constraint that the narrative track the actual facts of a life with a constraint that it be articulated to some degree; neo-Lockean psychological continuity views; and the anti-narrativist view that selves are not narrative at all but episodic. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; the scorecard is for orientation only.
