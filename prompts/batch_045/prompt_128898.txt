I'm a fourth-year PhD student in political theory, and I'm the graduate member of the editorial board of an interdisciplinary journal that sits between moral and political philosophy and science and technology studies. At our meeting in three weeks the board votes on a change to how we review manuscripts, and I have been asked to draft the position paper we will discuss and then vote on. I would like you to write it.

The relevant facts. An internal audit of the last five years of the journal found that roughly two thirds of all references in accepted articles go to six journals; under five percent of accepted articles cite anything from a literature other than the two the journal was founded to bridge; and in the fourteen cases where an editor, on their own initiative, sent a manuscript to a reviewer from the other side of that divide, nine of the published articles cited the outside literature exactly once, in a short paragraph near the end, introduced by some version of the remark that the outside work rests on framework assumptions this article does not share. In none of those nine did any claim in the body of the article change, and in three the outside work was cited in support of a claim it is standardly read as opposing.

The proposal on the table, which I will call Requirement R, has three parts. First, every conditionally accepted article is sent to one additional reviewer nominated from a counterpart literature designated by the handling editor, and the author may veto up to two nominees. Second, the published article must carry a short engagement note in which the author states the strongest objection to the article's central claim that would be raised from that counterpart literature, and answers it in about half the length. Third, compliance with the note is a condition of final acceptance.

I want the paper to do more than adjudicate R, and this is where I would like the real work to go. There are, as I see it, three different things that could be wrong with us, and they call for different remedies. The first is simple non-exposure: our authors and reviewers mostly do not encounter the outside literature, so the objection never arrives. The second is harder: our authors do encounter it, but something about our practices lowers the standing of outsiders, so that an outside objection can be discharged with a sentence about framework assumptions rather than answered, and answering it would cost the author standing with the readers whose opinion determines whether the article matters. The third is different again: nothing is missing and nobody's credibility has been attacked, but the journal has no mechanism by which an objection from outside could ever alter an article's central claim, because such objections are routinely filed as disagreements about framework and therefore as nobody's business. I call this third thing framework filing. My suspicion is that framework filing is our actual condition, that R treats it as if it were the first, and that R would make it worse rather than better by giving framework filing an official, visible, and apparently virtuous slot in the article: an author can now point to the engagement note as evidence of having engaged.

There is a second worry I want addressed head-on, and it is the strongest thing that can be said against my own inclination. The choice of which literature counts as the counterpart for a given manuscript is not a neutral administrative act, and a blanket duty to engage the other side does not fall evenly. It falls hardest on authors whose work is already outside the mainstream of the journal's two home literatures and who already spend a large share of their word budget establishing that their questions are legitimate at all. For those authors, R looks less like an openness requirement than like a tax, and there is a real risk that it sends exactly the submissions we most want to a friendlier venue. My early-career colleagues on the board put this point forcefully and I do not think it can be waved away by saying the burden is only a few hundred words.

Please write the position paper with the following in it. Give a bottom line in the first two sentences: adopt R, reject it, or adopt it in a modified form, and if modified, specify the modification precisely enough that the board can vote on it. Rule specifically on the author-veto clause, which I think is doing more work than it appears to and which may let authors select which outsider counts as representative. Say which of the three diagnoses the audit actually supports, be explicit that the evidence as it stands is compatible with more than one of them, and propose the cheapest further evidence the board could gather in the next eighteen months that would tell them apart. Address the mechanism by which a compulsory engagement requirement may convert non-exposure into something closer to the second condition, and consider whether the note should instead be drafted by the counterpart reviewer and published with it, with the author given a right of reply, so that the objection is not one the author has restated in terms they can defeat. Finally, since the board will need to know whether the policy worked, propose two or three measures that do not simply count cross-literature citations, and state in advance what result would count as a reason to drop the policy.

Constraints: around 1,200 to 1,500 words, in memo form with a header and a recommendation up front, addressed to a board split between two senior editors who think R is plainly right and several early-career members who think it is plainly a tax, so it should give neither side the impression of being patted on the head. No citations, footnotes, or reading suggestions: the board will not follow them up, and I cannot check them, so if you want to invoke a body of work, describe it in a phrase rather than naming authors or titles. Argue from the case.
