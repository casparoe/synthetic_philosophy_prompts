I'm a novelist — four books out, midlist, you won't have heard of them — and this happens to me three or four times a year, and I still don't know what to do about it.

My friend Keiko, who's Japanese, who I met at a residency years ago, has a debut coming in the autumn and she's asked me for a cover quote. I've read the manuscript. Its not good. Not terrible, just inert — the kind of book where nothing is quite at stake. There's no true sentence of praise I can give her that would sell a copy.

Here's why I can't just settle it. Everyone knows what a blurb is. Keiko knows, her editor knows, most readers know: a friend wrote this. When I write "a fearless, incandescent debut," nobody is going to believe that I believe it — least of all Keiko, who asked me precisely because I'm her friend. So it's hard for me to see who gets deceived. And yet it doesn't feel fine, and I don't think that's only squeamishness: my name goes on it, the book walks into the world with credibility borrowed from mine, some readers don't know the convention, and every time someone says yes it gets a little harder for the next person to say no.

Two other things tangle it up.

Rinke, a Dutch friend, thinks the whole dance is grotesque. His view: if I don't think the book is good I should say so to her face in plain words, and anything else is treating her like she can't handle it. When I told him how Keiko and I actually talk — a lot of indirection, "maybe the middle needs more room," her not asking twice — he said that's just lying with extra steps. But it isn't quite: she understood me perfectly well, and she got to decide what to do with it. Though honestly I don't know how much of that is her, how much is the language, how much is just that we're both writers.

And there's a version where I never say anything false and she still gets her quote: I could praise the typesetting, or say something technically true about the premise, and let the publisher set it under my name in the same big type. That feels worse to me then the outright lie, and I want to know if that's right or if I'm being precious.

So: does a sentence nobody believes still do the thing that makes it wrong? Is it different if I tell her privately what I really think first and write the quote anyway? Is saying it in plain words obviously the honest option just because its true, or does the plain version cost something the oblique one doesn't? Is declining in a way she can read — I never blurb anyone, sorry — honest, or the cruellest option on the table? And does it matter that one yes from me changes nothing about the convention?

Please skip the "honesty and kindness aren't really in conflict" thing. I've read that a hundred times and it doesn't reach this case.
