So: there's a motorised double pendulum in our department lobby, the kind that never repeats itself, and after a seminar on it I got into an argument with a friend that I now can't find the exit from. Here it is, roughly cleaned up. (1) Determinism as our lecturer defined it: the complete state of a system at one time together with the laws entails a unique state of that system at every other time — same state, same laws, same outcome, of necessity. (2) Sensitive dependence: a chaotic system has a positive Lyapunov exponent λ, so two initial states differing by δ end up differing by about δe^(λt), at least while the gap is still small compared to the size of the attractor. (3) So to get the state right to within ε at time T, you need the initial state to within εe^(−λT) — roughly 1.44λT more bits of initial data than there are bits in the answer itself, i.e. the data requirement grows only linearly in T. (4) Any predictor that actually exists is a physical system: finite memory, finite precision, and it can't outrun its own hardware. (5) So a real predictor with M bits to spare has a horizon H of about M·ln2/λ past which its trajectory predictions are no better than a guess, and doubling its memory only doubles H — you can't just scale your way out. (6) Worse, a predictor inside the world is part of the world's state, so it can't even in principle hold a complete specification of what it's trying to predict: that specification would have to contain a specification of itself. My tutor calls this the Laplace-with-a-clipboard move. (7) But if no physically possible predictor inside the world could ever have grounds for saying what the state is after H, then there's no fact of the matter about that state — a thesis whose only difference-making consequences are in principle unknowable isn't a thesis about the world. (8) Hence for a chaotic world like ours determinism in the sense of (1) is either false or empty: nothing in the world answers to it. (9) And yet the Lorenz system, the paradigm chaotic system, three coupled ODEs, is deterministic in exactly the sense of (1): every initial condition has a unique solution. So (2) through (6) can't be what kills determinism; they're all consistent with (1). (10) So something in (1)–(8) has to go, and I can't say what.
