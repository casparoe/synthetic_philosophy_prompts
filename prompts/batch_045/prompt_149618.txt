I have been arguing with a colleague for years — she is American, I was raised in Germany and have lived in Britain for two decades — about which of the two moral cultures has the better of it, and I have come to suspect that our disagreement may not be about the moral question at all. I want your number, and I want the decomposition that produces it.

The proposition, as precisely as I can state it. Consider a society of a familiar kind: large, wealthy, legally ordered, religiously mixed, administered by rules that it neither fully trusts nor could do without. Compare two folk moralities within it, differing only in the following respect. Under what I shall call *protest-immunity*, an agent who breaks a valid rule because her conscience, reflectively and sincerely exercised, forbids compliance has thereby done nothing for which she owes an account; the breach is discharged by the sincerity of the judgement behind it. Under *protest-accountability*, conscience can make the breach right, and can even make it obligatory, but it does not discharge it: the agent still owes those whom the rule was there to protect an account of why they should bear the cost of her judgement, and owes it even when her judgement was correct.

Let P be the claim that, for such a society, the second folk morality is, taken as a whole and as a package, more defensible than the first. I shall treat the matter as resolved one way or the other if an impartial enquirer with full access to the psychology, the history and the institutional evidence would think it more reasonable to want her own society's folk morality nudged in that direction than in the other, all things considered rather than by any single measure. My credence is about 0.6; my colleague puts hers at about 0.85 on the other side.

Please decompose. The terms I can see are these: whether protest-accountability is a coherent moral category at all, rather than a confused blend of consequentialist worry and social awkwardness masquerading as a moral remainder; whether I have drawn the contrast correctly — whether what I call protest-accountability is really what the German folk morality embodies, rather than a habit of deference to whoever happens to hold the pen, and whether protest-immunity is really what the American one embodies, rather than a standing demand that rules earn their authority continuously; whether the observed difference traces to moral disagreement at all or to background conditions such as trust in officials, the density of the administrative state and the anonymity of the society, in which case the question of which has the better of it is ill-posed because each is an adaptation to its setting; whether either can be detached from the package it travels in, so that a society might take on the one without the rest, failing which the comparison is really a comparison of whole forms of life; whether the two moralities share any currency at all, or differ not merely about the excusing force of conscience but about what morality is for — the maintenance of right relations among members as against the protection of individual integrity; and, at the empirical tail, the probability that each has a characteristic failure mode that swamps the comparison, accountability-talk becoming a shelter for the unimaginative and a licence for the powerful, or conscience-talk becoming a privilege of the articulate and a free-rider's charter.

Give me a rough probability and one line of justification for each term, say how they combine and where they are not independent, and give me your resulting figure for P. If any of these terms, resolving one way, would leave P indeterminate rather than false, say so and tell me how to read your number. Then tell me which term your final figure is most sensitive to, what single piece of evidence or argument would move it most, and — if you had to bet — which term my disagreement with my colleague actually lives in, and what each of us would have to concede in order to converge.
