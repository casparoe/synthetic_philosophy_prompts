I teach U.S. history at a community college, and two years ago I ended up on our county's volunteer oversight board for automated decision systems — not because I know anything about statistics, but because nobody else would do it. We review three things: the pretrial risk score used at first appearance, the hospital network's model for handing out a fixed number of intensive post-discharge follow-up slots, and a newer program that targets eviction-prevention money. I've read enough to know the shape of the standard impossibility results, and I've given up hoping for a model that satisfies every criterion at once: if a score is calibrated within each group and the groups have different base rates of the outcome, then at a single decision threshold the higher-base-rate group absorbs more false positives and fewer false negatives; forcing both error rates to match across groups destroys calibration; and, unless the predictions are perfect, matching error rates across groups is incompatible with matching what a positive prediction is worth. I argued about this with a friend who builds these systems for a living, and every time I said "this one feels unfair" he said "that's just the impossibility theorem," and I said "I know — but which side of it do we want to be on?" Neither of us could say what the question even turns on. So here are my verdicts, and I'd like to know whether they add up to anything.

(1) Pretrial, calibrated score, one cutoff for everyone: defendants booked out of the north-side precincts (where most misdemeanor arrests happen, and whose recorded reoffense rate is accordingly much higher) are detained despite not reoffending at about twice the rate of everyone else, while everyone else is released and then re-arrested at about twice the north-side rate. Unfair — the false detentions are the injury, and I want that rate equalized. (2) Same model, but the thresholds are tuned separately per group until the false-positive rates match: now more north-side defendants are released and re-arrested, and since arrests happen near home, most of the additional victims are north-side residents too. Not an improvement, and maybe worse — but I can't say why, since the disparity I objected to in (1) is gone. (3) Hospital slots: 200 slots, 1,000 patients, ranked by predicted readmission risk; because readmission is more common among the low-income patients the hospital serves, they take 140 of the slots. Our equity officer proposes capping each group at 100. The cap is the unfair move, not the original: under it, a patient at 45% risk is passed over for one at 28%, and I can't see who that serves. And yet the identical 45-vs-28 comparison at the cutoff inside a single group doesn't bother me at all. (4) Then we learn that the low-income group's readmission rate is elevated partly because those patients were least likely to get follow-up in the first place — the label is in part a record of past neglect. That makes me more comfortable with the uncapped allocation, not less, even though by the reasoning I used in (1) a corrupted base rate should make me warier of any disparity that tracks it. (5) A new pretrial model cuts false detentions by a third in both groups relative to the judges it would replace; because the judges were harsher on the north-side group to begin with, the absolute gap between the groups widens. Fine — deploy it. (6) A different model hits exact false-positive parity, but only by detaining north-side defendants the judges would have released, i.e. by making that group worse off than the process it replaces in order to hit the number. Not fine. (7) A third model drops prior-arrest count on the grounds that it is inflated by over-policing; it's less accurate, detention rates rise for both groups, and the north-side group ends up worse off than under the judges. I still want to deploy it — which suggests the rule I was reaching for in (5) and (6) isn't about being made worse off as such.

My tentative principle is something like: no group should be made worse off than under the decision process the model replaces, and parity among the usual error-rate criteria is worth pursuing only inside that constraint, ranked by whichever error does the harm in that setting. Two problems. First, (7) shows the rule needs a clause about whether the baseline was itself legitimate, and once I add that clause I'm not sure the rule has any content left beyond a general theory of what we owe each other. Second, the case I keep reversing on: the eviction-prevention money. There a positive prediction triggers a benefit, so the error I care about flips — I want to equalize the rate at which people are wrongly denied and then evicted — but that forces unequal false-positive rates, meaning one group receives more payments that turn out to have been unnecessary. I find I don't care about that at all, and that troubles me, because in (1) I cared enormously about exactly that kind of unequal error rate and my friend's theorem says the two situations are formally the same.

What I want from you: two to four candidate principles, each stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new vendor filing without asking you again, with the clause-motivating case named for each clause; a blunt statement of which of my verdicts each candidate forces me to abandon; and each candidate's verdict on the eviction-prevention case plus one case of your own construction that you think best separates the candidates from each other. One more thing, if your principle has anything to say about it: our numbers change substantially, and sometimes change sign, depending on whether we measure disparities across the two groups the county officially tracks, across precincts, or across age bands within each group — and nothing I've read tells me which partition of the population is the one fairness is supposed to be measured over. And if the honest answer is that no group-level criterion is doing any real work in my verdicts, and that they are all being generated by who bears which kind of harm, say that plainly, and tell me what I should be demanding from the vendors instead.
