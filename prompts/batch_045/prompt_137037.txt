I have recently and fairly quickly come to accept strong longtermism, and I am now in a position to act on it in a way that would be hard to reverse. So I want an honest map of what could make the view false, not a defense of it.

The position, as I hold it: the expected value of the very long-run future is astronomically large; some courses of action open to us now have a real, non-negligible chance of affecting it; and so across a wide range of decision contexts the option with the best long-run consequences just is the best option, with nearer-term and more certain goods mattering chiefly insofar as they bear on the long run. I am not interested in the associated research program as a whole; I want the core claim and how exposed it is.

Why this is live for me: my daughter was born last year, and I have just joined the board of a small family foundation, historically a funder of children's health and education in our region, whose charter requires it to spend its remaining corpus down within fifteen years. Assume that our legal room is wider than our history and that we could redirect a substantial share of the final grants toward work on the long-run future; assume too that my fellow trustees are open to argument and that one of them, a pediatrician, thinks this is straightforwardly a mistake. Give me the reasoning, not the legal or fiduciary question.

Please do the following.

1. Lay out the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, saying explicitly what the ordering turns on. I have in mind at least three families of objection, and I want them separated rather than blended: doubts about the value claim (whether the future is worth much in the first place, and whether it is worth positively rather than negatively — a future containing vastly more suffering than flourishing would flip the sign of the whole calculation); doubts about the difference-making claim (whether anything we do now has effects on the long run that survive counterfactual replacement, convergence, or institutional and cultural erosion — including whether we are really at a period of unusually high leverage, or whether that is a story we tell ourselves because we happen to live now); and doubts about the aggregation and decision-rule claim (whether expected value can bear this much weight when probabilities are tiny, whether the view is committed to a kind of fanaticism, and what happens to it under moral uncertainty and under different views in population ethics).

2. For each, say whether it is something a proponent of strong longtermism would themselves concede counts against the view, or something they would dismiss as resting on a rival commitment they reject. I do not want a critic's-only list; the interesting set is the defeaters an adherent would grant.

3. Give some attention to an objection I have not been able to shake and have not seen handled to my satisfaction, and tell me whether it is genuinely independent or just one of the above in costume. If I take anthropic reasoning seriously — the doomsday-argument style of updating on the fact that I find myself among the very earliest humans — then my expected number of future people is orders of magnitude smaller than the standard calculation assumes, and the stakes seem to deflate accordingly. Does the size of the deflation depend on whether one updates in the manner usually called the self-sampling assumption or the self-indication assumption? Either way, is this a real defeater to the value claim, a general worry about expected-value reasoning under deep uncertainty, or something that leaves the ranking of options unchanged even though it changes the headline numbers?

4. Tell me whether the view has been insulated into something unfalsifiable. A defender can always say that the claim is not that we can know the long-run consequences of our actions, only that expected value is the right rule to act on and that the long-run term dominates the sum. Is that a legitimate statement of the position or a retreat? And if it is a retreat, is it a damaging one for a view whose whole point is to redirect actual money and effort?

5. Tell me what would be left if the leverage premise or the tractability premise failed. Is there a defensible intermediate position distinct from both strong longtermism and ordinary concern for future generations, or does the view collapse into the weaker, commonsense one — and would that collapse count as a defeater or as a successful limiting of the claim?

6. Finally, two things about me. I find attacks on the difference-making premise far more persuasive than appeals to intuition of the form that one does not sacrifice a real child now for merely possible people later; tell me whether that weighting of mine is itself evidence that I have already absorbed a maximizing framework and am therefore a poor judge of what should move me. And I would like your own judgment on whether strong longtermism predicts anything its rivals do not — some observation, historical episode, or empirical finding that would count in its favor, including anything that has arguably already happened — or whether it is purely a principle for weighing considerations we already have.

If the fifteen-year spend-down matters to any of this, as opposed to the patient-philanthropy version of the position where one invests and waits for better opportunities, say so and explain why.
