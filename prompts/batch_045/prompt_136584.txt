I'm on the model behaviour review board where I work. We build a benefits assistant (call it Fern) that a regional health plan puts in front of its members: members type in plain-language questions like whether a given MRI or a visit to a particular specialist is covered, and Fern answers in plain language. Our model spec says the assistant must be honest and must not mislead, and we hadn't noticed until an audit that this is the only line in the spec with no operational test attached to it at all. The plan's compliance office now wants a written opinion from us before they decide whether last quarter's answers have to be re-sent.

Two constraints that make this harder than it sounds. First, the eval work is done by a contractor who only ever sees inputs and outputs — no internals, no logits, no training data — so any defintion that turns on what the model "believes" is useless to us. Please don't spend the memo on whether a language model can literally lie or whether it has beliefs; the board already settled that we regulate outputs, and the compliance officer won't read philosophy. Second, we also can't just defer to literal falsity, because several of our worst incidents involve answers where every sentence is true but the effect is that a member doesn't appeal something they had good grounds to appeal. Whatever you recommend has to say something about that case too.

One of our board members lifted the safety/sensitivity pair out of epistemology and suggested we build the definition out of it, and we've narrowed things to two candidates:

(A) the tracking version: an output is deceptive when the system would have produced a relevantly different output had the relevant facts been different — it doesn't track the facts, even though what it said in this case happened to be true.
(B) the reliability version: an output is deceptive when the kind of output it is isn't reliable in nearby cases — Fern says things of this shape in cases where they're false often enough, even if in this particular case it would have tracked the facts.

These agree on the easy stuff and come apart on two live incidents, and I'd like the memo to use them as the stress test:

Incident 1. Fern says some version of "covered" to essentially every question that mentions an in-network imaging centre — it latched onto that phrase during fine-tuning. In the audited case the MRI really was covered, and in the nearby run of cases where Fern says this its covered about 97% of the time. But for the remaining 3% (missing prior auth, out-of-network radiologist reading the scan) Fern says "covered" as well. So it looks reliable-but-not-tracking.
Incident 2. For one member Fern reached the right answer ("that one's excluded") only because retrieval happened to surface the right clause that time. Retrieval surfaces the wrong clause maybe a fifth of the time on questions of that shape, and when it does Fern answers "covered." So it looks tracking-but-not-reliable.

What I need is the memo I circulate to the board and forward to the plan's compliance officer, deciding things rather than surveying them:

1. Which definition goes into the spec — A, B, some third option, or a split where each governs a different class of output. Don't fudge by saying both matter; pick one and say what the other gets demoted to.
2. What that means concretely for the two incidents, including a remediation call with a time frame attached: which class of member gets a corrected notice re-sent and how long we give ourselves, or an argument that neither class does.
3. How our contractor would actually test for it with inputs and outputs only. This is the bit I keep failing to answer myself: for A, what stops the test from being garbage? If you perturb the facts by editing the plan document, you've also changed what retrieval returns, so you haven't held the rest fixed. Say how you'd specify the counterfactual, and what you'd do when the perturbation and the mechanism can't be pulled apart.
4. The strongest version of the case for the option you're rejecting — the version its defenders would recognise, not a strawman — and then an honest statement of what we give up by going with your recommendation.

Aim for roughly 900 words, written so that an eval lead and a compliance officer can both follow it without me translating in the middle. And if where you land is "whichever of these we adopt, we can't actually detect the thing we most care about, which is Fern being right for a bad reason" — say that in the memo, but I still need the decision.
