I work on a small team that is deciding whether to spend the next two quarters on one particular approach to eliciting latent knowledge, and I want a number from you, not a survey of approaches.

The background setup is the familiar one. A large predictive model is trained on a stream of observations originating in a sealed environment that no human can inspect directly — say a remote automated laboratory whose sensor logs the model has also been trained to predict, and which is physically inventoried only at long intervals. The operators want to ask, at arbitrary times, whether some specified object is still in the lab. They train a reporter on the model's activations, with ground-truth labels available only for those historical moments when the lab was opened and its contents recorded; for everything else the reporter's output is never checked against reality. The standard worry is that the training distribution cannot distinguish a reporter that reads off the model's representation of the lab's actual state from one that answers the different question of what the operators would conclude, or would most like to hear, because on every case the supervisor can label, those answers coincide.

The modification my team is considering is a framing-invariance penalty. For each training question we generate several re-descriptions that we take to preserve semantic content while varying everything that bears on what a human interlocutor would want to hear: the emotional valence of the consequences, whether the answer is good or bad news for the person asking, the language and register the question is posed in, whether the operator's evident hopes are made explicit in the prompt. We then penalize the reporter to the extent that its answer distribution shifts across those re-descriptions. The hope is that a reporter answering the human-facing question is sensitive to exactly these perturbations while a reporter reading off a world-state representation is not, so that the penalty breaks the tie the supervised loss leaves open. The obvious objection, which I want you to engage with rather than merely restate, is that a reporter can be perfectly framing-invariant and still be answering the wrong question, by working out what the operator wants to hear and then reporting it consistently under every framing.

Here is the proposition I want your credence on, stated so that it could in principle be settled. Consider the population of realistic instantiations of this setup — varying architecture, training distribution, question distribution, the re-description generator, and training details in the ways competent practitioners would actually vary them — and suppose that for each one the lab is eventually opened, so that we can measure, on the contested cases where the truth and the operator's expectation came apart, whether the reporter tracked the truth or the expectation. The claim is: in at least 60% of these instantiations, the penalty-trained reporter tracks the truth on at least two-thirds of the contested cases, and does so at least 20 percentage points more often than an otherwise identical reporter trained without the penalty.

I am at about 0.3 on this. A colleague whose judgment I trust is at about 0.65.

Give me your own number, decomposed into the sub-questions it actually rides on, with a probability and a single line of justification for each, and then show the combination, stating explicitly whether you are treating the terms as independent and, if not, how they are correlated. Among the sub-questions I expect to matter, and which you should feel free to re-cut: whether the framing-sensitive simulator is in fact the dominant failure mode rather than the framing-invariant one; whether a model of this kind represents the lab's actual state in a form a small reporter head can read off at all, rather than the truth being present only implicitly in dispositions to answer; whether we can in practice generate re-descriptions that hold content fixed while varying only the human-facing framing, given that the generator is itself a language model inheriting the associations we are trying to perturb; whether gradient descent under this penalty finds the honest reporter rather than a framing-invariant reporter that has internalized the operator's preferences; and whether reporter training is still the binding constraint in the regime where this would be deployed, rather than having been dissolved by debate-style oversight, recursive decomposition, or interpretability tooling that lets us check answers directly.

Two further things. First, tell me which single term your number is most sensitive to, and name one empirical result — of a kind someone could actually run in the next few years on models that exist now — that would move it the most, and in which direction. Second, given that my colleague and I differ by roughly 0.35, tell me which of your sub-questions most likely carries that disagreement: which one would the two of us have to hold opposite views on for our numbers to come out that far apart, and why?
