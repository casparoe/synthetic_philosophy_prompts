hey — i'm writing a paper on what should happen when a community's shared memory and the documentary record contradict each other, and I need a test case built to order. Here are the three positions I'm working with (my own labels, don't worry about matching anyone else's terminology):

- Record-first: memory is just more evidence. What happened is settled by the best available evidence; where memory and record conflict, the memory should be revised to fit the record.
- Memory-constitutive: the shared memory is part of what makes this community *this* community — it carries their obligations, their claims, their ongoing projects. Rewriting it is a genuine loss and not merely a correction, so the record doesn't automatically win.
- Custodial: nobody owns their own history outright; the community holds it in trust, for the people who lived it and for whoever comes after. What matters is fidelity to what was actually entrusted to them — a question about who handed what to whom, and not a question about accuracy, and not one about identity either.

What I need is a case where the first two land on the same practical verdict — keep the memory going, let the record give way, at least for now — while the custodial view says the opposite: correct it now, publicly.

I know that sounds backwards, and that's the point. Normally the hard-nosed evidence person demands correction and the romantic wants preservation, so the work is in engineering the situation so record-first arrives at "keep" on its own terms: its aim is that true things end up believed and that evidence survives, and I want that aim, plus the facts of the case, to be what pushes it there. Not sympathy for the town, not "it would be hurtful", not some imported duty of care. And the memory-constitutive view has to get to the same verdict by a genuinely different route — if both of them are basically saying "the ritual matters", the agreement is worthless to me.

Constraints:
- Realistic, 20th-century-ish, one paragraph for the scenario. A town, a minority community, a union local, a congregation — something with an annual commemoration and a committee that has to decide something this year.
- The conflict should be over the details, not the headline: the record should confirm that the atrocity happened but relocate it — who did it, where, when, how many. I don't want a case that turns on whether anything happened at all.
- The record has to be genuinely good: the kind of thing real archival and forensic work produces. No forged documents, no last-minute diary that settles everything, no survivor turning up alive.
- The community is neither dupes nor saints, and the memory isn't something they were tricked into last week.
- What I'll count as cheating: dissolving the conflict instead of resolving it. So no "actually the memory was true under a looser reading", no "the record turns out to be a fabrication", no "they never really believed it, it was always symbolic". The memory has to be straightforwardly false and the record straightforwardly right.

Deliverables after the scenario: (1) what each of the three views says and why, with the reasoning actually spelled out; (2) one short paragraph on which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to the verdicts if you delete just that feature — I want to know whether it collapses back into the boring 2-1 split the other way; (3) how someone who thinks the record should just win outright would attack your case, and whether that attack actually lands.

What the case is for, so you know what I'll reject: I want to argue that the custodial view isn't a mushy midpoint that always lands between the other two — here it should be the *only* one demanding correction. So a case where the custodial view just splits the difference, or where its verdict is a coin flip, is no use to me. Don't cite anything, don't recieve help from the literature — I want your own construction.
