I cover science and ideas for a magazine, which means I spend a lot of evenings at city council meetings I'm not really qualified to be at. Last week I was in a midsized city — call it Halton — where the council was voting on whether to renew its automated school-zone speed camera program, and I have 900 words on it due friday. The wrinkle that got under my skin: these are second-generation systems. Same poles, same housings, but the vendor now ships a dashboard where the city sets the trigger speed, the grace margin, and — this is the part I keep chewing on — which citations get auto-mailed and which get routed to a human reviewer before they go out. So the hardware and the enforcement policy have more or less come apart. A tenants'-rights organizer who's been campaigning against the program got up and said, in response to the usual "the cameras are just a neutral tool, the policy is yours" line from the vendor's lobbyist, roughly: "I mean, artifacts have politics." And then used that to argue the city should scrap the program outright, not tune the settings — that no configuration of it is neutral and that "let's just pick better thresholds" is a dodge. My friend who did a PhD in STS rolls her eyes every time I bring this up, but I genuinely can't tell what work that sentence was doing.

So: can you lay out the menu of precise claims "artifacts have politics" could be making, from the near-trivial to the wild? I know it comes from Langdon Winner's 1980 essay in Daedalus, and that he distinguishes at least things that require a particular social order from things that are merely strongly compatible with one, so I'm not asking for a summary of the article — I want the distinct theses sorted and given verdicts. Which readings are platitudes (designs reflect somebody's choices, sure), which are substantive and defensible, which are overstated or just false, and which are doing something different from what they look like — e.g. the reading where the real claim is about depoliticization, that classifying something as a technical matter is itself the political move, or the reading where it's really a claim about who bears responsibility rather than about objects at all. And I want to know which readings would actually license the organizer's conclusion: which ones get you "scrap it," which only get you "tune it with the right stakeholders in the room," and which only get you "this is a legitimately political question, stop pretending it's an engineering one."

The bit I most want your help with is where my runtimetime-configurable case leaves each reading. If the politics can be dialed by whoever holds the dashboard login, is it in the artifact at all — or does it just relocate, into the defaults, into who has admin, into the fact that enforcement became cheap and continuous and so politically invisible, into the lock-in of a five-year service contract? Does configurability dissolve the slogan or give it a new target? Also tell me whether any of the readings really amount to technological determinism in the crude sense, because I suspect that's the version the slogan's critics are attacking and I don't want to repeat that mistake in print. And be straight with me about your own confidence: which reading do you think the organizer actually meant, how sure are you, where is your disambiguation most likely to have been gerrymandered to fit a conclusion you already like, and what would change your mind — which consideration would move you to reclassify one of these readings from platitude to substantive or the other way around. Also flag the parts where you're effectively doing original argument rather than reporting what the slogan means, since I have to be able to tell my editor which is which.
