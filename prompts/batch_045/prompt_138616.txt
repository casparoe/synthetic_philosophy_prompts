I'm the patient representative on my hospital's clinical ethics committee, and we have to make a recommendation next week on whether to renew the pilot of an AI deterioration-alert system on two medical wards. I'd be grateful for a short written opinion I can circulate beforehand.

The setup: the system flags patients whose vitals suggest deterioration. Policy requires a clinician to open and acknowledge each alert in the chart, and nothing happens clinically without that acknowledgment. The vendor's audit shows alerts acknowledged within ninety seconds 96% of the time, with a median of about four seconds spent on the alert screen, and a decision not to act on the alert in under 2% of cases. Overrides turn out to cluster near the end of shifts and on the busiest days — that is, they track fatigue and workload rather than anything about the individual patient. During a two-day vendor outage, nobody on the wards noticed the alerts had stopped. There has been one serious incident: the alert fired, was acknowledged in six seconds, and no one went to the room; the patient was found an hour later. One of the nurses on our committee put the problem better than I can — she said she reads the alerts the way she reads the monitor's beeps, as background noise, and that in the one case where she caught something the machine missed, she had already been uneasy about that patient before any alert appeared.

The vendor says the acknowledgment step just is the safeguard, and that a low override rate reflects an accurate model. Our risk officer says the audit trail is what regulators will want to see. What I can't settle is a comparison a friend of mine made: digger wasps drag a paralyzed cricket to their burrow, leave it at the entrance, and go inside to inspect before hauling it in; if you move the cricket a few inches while they're inside, they come back out and repeat the inspection, and you can make them do it indefinitely. They look exactly like they're checking something. So what would the difference consist in for us — what would it take for an acknowledgment to be an actual judgment rather than a reflex, and is that something we could even detect from the outside?

Three options are on the table: renew as is; suspend; or renew with changes. People have floated requiring a short typed reason for every acknowledgment, building in a delay before the acknowledge button activates, or having the vendor inject occasional fake alerts to see who notices. Some of us worry those would manufacture the paperwork of judgment without the thing itself.

Please draft a memo of roughly 600–800 words that recommends one of the three options (or a specific variant), gives the reasoning, says what evidence would make us revisit it and on what time frame, and treats the strongest argument for the option you reject honestly — I have to defend whichever option to colleagues who disagree. Don't lean on authorities or literature I'd have to look up; just argue it through.
