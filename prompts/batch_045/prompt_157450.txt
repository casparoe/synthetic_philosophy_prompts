DRAFT

Anyone who likes amplification says some version of: the pieces are easier than the whole, so if you can do the pieces you can do the whole. I want to split that into two claims, because I think one of them is close to trivial and the other is doing all the work and is probably false.

Claim 1 (tractability): each sub-question is individually easier for a bounded agent to get right than the original question was.

Claim 2 (composition): a right answer to the original question can be assembled from right answers to the sub-questions by an aggregation step that is *itself* easier than the original question.

Claim 1 is what people picture when they say "decomposition". Claim 2 is what they need. And Claim 2 isn't a claim about difficulty at all — it's a claim about where the goodness of an answer lives. Call the *grain* of a task the level of description at which you'd have to look to tell a good answer from a mediocre one. Composition holds, I want to argue, only for *grain-stable* tasks: tasks where the grain at which the whole answer is good is also a grain the sub-questions can see.

The failure I care about isn't "you decomposed badly". It's "you decomposed fine, every node gave an answer its own overseer would endorse, and the thing that would have made the answer good was never represented at any node". Call this *grain loss*.

Example. A committee is restoring a fresco. They work patch by patch: for each square centimetre, clean, consolidate, or leave alone. Every patch-level decision is locally defensible; a supervisor inspecting any single decision signs off on it. But what made the fresco worth restoring was a property of the whole surface — the way light reads across it, the relation between the intact and ruined passages — and no patch-level question can ask about that. The committee can be perfectly competent at the grain they're working at and still produce a restoration no expert would accept. The interesting bit is that the loss is invisible from inside the process: the only feedback available is local endorsement.

Now amplify. In the tree, each node passes its children a summary of its situation and gets back verdicts. Two things follow. First, *summary bottleneck*: a child's competence is capped by the fidelity of the summary it receives, and writing a good summary is itself a judgment task with the same structure — so it's summaries all the way down, and summaries are lossy in a systematically biased direction. They keep what is easy to state and drop what is easy to see. Second, *deference gradient*: the deeper the tree, the more of the final answer is fixed by nodes whose entire world is somebody else's paraphrase, and paraphrase is exactly the operation that destroys grain.

Where distillation comes in: the usual worry is that distillation is lossy relative to amplification — the student can't fit the teacher. But if grain loss is real, the loss is already in the target. The amplified overseer's output *is* the locally-endorsed, globally-degraded answer, and a perfectly faithful distillation reproduces the degradation exactly. So amplification-and-distillation is a closed loop, and the error is not an approximation error. It's baked into the training signal, which is why per-example accuracy checks won't surface it.

If that's right, the practical upshot is that you should audit for grain, not for sub-answer accuracy. My suggested audit is the *swapping test*: hold the decomposition fixed, swap in a sub-agent that is equally competent but differently tempered at some node, and see whether the final answer moves along a dimension the top-level overseer can see and evaluate. If it does, fine — you've got a genuine difference of judgment. If the answer moves along a dimension nobody at the top can even describe, the grain has been lost somewhere below. And the design rule that falls out: *grain-anchored amplification* — before decomposing, have the overseer state the grain at which the answer is to be judged, and permit only decompositions in which questions at that grain remain askable. No such decomposition? Don't amplify; abstain, defer, or use some other oversight mechanism.
