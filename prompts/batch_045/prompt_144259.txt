I came to philosophy late, after a career in experimental physical chemistry, and I have been keeping a record of the causation verdicts given by the philosopher who leads the reading group I attend. Her rulings so far:

- Late preemption: of two ignition pulses sent to a sample, the first arrives and starts the burn, and the second would have started it forty milliseconds later had the first failed. She rules that the first caused the fire and the second did not.
- Symmetric overdetermination: two pulses arriving together, each sufficient on its own. Both caused it.
- Omission: a technician's failure to add a stabilizer, after which the sample degraded when it would not otherwise have. The omission caused the degradation, notwithstanding that there is no connecting process to point to.
- A star's having condensed with roughly eight solar masses: this caused it to end as a core-collapse supernova rather than as a white dwarf, even though no agency could ever set a star's birth mass.

The new case I want to put to her. A sealed chamber with two compartments, X and Y, divided by a membrane that a cofactor C crosses freely. The total amount M of C is fixed: C is neither created, destroyed, nor carried across the chamber wall, so at every instant C_X + C_Y = M. At t0 eighty percent of M lies in X, and the reaction rate in X at t1 is high. Had only twenty percent lain in X at t0, the remainder being in Y, that rate would have been low; and the dependence reproduces reliably across many runs and across a wide range of temperatures, pH values, and total M. But no procedure consistent with the conservation of C can set C_X while holding C_Y fixed, and no attainable evidence — nor any conceivable experiment — could tell us whether the rate in X answers to the local concentration in X or to the concentration in Y acting through the coupling.

Will she say that the cofactor's being in X at t0 caused the high rate in X at t1? Please set out the rules that fit her record equally well, what each of them predicts about this case, which prediction you would wager on and why, and one further case of your own devising that would separate the surviving rules most cleanly. Textbook labels matter to me less than the content of the rules. Kindly answer in American English, in under 700 words.
