Two friends of mine spent a whole cabin weekend arguing about this and I got stuck refereeing, badly. Setup: the pipes burst overnight because the wood stove went out. Rosa was on the stove rotation that week, and she'd have noticed the fire dying and thrown on a log; she didn't (she'd driven five hours and crashed). Ken was off rotation, asleep in the loft, but he's the type who always gets up sometime in the night and feeds the fire; that night he slept through. If either of them had gotten up, the pipes wouldn't have frozen. Neither did.

(1) Ken: neither of us caused it. Rosa not getting up only mattered because I didn't get up — if she'd stayed in bed and I'd gone down, nothing would have happened, and same the other way around. No but-for dependence either way, so whatever causation by omission is, this case doesn't have any. The burst had causes, just not us.
(2) Rosa: that's just ordinary overdetermination, and it doesn't pick on omissions. Two people separately starting a fire get the same but-for failure, and nobody concludes neither arsonist caused it. Hold the other person's behavior fixed and each of our omissions is a difference-maker. That's all causation by omission ever needed to be.
(3) Ken: then you've lost the thing people actually care about, which is that Rosa's the one who did it. On your story we're symmetric causes, and the only asymmetry left is that it was her week — a fact about what she was supposed to do. If omissions cause only relative to somebody's duties, then cause is just blame wearing a lab coat. Say instead that omissions never cause and people are responsible for them, and stop.
(4) Rosa: you're mixing up whether something causes with which one we bother to mention. Selection is interest-relative everywhere — nobody says the oxygen caused the fire even though it's a but-for condition, and nobody thinks that makes causation norm-dependent. The rotation picks which omission is worth citing; it doesn't decide whether causation happened. And your (1) wasn't about norms at all, so you can't use duties to rescue it.
(5) Ken: the oxygen case isn't like this and you know it. There's a process you can trace from the oxygen to the flames. There's nothing to trace from me asleep in a loft to a pipe in the wall — no contact, no signal, no transfer of anything. That's the real disanalogy with the arsonists: they each have a mechanism to the fire, we have none. A difference-maker with no process behind it isn't a cause, it's a bookkeeping entry.

Please go through the five moves and rate each one from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification apiece, then tell me what both of them are missing. One thing I want your read on specifically: does anything change if you ask not which omission was a difference-maker but which one you could actually intervene on, by itself, to have prevented the burst — and if so, which question is the real one?
