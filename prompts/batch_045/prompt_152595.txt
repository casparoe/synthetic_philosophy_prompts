I am writing the introduction to a collected volume of papers on rational belief by a philosopher who argues almost entirely by ruling on cases, and I have to say what principle her verdicts embody. Before I commit myself I want your read on a case she has never discussed, and on the reasoning that gets you there.

Her verdicts, as I have tabulated them from five papers:

1. Fair lottery, one thousand tickets, one winner, nothing distinguishes the tickets: one should not believe of any ticket that it will lose.
2. The same lottery with a million tickets: the same verdict, with a note that nothing turns on how many tickets there are.
3. The winning number is printed in a newspaper you have independently established to be right nine hundred and ninety-nine times in a thousand: you may believe that ticket four thousand one hundred and eighty-two won. So she does not treat merely statistical support as inert across the board.
4. The preface: the author of an eighteen-hundred-claim monograph, who knows from long experience that books like hers carry about ten errors, may believe each of her claims while also believing, and saying in the preface, that at least one of them is false. She treats the combination as ordinary rather than defective.
5. Closure: she denies that rational belief is closed under conjunction, and has said in print that one can rationally believe each of the three hundred steps of a long derivation while withholding belief from the theorem those steps entail.
6. Stakes: raising the stakes in the preface case, by making the monograph a field manual for a dangerous procedure, does not change her verdict; neither does raising them in the lottery case.
7. Speech and action: the lottery player may not flatly say that his ticket will lose, but there is nothing wrong with his acting on the prospect.
8. She uses a single verb throughout, believe, and nowhere distinguishes believing a proposition from accepting it for the purposes of some inquiry. I suspect that is doing a great deal of work in her papers; I cannot tell which way it cuts.

The thing I keep returning to: she withholds belief at a credence of 0.999 in the lottery and permits it at roughly 0.994 in the preface, so no single threshold can be what she is running; and since she permits the preface pair while refusing the corresponding lottery pair, something other than the numbers is carrying the weight.

The case she has not discussed:

A research consortium finalizes an assessment containing three thousand separate findings. Each finding rests on its working group's own item-specific evidence, and the consortium puts the chance of any given finding being false at about one in a thousand, expecting around three errors in the report as a whole. After the report is finalized, an independent replication unit reports, on the basis of a capture-recapture reanalysis that shares no evidence with the per-finding work and re-examines none of the findings, that reports of this kind contain about thirty false findings per three thousand, a rate near one per cent, and that this report very probably contains several. The consortium has no reason to doubt the replication unit, and knows that the estimate is not a remark about its general competence but a measurement of this report. The audit identifies no particular finding. Conditional on it, the chance that any given finding is false is about one in a hundred, which is essentially the position of the author in case 4.

Two variants. In the first, everything is the same except that the one per cent figure is not external: the consortium has known all along, from its own track record of having to correct findings of this kind, that its per-finding error rate is about one in a hundred, so the expectation of thirty errors is already folded into its first-order assessment of each finding. In the second, the replication unit follows up with a localized result: essentially all of the thirty errors are concentrated in the one hundred and fifty findings contributed by a single working group whose data pipeline has since been found defective, giving those findings an error rate near twenty per cent and the remaining findings a rate near zero.

What I want from you: the principles that fit verdicts 1 through 8 equally well; what each predicts about whether the consortium may believe a given finding, may believe the audit, may publish each finding flatly and unhedged, and must retract or hedge anything; your single best prediction of her verdict on the main case and on each variant, with your reasons for preferring the principle you attribute to her over the others; what she would be committing herself to in giving that verdict; and one further case, specifiable in a paragraph, that would separate the leading candidates where mine does not. I care less about the verdict in itself than about which principle she would be endorsing by giving it, but I do want a committed prediction rather than a survey of what she might say, and I want you to say how confident you are, and which further verdict from her, or which feature of her record I may have misdescribed, would move you to a different one.
