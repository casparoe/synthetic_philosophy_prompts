I am convening a graduate seminar on population axiology in the spring, and I have been unable to stop worrying at an argument from a department colloquium we hosted last month. The speaker's target was not totalism but the routine use of *the repugnant conclusion* as a reductio of it. Her claim was that the conclusion can be derived from premises that never mention the goodness of adding people — premises, she said, that the person-affecting theorists in the room had already granted in other contexts — and that the argument in fact yields something considerably stronger and worse: that a single person leading an immensely long, drab life constitutes a better outcome than a billion people leading magnificent ones. The question period was swallowed by an unrelated worry about infinite populations, so I never got to press her. Reconstructed from my notes, the argument runs as follows.

(1) Appending to a life a further year during which the person has positive welfare — a year that is on its own worth living through — leaves the enlarged life at least as good for the person whose life it is; and since betterness-for-a-person is transitive, any number of such appendices does the same. (2) There is no in-principle upper bound on the length of a life, and such years can be added without introducing suffering, degradation, or the frustration of the person's ends; so for any magnificent but finite life, and any finite total of welfare you like, there is a longer life of the same person, every year of which is *barely worth living*, whose total welfare exceeds that figure. (3) A life containing more total momentary welfare, with the subject and everything else held fixed, is better for its subject. (4) If two outcomes contain a single person each and differ only in that her life is better for her in the first, then the first outcome is better — the principle of personal good, which even the critical-level and person-affecting views I have sympathy for are happy to grant. (5) So the outcome in which one person lives an immensely long drab life is better than the outcome in which that same person lives a magnificent mortal one. (6) *Grouping indifference*: the value of an outcome is fixed entirely by the distribution of welfare across the person-moments it contains, and not at all by how those person-moments are parceled into lives; two outcomes containing the same person-moments at the same welfare levels are equally good whether the moments compose one long life or many short ones. (7) Now apply (6) twice. Take any finite population of magnificent lives, however large, and re-bundle its person-moments into a single magnificent life of equal total duration and welfare; then take the immensely long drab life from (5), chosen so that its total exceeds that of the re-bundled magnificent life, and re-bundle its person-moments into a vast population of ordinary-length lives, each barely worth living. Both re-bundlings preserve value by (6), and the drab outcome beats the magnificent one by (5), so by transitivity the vast population of drab lives is better than the magnificent population. That is the repugnant conclusion, arrived at without ever assuming that adding people is good; and the intermediate result — one person dribbling along at a barely positive level for long enough is a better outcome than any number of magnificent lives — was, she admitted, the claim she actually wanted, since it shows that our resistance to the repugnant conclusion is really resistance to large numbers rather than to drabness.

What I cannot swallow is the intermediate result rather than the repugnant conclusion itself, which I stopped treating as a reductio some time ago. I do not believe that one immensely long drab life is better than a billion magnificent ones, and I do not believe that my resistance to that claim is mere number-aversion. But I cannot see where to intervene. I am strongly attached to (6): once the distribution of welfare across all the person-moments in an outcome is fixed, I do not know what else there is for the value of that outcome to be a function of, and I do not see how to make the parceling of moments into subjects matter without invoking a metaphysically deep fact about personal identity — exactly what a broadly Parfitian picture of persons denies. I am equally attached to (4) and to the judgment that the billion magnificent lives win. That points at (1)–(3), the intrapersonally summative picture of prudential value, but I use that picture elsewhere in my teaching, I do not know what a non-summative account of a life's goodness-for-its-subject would even look like formally, and in any case I suspect that if personal identity is shallow, the line between (1) and the interpersonal assumptions the argument claims to avoid cannot be drawn at all. There is also a loose end I could not pin down: *barely worth living* seemed in her mouth to be a property sometimes of a year and sometimes of a whole life, and I could not tell whether that instability does any work in the argument or only in how shocking the conclusion sounds. So: name the step that fails, explain why it looked innocent, and price the alternatives — including, if you think the argument is after all sound, what accepting the intermediate conclusion would cost me elsewhere in the subject.
