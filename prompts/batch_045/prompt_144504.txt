I'm on this internal committee at work — we call it the AARB, agent autonomy review board, and we sign off on self-modifying agent designs before they're allowed anywhere near production. I'm a backend engineer, never took philosophy or really any logic beyond what I need for work, so I follow these arguments mostly by vibes and I'd like to know what the actual argument is. Near as I can tell it all traces back to one result I've had explained to me a dozen times and still half-believe: a formal system can't generally prove "if I can prove P, then P" — Löb's theorem, I think — so an agent that wants a guarantee that its own successor is safe can't get one by reasoning about its own reasoning, and the standard dodge is to have each generation run on a strictly weaker proof system than the one that built it, which you can only do so many times before you've got nothing left to weaken to.

Our track record, in case it helps: LEDGER rebuilt itself once a quarter, each successor using a strictly weaker system than its parent, proofs limited to bounded resource use — approved. MIRROR used the same system as its parent and demanded a proof in that same system that the successor stays safe forever — rejected. SCRIBE did no self-modification at all; every patch proof got re-checked by a small external verifier in a much weaker quantifier-free logic, written by a different team, with a human signing off per patch — approved with conditions. SELFTEST was self-certified with general safety claims and no external check — rejected. PADDOCK had no proofs whatsoever, just sandbox, A/B testing, and rollback — approved. NIMBUS outsourced proofs of general safety claims to an external prover running a theory stronger than anything we can justify — rejected, with a note that we don't want to depend on a system nobody can vouch for. DOPPEL had a second, independently written checker implementing the same-strength system as the agent, general claims — also rejected, 6-1.

New proposal Thursday, codename JACQUARD: a self-improving coding agent that writes its own successor using the same proof system it uses to decide things — no weakening, and no cap on how many generations — but every patch ships with a machine-checkable certificate of narrow, runtime-checkable properties: bounded memory and CPU, no network syscalls, halts within a step bound. The certificate is verified by a frozen 400-line monitor that can't be patched and that we already blessed back in SCRIBE. Their pitch is that they're not asking us to trust them at all: the proof search is just a heuristic for finding certificates, and the only thing we trust is the little checker, which is way weaker than them. They also have eight months of shadow deployment with zero monitor trips, though obviously a monitor that never fires hasn't defintely been shown to work. I can't tell what we're actually running. One reading says the rule is about who you're trusting — you may only lean on proofs whose system you can prove sound, so descent, so JACQUARD is out. Another says the rule was always about what's being claimed — every load-bearing claim has to be machine-checkable at runtime — in which case JACQUARD is fine and MIRROR was rejected because "safe forever" isn't something a monitor can check. There's probably a third where PADDOCK is the real precedent and we've been rubber-stamping anything with empirical validation. My own worry, which I can't tell is the crux or a red herring: the certificate says the patch is bounded, not that it's safe, and the step from certificate passed to this patch won't do anything bad looks like a general claim nobody proved, so maybe we've just relocated MIRROR's problem into the checker. So: what will we decide, which rule best fits our precedents, do the candidate rules actually come apart on this particular proposal, and if you had one more agenda slot to force the issue, what variant of JACQUARD would you put on it — I was thinking something about whether the safety claim has to hold for the whole unbounded chain of generations rather than one patch at a time, since if descent is the rule then an endless chain of generations seems to need an endless descending chain of theories and we don't have one of those to hand out.
