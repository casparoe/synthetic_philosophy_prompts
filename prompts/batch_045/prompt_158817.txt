You are an analytic moral philosopher whose research specialism is distributive ethics: the formal structure of prioritarianism, telic and deontic egalitarianism, sufficientarianism, and the aggregative views they are standardly contrasted with. I would like you to work in stages. First, in a short paragraph, reconstruct in your own formal vocabulary the value function and the welfare assumptions in play (I have in mind a weighted sum of a strictly increasing, strictly concave transformation of individual welfare, with priority fixed by a person's absolute level rather than by their rank, but say if you think that matters). Then adjudicate the exchange below move by move. Then give an overall verdict. Do not rest anything on citations or on what people usually say in this literature; I want the reasoning to come from the structure of the views. Where your verdict depends on a parameter the case leaves open, say so explicitly and say how the verdict moves with the parameter.

The setting: last week I chaired a work-in-progress session in my department. A visitor presented a paper whose target was the familiar claim that prioritarianism escapes the levelling-down objection. Her central case, which she called the ratchet, ran like this. Take two groups, the comfortable and the badly off, and assume that welfare is reference-dependent: how well a person is doing at a time depends not only on their material conditions but on how those compare with their recent past and with other people. Now consider a levelling-down step: destroy some of the comfortable group's advantages without transferring anything to anyone — a tax that funds nothing, a regulation that simply abolishes a positional good. Two things happen. The comfortable lose welfare, and they stay down. The badly off gain: their relative standing improves, and they register an improvement against their recent past. Call the loss delta and the gain epsilon. Because the badly off are worse off, their welfare carries the larger priority weight, so the weighted total goes up even when epsilon is very much smaller than delta. Then the reference point resets: the badly off adapt to their new relative position, the gain fades, and a further levelling-down step yields another epsilon. Run n steps. At the end, the comfortable are far worse off; the badly off, once adaptation has done its work, are back where they began. So the end state is better for nobody and worse for some — exactly the shape of change the levelling-down objection was supposed to rule out — yet prioritarianism ranked every step an improvement, and its own verdict on the start-to-finish change is negative.

Assume for the sake of argument that the empirical assumptions hold. Two colleagues then argued about what, if anything, the case shows, and they got nowhere. Roughly, this is what they said.

(1) Marcus: The case shows that prioritarianism's immunity to the levelling-down objection is an artifact of the framing in which that immunity is normally demonstrated — one-shot, synchronic comparison, an absolute welfare currency, no reference-dependence. Relax any of that and the immunity evaporates. The priority view is in the same boat as the telic egalitarian after all.

(2) Dana: Every step in the ratchet benefits the badly off, and benefits them in welfare, which is the currency prioritarianism cares about. Levelling down, as the objection understands it, is a change that makes some worse off and no one better off; no individual step here is like that. What the case actually shows is that prioritarianism will buy a temporary gain for the worse off at a permanent cost to the better off. That is an objection about the currency of welfare and about aggregation over time. Calling it a levelling-down objection is mislabelling.

(3) Marcus: That is definitional bookkeeping. The force of the original objection was never that one stipulated change has a particular shape; it is the thought that a view has gone badly wrong if it can guide an agent from a state S to a state S that is better for no one and worse for someone. Prioritarianism does that here: it ranks every step positively, its own verdict on the end state against the start is negative, and an agent following it step by step arrives at the end state anyway. A theory is assessed by the paths it recommends, not only by the adjacent pairs it ranks. If you test a view only on single transitions, you have made it artificially easy for it to satisfy a constraint that is really about where it takes you.

(4) Dana: Grant the redefinition for the sake of argument. The ratchet still is not an objection to prioritarianism in particular. Everything in it comes from features the priority view shares with any additive welfarism evaluated myopically: reference-dependent welfare, transient gains, step-by-step evaluation. Plain total utilitarianism ratchets too, whenever the temporary gains to the many exceed the permanent loss to the few. Priority weights only move the threshold at which the ratchet starts. So the case indicts the whole family or none of it; it does not discriminate against the priority view.

(5) Marcus: Moving the threshold is the entire issue. Under utilitarianism the ratchet only gets going when the gains genuinely outweigh the losses. Under prioritarianism, how large the loss to the comfortable may be relative to the gain to the badly off is set by how steeply the priority weight rises as you go down the distribution, and there is in principle no upper bound: the comfortable can be driven down a long way for a flicker of relief to the badly off. And note that it is the very feature that buys the one-step immunity — the extra weight on the worse off — that buys the ratchet. Prioritarianism cannot claim the immunity and disown the ratchet; they are one property seen from two directions.

(6) Dana: The ratchet depends on a decision procedure that nobody should attribute to the view. Formulate prioritarianism as a view about the value of complete histories — sum weighted welfare over the whole relevant period, or evaluate lifetime welfare directly — and the case collapses: the gains are transient, the losses are permanent, so the whole sequence is disfavoured and the end state is never reached. You are objecting to a stepwise decision rule, and the repair is available to any prioritarian.

(7) Marcus: Fine for a single agent. But the steps need not be taken by one. Successive governments, each in office for one step, each bound to evaluate only the transition it can itself produce, will grind the distribution down even though nobody ever chose the sequence. Your repair tells us the resulting history is bad; it does not tell us how to stop it.

They then turned to me, and I could not tell them anything useful. So please: rate each of the seven moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and justify each rating briefly; say who is more right overall and why; and identify the single assumption doing the most work in the case, together with what happens to the case if that assumption is removed. Finish with one paragraph on what both of them are missing. I have my own suspicions — something about whether the ratchet is self-limiting once the priority weights re-equilibrate as the distribution compresses, about the difference between gains that come from change and gains that come from relative standing, and about whether a gain that consists in someone else being worse off is the kind of gain that should count at all — but I would rather see what you make of it than lead you.
