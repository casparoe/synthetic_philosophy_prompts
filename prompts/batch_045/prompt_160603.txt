I teach an upper-level value theory seminar — 16 students, one 80-minute session, and I'd rather they not have read anything on this beforehand. I want a runnable in-class activity that makes them feel the force of a spectrum argument against the transitivity of better-than, by having them build the chain themselves and only discover where it has taken them at the end.

The device I have in mind: about twenty index cards, each describing a possible life scored on three goods (pleasure, achievement, friendship — or whatever makes the trades vivid for 20-year-olds), arranged in a closed loop. Each adjacent pair differs by a single small trade: the next card gains a noticeably larger amount of one good and gives up a slightly smaller amount of another, with the trades cycling so that after twenty steps every good is back where it started, or lower. Students work in pairs, see only the pair in front of them, never the loop or the endpoints, and vote under a forced-choice rule: for each pair, which card is better? No ties permitted in the first round. Half the room starts at card 1 and half at card 11, so the loop is traversed from both sides. Only at the end do we tape the ballots into a chain on the board and let them read out what they have collectively endorsed.

Give me the actual numbers for the cards and the gain/loss scheme that makes every single step look like a clear improvement while the whole trip clearly is not; the ballot sheets; a minute-by-minute timeline for 80 minutes including the reveal; and rules that stop a sharp student from inferring the loop early and going meta instead of judging. Tell me too what to do when a pair insists on ties, and when someone proposes a lexical ordering of the goods that short-circuits the chain.

Then the debrief, mapped onto the activity: each link looks impeccable, the chain says the first card is better than itself, so something must give — deny a link, deny transitivity, or deny that the forced choice was legitimate (parity, incomparability, vagueness with a cutoff nobody can locate). Show me where the scattered individual cutoffs will show up in the ballots and how to read them aloud. Be explicit that this is a cycle in betterness, not the self-torturer style of cycle where each step is merely rationally permissible, and say why that difference matters. Close with an honest note on what the activity cannot stage and what the facilitator has to say in so many words: the gap between not better as parity and not better as incomparability, and what a cyclic better-than would do to choice.
