Hey — I need a hand with a memo and I'm definately a bit out of my depth on the technical bits.

I'm on the Architecture Review Board at the lab where I work. I'm the token non-ML person: I did some philosophical logic in grad school about a hundred years ago and then drifted into research ops, so I get handed anything that smells like a proof. We have to circulate a written recommendation in about five weeks on a proposed change to how our main agent system picks actions.

Setup. The planner is "proof-gated": it can only execute an action if its built-in verifier V hands over a machine-checkable proof that the action stays under a risk threshold. The team now wants V to be able to reason about its own proofs — to trust its own soundness, basically. Two reasons given: the planner needs to simulate its own future proof search in order to plan at all, and when the system rewrites parts of itself, each version has to be able to inherit the previous version's verdicts, or capability falls off a cliff at every generation. As I understand it this is roughly the thing people call the Löbian obstacle: Löb's theorem says that if a system T can prove "if T proves φ then φ", then T just proves φ, for arbitrary φ. So a reflection rule — from a proof that φ is provable, conclude φ — if it's available for every sentence, makes the verifier prove everything, and the gate ends up certifying whatever the planner likes. Everyone on the board agrees on that much.

Where we're split is over the workarounds.

(B) is the team's actual proposal: typed or tiered reflection. V_{n+1} may reflect on V_n's proofs and never on its own; the stack is fixed at eight levels, and each self-rewrite has to move down a level, so you run out of headroom after eight rounds and have to stop or restart. My worry here is a note I got from a friend saying the standard derivation of Löb's theorem only needs the reflection rule nested about two deep — one use inside a conditional proof, one at the top — in which case a cap of eight buys nothing and even a cap of two might not. I can't reconstruct the derivation myself, so I don't know if that's right.

(C) is the fallback someone floated: keep reflection, but quarantine self-reference. Safety claims get written in an object language that only quantifies over the next H steps of the world and has no way to name V, the agent's own source code, or provability-in-V, so the fixed point sentence the theorem needs can't be stated. My worry here is different: the agent's world model contains physical machines running something isomorphic to V, and you can describe those without naming them. If the object language has enough arithmetic to describe such a machine, it seems like Gödel numbering walks back in through the world model rather than through the syntax. Maybe that's confused.

(D) is the boring option: no self-trust at all, an external kernel too weak to talk about itself. The team says this is what they tried first and that it costs them the whole capability they need.

Then there's the person on the board who says the entire apparatus is decorative anyway, because a proof of safety is relative to a world model whose accuracy we can't establish — so the real weak point is the axioms, not the reflection rule, and we're polishing the lock on a door made of paper.

It has to be followable by both the ML people and the two people who actually know this area.
