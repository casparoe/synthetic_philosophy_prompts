I've been writing up a piece on consent for a reading group and I keep finding that my gut verdicts on cases don't line up with any one thing I can state. I'm hoping you can help me find the principle — or tell me which verdicts I have to give up. Roughly my tentative idea is: a yes counts as consent when (a) the person could actually have said no without landing in something someone else was responsible for, (b) nobody lied to them about what the act *was* — what it does to them, who's doing it, what the deal is — as opposed to lying about incidental stuff, and (c) the yes really came from them and wasn't just their mouth moving. But I don't trust it. Here's what I actually think, case by case.

Deception first. Guy says he's single, isn't: feels gross, but I don't think it voids consent. Someone pretends to be a clinician to perform an exam: obviously void, consent to something else entirely. Someone who's HIV-positive and says they aren't: void. Condom comes off mid-act without her knowing: void, and it feels *worse* than the HIV lie somehow, like a different kind of failure rather than just more of it. Someone says "I love you" to get a yes and doesn't mean it: my gut says valid, which bugs me. A researcher tells me the trial is placebo-controlled when actually I'm guaranteed the active drug — so the lie made me *less* likely to say yes, and the truth would have made me keener: my gut still says void. And here's the one I can't square with that: someone sleeps with a person who, as it happens, belongs to a group they'd have refused on straightforwardly racist grounds, and the person knows this and stays quiet. My gut says the consent is fine. But both are lies (or omissions) about a peripheral fact that changed what was chosen, so any pure but-for test has to give the same answer on both, and I don't want the same answer.

Coercion. Sleep with me or I fire you, where the firing isn't otherwise justified: not consent. Sleep with me and I'll promote you, where the promotion isn't owed and he's the strongest candidate anyway: I want to say it's slimy but it's consent, and I'm not confident. Lifeguard who'll only throw the ring if you sign over everything you own: not consent. A prison guard offers a prisoner extra food — more than the rules require — for sex: my gut says coerced, even though nothing was taken away. Same guard instead says "sleep with me or I'll put you in the hole," where the hole isn't justified: obviously coerced. A third party says "sleep with him or I leak your photos," and the guy she sleeps with knows nothing about it: I think her yes doesn't count, but I can't say whether that's about her options or about his knowledge. And a driver who happens upon you stranded in the desert and offers a ride only on condition of sex: I lean valid if he didn't put you there, invalid if he did.

Competence. Blackout drunk: no. Tipsy but coherent: yes. Drunk, coherent, says yes, and would never have said yes sober and hates it the next morning: I lean yes, and I'm uneasy about how confident I am. A woman with dementia who years earlier wrote that if she later initiates sexual contact with her husband and seems content, that should count as her consent even when she can't pass a capacity test — and now she does exactly that: my gut says yes, valid. But the same document saying "go ahead even if I seem to refuse": no way. Surgeon describes the operation as "90% survival" and I consent, where "10% mortality" would have gotten a no: my gut says valid, and valid even if he picked the frame knowing it would work, which feels like it should matter and doesn't.

Two more I go back and forth on and haven't decided: a friend who said yes mainly because she was scared he'd turn violent, where he said nothing threatening and never would have been — coerced or not? And the yes of someone who thinks, wrongly, that they have no legal right to refuse, and only says yes because refusal seems pointless: my gut says that one is void, which sits badly with my drunk verdict, since both are yeses from a compromised agent.

So: is there one principle — mine, repaired, or a different one — that gets most of this? I'd like it stated clause by clause with the case that motivates each clause, and I want it to actually say something about cases I haven't listed, especially: is consent obtained through a deliberately annoying app interface (the kind where saying no takes eleven clicks) consent? What about a surgeon who, once you're under, switches to a different technique that's equally good and never mentions it? And does whatever principle you land on have any implications for the friend case and the "no legal right to refuse" case, or does it just punt on them? Please be honest about which of my verdicts your principle can't accommodate — I'd rather drop two verdicts than keep a principle that's really four principles in a trenchcoat.
