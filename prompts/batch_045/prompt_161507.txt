Our university has a standing panel that rules on which preparations count as subjects of moral consideration and how much weight they get. Its verdicts so far: an adult pig, substantial; an octopus, substantial; a lobster, moderate — it may be killed for food but must not be boiled alive, and its welfare must be weighed; an individual honeybee, minimal but not zero, so a bee's welfare counts for something in the ledger; an intact honeybee colony, nothing at all, even though the colony solves problems no individual bee can solve and tracks the environment over months; a human cortical organoid with no sensory input and no motor output, nothing; a current large language model, nothing, with a note to revisit in five years; a learning thermostat, nothing.

I run a small lab on insect learning, and next month my postdoc's project comes before this panel — the outcome decides whether the work continues. We have been replacing regions of a honeybee's brain a few hundred neurons at a time with a neuromorphic implant that reproduces each region's measured input-output function. The current preparation is roughly 60 percent implant by cell count. It still learns an odor-shock association, still gives the sting-extension response to the trained odor, and still returns to a feeder it has learned, all of it stable over days. One preliminary result: its learned aversions now persist noticeably longer than an intact bee's, because the implant's memory does not decay the way the tissue did.
