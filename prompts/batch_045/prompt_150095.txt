After forty years in experimental geophysics I have started attending a WEA evening class in political philosophy, and I find the subject frustrating in a way my own never was: in the laboratory one decided which quantity was being measured, agreed on how to measure it, and then argued about the number. Our tutor, Dr. Ferreira, has spent three sessions on the question of what exactly it is that should be equalised. She keeps three candidate metrics on the board — roughly, welfare, resources, and capabilities, the last understood as being about what people are actually able to do and be rather than about what they hold or about how well off they feel — and she has remarked more than once that in her view the three come apart far less often than the literature suggests. She has also been unusually willing to commit herself to a verdict on any case we put to her. Her record so far: (i) Ada, paraplegic since a car crash at nineteen that was not her fault, needing a chair, an adapted kitchen and hand controls, gets more than the standard share, on the grounds that she should be placed where getting about and looking after herself cost her no more than they cost anyone else; (ii) Bernard, who after the initial division deliberately set out to acquire a taste for vintage claret and is now unhappy with anything cheaper, gets nothing extra; (iii) Cora, born with a rare metabolic disorder that means she needs roughly twice the calories of an average person and a diet costing several times as much in order to stay nourished, gets extra, and was called the clearest case on the list; (iv) Dmitri, who spent his share on a cello and lessons and now cannot afford to heat his flat, gets nothing, because he had his share and chose what to do with it; (v) Ewan, who has a mild, untreatable condition causing constant low-grade pain but who is perfectly contented and whose health and abilities are otherwise entirely ordinary, gets nothing — a verdict that surprised me, since the brute-luck reading I had inferred from (i) and (iii) seemed to point the other way, and I should like to know what rule survives it.

The case I want to put to her next is one I have constructed myself. Farah has no impairment, ordinary talents and ordinary health, and was raised from birth in a small religious minority, a few hundred households in the town. Observance requires her to eat a diet that locally costs about three times an ordinary food budget, because nothing suitable is stocked nearby and certified supplies have to be brought in, and to keep a ten-day annual observance that costs her two weeks of unpaid leave plus the fare to a distant community; the total comes to something between a tenth and a fifth of an ordinary lifetime share. Two features matter to me. First, with a plain equal share she would be as healthy, as mobile, as well educated and as socially included as anyone else, so the extra money buys her no additional capability in any of those respects; what it buys, in her own words, is being able to be the person she actually is, and she describes the commitment as one she did not choose while conceding that people do leave and that she has stayed. Second, there is a much cheaper way of removing most of the cost: the shops could stock the foods and her employer could accommodate the ten days at almost no expense to anyone, in which case nobody would need a larger share at all; and because the minority is small, the cost of compensating every member in cash would be a noticeable slice of the common budget. So the question comes in three possible shapes: a larger share for Farah, a change in how the town and the employer arrange things, or nothing at all.

What I would like from you is a prediction of what Dr. Ferreira would say about Farah, and of what the most precedent-consistent answer would be should the two diverge — though I am more interested in the apparatus than in the verdict: the rival rules that fit her five verdicts, what each predicts about Farah, which fits best and why, which of her existing verdicts strains which rule, and what further case I could put to her that would best separate the surviving candidates. If the honest answer is that the five verdicts determine no rule at all, say so and tell me which verdict I should press her to reconsider in order to make them determine one. Please also separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on these questions, and do not present a contested view as though it were the received one; and where your prediction rests on a guess about how she would resolve some contest, flag that rather than smoothing it over. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and give the reasoning afterwards, with no preamble and no restatement of what I have asked. British English throughout, please.
