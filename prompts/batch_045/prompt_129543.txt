I am working through the animal-machine discussion in the fifth part of Descartes's *Discourse on Method*, and I have paraphrased the central passage as follows:

Passage (paraphrased): Descartes says that if there were machines having the organs and outward shape of an ape or of some other animal without reason, we would have no way of recognizing that they were not of the same nature as those animals. By contrast, if there were machines that resembled our bodies and imitated our actions as far as it is morally possible, we would still have two very certain tests for recognizing that they were not true human beings. The first test is that such machines could never use words or other signs by putting them together as we do in order to declare thoughts to others. Even if a machine or an animal could utter words, it could not arrange them in various ways so as to reply appropriately to everything said in its presence, which even the dullest human being can do. The second test is that, although such machines might do many things as well as or better than we do, they would inevitably fail in other things, and this would reveal that they act not through knowledge but only through the disposition of their organs. The reason is that reason is a universal instrument that can serve in all kinds of encounters, whereas a machine needs a particular contrivance for each particular action; hence it is morally impossible for a machine to contain enough distinct contrivances to act as our reason enables us to act in all the contingencies of life.
