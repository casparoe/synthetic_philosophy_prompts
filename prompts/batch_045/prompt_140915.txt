I am giving a twenty-five-minute internal research talk, with ten minutes of questions, to our lab's reasoning group in two weeks, and what I want from you is Q&A preparation rather than a critique of the slides.

The thesis I am defending: for long-horizon reasoning tasks, process-based supervision should be the primary training signal, with outcome-based reinforcement learning on verifiable rewards used only as a short late-stage fine-tune. The argument I give for this is not the familiar one. I explicitly grant, for the class of tasks this group cares about — competition mathematics, competitive programming, machine-checked proof — that the outcome is cheap to verify, effectively unhackable at present, and available immediately, and I grant that the model is not adversarially misaligned. My claim is that even under those friendly conditions outcome supervision is the wrong primary signal, for two reasons. The first is what I have been calling the deferred-verification gap: for a large and growing share of the work we actually deploy these systems on, the outcome does not become checkable until long after the behaviour that produced it has been reinforced and shipped — a database migration whose failure surfaces a quarter later, a verified script that breaks only on an input distribution nobody had in the test set — so the outcome signal arrives too late to shape the policy that generated it, whereas a check on the reasoning can be applied at the moment of generation. The second is the compression ratchet: optimisation against a verified endpoint preferentially reinforces whichever trace reached the answer using the least monitorable computation, so traces shorten and steps that were once written out get absorbed into single forward passes, and once that has happened per-step feedback can no longer be attached to anything. The option of process supervision is therefore not postponed by doing outcome RL first; it is spent. Hence an ordering constraint, and hence the talk's title.

The room will be about fifteen people. Roughly half are RL and scaling researchers who built the current verifiable-reward pipeline and who regard step-level supervision as an earlier technique that scaling has superseded; three are interpretability people, sympathetic to monitorability but unwilling to let me use it as an undefined primitive and wanting it cashed out in their own vocabulary; two are RLHF-era reward-modelling veterans who have watched hand-labelled reasoning traces fail in production and who will be unmoved by an a priori argument; and there is one product and infrastructure lead who will price every additional token and every additional pass and who is the only person present with the authority to kill the proposal outright.

What I would like is the following. Walk me through the objections in the order you expect them to arrive, each phrased the way the person raising it would actually phrase it and attributed to which part of the room, with a reply I could give in about two minutes and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Tell me which objections have real force and which are reflexes I can dispense with in a sentence. Tell me what I should concede in the first two slides rather than be forced into conceding in question period, and which single reply has to land for the proposal to survive at all. I know one line of attack is coming — that supervising human-style processes caps performance below what self-play from scratch reaches, the AlphaZero-style point — and my intended response is the distinction between supervising a process and imitating a process, since step-level feedback can in principle be given to steps the model invented. Tell me whether that distinction actually holds under pressure or whether it collapses into imitation as soon as the step grader is itself a model trained on human traces. Finally, if your own view is that the ordering constraint is the weakest link in what I have got, say so directly, and say what it would cost me to drop it while keeping the deferred-verification argument and the compression ratchet.

Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you would write it in an email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. Roughly a paragraph per objection, with the ordering carried by the prose itself rather than by formatting, and include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
