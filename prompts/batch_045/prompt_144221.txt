so I've been trying to work out what I actually think about assigning probabilities to mathematical claims, and I want the principle behind my own numbers rather than a tour of the literature. I know the general shape of the problem — bounded agents, no coherent distribution over the logically decidable, all that — I've read the blog posts, don't summarise them back at me. What I want is: here are my verdicts on a bunch of concrete cases, is there one account they fit, and which ones does it make me give up.

Some setup on where the numbers come from. My research group keeps what we jokingly call the ledger: before anyone sends out a result, we write down a number for how confident we are that it's right, and we've been doing this for about six years, roughly 400 entries. Two things came out of it that I can't stop thinking about. Entries at 0.95 and above were wrong about 3% of the time. Entries in the 0.6 to 0.7 band were wrong around 40% of the time. And the errors aren't spread out — eleven of the fourteen total errors happened in two years when all of us were pushing the same technique, which makes me suspect the mistakes were in something we shared rather than in anybodies individual judgement.

My cases:

1. Whether the ten-thousandth digit of pi after the decimal point is a 7. I've got 1/10 and I feel completely fine about it, including the fact that I could settle it in five seconds and am not going to. My friend says this shows the 1/10 isn't a credence at all, just a shrug wearing a number. I say it's exactly a credence.

2. Goldbach. I'm at about 0.99, and I genuinely cannot tell whether the computer verification out to those absurd bounds is doing any of the work or whether it's all coming from the heuristic arguments. It feels like it should count — someone searched an enormous space and found nothing — but it also feels like the kind of evidence that couldn't possibly matter for a claim about all even numbers.

3. Riemann. Also high, but the fact that the first trillion zeros sit on the line moves me essentially not at all, which by my own lights in case 2 is inconsistent. Something seperates these and I don't know what.

4. P is not NP. I moved from about 0.7 to about 0.8 after reading a survey that killed off a whole family of proof strategies. Nothing was verified; a region of technique-space was just emptied out. I think that's real evidence and it's nothing like the evidence in 2 or 3.

5. A big famous theorem whose proof is over a hundred pages and which I have read none of, but which the community has chewed on for decades. I'm at 0.999 and I'd bet that way.

6. A three-line argument I came up with myself last night that feels airtight. My gut says 0.97. The ledger says I should say 0.92. Saying 0.92 feels like false modesty and saying 0.97 feels like ignoring data I collected on purpose. I don't know which of these is the honest one.

7. A theorem that's been checked in a proof assistant. I'm more confident in it than in the same theorem after ordinary refereeing — call it 0.9999 versus 0.99. But when I think about it, the formal check only tells me the formalised statement follows; what I care about is whether the formalisation says what the English says, and there I'm at maybe 0.99. So it looks like formalisation just relocates the uncertainty and my overall confidence shouldn't really have risen. Am I kidding myself, or is something being gained?

8. Consistency of the set theory or arithmetic I work in every day. I'm at something like 0.99 and I think that's the right attitude, but I keep being told that this is incoherent, because if it's false then every other number I have collapses and my whole practice is worthless. That argument feels like a trick and I can't say why.

9. The one that bothers me most. I'm as confident as I ever get in T, and as confident as I ever get that T implies C, and when I put those together my credence in C does not move, and I think that's right. If I said it jumped I'd be pretending to feel something I don't feel. But I'm also pretty sure that if I learned C is equivalent to some other claim I'm already at 0.99 on, my credence in C should go up, and it does feel like it goes up, which is the same move in the other direction. I can't have it both ways and I don't know which way to fall.

The only principle I've managed to articulate for myself is this, which I've been calling the searchlight rule: my confidence in a claim is basically how much of the space of possible counterexamples-and-refutations I've effectively swept, given the resources actually spent, discounted by how error-prone the sweeping is. It handles 2 and 3 and 4 quite nicely, I think. It does something silly on case 1, where the space I've swept is empty and it wants to give me no number at all rather than 1/10, which is exactly the case I feel most comfortable about. So it needs repairing or replacing.

One of my colleagues, call her Vera, has given up on single numbers entirely. She keeps two: how hard she expects it to be to settle the claim, and what odds she'd actually bet at. She claims the two come apart in precisely the cases where I get stuck, and she is better calibrated than me on the ledger. I don't want to just adopt her scheme because it's convenient.

What I'd like: state the principle, or the two or three serious candidates, tightly enough that I could go apply it tomorrow. For each clause say which of my cases is doing the work. Then give me its verdicts on cases I haven't listed — a 200-page proof that twelve people have read closely with no error found but which has never been formalised; and a claim I'd put at 0.5 today where I know a journal I trust publishes the answer tomorrow. And be straight about which of my verdicts no decent principle can save, because I'd rather drop a number than keep a fake one.
