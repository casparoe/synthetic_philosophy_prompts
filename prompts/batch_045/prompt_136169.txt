I am second-reading a take-home exam for an undergraduate seminar on strategic reasoning, and I would like an independent reader on one answer before I finalise marks.

The question I set was this: Nuclear deterrence appears to rest on a paradox. A deterrent threat works only if the adversary believes you would carry it out, yet carrying out an all-out retaliatory strike after your own society has already been destroyed would be self-destructive and could not undo the damage, so a fully rational adversary should treat the threat as incredible. Set out the paradox and assess three responses to it: (i) make retaliation automatic, by a device that removes the decision altogether; (ii) rely on threats that leave something to chance, arranging matters so that escalation is possible but not under either side's control; (iii) adopt existential deterrence, the view that no plan or threat needs to be credible at all because the bare existence of the weapons and the irreducible uncertainty about what would happen if they were used do the deterring. Say which, if any, actually dissolves the paradox, and what each response costs.

One answer reads as follows.

Deterrence is a problem about credibility, not capability. It deters by threatening punishment, so it is only as good as the adversary's belief that the punishment would actually be inflicted. If retaliation would be pointless, because the cities are already gone, then the threat is a threat to do something no rational agent would do once the moment arrived, and the adversary can see that too. The brinkmanship literature's solution, in Schelling, is the threat that leaves something to chance: rather than threaten retaliation outright, you commit in advance to a probability of retaliation, binding yourself to a lottery you cannot rerun, and this is credible precisely because you no longer decide. One could imagine announcing that a first strike carries a thirty percent chance of retaliation, and the figure does not have to be high; what matters is that it is no longer up to you. The automatic device is just the limiting case where the probability is one, and existential deterrence is the strongest version of the same mechanism, since it removes the human decision entirely and leaves only the weapons and the uncertainty. On this reading the paradox dissolves, and we can tell that it does: the superpowers never fought a direct war, which shows the threats were believed despite their irrationality. Rationality of retaliation turns out to be irrelevant to deterrence, because the threat that deters is the one you cannot help carrying out, or the one nobody ever has to make.

I am especially interested in anything that reads smoothly enough that I might be tempted to pass over it.
