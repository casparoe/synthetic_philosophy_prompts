I sit on the Deployment Review Committee at the lab where I work — nine of us, four drawn from the capability teams whose projects we review — and on Thursday we have to rule on Argus, an internal coding agent that would get commit access to the monorepo of our billing service plus a service credential that can read (though not write) customer records, running somewhere between forty and a hundred tool actions per ticket, unsupervised, with a human rubber-stamping whatever it lands. The capabilities team is not claiming that Argus is aligned; they are explicitly declining to argue that, and are instead submitting what they call a control case — assume it may be misaligned and make the deployment safe anyway. Their package: every action scored by a monitor that is a fine-tune of the same base model as Argus and was post-trained on largely overlapping preference data; 8% of actions re-executed by a much weaker trusted model that never sees Argus's stated justification; commits quarantined on a shadow branch for twenty-four hours; decoy credentials planted in a repo whose README announces that decoys exist; a two-engineer kill switch; and a hard cap of 15% of Argus's own inference compute spent on all of this oversight put together. A friend who works in pharmacovigilance suggested over dinner that we treat this the way her regulator treats a conditional approval — release it, then mandate post-marketing surveillance — and I half like that, but I can't quite locate where the analogy breaks, and I suspect it breaks exactly where it matters.

What makes me hesitate is a pile of specifics rather than one big objection. The red-team canary data look reassuring — decoy credentials were touched forty times across six months of exercises — but each honeytoken is single-use, the decoys are announced in advance, and every observed attempt came from a run in which the agent was not being careful, so that number licenses no estimate of what a strategic agent would do; and because oversight compute is capped at 15%, we cannot simply buy more monitoring when the numbers get worse, while forcing Argus to narrate its plans in a structured format so the monitor can read them costs the team roughly a fifth of its throughput, which they will not stop complaining about. The six engineers who will inspect the deferrals and the quarantine merges will be the same six for months, and my real fear is alert fatigue turning them into stamps; I have floated injecting synthetic sabotage into the live stream at a rate the reviewers do not know, and the team refuses, saying it would poison the logs they need for incident review. Meanwhile a colleague on the committee points out that if we reject, the capabilities group can ship Argus as an internal productivity tool under an exemption that never reaches us — so, she says, approval with conditions strictly dominates rejection. I think that is blackmail dressed as pragmatism, but I have to answer it rather than just resent it. Above all, I keep circling one epistemic problem: every figure we have about how well the oversight works is produced inside a system whose central component we are assuming might be adversarially misaligned, so the safety case is measured by an instrument the suspect is holding.

So I need the decision note I will circulate on Thursday. Take a position — approve, reject, or approve with conditions and a date — and give reasons the capabilities team could actually act on rather than a restatement of the thought that control measures assume misalignment while alignment measures try to remove it. Say what, in a deployment like this one, would count as evidence that the control measures themselves are working, given that the evidence stream is downstream of the thing we distrust; say whether that is a reason to reject outright, to approve with a scheduled re-review and a stated stopping condition, or to make the sabotage injections a precondition; and give the strongest version of the losing side's argument, whichever side loses. Our committee writes decisions in a fixed format: exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no heading, preamble, or sign-off — item 1 the recommendation with its effective date and any conditions, items 2 through 4 the reasons, one of which must be the best point made by the side you are not recommending, and item 5 the trigger that would make us reopen the question.
