I'm a senior in high school finishing an extended essay on the ethics of humanitarian aid, and my debate partner and I keep going round in circles about Live Aid — the two-venue concert Bob Geldof and Midge Ure organised for July 1985, at Wembley and JFK, following Band Aid's single the previous Christmas, to raise money for the Ethiopian famine of the mid-1980s, which as I understand it was entangled with the Derg's counter-insurgency and resettlement policies rather than being a pure drought. Her line is that this is just Singer's shallow pond played out at scale: same principle, more people, and the fact that millions bought tickets instead of wading in only shows that they finally saw the argument. I think the analogy is doing a lot of quiet work, and I would like someone to take it apart carefully in both directions.

What I want first is an actual mapping rather than a restatement. Who occupies the role of the passer-by, and what in the episode plays the role of the muddy clothes — the ticket price, the donation, the attention, something else? What does the principle's exemption for sacrificing nothing of comparable moral importance actually bite on here, and does the fact that the beneficiaries are thousands rather than one child, and unseen rather than in front of you, alter the duty itself or only our feelings about it? Then the three things I keep circling. The first is a distinction of my own, which may simply be muddled: I want to split rescue duties, which are one-off, discrete, near-certain to succeed, and finished when the person is standing on dry ground, from upkeep duties, which are open-ended, run through institutions, and never obviously complete — and my question is whether the principle generates the second kind at all, or whether it only ever yields a long series of rescue obligations, with Live Aid better understood as an attempt to build an institution for discharging them. The second is overdetermination: in the pond there is one bystander and the child dies if he walks on, so his act is the difference, whereas at a telethon with millions of donors and governments already shipping grain it is far from clear what my own contribution caused, and I do not know whether a duty of rescue has anything to say when the outcome would have happened anyway. The third is harm transmitted downstream: suppose, purely hypothetically, since I gather the versions of this involving diverted money are disputed, that some of the relief was captured by the government whose policies helped produce the famine, or that free grain pushed down prices for farmers who were not themselves starving — does that get counted under the comparable-importance exemption, or is it the sort of effect the principle has no vocabulary for at all? I would also like your view on the awkward fact that the entire mechanism ran on film of identifiable starving children and famous performers on a stage, which is exactly the vividness and identifiability that the principle declares morally irrelevant, and apparently the reason anybody gave.

Please don't spend the answer reciting the principle and then the standard demandingness objection; I have read both, and I only want them if the case drives you back to them. Be explicit about where the theory is silent instead of forcing it to return a verdict, tell me if my rescue-versus-upkeep split is confused or just reinvents something people already dispute, and give me the other half of the analysis as well: does Live Aid vindicate the argument, sit as an anomaly for it, or show that it was built for ponds and never anticipated anything of this shape? And please finish by saying how confident you are in that overall verdict, and by naming the single claim in your answer you are least certain of, along with why that is the weak point.
