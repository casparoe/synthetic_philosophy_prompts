In a fortnight I am giving a departmental seminar paper in which I defend question-relative verisimilitude. The thesis is that closeness to the truth is not a two-place relation between theories but a three-place one: T is more verisimilar than T* relative to a question Q, where Q is a finite set of yes/no questions, and where the distance between two possible worlds is read off Q rather than assumed, the Q-distance between worlds being the number of questions in Q that they answer differently, weighted by a cognitive-stake weighting over Q fixed by the inquiry. The verisimilitude of T relative to Q is then a function of the weighted Q-distance from the actual world to the nearest world at which T is true. Two supporting claims: first, the dependence on a chosen metric that dogs the likeness approach associated with Tichý, Oddie and Niiniluoto is dissolved rather than solved, because the metric is no longer a free parameter but is inherited from the question; second, the Miller–Tichý result that Popper's content-based definition makes no false theory closer to the truth than any other false theory should be read not as a refutation of the content approach but as a diagnosis, namely that question-free comparisons of truthlikeness are ill-formed. A corollary I accept: when the questions themselves change, verdicts of comparative progress across epochs are unavailable, and I argue that this is a virtue rather than a cost.

The audience is roughly this: one formal epistemologist who works on accuracy and scoring rules and who regards talk of truthlikeness as a poor substitute for expected inaccuracy; one Popperian historian of science who wants to know only whether the notion does any work for scientific realism; one logician who works on AGM belief revision and Spohn-style ranking functions; a scattering of graduate students; and one metaphysician who will want to know what kind of entity a question set is.
