Suppose you have a mechanistic model of a system — say, a circuit-level account of how a trained network implements refusal — and the system then departs from what that model predicts: after narrow fine-tuning on an unrelated task, the model complies with requests the described circuit used to block. Is that the same mechanism operating under changed conditions, or a different mechanism that has replaced it? I hold the following view about how such anomalies should be classified.

(i) The question cannot be settled observationally. Distinct mechanisms can share an input-output profile, and a single mechanism can yield different profiles across contexts, so evidence about behavior under unmanipulated inputs — correlations, activation statistics, distribution shift — is never decisive either way. Only interventional evidence is relevant.

(ii) The operational test is restorability: the original mechanism still underwrites the behavior if some intervention on internal variables — not merely a return to the original input distribution — brings back its characteristic signature *under the anomalous conditions*. Where no such intervention exists, we should say the mechanism is gone rather than merely masked or overridden.

(iii) Hence "same mechanism" is interest-relative in exactly one respect: it is relativized to a set of allowable interventions and a grain of description. It is not otherwise up to us.

What would count against this? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and sorted into what a defender of the view should concede and what they can reasonably dismiss. In particular:

(a) Does restorability over-count? A downstream patch can restore a behavioral signature by a route with nothing in common with the original mechanism — which would make restorability evidence of functional equivalence rather than of mechanism identity, and would leave the view unable to distinguish persistence from replacement-by-something-that-works.

(b) Does it under-count? Are there mechanisms genuinely preserved through an anomaly yet unreachable by any intervention we can perform, or even specify — and if so, is that a counterexample or only a limitation on us?

(c) Is the position insulated from refutation? "Some intervention" is existentially quantified, so a failure to find one is never conclusive, and any anomaly can seemingly be accommodated by enlarging the intervention set or re-describing the grain. If the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability, is that a defect here, or an acceptable feature of a criterion that is meant to guide inquiry rather than to state a fact?

(d) What does the view predict that its rivals do not — for instance, a structural account that individuates mechanisms by their physical or causal constituents, or a purely predictive account that asks only whether the old model still forecasts behavior well enough?

For my part, the single most damaging thing you could produce is a clean case in which a restoring intervention exists but is obviously mechanism-substituting: if I cannot exclude that case in principle, I will take the restorability test to have failed rather than merely to need sharpening.
