I'm one of two people who keep the code of conduct for a professional association of estate agents, about five thousand members. Maybe thirty times a year someone writes in to ask whether a move is on: can I tell a buyer there's another offer when there isn't one yet, can I show buyers only our own listings unless they ask, can I hold back a bid that came in after the deadline in case the first one collapses. Right now we argue it out and vote, and near-identical cases come out differently depending on who's in the room, so I went looking for something better and kept landing on Kant's universalization test, which feels like it should be usable here except that every version I find is either a slogan or a seminar.

What I need is an actual procedure. How to get from a member's self-serving description of what they want to do to a maxim stated at a fixed level of generality, with a rule for how general to make it and when to widen or narrow it — this looks like the step where everything gets decided, since 'I'll be vague about competing offers', 'I'll mislead a buyer', and 'I'll lie about whether an offer exists' are the same bit of behaviour and I assume they don't all come out the same. Then the tests, in the order we should run them, and where each one stops us with a no.

Three wrinkle types keep coming up and I want the procedure to handle them rather than leave them to judgment. Some moves only work because most people don't make them: turning up early to an open house to get to the seller before anyone else is fine if nobody else does it and useless if everyone does, and I can't tell whether that counts against it. Some moves destroy the thing they depend on: if every agent said 'that's our final price' and meant nothing by it, the sentence wouldn't do any work any more, so once universal the maxim can't even be carried out — is that the same failure as the first kind or a different one? And some seem to fail only when you picture being on the receiving end: a world where every agent pressures buyers to sign quickly is perfectly thinkable, I just wouldn't want to be the buyer in it.

Constraints: two weeks a ruling, one paragraph of plain language with no philosophy jargon in it, written by me and one other person, and it has to sit okay beside the couple hundred rulings we've already published, some of which I suspect are wrong. Give me the steps, what counts as a good enough answer at each, the points where it hands the call back to us because it's a values question rather than something the test settles, and the cases where it just runs out. Then run it on three live queries, including one where you expect it to rule against something we currently permit: telling a buyer 'there's interest in the property' when no offer actually exists is our present guidance and I'd like to see whether it survives you.
