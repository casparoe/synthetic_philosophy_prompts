Hey — I'm helping run a discussion night for a film club and we're doing Parasite next month. I volunteered to take the Marx angle, exploitation and alienation specifically, and I want it to be more than rich people bad. But I've hit a snag and I'd like you to think it through with me rather than just hand me the obvious reading.

Here's the snag. The Kims obviously work for the Parks: driver, housekeeper, art tutor. But they're domestic servants, and Marx's distinction between productive and unproductive labour — labour that produces surplus value for capital versus labour paid out of revenue, the standard example being servants — seems to say that what the Parks get out of them isn't surplus value at all. Mr. Park isn't a capitalist with respect to them in the relevant sense. So the relation sitting at the centre of the film might not be a case of exploitation in the technical sense, and if that's right the whole reading needs rethinking.

So three candidate frames, and I want you to say which one actually earns its keep. One: it is about exploitation and I'm overthinking the productive/unproductive thing. Two: it's really about alienation instead — the early fourfold estrangement from the product, the activity, species-being, and other people — and the film is a case of alienation without exploitation, which would be interesting if that combination is even coherent. Three: it's about neither, but about surplus population: the people capital has no use for. Geun-sae living in the bunker and the flooded semi-basement aren't being exploited, they're being discarded, and the Kims' whole scheme is competition over a shrinking number of service jobs. If you think the right category is the lumpenproletariat rather than the reserve army, say why.

The bit I keep circling back to is the smell. It's involuntary and bodily, and the Kims only register it as class when the Parks notice it. Does that push toward a reading where alienation is a matter of how people experience their situation, or one where it's an objective fact about your position whether or not you feel it? Does the film take a side, or is it deliberately toying with the ambiguity?

And the ending: the son's fantasy of buying the house and freeing his father. Does that undercut a Marxian reading, since the horizon is still private property and a wage, or is the film being bleak on purpose? I'd rather you say a frame is a bad fit then force it.

Also: which two or three scenes would you put in front of a room to get this going, where does the Marx reading genuinely illuminate something and where does it go inert, and where does the film resist it enough that an honest critic has to drop it?

Last thing, because a couple of people coming actually know this literature: please separate what's settled among Marx scholars from what's contested — whether the productive/unproductive labour distinction still does any work, whether the alienation material is continuous with the later critique of political economy or whether there's a break, that kind of thing. Don't give me the contested version as the consensus. And flag where your reading leans on one side of those fights, and where it takes liberties with the film to make a point.
