so I'm a novelist and I've written myself into a corner on a near-future book, and I need the spec before I need the verdict. The plot, such as it is: a small hardware lab announces a "halfstep engine" — a box that performs its first operation in one second, its second in half a second, its third in a quarter, and so on, so that two seconds after you switch it on it has finished infinitely many steps and printed an answer to whether an arbitrary program halts. Running alongside it is a black box out of a defense contract that answers halting questions correctly for every program whose description comes in under a million bits and then just returns "unknown" for anything longer. And a mathematician character invents a third thing on a napkin: a model of computation that looks obviously mechanical to everyone in the room but provably outruns Turing machines if some unproved conjecture in number theory turns out true. My characters spend half the book fighting about whether any of this is a counterexample to the Church-Turing thesis, and every time I draft the fight it collapses into one person saying "it's a thesis about effective calculability, not a theorem" and another saying "only the physical version is worth caring about" and me having no idea what the actual standard of adequacy is.

So: what does an account of the epistemic status of the Church-Turing thesis have to deliver to count as adequate? I want the requirements list, not a tour of the positions — the things it must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, with the consideration behind each. Obvious candidates I can already hear people demanding: why so many independent-looking formalizations from the 1930s landed on exactly the same class of functions and whether that convergence is real evidence or just sociology; whether the claim about our informal notion of a procedure following rules can be kept apart from the claim about what's physically buildable and from the sharp statement you can actually prove; whether "mechanical" or "effective" can be sharpened at all without presupposing the thesis; who bears the burden of proof and what has to count as a refutation, especially for a device that's only good up to a bound or only good if some conjecture holds; and whether the thesis could be true of our world by accident, and if so what our warrant for it is and how revisable it's supposed to be. Mark which of these are non-negotiable and which are just what one camp wants, and use my three devices as the running test cases where they help — I specifically want to know whether the halfstep engine and the million-bit box are threats of the same kind, and what an account has to say about the napkin model, where expert intuition says "clearly mechanical" but the math is hostage to an open problem.

Give it to me as exactly six numbered items: items one through five are the desiderata, each with its motivating case, and item six says which pair of them you think can't be jointly satisfied and which single requirement does the most work in ruling out otherwise popular accounts (the ones that rest everything on convergence, the ones that treat the thesis as analytic, the ones that treat it as an empirical law of nature). Each item a sentence or two, no introduction, no wrap-up at the end. And please resist giving me a scorecard of named positions — I can fake that part myself, it's the criteria I can't fake.
