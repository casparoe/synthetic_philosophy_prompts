I've been reading the rationalist blogs since roughly the Sequences days — SSC, ACX, LessWrong, the usual crowd — and Moloch is one of those ideas I keep circling back to. I've finally got some room to do something with it: call it 18 months of most of my working time, plus a small budget for travel and maybe some contract research help, no institution or lab behind me. So I need to decide what to actually point that at, and I want a prioritization, not a survey.

Here's what I keep getting stuck on, and I want you to push back if you think I'm wrong. I've started to suspect that "multipolar trap" is doing more work than it deserves because it quietly names two different mechanisms. Call the first one ratchet-Moloch: there's an actual race, each increment is locally rational for whoever moves, the thing ratchets, and everyone ends up worse (defect-defect equilibria, credential inflation, ad load, the electric-shock country from the old Meditations on Moloch post). Call the second one filter-Moloch: nobody is racing, there's no visible ratchet, but the equilibria that survive and reproduce are the extractive ones, so the population drifts toward worse arrangements without anyone defecting against anyone — selection on firms, institutions, memes, or subagents rather than a race between them. My rough sense is that these need different medicine: the first wants a coordination or enforcement technology, the second wants you to change the selection environment itself, and if you misdiagnose which one you're in you'll spend years building the wrong tool. I don't know how to tell them apart in a given case, or whether the distinction is even clean, or which of the canonical examples land on which side.

Second stuck point: the baseline problem. When someone points at a domain and says "that's Moloch," how much of the observed badness is actually attributable to the trap, versus just being what you'd get from a bunch of agents with ordinary preferences who can't coordinate, versus being what you'd get even with perfect coordination because people's actual preferences are the problem? My worry is that a big fraction of the Moloch discourse is doing attribution work by vibes, and if the counterfactual baseline is bad anyway then the prize from "solving the trap" is way smaller than the rhetoric implies. Relatedly: is there any way to make a Moloch audit operational — a method that takes a domain and spits out a defensible estimate of the gap between the current equilibrium and the best reachable one, and of how much of that gap is recoverable with realistic coordination tech? Or is that hopelessly domain-specific and I should drop it?

Third: I'd like to know what the Moloch frame adds over the older stuff — the commons stuff, Schelling's micromotives-and-macrobehavior book, Olson on collective action, the literature on arms races and dissipative structures. If the honest answer is "it's a nicer meme for things game theorists already formalized in 1960," then a lot of what I'd be doing is relabeling, and I'd rather know that now. If it does add something, what? My guess is it's something about the phenomenology and the search — noticing the pattern across domains, the god's-eye-view move, the thing where naming the system as an agent makes vivid that it isn't one — but I'm not sure that's a research contribution rather than a marketing one.

Some candidate lines I'm weighing, in no order: (a) the ratchet/filter distinction itself, and a diagnostic for telling them apart; (b) the audit / prize-size question, including picking two or three domains and actually doing it; (c) the flagship applied case — whether the race-to-the-bottom framing of competitive AI development is ratchet-Moloch, filter-Moloch, both layered, or neither, and whether getting that right changes what intervention you'd actually push; (d) "interior" Moloch: whether the same formalism buys anything when the competing agents are parts of one mind or one org — akrasia, internal markets, motivational subsystems — or whether that's a bad metaphor that makes people feel profound instead of doing anything; (e) the inverse case, where the dynamic under-produces something good rather than over-producing something bad, and whether that's the same phenomenon or just the public goods problem wearing a costume.

So: rank these, add ones I'm missing, and defend the ordering. What I want is the reasoning, not the list. For each, how much other work or real-world action depends on it (upstreamness), how tractable it is for one person with no lab and no institutional backing in 18 months, how neglected it is relative to its importance, and how those trade off against each other — I'd rather fund something tractable-but-downstream than something foundational that I can't finish. Tell me which one or two you'd actively talk me out of and why, including the ones where you think an answer wouldn't change anyone's behavior. And tell me what evidence or results over the next 18 months should move things up or down the list — what would make you demote your own top pick. Don't bother with citations or a reading list; I've read the classics and I can find the rest myself. I want a defended agenda I could actually run.
