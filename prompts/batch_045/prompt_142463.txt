I'm running a discussion section for an intro Chinese philosophy course and we just spent two weeks on the Mencius–Xunzi fight about human nature, and I want to replace next week's seminar with a handout that actually sorts people. Every time I ask students where they stand they just announce "I'm with Mencius" or "I'm with Xunzi" and then can't say anything about any case, so: please build me a decision tree, 8–10 leaves, that starts from scratch and lands on a named position, where every internal node is a question a first-year could answer without knowing any of the vocabulary.

My idea is that the top branch should not be "is nature good or bad?" — that's the question where everyone just parrots something. I want the first split to be about what the claim is even aimed at: whether you're making a claim about the motivational equipment we're born with, about how reliably a normally-reared person ends up decent, about how much resistance the material puts up when you try to shape it, or about where the standard we're being measured against comes from in the first place. (My hunch is they disagree hardest on that last one and hardly at all on the second, but tell me if I've got it backwards.) Then I want a branch I invented and can't find in anything we've read: the upkeep question. Once someone is actually good, does that state hold on its own — more like a plant that keeps growing once you stop hacking at it — or does it need permanent external scaffolding, teachers and rules and institutions, such that pulling the scaffolding away sends them right back? I think this separates people who otherwise give the same verdict on "good or bad," and also splits certain readings of Mencius from certain readings of Xunzi, but I can't get the students to see it.

Leaves I want on the tree: the textbook Mencian and the textbook Xunzian positions; the Gaozi-style neutrality (which I realize is really more than one thing, so split it if the tree needs); the Han-era view that nature is a mixture you cultivate in one direction or the other (I always forget which author and which text — don't bother naming them, just describe it); the later Confucian move of splitting an "original" nature from a psychophysical or "material" one; and at least two leaves I doubt any classical author occupies: a "calibration mismatch" view where nothing in nature is bad but our desires are tuned for scarcity and reliably generate conflict when conditions change, so the remedy is material and institutional rather than moral, and something like a niche-construction or developmental-systems view where the nature-versus-cultivation distinction itself breaks down. For each unoccupied leaf say whether you think it's empty because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just untried. Use concrete cases rather than jargon — the child at the well, the toddler who shares only when watched, the feral child who later learns some things but not others, the person who is decent only inside well-designed institutions, the sage who still has to keep practising — yours or better ones. Where the tree genuinely can't separate two positions, flag it instead of inventing a branch, especially around that "they were answering different questions all along" reading. Also flag anywhere you're unsure whether a leaf is really occupied by anyone in the texts; I'd rather hand out a tree with a couple of honest question marks than a tidy one. At the end tell me how confident you are in the tree overall, and which single branch point you'd most expect a specialist to say I got wrong.
