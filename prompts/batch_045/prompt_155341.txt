I sit on the clinical research review panel of a teaching hospital that runs a sight-restoration programme for people blind from birth — dense congenital cataracts, mostly teenagers and young adults. Our surgeon-investigator has submitted a protocol I must vote on next week. At the moment the post-operative dressings come off, and before the patient is allowed to touch anything, a tray of objects they have handled daily for years and know well by touch and by description is placed in front of them, and they are asked to name or sort the objects by sight alone; only afterwards are the objects put into their hands. He calls this the Molyneux window and argues it is the only opportunity anyone will get to settle Molyneux's question properly. Several colleagues want to reject it outright: we would be spending a patient's first minutes of sight on a three-century-old parlour puzzle whose answer, either way, changes nothing about the rehabilitation we owe them.

I think that objection, as stated, misdescribes what we are voting on. What the panel should be testing is not whether tactile knowledge transfers to vision wholesale, but two separable things: whether a newly sighted patient can match a shape-kind known only by touch (a globe against a cube, say), and whether they can visually re-identify a particular individual known only by touch — this cup, this key, their mother's face. These can in principle come apart, and only the second seems to bear on whether the patient's existing tactile and linguistic competence is of any use to them in learning to see, whereas the protocol as written is built to detect only the first. I also want the panel to consider a control the investigator has not proposed: newly blind patients asked to identify by touch alone objects they have only ever seen. If reverse-direction matching succeeds while forward matching fails, that asymmetry is itself the finding.

Please draft the panel's decision memo: approve, reject, or approve with conditions, with reasons the surgeon could actually act on and a fair statement of the strongest version of the objection — that the first sighted minutes of someone who has waited years for surgery are not ours to spend on a philosophical experiment. If you recommend conditions, make them concrete about what is measured, for how long, what the patient is told beforehand, and what the panel should conclude if the two parts of the question come apart in the results. The panel has its own clinical evidence summary, so do not rest the recommendation on published findings you cannot verify; argue from what the question actually asks and from what would follow for the patient's rehabilitation either way.
