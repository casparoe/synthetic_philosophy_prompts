I write about science and ideas for a broadsheet, and I have been given roughly three months of reporting time and about 4,000 words for a single feature. The question I want the piece to be about is what we are actually doing when we claim to know something about the distant past, and how much of that knowledge is likely to survive the next fifty years.

Two framings I have taken from recent interviews have been competing for the centre of the piece, and I would like you to adjudicate and then go further.

The first, from a historian: that the basic unit of historical knowledge should not be the source but the transmission event. On this view, any claim about the deep past is graded by reconstructing the chain behind it — an observation, a report, a copy, a selection, a translation, an excavation record, a restoration — and then grading the claim by its weakest link rather than by the number of apparently independent sources that repeat it, on the grounds that apparent independence usually conceals a shared ancestor. He wants this taught to first-year undergraduates as the successor discipline to source criticism, and he thinks the older habit of asking who wrote a text, when, for whom and with what bias is a special case of it.

The second, from an archaeologist: that the growth is all in non-testimonial evidence — radiocarbon, ancient DNA, isotopes, tree rings, remote sensing — where there is no reporter to interrogate and where the traditional tools have nothing to grip; on her account, I am paraphrasing, the history of periods without written sources becomes the model, and text-based history is reduced to a literature of illustration built around a skeleton of measurement.

A third thing I keep running into, which neither of them quite addresses: damaged texts are increasingly completed by models trained on the surviving corpus, and a machine-proposed restoration can end up in the published record looking like a reading of the physical evidence. The worry is not primarily forgery. It is that the reconstruction is informed by the very body of material it is subsequently taken to corroborate, so that a chain can acquire a link that is at once highly probable and evidentially empty.

What I want is not a survey. Given my constraints — 4,000 words, three months, readers who are educated but not specialists — which two or three open questions should the piece be built around, in what order, and why? Please make the criteria explicit: how much of the rest of the terrain hangs on each question; whether it can be made intelligible and genuinely interesting to a general reader in the space I have; whether it is live rather than settled in disguise; and whether an answer would change a practice outside the seminar room, since courts, legislatures and museums do act on claims about the deep past. Name at least one question in this area that sounds important and that you would cut, and say why it is overrated. Then tell me what evidence or event over the next two or three years would move things up or down your list.

Please close with a short note on your own confidence: how confident you are in each of your main claims, and which single point you are least sure of. I would rather that was flagged plainly than smoothed into prose that reads as confidence. I do not want a reading list or citations; if you mention a particular author, work or finding, be sure of it from memory or leave it out. Please write in British English.
