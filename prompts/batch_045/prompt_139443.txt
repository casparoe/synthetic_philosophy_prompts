I'm retired now — used to do atmospheric chemistry, lots of time series, error budgets, that sort of thing — and a couple of years ago I joined a philosophy reading group at the local library. Last month someone dragged us into the historical Jesus, specifically the criteria of authenticity, and I came away annoyed enough to write about 1100 words. I'd like your comments on it before I inflict it on the group.

Quick ground rule: please don't send me reading lists, bibliographies, or citations — I can't chase references and I've already got more books on this than I'll ever finish. Just argue with me.

Here's roughly what I wrote.

***

The criteria of authenticity (multiple attestation, dissimilarity, embarrassment, coherence, and the general appeal to Aramaic or Palestinian plausibility) are usually presented as filters: run a saying through them and what survives is what the historical Jesus probably said. I want to claim something weaker and, I think, more damaging: they aren't filters at all, they're likelihood ratios, and once you see that, three things follow that their defenders don't usually say out loud.

First: combining them is where the trouble starts. If each criterion is a likelihood ratio, then multiplying them together — which is what people are doing when they say a pericope is "very strong" because it's attested twice, embarassing, and Semitic in flavour — assumes the criteria are independent. In my old field we spent most of our time worrying about correlated errors: two instruments that look independent but share a calibration, a power supply, or an operator. Multiple attestation has exactly this structure. If two of our sources share a written source, or a stream of oral tradition, or simply a shared idea of what a teacher of that kind would say, they are not two measurements, they're one measurement with extra steps. And the awkward cases are the interesting ones: the material that looks doubly attested is often precisely the material that circulated most freely, which means circulation, not authenticity, may be what's being measured.

Second, and this is the bit I like: the criterion of dissimilarity is self-emptying. It says, in effect, keep what doesn't fit first-century Judaism and doesn't fit the early church either. But the size of its output depends on how well we know those two reference classes. When the Qumran scrolls turned up and the Jewish reference class got much richer and weirder, a lot of things that had looked "without parallel" stopped looking so. So the criterion's yield is inversely proportional to our ignorance of the context. That is a hell of a property for a discovery procedure: it means the Jesus it returns is partly an artifact of how much we don't know, and the artifact shrinks as we learn more. Its not a portrait, its a residue — the criterion is a difference-detector, and difference-detectors return what's left over, not what was there.

Third — I call this "the coherence ratchet", and I'm not sure its a real problem or just what inference looks like when you describe it unkindly. Embarrassment and dissimilarity are trimming criteria: they cut in the direction away from what the later church would have wanted. Coherence then re-admits material because it fits whatever survived the trimming. But the survivor set has already been shaped by a directional cut, so coherence is now amplifying a biased template. Subtract in one direction, re-add on the basis of what's left, and nobody is tracking the order of operations or the net bias. Anybody who's watched a meta-analysis inflate its own effect size by pruning outliers and then recalculating the variance from the pruned set will recognise the shape of this.

So what do I want to do with the criteria? Not scrap them. Demote them, from a discovery procedure to a sensitivity analysis. Concretely: for each pericope, score it on each criterion, then do what we used to call a leave-one-out — drop each criterion in turn and see whether the verdict flips. Report the flip rate rather than the verdict. And pre-commit, before the scoring, to what would have counted as disqualifying evidence, because otherwise you can always re-weight after the fact. The output of the whole exercise shouldn't be a list of authentic sayings; it should be a map of which conclusions depend on which commitments. If the map turns out to be flat — if nothing much depends on anything — that's a finding too, and probably the honest one.

Which leaves the question the group actually asked: can criteria of authenticity ever settle who he was? I think no, and not because the evidence is thin, but because the instruments are directional. You cannot manufacture signal by filtering, you can only lose it, and a battery of directional filters will reliably return the shape of the filters.

What I want from you:

- The coherence ratchet: genuine defect, or am I just redescribing ordinary coherentist inference in a way that makes it sound pathological? If it is just that, what would have to be true for it to be a real problem?
