Topic is Good's theorem: for an agent who maximizes expected utility, updates by conditionalization, and then takes the best available act, the expected value of free information is never negative — and in the Blackwell ordering a more informative signal is weakly better than a less informative one for every such agent. Both characters should accept the math completely. The fight is over what the theorem is supposed to do for us.

Character one, Priya: says the theorem is a real rational requirement with teeth rather than a tautology, precisely because it can fail and it tells you where. For any agent who won't use the evidence the way EU maximization says, the value of information can go negative. Her practical upshot: if looking looks bad to you, the thing to fix is your response rule, not the looking. She has the food-isn't-bad-for-you-just-because-some-people-chew-wrong move and should get to use it.

Character two, Theo: says the theorem is a constraint on the ideal agent and therefore nearly idle as a guide for anyone else. Give him a positive framework and make it exact: a value-of-information figure computed relative to the response rule the agent will actually follow rather than the optimal one — call it the R-value, expected utility of your actual post-signal policy minus expected utility of your actual no-signal act. Make him face the fact that this cuts both ways: an agent whose default act is bad can gain more from information than the optimal agent does, so R-value isn't just Good's value with a penalty subtracted.

The bit I really want them to grind on, because I don't think either of them can just recite it: Theo claims the comparative half collapses first. Blackwell monotonicity holds only at the optimal response rule. So have him put a tiny worked example on a napkin, numbers small enough to check in you head. Result: the finer signal is strictly worse for her and exactly neutral for the well-calibrated agent. Then the argument: the part of the theorem that actually licenses running the bigger trial or buying the finer instrument is exactly the part that doesn't survive a nearly-right agent.
