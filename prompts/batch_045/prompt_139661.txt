I translate early modern song texts and verse, and I am putting together the words for a recital of sixteenth-century French chansons at a small festival in York this autumn. The programme prints the French on the left-hand page with an English version opposite; the songs themselves will be sung in French, so the English has only to be read, not sung. A friend lent me Douglas Hofstadter's *Le Ton beau de Marot* — his 1997 book that works through dozens of rival versions of a short poem by Clément Marot in order to ask what translation is — and reading it has left me quite unable to decide what to do with one poem in my programme.

The poem is by a contemporary of Marot and survives in a single manuscript. It runs to twelve lines in three quatrains of eight syllables, rhyming abab cdcd efef. The speaker, a man of no consequence, tells a lady that he has nothing to offer her but his constancy, and each quatrain closes by joining two things he says cannot be pulled apart. Nothing out of the ordinary. Some weeks ago, though, I noticed that the words standing at the ends of lines 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 and 12 — the b, d and f lines — read downwards as a sentence of their own: AMOUR TOUJOURS, DOULEUR BONHEUR, VIE ENVIE. The poem's rhymes, in other words, are also its argument in miniature, and each of the three pairs is a pairing the body of the poem insists upon.

I now have three drafts and can print only one. The first keeps the sense and the eight-syllable line, rhymes naturally, and loses the motto entirely. The second forces the rhyme-words into the same six slots, so that they read LOVE ABOVE, GRIEF RELIEF, LIFE STRIFE; to do this I had to make the speaker accuse the lady in the last quatrain, where in French he only longs for her, and to drag a vertical, almost devotional note into the first. The third is a friend's suggestion: settle on the six English words as the real constraint, write a fresh twelve-line English poem around them, and let the sense fall where it will. She says this is more faithful than anything that fusses over individual words; I suspect it is simply a new poem carrying the old poem's initials. My editor, meanwhile, says to print the first version and explain the device in a footnote, and the singer has asked that whatever goes in be readable at a glance.

What troubles me is that I cannot tell whether the motto is mine or the poet's. Two other poems in the same manuscript have rhyme-words that very nearly compose a sentence but not quite, and anyone rhyming on those three sounds might land on something like this by accident.

Which should I print, and what ought I to be weighing in order to decide? Specifically: is the sort of thing I have found a feature of the poem or a pattern I have imposed on it; does it matter which, for what I should do with it; and if I print a version that loses the device, can a footnote recover what is lost, or is a note about a formal device simply not the same thing as the device? Please do not tell me that it comes down to what I happen to prefer — I have to choose one version by the end of the month, and I want to know what the choice genuinely turns on.
