I am a features writer for a science-and-ideas outlet, and the piece I am working on is about the family of situations that gets labelled — loosely, and far too often — with the name Moloch: arrangements in which each participant's locally sensible move drags everyone towards an outcome that nearly all of them would rather avoid, and in which no single participant can stop the drift. Arms build-ups, credential inflation, doping in endurance sport, tax competition between small jurisdictions, the race among laboratories to ship capable systems, the attention economy's war for engagement. Before I write a sentence of it I want a specification rather than a story: what would a serious account of these dynamics have to get right before it deserves to be quoted in print?

Write me that list of desiderata. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of one conventional framing of the problem. Please do not pad the answer with references or reading lists; the argument should rest on the cases themselves.

The requirements I most want you to work out, rather than assume, are these.

First, discrimination from lookalikes. A trap of this kind is not the same as a coordination failure in which everyone wants the same outcome and cannot find it, nor a distributional conflict in which one party gains what another loses, nor an informational cascade, nor a mere treadmill — competition over relative position in which each defection is individually rewarded and the aggregate is not clearly bad for the participants. A journalist never sees the payoff matrix, only the surface pattern: escalating costs, a stable outcome everyone complains about, participants who denounce the race while continuing to run it. So the theory owes observable, outside-view signatures that separate a genuine trap from these neighbours, and it should say how much uncertainty about the payoffs the diagnosis can survive.

Second, traps with bystanders. In some of the central cases the competitors are not the sufferers: antibiotic resistance, carbon emissions, fisheries. The theory must distinguish Moloch among the players from Moloch imposed on third parties, and say whether both need the same machinery or whether one is a different animal wearing the same name.

Third, the asymmetry of entry and exit. Some of these arrangements are cheap to enter and ruinous to leave; others are easy to leave and yet nobody leaves. I want the theory to say what makes exit hard — sunk commitments, earlier self-binding choices, restraint whose cost is immediate and concentrated while its benefit is delayed and diffuse — and to take a position on whether an escape that is not self-enforcing deserves to be called an escape at all.

Fourth, traps about traps. The machinery of escape is usually itself a site of competition, as when jurisdictions compete over how much restraint to impose, or when a regulator is captured by the racers it was meant to restrain. The theory must not treat the cure as exogenous: it should say when a cure is available, when the cure is itself another race, and how to handle the case where Moloch's medicine is worse than the disease — cartels, licensing regimes and moratoria that entrench incumbents under the banner of restraint.

Fifth, Moloch without agents. In evolutionary or cultural selection analogues there are no deliberating parties, no possible bargain, and nobody who could have preferred the alternative. The theory must say whether agency and foresight belong in the definition or are only the usual setting; and if the concept is meant to extend to selection dynamics, it owes an account of what plays the role of the claim that everyone would prefer otherwise, when there is no one to do the preferring.

Two further items are distinctions I have been toying with and want stress-tested rather than flattered — if either is confused, say so and say what should replace it. The first is what I call a credence trap: a race in which the currency competed over is what to say or profess, so that participants escalate public positions nobody privately endorses and the equilibrium is a set of commitments detached from anyone's actual view. Is this the same structure, and what is the cost when the currency is accuracy or sincerity rather than money or effort? The second is the class of races worth running: priority races in science, or competitions that impose real costs on participants while handing large benefits to everyone else, where slowing the field down would be a straightforward loss. Any theory that cannot state the condition under which a race should be left alone — without that condition collapsing into I dislike the outcome — is a machine for relabelling costly competition as failure.

Finally, I want the list to police itself. Which pairs of desiderata do you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — a definition wide enough to cover agentless selection dynamics against one that keeps the everyone-would-prefer-otherwise clause meaningful, say, or a demand that the diagnosis be refutable against the possibility of traps that leave no visible trace — and which single requirement does the most work in excluding the sloppy popular usage of the label? Add a short closing table of no more than a few lines scoring the three standard framings — commons-style tragedy, iterated arms-race or prisoner's-dilemma logic, and cultural or multi-level selection — against the list; the criteria are the deliverable, and the scoring is a check on them rather than the point.

Please write in British English.
