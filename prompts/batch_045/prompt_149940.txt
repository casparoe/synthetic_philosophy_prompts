My friend and I spent like three hours after our data ethics night class arguing about algorithmic fairness and I came away not sure which of my own reactions I actually believe, so I want to write down my gut verdicts and see whether they add up to a principle or whether some of them have to go. The setup we kept circling: a bank's small-business loan model, where group A's default rate is 20% and group B's is 10%, and my friend kept invoking one of those impossibility results — you can't simultaneously have a score that means the same thing for everyone (of the people given a 7, the same share default, whatever group they're in) and equal false-positive and false-negative rates across groups, unless the base rates are identical or the model is perfect — and he said that just settles it, you pick a criterion and eat the cost. Here's where I land on cases. (1) The calibrated version, where the score means the same thing but A eats more of the false positives and B eats more of the false negatives: feels fine to me. (2) The equalized-error-rate version, where we slide the cutoff per group so A and B have identical false positive and false negative rates, with the result that among everyone we flag, 24% of the A borrowers default and 14% of the B borrowers do: feels wrong, and not just because it's worse for the bank — I can't look a rejected applicant in the eye and explain why their 7 isn't the other person's 7. (3) A hospital model predicting surgical complications uses "number of prior C-sections," which tracks the patient's group mostly through unequal access to obstetric care: uncomfortable, but I don't think it has to go, it's carrying information about the body and not only about the hospital. (4) A hiring model uses "completed a coding bootcamp," which tracks the applicant's group mostly through who got pulled into those networks: drop it, that's just laundering group membership. (3) and (4) are the pair I can't reconcile — both are proxies, both add predictive power, I treat them differently. (5) Two vendors: one screens resumes and is individually fair by its own similarity metric, the other ranks the survivors and is individually fair by its metric, and the combined pipeline hires A at half the rate of B. I say that's a real fairness violation even though no component is unfair and neither vendor is allowed to see group; my friend says if every part is fair the whole is fair, which to me is like saying a meal can't poison you if every ingredient is safe. (6) A pretrial release tool: after it's deployed judges release more low-scoring B defendants, and a year later rearrest among released B defendants has dropped, so staying calibrated now requires different cutoffs by group. I think you re-fit and I don't think it's unfair, though my friend calls it ratcheting — the tool's own effect becomes an argument for treating the groups differently.

My tentative principle, which I made up on the walk home: the right fairness criterion is whichever one constrains a quantity that the person on the receiving end of the decision could be told and act on, so violations live in the *use* of a score, not in the number itself. I'd like you to test that against my six verdicts and repair it — ideally as a short set of candidate principles, each with the clause that buys each verdict and the case that motivates that clause. Then push it on the cases I haven't settled: (a) a hospital that puts the protected attribute directly into a kidney-function estimate because the standard formula mis-estimates for one group, so using it makes everyone's number more accurate; (b) the only way to equalize error rates in the loan model is to deliberately add noise to some scores, so a few genuinely high-risk borrowers get low ones; and (c) the "group" in question is one the system created — everybody the previous version of the model flagged, a class that cuts across A and B and exists only because the model existed. Does my principle even apply to (c), or does it silently assume groups are given in advance? And be honest about which of my verdicts no tidy principle can keep — I'd rather drop one than keep a rule that's definately just a description of what I already felt.
