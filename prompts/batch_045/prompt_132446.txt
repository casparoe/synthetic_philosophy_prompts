Hey — I write a monthly humor column for a science-and-ideas magazine, the slot that runs near the back next to the book reviews, and this month I finally want to do something on the AI-welfare beat. I've been half-covering it since I sat in on a panel last year where two grown adults spent forty minutes arguing about whether a model that says it doesn't want to be shut down is giving us evidence or just completing a pattern very confidently. What I want for the column is a piece of straight-faced news satire, and I'd rather not write it alone.

Here's the setup I've got. The article reports on the launch of a well-funded startup that sells moral-status certification as a service: labs pay to have their systems audited and come away with a tiered certificate — something like Presumed Inert, Welfare-Relevant, Provisional Moral Patient — plus a composite numerical score. I want you to invent the rubric concretely enough that it reads like a real audit methodology: fold in the obvious indicator stuff (self-model, reports of aversive states, persistence of anything across checkpoints and across copies) and then give them one proprietary axis that's clearly nonsense but sounds expensive, and have the protocol described with a straight face. The counting problem has to come up: is the certificate issued to the weights or to the running instances, and how many moral patients is one flagship model that serves forty million conversations a day? Give me the company's actual answer, in quotes. The jokes should all come out of the structure of the thing rather than out of robots being silly: the audit interviews the system about its inner life even though the system has every training-shaped reason to sound either suffer-y or breezily fine depending on which one helps it, and no procedural fix closes that gap; the asymmetry between a false positive (money and caution wasted) and a false negative (something nobody can quite name); and the bit I think is the real story, that the marketing departments figure out a high score is a premium signal, so labs start shopping for friendlier auditors and quietly lobby to have their flagship declared at least a little conscious while the safety people are rooting for the low score. Headline plus 550-650 words, deadpan straight-news voice, no winking, and I want at least three quotes: the founder, someone at a rival lab, and one skeptic who gets a genuine objection rather than being a cartoon — plus, if you can make it land, one quote from a certified model that's funny precisely because it's evidence for nothing at all.

Two things I'm firm about. Don't let it slide into tech-bro mockery or anti-AI doom — the target is the certification practice and the epistemic mess behind it, not the silly people who love their chatbots — so no beep-boop, no silicon puns in the headline, no Terminator. And don't have the piece come down on either side; if someone who actually works on this reads it I want them to wince and put it in the group chat, not feel strawmanned, and the last line should leave it honestly unclear whether there's anybody home. A correction box at the bottom is fine if you can think of one that isn't too cute about it.
