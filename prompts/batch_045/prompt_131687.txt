I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about what makes it permissible to take someone's word for something — she works on model evaluation, I work on provenance tooling for a fact-checking desk — and we kept circling one structure of disagreement: every condition I proposed she could defeat with a case where it was obviously too demanding, and every condition she proposed I could defeat with a case where it was obviously too permissive. So before we go round again I want the specification rather than another round of verdicts. Fix the purpose of the theory as follows: it should be good enough to underwrite working norms for an intermediary — a newsroom desk, a fact-checking team, a pipeline that relays onward what it has been told — about the circumstances in which a source's say-so gives the hearer enough to pass the claim on. What I want from you is what a theory of testimonial warrant would have to deliver to count as adequate for that purpose, not a ranking of the usual candidates.

The raw material I want you to organise into requirements is roughly this. Cases it has to get right: the one-off stranger giving directions, where there is no track record and no independent check and belief seems fine anyway; opaque expertise, where nobody downstream can assess the claim at all; a group or institutional assertion that no individual member believes, such as a committee statement or a press release issued in a company's name; assertion-shaped output from something with no beliefs, intentions, or stake in being trusted — a language-model completion, or a relay that has been machine-translated and auto-summarised on the way, which was my friend's favourite case and, I suspect, the one that does the most damage; the reliable but insincere speaker and the sincere but unreliable one; long chains in which the individual links are unreliable but the errors happen to cancel, or in which the claim arrives true by luck; source amnesia, where the hearer keeps the belief but loses or misremembers the source; assertion-shaped speech that is not assertion — jokes, fiction, seminar hypotheticals, sarcasm, implicature, an actor's lines — and, separately, genuine assertion not addressed to the hearer, such as an overheard conversation, a leaked memo, an anonymous tip, or a dead author's notebooks; testimony that collides with the hearer's own evidence; and testimony about matters of value, and religious testimony, where the question is whether these may be special-cased or must fall out of the general account. Distinctions I suspect it cannot collapse: the hearer's warrant versus the speaker's; justification versus knowledge; the speaker's reliability versus her taking responsibility for what she says; being warranted versus being able to produce a reason when challenged; and the grain at which calibration is tracked, since she kept insisting that warrant is indexed to a domain rather than to a person or to a single claim, and I could not tell whether that was a genuine desideratum or just a feature of the view she already liked. She also pressed two empirical claims I am not certain of — that young children selectively discount informants they have caught being wrong, and that unaided hearers detect deception at not much better than chance — so tell me if either is doing work it cannot bear.

So: give me the requirements, each with the case or consideration behind it, and mark which are non-negotiable for the purpose I described and which are preferences of one camp or another disguised as requirements. Add the ones I have missed. Then flag any subset you think cannot be jointly satisfied — I have two suspicions, that cheap default entitlement cannot coexist with a requirement that a hearer with no calibration data at all be unwarranted, and that warrant through long chains cannot coexist with any serious no-luck condition, but I would rather you told me which, if any, is a real inconsistency and what it would cost to drop. Say which single requirement excludes the most otherwise-plausible theories, and why. Then a compact scorecard — a table is fine — for four broad families only: global reductionism, default-entitlement anti-reductionism, tell-tale-sign or local reductionist views, and views on which what matters is the speaker's taking of responsibility rather than her reliability; where a family is silent on a requirement rather than wrong about it, say so. No reading list and no citations to papers; I have read the surveys, and it is the criteria I am missing. Close with a short note on where your own list is most likely to be wrong, and which single consideration, if I pressed it next time, would make you reorganise the whole specification.
