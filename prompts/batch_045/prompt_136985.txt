I have let a colleague in the psychology department talk me into a decision experiment, and I must give my answer in writing tomorrow, so I am asking for help with the case itself rather than for anything I could look up.

A week ago, with my consent, the lab made a high-fidelity computational copy of me from a brain scan, ran it briefly, and then deleted it. Yesterday the copy was offered this: it could authorize a $500 payment from funds held on its behalf, in which case $1,000 would go to a charity I have supported for years; or it could authorize nothing. It was told truthfully that today I would be offered the mirror transaction — $500 of my own money, in return for which $1,000 goes to a cause the copy itself named — and that its decision would be sealed before I learned anything about it. The lab has now told me that the transfer to my charity did go through: the copy paid, the record is sealed, and nothing I do can alter it. So my instinct says that paying now is simply handing over $500 for a benefit already in hand; and yet I cannot shake the sense that the copy's having paid is itself a reason for me to pay, and I cannot say what kind of reason that would be.

Two complications. Since the scan, I have learned that the cause the copy named is far less effective than we then believed, so the $1,000 I would release would do little real good; the copy, deciding yesterday, could not have known this. And the copy knew it was choosing first, whereas I know that it has already chosen.

Should I authorize the payment? Does the fact that the benefit is secured, and that I now know more and want something slightly different from what the copy wanted, change the answer from what it would have been had we both chosen in ignorance and at the same time? Would your answer change if the lab had told me the transfer did not go through?

Please answer in a single paragraph of no more than 300 words: a verdict, and the argument doing the work, not a tour of the options.
