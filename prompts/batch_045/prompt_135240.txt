I'm a second-year PhD student in political theory, and I'm assembling a problem set for an undergraduate course on the three-way taxonomy of freedom: negative liberty (roughly, absence of interference by others), positive liberty (roughly, self-mastery or authorship of one's own ends), and republican liberty (roughly, non-domination — not standing in the power of another, even a power that is never exercised). I want the final problem to turn on a passage I keep mis-reading, from Kant's 1784 essay answering the question "What is enlightenment?" (I'm working from Mary Gregor's translation in the Cambridge Practical Philosophy volume). The passage, in two excerpts:

"Enlightenment is the human being's emergence from his self-incurred minority. Minority is inability to make use of one's own understanding without direction from another. This minority is self-incurred when its cause lies not in lack of understanding but in lack of resolution and courage to use it without direction from another. Sapere aude! [dare to be wise] Have courage to make use of your own understanding! is thus the motto of enlightenment."

"It is because of laziness and cowardice that so great a part of humankind, after nature has long since emancipated them from other people's direction (naturaliter maiorennes), nevertheless gladly remains minors for life, and that it becomes so easy for others to set themselves up as their guardians. It is so comfortable to be a minor! If I have a book that understands for me, a spiritual advisor who has a conscience for me, a doctor who decides upon a regimen for me, and so forth, I need not trouble myself at all. I need not think, if only I can pay; others will readily undertake the irksome business for me. That by far the greatest part of humankind (including the entire fair sex) should hold the step toward majority to be not only troublesome but also highly dangerous will soon be seen to by those guardians who have kindly taken it upon themselves to supervise them; after they have made their domesticated animals dumb and carefully prevented these placid creatures from daring to take a single step without the walking cart in which they have confined them, they then show them the danger that threatens them if they try to walk alone. Now this danger is not in fact so great, for by a few falls they would eventually learn to walk; but an example of this kind makes them timid and usually frightens them away from any further attempt."

My difficulty is this. The reflex move is to file Kant under positive liberty, since he seems to locate unfreedom in the agent's failure to think for himself rather than in anyone's interference. But he also calls minority "self-incurred," which makes it odd as a claim about unfreedom at all: my supervisor argues that Kant isn't saying these people are unfree, he's saying they are lazy and cowardly — a charge about character or intellectual virtue, not about liberty. And there is a third option I can't get rid of: the guardians have made their charges dumb and then manufactured the appearance of danger, which sounds like a claim about a power relation the guardians maintain over them. To force the question, I've built the problem around two invented cases. Case A: "Helix," a municipal well-being platform. Enrolment is formally voluntary; Helix schedules residents' work, assigns housing, selects a medical regimen, and shifts money between their spending and savings pots. It uses no force and threatens none — every assignment can be refused or appealed in writing, and the appeals panel reverses about nine per cent of refusals. Its rules are published in full and applied uniformly, so residents can predict exactly what Helix will do; but no resident has any vote or binding say over the objective those rules optimise, which was fixed by a technical commission under a statute passed thirty years ago and never revised. Residents who decline an assignment are not punished; they lose access to a modest bundle of conveniences (priority booking, subsidised transit, automatic tenancy renewal). Polling shows most residents are content and describe themselves as "free from all that admin." Case B is identical in every respect except one: the objective function must be re-ratified every two years by a randomly selected citizens' assembly with binding power, and any resident may petition that assembly between ballots. I've also been toying, in the coding scheme for the course, with a set of labels that deliberately cross-cuts the trichotomy — "option-loss" (your feasible set shrinks), "will-dependence" (your feasible set is fixed by another's will, however benignly), "authorship-failure" (you do not set your own ends) — and I can't tell whether these are three genuine dimensions or one dimension described three ways.

So: what is Kant's indictment actually a claim about, and what does it say about Helix? Please don't point me at secondary literature — I have the reading list; I want the argument.
