Draft:

The two inversions are usually run together: Darwin shows how the appearance of understanding can be produced without an understander, and Turing shows how the performance of reasoning can be produced without a reasoner. Dennett's label for both is competence without comprehension. But the two arguments have different logical shapes, and the difference matters.

Darwin's inversion is what I'll call first-order. It removes a producer while keeping the product: adaptation is real, but the intending designer is replaced by filtration over heritable variation. Nothing in the explanation requires that the mechanism grasp what it is producing, and once you see that, the inference from apparent design to an actual designer loses its force. Turing's inversion is what I'll call recursive, and it is more unsettling, because it does not merely remove an upstream producer; it explains the very thing that was supposed to do the producing — comprehension itself — as a product of processes that lack it. The step from a designer-substitute to a mind-substitute is a step from explaining the effects of comprehension to explaining comprehension as an effect.

Now the familiar charge. When people say of a large language model that it exhibits competence without comprehension, the phrase is being used as a defect report: the system produces the right outputs but has no grip on what they mean. I want to argue that this use inverts the explanatory order that the recursive inversion establishes. If comprehension is itself a product of competences, then the presence of competences is not evidence about comprehension one way or the other, and the interesting question is not whether comprehension is present but what comprehension is for.

My positive proposal is what I'll call the audit model. Comprehension is not a property that a process either has or lacks but an achievement of a system: the capacity to maintain, and to act on, a working model of its own competences — their scope, their limits, their failure conditions. Crucially, that audit is itself executed by competences, which need not comprehend themselves. So comprehension is always downstream of competence; it never explains competence from above, it is built from competences below. Two consequences follow. First, competence without comprehension is possible in principle and common in fact, but comprehension without the corresponding competence is not: what looks like it is confabulation, i.e. a distinct competence at producing reports. Second, we should stop asking whether a system understands and ask whether its audit is derived from the mechanisms it audits or merely confabulated alongside them.

That gives a test, which I'll call the perturbation-traceability test. Introduce a targeted, novel degradation of a specific competence — a lesion, in the neuropsychological sense, or its functional analogue — and check not whether performance drops, since that is compatible with any story, but whether the system's account of its own capacities shifts in the corresponding direction, and whether that shift survives when the ordinary channels for observing its own failures are blocked. Human subjects sometimes fail this test: they confabulate fluent, confident, and wrong accounts of capacities they have just lost. A system whose self-report tracks a genuine lesion across blocked channels is comprehending in the only sense the audit model leaves available.

The worry I can't shake is that this makes comprehension cheap. If comprehension is just a well-trained audit, then a system trained on its own error traces passes, and the concept seems to have been laundered rather than explained. A second worry: the audit model may simply be a higher-order theory of consciousness wearing a different hat, in which case my account inherits that theory's problems, including the regress worry about whether the auditing state must itself be comprehended. I don't know which of these is the more serious threat.
