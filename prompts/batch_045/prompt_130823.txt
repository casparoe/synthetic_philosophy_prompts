Draft the joint statement that would emerge from a two-year adversarial collaboration on the norm of assertion, between the **Knowledge camp** — which holds that flat-out assertion is governed by a rule requiring knowledge, and on its stronger version holds that this rule is constitutive of the force rather than merely regulative of its exercise — and the **Reasonable-Belief camp** — which holds that what assertion requires is reasonable or justified belief, with truth entering, if at all, only as an independent standard by which assertions are assessed rather than as the condition on their being in order. Both camps have agreed in advance that the collaboration fails unless the residual disagreement is stated in a form each side would sign as its own.

Three ground rules both camps have accepted, and which the document must respect: no appeal to the literature, to authority, or to what has been said before — every claim must be made out by a case, an argument, or a prediction, since the statement will be read by people outside the debate; no testing of a norm on a case in which the speaker's evidence base, or what the hearer takes it to be, is left unspecified; and no describing the data at a granularity so coarse that the two norms have not yet come apart.

Write the document with the following structure.

**1. Agreed claims, numbered, 12 to 16 of them.** These must be substantial enough that a neutral reader learns something from the list, and must cover at least: that the two norms coincide on the ordinary central cases and therefore that only the divergence cases carry evidential weight; that the unassertability of the Moore-paradoxical conjunction, and the reluctance to flat-out assert a lottery proposition on purely statistical grounds, are data to be explained rather than data that settle anything on their own; that "improper" (criticism-worthy, something the speaker ought not to have done), "defective" (fails the governing standard but not the speaker's fault), and "infelicitous" (in some further way off) are three verdicts and not one, and that a large share of the recorded disagreement may collapse into a mismatch about which verdict is being asked for; that there are norms governing hedged, prefaced, and relayed speech and not only flat-out assertion, and that a complete account must say how those norms relate to the norm for the unhedged case; and that the practice of assertion has some point — whether knowledge-transmission, true-belief transmission, evidence-pooling, or the creation of a common deliberative record — such that the point constrains which norms can govern it, even though the camps disagree about what that point is. Mark any agreed claim that you suspect is agreed only because of how it is worded.

**2. A disagreements table.** Rows for each residual disagreement; columns for a neutral statement of the issue, the Knowledge camp's own formulation of its position on it, the Reasonable-Belief camp's own formulation of its position on it, an honest verdict on whether the disagreement is substantive or partly verbal, and a note on what would have to be different in the data for the issue to matter. Include among the rows: whether the governing rule is constitutive or regulative; whether the relevant evidence base is the speaker's personal evidence or some wider base she may legitimately draw on; whether the norm is invariant across contexts or indexed to a locally stipulated standard; whether the truth rule — the view that assertion is assessed by truth alone, with knowledge and justification entering only as ways of getting truth — is a genuine rival or a view that collapses into one of the two on examination; and whether the point of the practice settles the norm or merely constrains it.

**3. Cruxes, each with a test and pre-registered predictions.** For each crux, give the sub-question, say why settling it would move at least one side, specify a case, argument, or measurement both camps accept in advance as bearing on it, and record what each camp predicts it will show — written so that a prediction cannot be quietly revised after the fact. Address at least these five, reorganising them if you think they are mis-cut:

- *The retrospective case.* An assertion, fully reasonable when made, of a proposition that was never known and turns out false. What verdict does each norm predict, and do the camps disagree about the verdict or about which verdict the word "improper" picks out?
- *The spokesperson's case.* A designated spokesperson for an investigative committee flat-out asserts a safety claim on the strength of a report she has read but cannot independently check; asked in her personal capacity she would only say that she thinks it is safe. Is the relevant standard fixed by her personal evidence, or by the evidence available to the office she speaks from? Predict how naive assessors distribute blame when the institutional framing and the personal framing are varied.
- *The evidentiality test.* In languages with obligatory grammatical marking of information source, the unmarked flat-out form is not generally available: a speaker must mark something as seen, inferred, or reported. If a single unmarked assertive force is constitutively governed by one rule, some things should follow about how such languages apportion the work that hedging does in English; if assertion is rather a family of graded commitments, something else should follow. State what each camp predicts a typological survey and a corpus study would find.
- *The audit game.* A sender relays a claim to a decider who acts on it; vary the sender's position across perception, reliable testimony, testimony from a source she generally distrusts but has no specific reason to doubt here, purely statistical base-rate evidence, and a Gettier-style construction; vary separately whether the claim is true. Measure blame and the willingness to call the contribution improper. Each camp should predict which variable the verdicts track, and should predict in advance where the impropriety/defect split will fall.
- *The local-standard case.* Two working contexts with explicitly different evidentiary thresholds — a lab meeting in which nothing may be flatly asserted until it has been replicated, and a newsroom in which attributed official claims are flatly asserted daily. Predict whether competent participants' readiness to assert, and their readiness to criticise assertion, track the local convention; say what each camp takes this to show about whether the norm is contextual.

**4. What would move us.** For each camp, the specific finding or argument it would count as evidence against itself, stated precisely enough that the other camp could in principle produce it, together with what the camp would then have to give up.

**5. A closing paragraph** separating, honestly, the disputes that turned out to be about words, the ones that turned out to be genuinely evaluative, and the ones that turned out to be answerable to the tests in section 3. Where a crux is genuinely empirical but neither camp's prediction is yet available, say so rather than manufacturing agreement.

Constraints: the formulations of each camp's position must be non-vacuous but must be ones that camp would actually sign; do not resolve a disagreement by stipulating a definition; do not let either camp win by appeal to the bare claim that its rule is constitutive unless a testable consequence is supplied. Target 1,200 to 1,600 words.
