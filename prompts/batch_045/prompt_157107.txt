I'm trying to work out what I actually think about what speakers mean beyond what they say, going by my own reactions to cases rather than by any theory I can recall. My verdicts:

1. A professor asked how many students passed says "Some of them passed," knowing all of them did. She implicated that not all passed, and that was misleading; she said nothing false.
2. A recommendation letter saying only "Ms. Alvarez has beautiful handwriting and never missed a class" implicates that she is a weak candidate. The very same sentences, written by a registrar who knows nothing else about her and says so in the letter, implicate nothing.
3. "Lovely weather," said in a downpour: she means the weather is terrible, although the sentence says the opposite.
4. A guest speaking through a phone translation app says something perfectly polite in her own language; the app outputs "I don't want any more," and the host is offended. The guest implicated no rudeness, yet the host's reading was reasonable.
5. I tapped my phone's suggested completion without reading it and sent my sister "Fine, whatever you decide." I did not mean the resentment she took from it, and I say I didn't mean it — but I'm not sure I'm allowed to disown it.
6. Our department's rejection letters always include "We had an unusually strong field." One batch went out with that line dropped by a template error. Applicants read the omission as meaning the field was weak, and I think they were right to — even though no individual chose those words.
7. In a meeting with four colleagues, I say "That budget item has been reviewed," using a phrasing that only one of them, with whom I share a private code, will read as "it was rejected." I implicated rejection to him and nothing beyond my words to the other three.

My tentative principle: an utterance carries an implicature that p just in case some agent answerable for it could reasonably have been expected to foresee that hearers would take it that way, and did not block that reading. I don't think that handles 3, and I suspect it can't keep both 5 and 6.

Please give me the principle, or the small set of rival candidates, that best fits these verdicts — stated precisely, in numbered clauses, with the case that motivates each clause. Then apply it to two cases I haven't put to it: (a) a press officer who reads out, and says she stands behind, a statement drafted for her by a language model; (b) a speaker whose hearer draws an inference she neither intended nor foresaw, but which she would endorse if it were pointed out to her. Finally, say plainly which of my verdicts your account cannot accommodate and which one you would overturn. I don't want citations, reading lists, or surveys of the literature — just the principle and its consequences. Answer in British English, in no more than 650 words.
