I sit on my faculty's postgraduate assessment standards committee, which for the past two years has been ruling on individual requests to use generative AI tools in assessed work. The only rationale our minutes ever invoke is that a use should be refused where it would erode the understanding that the degree certifies. Nobody on the committee has ever written down what eroding amounts to, and the eight rulings below were reached case by case, each unanimously:

(i) Allowed: a student uses a model to generate practice problems in a topic she is learning, then answers them unaided.
(ii) Allowed: a model is used to look for errors in a proof the student has already written and cannot fault; the student then checks the complaint himself.
(iii) Refused: a model drafts the literature section of a thesis chapter, which the student then rewrites heavily. The minute records that the synthesis was not his.
(iv) Refused: a model is used to summarise papers the student has not read, and the summaries are the basis for claims in the submitted work.
(v) Allowed, and encouraged: a model instructed only to ask questions and never to assert, even where the student would not have thought of the questions unaided.
(vi) Refused: a model is asked which statistical test to use, and the recommendation is followed.
(vii) Allowed: a model writes code implementing a method the student has already specified in complete detail.
(viii) Allowed, subject to a condition: a model translates a passage in a language the student does not read, provided the translation is not relied upon for any claim about the text's argument.

The request now in front of us. A fourth-year doctoral student asks to use a model that has been fine-tuned on her own supervision notes, chapter drafts and published papers — a corpus nobody else has access to. Having first written her own provisional answer, she would ask the model what her answer to a given objection would be, check the output against the notes she still has, rewrite it, and use it as the skeleton of a section. Her account, which we have no reason to disbelieve, is that she could still reconstruct the position unaided, but only over several days she does not have, and that left to herself she would arrive at substantially the same thing: what she has lost is the ability to reconstruct it quickly, not the ability to recognise it as hers and defend it once it is in front of her. Two features of the case pull in opposite directions, and the committee has split on exactly this. First, the output is continuous with her earlier work in a way no general-purpose model's would be, which makes some of us want to treat the model as externalised memory rather than as dependence on another mind. Second, and against that: precisely because the output is continuous with her own prose and vocabulary, no reader and no examiner could detect that it was involved — which is true of none of the uses we have allowed so far, and which some of us think makes this case worse rather than better than dependence on a generic model.

What I want from you, in this order:

1. Reconstruct the rule, or rules, that fit all eight earlier rulings. I can think of several candidates and I do not believe our minutes distinguish between them; set out the ones that survive all eight, and say whether any of them is already eliminated by (vi) or (viii).
2. For each surviving rule, give its verdict on this request, with the reasoning made explicit rather than asserted. I am less interested in the verdict than in the rule that generates it.
3. Which precedent is the closest analogue, and why? (iii) has been argued on the ground that in both cases a model-generated skeleton is rewritten; (viii) on the ground that in both cases the model functions as a transducer of content the student already has rather than a source of new content. But (viii) carries a condition this request does not obviously satisfy, and that seems to me to cut the other way.
4. Recommend the decision, supposing we want above all to be consistent with ourselves, and say plainly if our precedents genuinely underdetermine the case. If they do, state for each option the principle we would be committing ourselves to by choosing it — in particular, if we allow it, are we conceding that what our terms of reference were protecting all along was not understanding but its auditability to others?
5. Does your recommendation change if the student is in her first year rather than her fourth? If it does, say what that reveals about whether the rule we are running concerns the student's understanding, the trajectory of that understanding, or the stage at which the degree certifies it.
6. Finally, name the minimal variant of this case that would tell the surviving rules apart. My own candidate is to have her use the same fine-tuned model on a question she has never addressed at all, so that the output reconstructs what she would have said rather than anything she ever did say. Tell me whether that is the sharpest test available, and if not, what is, and what each rule predicts of it.
