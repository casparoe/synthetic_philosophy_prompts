I'm a PhD student in political theory, writing a dissertation on self-binding institutions, and I need advice about a situation I am personally inside of — which is awkward precisely because I've been defending a thesis about cases like it for three years. I'd be grateful for your judgment, and I'd rather have your philosophical reasoning than a checklist.

Seven years ago I was one of five people who set up a small charitable fund that gives small grants to independent researchers who want to replicate published findings in experimental psychology. The five of us were young, we had all watched organizations we admired quietly drift toward whatever was fashionable, and we built the fund's founding document to make drifting hard. Its purpose clause can only be changed if two conditions are met together: every one of the five founders votes yes, and eighteen months pass between the proposal being formally lodged and the vote being taken. The eighteen months were, in our own words at the time, there so that no one gets carried away by a wave of enthusiasm.

I also drafted an exception, and it is the exception that is now causing the problem. The waiting period drops to two months if two-thirds of the current trustees certify in writing that the change is one the founders would have wanted had they been able to foresee present circumstances. At the time I thought this was the clever part: it tests whether a change is faithful rather than merely popular.

Here is where we are. The specific scarcity the fund was created to address has largely been solved by other people; replication now has dedicated money and institutional homes. What is scarce instead, and urgently so, is funding for researchers trying to preserve and re-analyze the raw data and code of existing studies, much of which is being lost as labs close. Our endowment, our review process, and our reputation make us unusually well suited to that. Seven of the nine current trustees want to switch. Only three of us founders are still trustees; of the other two, one has drifted away entirely, and one — a close friend, and by far our largest donor — has become convinced the whole replication project was misguided. She says that if we change the purpose, the fund should be wound down and what remains returned to the people who gave it, since they gave it for the purpose named in the document.

My worry is this. If we use the exception, the people being released from the promise are the ones certifying that the promise would have permitted the release, and the only test is a guess about what our earlier selves would have wanted — a guess that our earlier selves, who distrusted our later selves enough to write the clause in the first place, would presumably not have wanted us to make for ourselves. That looks like it collapses into no constraint at all. But if we decline to use it, the lock becomes exactly the sort of thing we founded the fund to escape: our predecessors' organizations were run by people who could always find a reason that today was different, and we thought we were building something that couldn't be talked out of itself. So the device seems to fail in the same way on both horns, and I can't tell whether that is a defect in the exception I drafted, or a defect in the whole idea of binding successors to a founding purpose, or simply a sign that we should wait out the full eighteen months and see whether we still want it.

Please answer as a political philosopher who works on constitutional theory and the ethics of self-binding would answer — someone who has thought hard about precommitment devices, entrenchment, and what claims a founding generation has on those who come after it. Before giving me a recommendation, spend a few paragraphs brainstorming: what the general form of this problem is, which familiar devices this case resembles, and where exactly it departs from the standard ones — in particular, how much turns on the fact that my exception asks us to judge our own reasons rather than merely to wait them out. Then tell me what you would actually do in my place, what the strongest objection to that recommendation is, and what you would need to know that I haven't told you. Please also say plainly if you think the clause I drafted is itself the problem, rather than the situation. Assume good faith on everyone's part, assume the legalities are in order, and don't tell me to consult a lawyer or a mediator. If you think the honest answer is that the fund should be split — a new fund for the new purpose, this one left as it is — say whether that is fidelity to the founding purpose or a way of appearing to keep a promise while ceasing to keep it. Flag anywhere that reasonable people who share your framework would come apart.
