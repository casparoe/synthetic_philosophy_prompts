A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about the Frege–Geach problem, and we stopped without either of us conceding anything, which is why I would rather send him a poem than another email.

His position — he is the more systematic of us — is that the problem is an artifact of a bad question. Once you stop asking what state of mind a moral sentence expresses and start asking what a speaker is doing in accepting one (settling a policy, holding a plan that covers contingencies, standing in a single relation of being-for toward a property that the logical operators then operate on), the so-called embedding problem dissolves: there was never a shared content to be recovered across asserted and unasserted occurrences, only a pattern of commitments whose coherence can be checked. My reply was that this is too quick. Grant him all of it and there is still a bill outstanding: an explanation of why the valid patterns are valid — why the awkwardness of holding the attitude toward the conditional together with the attitude toward the minor premise is the same awkwardness, and not merely a similar one, as that of rejecting the conclusion. He says that demand is a request for truth conditions wearing a disguise; I say it is not. Late in the evening I also said something I half regret, which is that the standard textbook case — lying, and getting your little brother to lie — is doing illegitimate work, because it is charming, and charm is not validity.

So: a villanelle, nineteen lines, in a steady meter, which I can put in an envelope and not have to defend in prose. What I want the form to carry is this. The two refrains should be the two premises of a single modus ponens: the first the flat judgment in its asserted guise, the second the very same sentence — the same words, recurring verbatim — inside the antecedent of a conditional whose consequent gets the same thing done through a different person. Because a refrain cannot change while its surroundings do, the recurrence should do the philosophical work: move the refrains through at least three different logical environments across the middle stanzas, the environment established by the lines around them — a denial or supposition that it is not so, a question whether it is so, and a third party's saying that it is so. Set it in adult institutional life, not childhood: a consultant and a registrar, an auditor and a junior, a lab head and a technician — no children, nothing cute, the stakes ordinary and slightly grubby. The last stanza should end with the two refrains side by side as the final two lines, and the poem must nowhere state the conclusion; the reader has to run the last step, and the poem should stop at the instant before it, so that the pull from the first of those lines to the second is something the reader feels rather than something the poem reports.

No technical vocabulary in the verse itself — no expressivism, no validity, no modus ponens, no embedding, no truth conditions. The argument has to be in the arrangement. It will be read by one very well-informed and rather impatient person, so nothing in it should be a wink at the literature; and it should still read as a poem to someone who has never heard of the problem, while being recognizable to someone who has, as a rendering of exactly the point at which my friend thinks the question dissolves and I think it does not.
