hey — I run a tiny reading group (about a dozen people: two philosophy grad students, a few ML folks, a therapist, some friends who just showed up) and we spent the last several weeks on theories of a person's good that idealize their preferences in some way. Roughly the menu we covered: simple present-desire views where nothing gets idealized; informed-desire views where you add full factual information; views where you also have to actually *experience* the thing (knowing the facts about pineapple isn't the same as tasting it); the Brandt-ish cognitive-psychotherapy version where desires that dissolve under repeated clear presentation of the facts get deleted; the dispositional "what you'd want under ideal reasoning" family; Railton-style objective-interest views where an ideally situated version of you wants something *for* you; Yudkowsky-style coherent extrapolated volition, where you run the extrapolation on everybody together until it converges into something coherent; ideal-advisor views; and views where rationality itself is partly moralized, so a perfectly coherent informed racist still doesn't get improved welfare by staying racist.

Everyone kept using the same vocabulary for different machinery, so before the next session I want a diagnostic: a "which idealization procedure are you actually committed to?" quiz. Ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, phrased so someone who's never read any of this can answer, and so that nobody can tell which label an answer points to — sort people by what they say about cases, not by which name they like. Absolutely no questions like "do you think your good consists in the satisfaction of your desires."

Cases I definately want in there, or ones like them: someone who has never tried a thing and doesn't want it and then loves it after trying; someone with a fear of flying who knows all the statistics; someone whose aspirations have quietly shrunk to fit a bad situation; a verdict delivered at 3am while exhausted; a person who after full information still has two fully coherent but incompatible lives available (the pianist self and the mathematician self, each fine once you're in it); a case where the extrapolation would replace a deep commitment you currently treasure; a case where present-you violently rejects what the extrapolated self endorses; a case where someone's awful values survive every informational and coherence improvement; a whole group whose individual extrapolations conflict; and a case where idealizing further keeps changing the answer instead of settling.

Two parts to the deliverable.

First, build the key from the logical space, not from the list of names. I care about these five splits, so use them as the branch labels of a small taxonomy: how much the procedure is allowed to *correct* versus merely *accommodate* your current values (call it corrective depth); whether the procedure has to hand back a single convergent answer or may hand back several; whether the idealized self still has to be *you* in any sense; whether present-you keeps any veto over the result; and whether extrapolating harder always leaves the answer fixed or can move it again. Then locate the named positions as leaves in that space, including all of the ones on my menu above plus a pluralist "no fact of the matter, multiplicity is irreducible" leaf. For empty combinations, say briefly whether they're empty because incoherent, unattractive, or just unoccupied because nobody bothered.

Second, the scoring key: map answer *patterns* (not individual answers) onto leaves, and be explicit about which pairs of leaves your ten questions can't seperate and why — including whether some extra case would pry them apart or whether they're genuinely the same view wearing different hats. Also flag answer patterns that come out mixed, and say what I should tell a person whose pattern is mixed rather than just assigning them whichever leaf they came closest to.
