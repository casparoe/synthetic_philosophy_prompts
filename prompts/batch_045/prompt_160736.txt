I'm not really a Hegel person — I work mostly in ethics — but I got talked into a chapter for a volume on Hegel and international relations, and I keep tripping over the same bit. It's the passage toward the end of the Philosophy of Right where he says, roughly, that war preserves the ethical health of a people the way the movement of the winds keeps the sea from going foul in a long calm: a lasting peace lets everyone sink into their private ends in civil society, and war is what stirs them out of it. My colleague in politics, who teaches Hegel as a kind of proto-realist, uses this to argue that Hegel thinks periodic war is a functional necessity for states — something a statesman should be willing to reach for, more or less like a safety valve — and that this is the hardheaded core of the theory. One of my PhD students reads the same lines the opposite way: they show Hegel is just a militarist, and the doctrine of ethical life is poisoned by it. I think both are moving too fast and that the image is doing most of the work.

So what I want is an audit I can actually use in the chapter. Lay out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the wind, of the sea, of the calm, of the foulness, of the health, of whatever cycle is supposed to be in play — and then where it breaks. Some breaks I can see but can't price: winds aren't agents, nothing is decided, there are no victims, and the sea isn't the sort of thing whose condition anyone could be answerable for; more importantly, the picture is cyclical and homeostatic, a repeating natural rhythm that keeps a system at a level, whereas everything else Hegel says about spirit is progressive and cumulative — freedom developing, not merely failing to stagnate — so the analogy may be quietly importing a naturalistic, non-teleological view that the rest of the system cannot have. Tell me which disanalogies matter for the two readings I described and which are noise, whether either conclusion survives with them on the table, and — the part I'm actually stuck on — what an analogy would have to be like for the realist reading to get any grip at all.

My own stab at a replacement is that war is less like weather and more like a contest for recognition with no court above the parties: what makes a state's claim serious is that it will stake the lives of its members on it, the way risking life is what separates a claim from a word in the earlier stuff about masters and servants. That feels closer to what Hegel needs, but I suspect it smuggles in a symmetry between the parties, a stable post-conflict relation that war mostly doesn't produce, and an endpoint, and it makes the whole thing look intentional in a way the wind image doesn't. Is it better? What does it import? If both are bad, give me one that carries whatever is actually defensible in the claim — that there is no judge above states, that the willingness of individuals to sacrifice is what makes the state's independence more than a legal fiction — without carrying the apologetics. Also: the editor says the line comes from the student lecture notes rather than Hegel's own numbered paragraph. I haven't checked, and I'd recieve pushback either way, but if that's right does it change how much weight any of this should bear, or is it only the kind of thing that matters to people who care about the Zusatz?
