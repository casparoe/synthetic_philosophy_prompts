I am a doctoral candidate in history, finishing the methodology chapter of a dissertation on district-level famine relief under a late-colonial administration, and I would like an audit of the position I have assembled before I commit it to print. Each of the following seems to me defensible on its own; what I cannot see is whether the set is stable, and I would rather find out now than in the viva.

The commitments:

(1) There is a determinate fact of the matter about what happened in the past, independent of anyone's account of it. Some accounts are simply false. The difference between a well-attested account and a denialist one is not a difference of perspective, and I want to keep saying that.

(2) Nothing counts as the facts of a period apart from a question. Which facts about the past become historical facts is a function of the historian's question, and the question is ours rather than the period's: a question-neutral, complete account of any stretch of time is not merely unattainable but incoherent. This is the Carr point that I take to be settled.

(3) Narrative form is brought to the material rather than extracted from it. The same set of accepted event-statements will standardly sustain several mutually incompatible emplotments, and the documentary record does not adjudicate among them. This is the Hayden White point, in the moderate form I can actually use.

(4) Because (1) and (3) together leave me without any way to grade whole narratives, I have been proposing a surrogate for objectivity in my own chapter — call it emplotment-invariance. Take the admissible emplotments of a given sequence; the claims that remain assertable under all of them are the ones the evidence underwrites; everything else is literary, standpoint-relative, or merely not yet decided. I need this criterion to do real work: if it returns only what was already in the event-statements, it tells me nothing I did not have from source criticism alone.

(5) Past agents are to be explained in terms they could in principle have availed themselves of. Appraising them by present moral standards is a category error, and anachronism in explanation is a defect rather than a stylistic choice.

(6) Objectivity is a property of a practice — source criticism, disclosure of procedure, adversarial peer critique — and not of a standpoint. There is no view from nowhere for the historian to occupy, and nothing is lost by admitting it.

(7) Where the record is systematically silent, the historian is sometimes obliged to go beyond what it states. My district records almost never name the recipients of relief and never in their own words. I take it that reconstructing them by inference from structure, and by reading the record against its own grain, is legitimate historical work, and that a history which stopped at what the archive states would reproduce the administration's blind spots as facts.

(8) One concrete datum, since abstract audits are easy to satisfy: in chapter four I argue that a particular settlement officer's relief policy failed. I want that to be a factual claim about the past, not a verdict relative to my question, my emplotment, or my politics.

Three requests about the shape of the answer. First, please discriminate: I strongly suspect that at least one apparent collision among these dissolves on inspection, and an answer that flags all eight as equally in tension is not useful to me. Second, do not simply tell me which commitment to drop; say what dropping it would cost me in the chapters I have already drafted. Third, I do not know where the difficulty is, and I have no attachment to (4) in particular — please do not read my having coined it as a signal that I take it to be the weak point.

Please structure the response as follows: a section headed Bottom line of roughly 120 words; then Genuine tensions, ranked by severity, roughly 450 words, ordered rather than listed as equals, with what would have to give in each case; then Apparent tensions that dissolve, roughly 250 words, saying why they dissolve; then Minimal repairs, roughly 350 words, including the single revision that would clean up the most; and finally a Confidence note of roughly 100 words in which you state how confident you are in the diagnosis overall and single out the one point you are least sure of.
