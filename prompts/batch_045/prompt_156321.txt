The paper I want you to read, reproduced as written apart from my having cut a paragraph of throat-clearing, runs as follows: Al-Ghazālī's target is Avicenna's view that causes bring about their effects by their natures, so that given the cause the effect follows necessarily and no other outcome is even possible. Against this he concedes (i) without argument: he never denies that in our experience fire is constantly followed by the burning of the cotton, and he is happy to speak of the sequence as holding. What he denies is (ii) and (iii). The burning is created with the contact rather than by it; no nature in the fire produces it, and the true agent is God alone. The possibility cases do the real work. Creating satiety without eating, or death without beheading, or burning without contact involves no contradiction, and whatever is not contradictory is within divine power; so the philosophers are not entitled to say that God could not bring about the one without the other. But the necessity claim also cannot be established by observation or a priori, since observation shows only that the one item follows the other and never any productive power or necessary tie between them, and we have no impression from which an idea of necessity could be derived; the connection is therefore at bottom a habit of expectation in us, which is why al-Ghazālī holds that causal necessity is a projection of the mind. Since the philosophers cannot prove the necessity, he has shown that no such necessity obtains, and hence that there is no causation among created things at all. This matters religiously because if the philosophers were right the familiar miracle narratives would have to be denied or allegorized away, and this is why al-Ghazālī counts this doctrine among the three points on which he declares the philosophers guilty of unbelief rather than mere innovation. The notion of habit is only a name for the fact that God has so far chosen to act uniformly; because His will is unconstrained and not bound even by His own custom, nothing guarantees that the next case will follow the pattern, so on al-Ghazālī's own view no prediction is rationally grounded and natural science collapses into a record of what has happened so far. That is the price of the position, and it is what makes it a form of skepticism. Hume reaches the same conclusion by the same route six centuries later, the difference being only that Hume drops the theological premises and so is left with a merely subjective necessity. The view is therefore best called occasionalism in the strict sense: created things are not causes at all but only occasions, and in every case the true cause is God.
