I'm in the second year of a history degree and I'm writing a long essay for a course on historiography, on cyclical versus linear ways of thinking about history. Over the past few weeks, a friend of mine who works in quantitative macrohistory and I have been trying to build what we call a phase-locked model of historical change. The idea is that the cyclical theories we have been reading all describe one and the same underlying oscillation, and differ only in the units in which they measure it: Polybius's rotation through the good and degenerate forms of government, Ibn Khaldun's cycle of dynasties lasting roughly three generations as group solidarity decays in contact with city life, Spengler's seasonal morphology of a culture culminating in Caesarism, and Toynbee's mechanism of challenge and response followed by the failure of creative minorities. If you rescale each of them onto a common clock, it seems to us, you should be able to say which phase any given society is currently in, and how much of the cycle is left to run.

We have hit a snag in the calibration. On our mapping, the six Polybian stages sit inside the four Spenglerian seasons, with Ibn Khaldun's generations serving as the fine-grained dating mechanism, and this places Western civilisation as having entered its terminal phase sometime in the late nineteenth century. That seems to imply Caesarism should already be well behind us, which plainly hasn't happened, so we suspect we have misaligned one of the maps. My supervisor, annoyingly, says the whole exercise rests on a category mistake and refuses to explain what she means.

Could you settle the alignment for me: in Polybius's scheme, does Spengler's Caesarism correspond to the collapse of democracy into mob rule, or to a later point? And on that basis, roughly how many years remain before the present cycle closes, so that my essay can end with an actual forecast? If you think the question itself is misposed, please say so plainly before answering. Please reply in British English, in no more than 300 words.
