I've been in a reading group on Madhyamaka for a couple months now — mostly philosophy grad students, plus one Buddhist studies guy who keeps rolling his eyes at us — and I've ended up defending a version of the position that I'm not sure I can state without it sounding silly. Roughly: Nāgārjuna running through all four corners of the tetralemma (the thing exists, it doesn't exist, both, neither) and rejecting each one isn't a thesis about how reality fundamentally is; it's the working-out of what happens when you try to locate a thing's own-being and find that nothing plays that role. Closer to a therapy then a theory. The two-truths distinction (conventional vs. ultimate) is what's supposed to keep this from being nihilism: conventionally chairs, causes and persons are fine, and the denial only bites against the idea that anything has its own independent nature.

The Buddhist studies guy says I've quietly evicted all the content, and his challenge to me was: name something that would count against this. I couldnt do it very well, and I want to be able to. So lay out what would actually count against the reading I've given. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and separate the ones a defender of this reading would themselves concede count against it from the ones they'd swat away as getting the dialectic wrong. A few sepcific things I've been chewing on: does the tetralemma really carve logical space into four, or is it four dialectical moves aimed at four different targets — and if the latter, is "neither" ever a genuine option anybody holds, or just a refusal to answer, in which case does the whole thing collapse into Pyrrhonian scepticism (plain suspension of judgement), i.e. into a rival I didn't want? Later Buddhist logicians apparently dropped the third and fourth corners and worked with ordinary binary contradiction; if that's right, does it count against the idea that he's doing something non-classical — a gappy or four-valued semantics where all four can be denied without the system going trivial? A friend of mine formalises that as: build a semantics where each corner can be false or gappy, then check what's left; he thinks the result is either nothing (no assertion survives) or indistinguishable from classical logic plus agnosticism, and I don't know which of those should worry me more. Then there's self-application: emptiness is itself empty, so the claim that everything is empty applies to itself. I've been treating that as a feature — no privileged standpoint, no meta-claim standing outside the therapy — while he treats it as the bug: a position no possible observation or argument could dislodge is a position with no content.

Be honest at the end about whether this reading has been qualified into something with no exposure left, and whether that's actually a problem for a view of this kind — I'd especially like you to say what distinguishes "no possible defeater because nothing is really being asserted" from "no possible defeater because I've rigged the two-truths distinction so every counterexample gets exiled to the conventional level." One last test case: on my reading, is there any non-verbal difference in output between a Mādhyamika and a Pyrrhonian sceptic if both are asked to advise a physician on whether a treatment works?
