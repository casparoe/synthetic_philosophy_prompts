Hey — I edit the ideas section at a magazine, and my desk is where every 400-page sweep of human history lands: books that start with the first upright footsteps and end with a swipe on a phone. In the last eighteen months I've had three pitches that were basically Sapiens again, one Diamond-with-attitude, and one Graeber-and-Wengrow-flavoured everything-was-a-deliberate-political-choice thing. My reviewers keep handing me "this felt reductive" or "the chapter on money is great," and I want something better than vibes before the next one lands.

So: write me the spec I should be holding these books to. Not a checklist of factual gotchas — I mean the desiderata for a grand narrative of human history *as an explanation*. What does one have to deliver to be worth a reader's belief for more than a week, as opposed to being a good read? Cases it must get right, distinctions it can't collapse, things it must be consistent with — with the reason behind each, in a form I can skim before a commissioning meeting.

Here are the half-formed ones I keep circling, so you can tell me which are real and which are just my taste: it should state its mechanism in a sentence that still does work after you delete the anecdotes; it should name something in the archaeological or genetic record that would sink it; it shouldn't let one variable carry the whole story across every timescale — the two-clocks problem, where the same explanation has to run at the speed of a generation and the speed of ten millennia, and nobody says how it scales; it should survive being applied to a region or period that isn't in its own examples (highland New Guinea, the Andes, wherever — pick your own); it shouldn't treat a category like the Agricultural Revolution, or civilization, or hunter-gatherers, as one thing when the variation inside it is bigger than the difference between it and the next one; it has to keep separate this is how it went from this is what it had to do; it has to be honest about whether it's grading the past by present moral standards; it has to deal with the fact that what survives is what was made of stone in dry places; it can't smuggle the endpoint in as the cause — we ended up here, therefore here is what was driving it; and it has to leave room for people refusing, exiting, and designing institutions on purpose, not just being pushed around by grains and germs.

Tell me which of those are genuine requirements, which are just one camp's prejudices, and which are negotiable given my purpose (a ~1,200-word review, for a general reader who will never see the footnotes, signed by name). Then the part I actually care about: where the list is jointly unsatisfiable — compression versus hedging, one-mechanism elegance versus covering the outliers, moral verdict versus no anachronism — and which single requirement knocks out the most of the books currently on my shelf. Keep the scoring of actual authors to a few lines at the end; the criteria are the deliverable.

Two more things. First: no bibliography, no citations, no reading list — I want your reasoning, not other people's authority, and I'm not going to chase footnotes. Second, and this matters because I'm printing it: mark, per requirement or in a short section at the end, which points are settled among the archaeologists, anthropologists and historians who actually know these periods, and which are live fights. I don't want to run something as consensus that half the field would dispute, and I don't want to run as a coin-flip something that's about as settled as this stuff gets. Be blunt about where the confidence runs out.
