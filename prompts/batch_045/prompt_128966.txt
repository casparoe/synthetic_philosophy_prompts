Short version of my situation: I'm a retired physical chemist — I spent most of my career fitting rate laws and doing reaction-kinetics modelling, so I used a great deal of mathematics for forty years without once wondering what kind of thing a number is — and about a year ago I joined a reading group at the public library where a retired philosophy lecturer walks us through bits of analytic philosophy. Last week the session was on Benacerraf, whom I'd never heard of. I went home and read around a bit, and I recieved a popular book on mathematics from my daughter that has a passage I can't get out of my head. It runs roughly like this:

'Benacerraf's dilemma is this. Our best theory of knowledge says that a person can only know something if there is a causal chain running from the fact that makes it true to the person who knows it. Numbers, if they exist at all, are abstract objects — outside space and time, causally inert — so no such chain can connect us to, say, the fact that every number has a successor. So we must choose: keep the causal theory of knowledge and give up on numbers existing and on mathematical statements being literally true, or keep mathematical truth and give up on mathematical knowledge. Benacerraf took the first horn, and his other famous argument supports the same conclusion: there are several equally good ways of identifying the natural numbers with sets, Zermelo's and von Neumann's for instance, and nothing decides between them, so there is no fact of the matter about which objects the numbers really are. The moral is that mathematics is invented rather than discovered: our mathematical knowledge is only knowledge of what the rules we ourselves laid down permit, which is why it carries none of the uncertainty that attaches to the natural sciences.'

Something in that feels like a bait and switch to me, but I don't have the vocabulary to say where, and the book is by a real mathematician, so I'm assuming it isn't simply wrong — maybe compressed in ways that mislead. I'd be grateful for a sentence-by-sentence audit: which bits a working philosopher of mathematics would be happy to say out loud, which they'd wince at, and which are just wrong. In particular I want to know whether the identification-with-sets argument is really doing the same work as the causal-knowledge argument or whether the summary is quietly fusing two separate arguments; whether Benacerraf himself actually ends up anywhere near 'mathematics is invented and therefore certain', or whether that last sentence is a slide into a position he never held; and how much of the dilemma survives if, as one of our group pointed out, hardly anyone defends a causal theory of knowledge these days. For context, my own instinct as a scientist is that the maths I used did seem to be *about* something I hadn't stipulated, and that the leap from 'we can't causally bump into numbers' to 'mathematical certainty is explained by our having made the rules up' smuggles in a lot. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion the book draws follows from the accurate parts, and briefly what the actual menu of live responses is — which horn people really take, and whether the argument is still thought to have force or is period piece from the 1960s and 70s. Oh, and one man at the group claims Benacerraf later backed away from his own positive proposal; I couldn't confirm that and don't want to lean on it, but if it's relevant to how I should read the summary I'd like to know.
