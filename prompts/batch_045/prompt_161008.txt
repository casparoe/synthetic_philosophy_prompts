I am the managing editor of a mid-sized journal in molecular and cell biology, and I am rewriting the section of our house style guide that deals with what we loosely call 'metaphorical language'. The people who have to enforce it are my copy-editors: they have degrees in biology but no philosophy, they process thirty or forty manuscripts a month, and they have roughly two minutes per flagged sentence before the production schedule slips. They can refer a problem to the handling editor, who has the final say, but in practice they must make the call themselves, and they need a sentence they can paste into an email to an author who has written back furious that we have flattened their prose — last year one such exchange ended up in a blog post accusing us of censorship, which is the sort of thing my editor-in-chief would rather not repeat. Our current rules are pure inherited precedent: we let through 'the genetic code', 'transcription', 'translation', 'reading frame', 'start codon' and 'misreading' without comment; we routinely strike 'the genome is the blueprint of the organism', 'genes for aggression', 'the cell interprets the signal', 'the instruction manual of life'; and we have no principled way of saying why one list is acceptable and the other is not, nor any way of handling the very large number of phrases that sit in between them — 'encodes', 'specifies', 'carries information', 'instructs', 'targets', 'reads out', 'programmes', 'switches on'.

What I want from you is a decision procedure my copy-editors can actually run, grounded in whatever the philosophy of biology has to say on this that is usable at two minutes per sentence. No literature review, no references, no history of the debate: just the distinctions that have to survive the simplification, and an honest note on which ones can be dropped. The distinctions I think matter are these, though the procedure should correct me if I have mis-described them: (i) the narrow sense in which there is a code at all — a mapping from codons to amino acids that is physically implemented by adaptor molecules rather than settled by convention or by what an interpreter intends — as against the broad sense in which a genome is called a programme, blueprint, recipe or text; (ii) 'information' in the sense of a reduction in uncertainty at a receiver, as against information about the phenotype, i.e. content that is about something and can in principle be wrong about it; (iii) the awkward fact that which content a stretch of sequence bears seems to depend on the machinery present — reading frame, splicing, chemical marks — so that the string on its own may not fix any message at all; (iv) whether anything in the cell occupies the role that would make 'error' and 'misreading' more than a façon de parler, or whether there is only causation with a norm projected onto it by us; (v) the difference between a representation of what is to be built and a trigger or resource that a developing system simply uses. I gather there are live disputes here — some people want content grounded in selection history, others want to treat the whole developmental system rather than the sequence as the unit — and I do not need the procedure to settle those, only to be robust to them. Give me the tests in the order they should be applied, what counts as a pass at each, where the procedure halts with a definite yes or no, and where it must hand the decision back to a person because the question is really one of our editorial policy, or of what the field's usage has already made standard, rather than one of fact. Mark clearly which steps are judgement calls dressed up as tests.

Then run it, as a copy-editor would, on three sentences from recent submissions: (a) 'the transcription factor reads out the regulatory information encoded in the enhancer sequence'; (b) 'the genome contains the complete programme for building the organism'; (c) 'methylation marks carry epigenetic information that the cell reads and that specify which genes to express'. For each, tell me what the procedure outputs, what it does to the sentence, and the one-line justification the editor can send the author. I also suspect that at least one of the phrases we currently pass — 'genetic information' is my own worry, but it may be 'the genetic code' — fails the tests you are going to give me; if so, say so plainly and explain why, because I would much rather hear it from you than discover it while defending house style to an author. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: the sentences it must simply leave alone, the cases where it will misfire badly, and the kind of claim it has no purchase on at all.
