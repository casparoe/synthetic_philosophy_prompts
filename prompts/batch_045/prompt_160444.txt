I'm putting together a short position paper for a workshop on character and moral psychology later this term — the organizers want each participant to circulate a numbered list of commitments in advance so that the respondents can press on specific theses rather than on our general sensibility — and before I send mine out I would like an audit rather than a defense. I have been teaching a graduate seminar in this area for some years and, after a colloquium last month, a visitor raised an objection I could not answer cleanly, which is what prompted me to try to write the list down properly. Here it is.

(1) I accept the situationist critique as an empirical thesis about the psychological bases of ordinary moral behaviour, at least in its moderate form: cross-situational consistency in trait-relevant behaviour is much lower than both folk psychology and neo-Aristotelian moral psychology have supposed, and a substantial share of the variance is accounted for by features of the setting that are morally irrelevant — whether one has just found a coin, whether one is in a hurry, whether the room smells unpleasant, whether an authority figure is nearby. I hold this while thinking the critics have overreached in one respect: I lean on the older, multiply-replicated demonstrations (the obedience experiments, the seminarian-in-a-hurry study, the phone-booth dime study) and I discount the later social-priming literature, which I take to have largely failed to replicate. I do not think the conclusion is that there is no such thing as character; I do think it rules out robust global traits of the sort virtue ethics has traditionally postulated.

(2) I am nonetheless a neo-Aristotelian of a fairly orthodox sort: the virtues are the fundamental normative notions, and rightness is not to be defined by way of consequences or rules with the virtues then derived from it. I also want the account of right action to remain agent-involving — the right thing to do here is what the virtuous agent would characteristically do here — and I resist replacing this with a list of rules read off the virtue names, because I think the appeal to the agent is what does the real work in hard cases.

(3) My way of keeping (2) given (1) is to reconstruct the virtues as fine-grained, situationally indexed dispositions — not honesty but honesty-under-conditions-of-type-C — with whatever unity a virtue has supplied by a stable if-then signature of the kind the personologists describe, rather than by sameness of behaviour across situations. I have also been toying with a further move I have started calling the scaffolding reply: much of what we praise in a praiseworthy person is their skill at arranging which situations they end up in — the reliable friend does not put herself in the position where returning the wallet is costly — so that on my view virtue includes a competence in managing one's circumstances, not merely a competence in responding to them.

(4) Whatever the reconstruction, I want virtue to keep its practical role: it must be something one can be educated into, aspire to, and deliberately cultivate over a life, and it must be able to underwrite our practices of holding people responsible. I am drawn to blaming for quality of will — for what the action showed about the agent's judgments and concerns — rather than for the act token or its downstream effects.

(5) I reject moral luck, at least in its constitutive and circumstantial forms: whether an act is right, and how much credit or discredit an agent incurs for it, should not ride on factors outside the agent's control. This is a large part of why I moved away from consequentialism, and I would give up a tidy decision procedure rather than give it up.

(6) Methodologically, I think claims about the structure of character are answerable to empirical psychology, and that where psychology delivers a clear verdict, philosophical accounts of character must accommodate it rather than reinterpret it away.

(7) I also want to be able to say that some people really are cross-situationally reliable in the way the tradition claimed — I can think of one or two — that this is a genuine feature of them rather than a run of situational luck, and that it makes them better people rather than merely luckier ones.

Two verdicts I am attached to, since they are where I feel the strain most: the seminarian who was in a hurry and stepped past the slumped stranger did something wrong, though he is less blameworthy than someone who stepped past her in cold blood; and the passer-by who helped because he had just found a coin did the right thing and is creditable for it — I do not want to discount his credit on the ground that the cause of his helpfulness was a morally irrelevant piece of luck, and I do not want to say either that he merely got lucky in doing what he did.

What I want from you is an audit of this package, not a survey of the literature. Tell me which pairs of these commitments are actually in tension and how badly, ordered by severity rather than all flagged equally, and for each tension say precisely which numbered item has to give and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the list. I take it that the familiar tension between (1) and (2) is less bad than it looks, and I would like you to confirm or deny that quickly and then spend the rest of your answer on the trouble I have not noticed — including the verdicts, which I suspect are where my stated principle fails to deliver. Please also mark any pair that only looks inconsistent and dissolves on inspection, so that I can see what I am entitled to keep, and finish by naming the single revision that would clean up the most and what it would leave me unable to say.
