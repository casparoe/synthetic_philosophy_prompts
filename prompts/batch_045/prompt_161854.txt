I'm a software engineer — fifteen years of backend and infrastructure work, no philosophy beyond a couple of MOOCs — and for about six years I've been having the same argument with a friend who works in quantitative research. It is the argument about whether market prices actually know anything. Neither of us has moved in three years, and I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict.

The concrete setting, since I think it matters: at my last company I ran an internal prediction market for two years. The contracts settled on machine-checkable values — whether a release train went green by a given date, the quarter's signup count, whether an incident review would name a root cause — small stakes, a few hundred engineers, full order history. Two camps formed around what to make of it.

My friend's camp treats the no-trade result as the correct null hypothesis: given common priors, common knowledge of rationality, and purely speculative motives, expected volume is zero, so any observed volume is evidence of something outside the model — risk-sharing needs, liquidity shocks, genuinely new information arriving, or priors that differ. None of that implies the price is wrong. And because deviations get arbitraged, they are bounded by transaction costs and risk-bearing capacity: prices are, at worst, the best estimator available. He also likes the impossibility argument — if prices fully reflected all information, nobody would pay to gather it, so a market cannot be perfectly informationally efficient; there must be exactly enough residual inefficiency to pay for information production.

My camp reads the same theorem as a reductio. Real markets, including mine, show enormous volume on days with no news at all, which suggests the assumptions fail in the way that matters: people trade on narrative, on noise, on the disposition effect, and prices can sit far from any defensible estimate for months because arbitrage is capital-constrained and every model of "fundamental value" is itself contested.

The stalemate has a recognizable texture. Every anomaly I produce gets re-described as a risk premium, a transactions-cost artifact, or something found in-sample; every efficiency claim I press on gets met with the point that efficiency cannot be tested without a model of expected returns, so no test bites. We cite the same order book. What I find suspicious is that the same formal result is being used in two opposite directions — as a null model whose violations need explaining, and as a reductio of the model's assumptions — and I can't tell whether that is a genuine inconsistency or just two legitimate uses of an idealization.

My hypothesis, which I can't fully defend, is threefold: that "the market is efficient" is doing two jobs that come apart — prices are the best available estimator versus there is no exploitable edge after costs and risk — so the dispute is partly verbal; that it is partly a dispute about which cases count as data, anomalies with a story attached or the whole distribution of price paths; and that underneath both there is an ordinary empirical question about the size and half-life of deviations and the amount of arbitrage capital actually available.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, really? Verbal, methodological, or a hidden empirical question? Is the question "do prices know anything" ill-posed as stated, or merely hard? And here is my candidate for progress, which I want you to price rather than applaud: build a reference market where the settlement value is machine-checkable, the data-generating process is published and fully specified so priors are common by construction, and before any trading opens each participant's full probability distribution is elicited with a proper scoring rule — so priors can be measured rather than assumed, and residual trade can be split into information-driven, miscalibration-driven (scoreable as error, not merely a different prior), and risk-preference-driven components. Given your diagnosis, would that settle anything, or does it smuggle in exactly what is contested?

Please skip the literature survey and the citations — I've read the surface material and the disagreement isn't about who said what. I want the structure of the disagreement named precisely, an honest account of whether progress is available, and if it is, whether it comes as a distinction, a formal result, an experiment like the one above, or a change of question.
