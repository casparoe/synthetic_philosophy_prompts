I sit on the grants committee of a small foundation that funds work on farmed-animal welfare, and I have become uneasy about the machinery we use to rank applications. The practice is this: for every intervention we estimate the number of animals affected, the probability that animals of that kind are sentient at all, and a welfare-range multiplier meant to capture how much welfare — positive and negative — a member of that species can realize relative to a human baseline. The multiplier is set by a scientific panel that leans heavily on neural metrics: neuron counts in the relevant forebrain structures, packing density, and a few connectivity proxies. In practice this means a grant helping a few thousand pigs outranks one helping a far larger number of chickens; that proposals concerning farmed fish or shrimp, where the numbers affected run into the billions but the brains are tiny, score very low; and that octopus work punches well above its weight. I came onto the board from a finance background and I keep being told that these multipliers are just philosophy and should not be driving allocation decisions, which is part of why I am asking.

What I cannot get straight is whether the practice commits us to a view I am not sure I hold, and whether its parts are even consistent with one another. Three things in particular. First, the same committee would be appalled by the suggestion that we rank grants to human beneficiaries by brain size or neuron count; we treat every human beneficiary as counting for one, full stop. Either that is flatly inconsistent with the cross-species case or there is a principled difference between them, and I want to know what that difference would have to be and whether it can bear the weight. Second, a trustee argues that what we really care about is not how much a creature can suffer but how fine-grained its affective life is — the number of distinguishable good and bad states it can occupy — and that this can come apart from neuron count: she points to birds, whose small forebrains pack neurons far more densely than a much larger mammalian brain does, and insists that a crow's possible states are richer than the mass of its brain suggests. Everyone on the committee agrees the underlying figures are contested; for my question, simply grant her the ranking. Third, and this is what has actually split us: the same trustee has raised the question of subjective time. Small, fast animals appear to resolve the world at a much finer temporal grain than we do, so that an objective minute may contain many more moments of experience for them than it does for us. If that is right, then multiplying an intensity by an objective duration may be the wrong currency altogether, and our cost-effectiveness numbers could be wrong by an order of magnitude in either direction — or so it is claimed.

So my question is: what view of moral status, and of welfare, does a ranking practice like ours actually presuppose? I want the implicit theory reconstructed as precisely as you can, and I want to know which parts of it the practice strictly requires as opposed to merely sitting comfortably with — I suspect, for instance, that some critics hear welfare range and conclude we have adopted a hierarchical view on which some creatures matter less, when what may really be going on is something closer to weighing equal interests equally, and I would like that distinction drawn cleanly. I also want to know where our practice is philosophically underdetermined: which rival theories of welfare, or of moral standing, would license exactly the same grant rankings, so that our spreadsheet settles nothing between them. And I want to know what would have to change — in what we measure, and in what we say when a critic accuses us of neuron-counting speciesism — if we accepted each of the three challenges above rather than the others.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning: no preamble, and no restating my question back to me.
