I'm a third-year in a joint History and Philosophy of Science programme, and this term I'm in a twelve-person seminar on periodisation: the first half is spent on the "Middle Ages / Renaissance / Scientific Revolution" triad, the second on whether any of them is anything more than a filing convention. Last week a classmate pitched a paper proposal titled "Why did the Scientific Revolution happen in Europe and not elsewhere?" and our professor — an historian of early modern astronomy who is visibly tired of that question — replied with a flat "there was no Scientific Revolution" and moved on without saying which of several different things he might have meant. The proposal deadline is Friday and my classmate is now rewriting in a panic. The same move recurs in his margin comments: he also says that the Middle Ages were an invention of the Renaissance, and that the phrase "Scientific Revolution" only became standard historiographical currency in the mid-twentieth century. (A well-known 1996 book by Steven Shapin is famous for denying that there was any such thing and then devoting itself to it anyway, which he cites as the gesture he's making.) What I can't work out is how much of this is deep and how much is a conversation-stopper: the sentence is doing real work in that seminar room, namely discrediting a whole research project, and I want to know which precise thesis would actually have to be true for it to do that work.

I've made a start and I'd like you to push back on it. My rough typology of what "there was no Scientific Revolution" could mean: (i) a dispute only about where the boundaries fall, not about whether anything happened; (ii) gradualism — change was real but not accelerated or discontinuous enough to be "revolutionary"; (iii) nobody at the time thought they were living through one, so the period has no contemporaneous self-understanding; (iv) the "wrong object" reading — the relevant changes were in printing, patronage, craft, religion, colonial encounter, so the boundary around "science" is itself the error; (v) export failure — the periodisation is licensed by astronomy and mechanics but leaks badly when applied to chemistry, medicine, natural history, art or political economy; (vi) causal inertness — no causal generalisation in the history of the period actually quantifies over the unit, so deleting the label costs nothing explanatory; (vii) retrojection — the period exists only as a device in a later progress narrative; (viii) full eliminativism — there is no robust phenomenon there at all, only selective attention; (ix) the ideological reading — the concept encodes a story about Europe's unique rational breakthrough and therefore distorts whatever it touches. I've also invented two distinctions I quite like and can't tell whether they're doing any work: a *forced cut* versus an *optional cut* (a boundary is forced if any historian, working at that grain and in any lineage, would have to place a break roughly there; optional if a historian could tell the story without it), and the thought that periods might simply be vague, grain-relative objects, so that "there was no Scientific Revolution" is neither straightforwardly true nor false until you fix a grain, a domain and a purpose — analogous, perhaps, to saying there is no heap.
