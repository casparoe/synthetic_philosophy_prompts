I teach an undergraduate seminar on social epistemology: 18 students, one 75-minute session, with no reading assigned for that day. I want them to feel the difference between two ways a group can end up confident and wrong: one where the contrary evidence simply never reaches you (omission or exclusion), and one where it does reach you but the group has arranged things so that you don't trust the people who bring it — so that supplying the missing evidence fixes nothing. Design an in-class activity that produces both effects in the room without either one being named until afterward.

Constraints. Keep the subject matter fictional and nonpolitical — a made-up municipal or campus controversy of your choosing — so students don't bring prior commitments with them; I'll supply whatever the true answer is. Use small real stakes (chocolate, or points on a low-value quiz), not hypothetical payoffs. At least one condition should collapse in the expected way once the students are exposed to what they were missing, and at least one should resist that exposure. I also want a third moment that complicates the lesson: students should hit a case where the group's refusal to trust an outsider tracks a genuine difference in the reliability of sources, so the session cannot end with the takeaway that trusting your own people is always irrational.

Give me the rules, the materials, any roles, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the mechanisms at work. Be committed: pick the controversy, the payoff scheme, and the exact wording of whatever device discredits the outside sources, and say why you chose each. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it, and be frank about which feature of the distinction the activity cannot stage, so that I know what I have to say myself.
