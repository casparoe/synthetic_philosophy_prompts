I'm in my fourth year, working on modal epistemology from the consciousness side (yes, the zombie argument is load-bearing in my second chapter, no, I don't want to talk about it), and I'm TAing intro modal logic this term, which means I've been going round on this with a friend from the maths department who doesn't believe in any of it. He sent me this version of an argument last night and I've been loosing sleep over it, partly because I can't find the exit and partly because the conclusion would sink the chapter.

(1) The bridge, in its careful form: if a scenario S is ideally conceivable — positively, not merely not-ruled-out-by-us-so-far, i.e. it survives ideal a priori reflection — then S is metaphysically possible.
(2) Consider a localized modal scotoma. Not the boring global worry that our whole modal faculty is broken; he grants that one is self-undermining, you can't run that doubt from the inside. His version is local: some restricted region of modal space where ideal reflection is systematically blind, so that a scenario we cannot exclude is in fact impossible. And he insists the scotoma is *positively* conceivable, not just negatively, because you can model it: imagine a reasoner indistinguishable from us in every respect that is a priori checkable — same concepts, same idealization procedure, same verdicts on everything — except that on one of them it is wrong. Grant that much, he says, and you've granted the scotoma is ideally conceivable. A wrinkle I noticed: he wants the blind region left unspecified. He won't say which region, and says he doesn't have to.
(3) So by (1) the scotoma scenario is possible.
(4) If it's possible, then for all we can tell a priori the zombie scenario sits inside the blind region — so (1) gives us no grip on the case my chapter needs.
(5) And (4) generalizes: take any a priori modal argument you like — Kripke-style cases like water and H2O, or something as basic as nothing being red and green all over at once, or the necessity of elementary arithmetic truths — the same move applies. We might be blind there too.
(6) But a modal epistemology that leaves us with no modal knowledge anywhere is worthless. So (1) is false, or at least it can't do any work: conceivability is not a guide to possibility, and the chapter is dead.

I'd much rather keep (1) than swallow (6), but I don't want to just dig in either. My guess is something slips between (4) and (5), although (2) to (3) also feels like it moves faster than it should, and I genuinely can't tell whether this is an invalid argument or a valid one with an uncomfortable premise. So: tell me which step you reject — or tell me it's valid and which premise is cheapest to drop — explain why the step looked fine, and price the alternatives for me. Specifically I want to know whether the zombie argument survives in any weakened form, and if there's a fix, whether the scotoma worry just reappears one level up, since whatever lets me certify that a scenario is positively conceivable is itself something I'd be certifying by a priori means.
