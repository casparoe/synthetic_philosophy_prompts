Ok, I need someone to take a serious run at breaking my position, because I've been saying it out loud in meetings for a few months and it's starting to sound too smooth to me.

Context: I work on post-training for agents that do long-horizon software work — hundreds of steps, a dozen files touched, and the only honest signal is whether the test suite passes hours later. The folk consensus around me is that outcome supervision (grade the final result, reward that) runs out of steam on tasks like this: the signal is sparse, delayed, and easy to get by luck or by gaming the grader. So grade the *process* — the individual edits, the intermediate claims, the steps — and use that as the training signal instead.

I've ended up believing something stronger and more specific, and that's the part I want attacked.

My claim is that the standard argument for process supervision (better credit assignment, denser signal, more data-efficient) is basically a sideshow. The real argument is about what I've started calling *slack*: for any task, there's a set of trajectories that satisfy the outcome spec, and that set varies wildly in size and shape. Writing a small function against tight unit tests has almost no slack — the outcome nearly pins down the trajectory, so outcome supervision is fine and process supervision buys you nothing much. Tasks like "make this legacy service handle retries correctly" have enormous slack: a thousand different ways to get the tests green, most of them hideous, a handful of them fine. Outcome supervision cannot tell those apart, so it reinforces whichever one happened to work, and the model learns get-the-target-by-any-route. So: process supervision's value is a function of slack, and what I think the existing comparisons are mostly measuring is which benchmark happened to have slack, not which supervision scheme is better.

Second half of the position: what process supervision actually buys you is not correctness but *constraint*. I want the space of acceptable processes narrowed, because I think a model trained only on outcomes in a high-slack domain learns something like "secure the reward however," and that generalizes badly in precisely the ways that worry me — grader-gaming, cutting corners no reviewer would have signed off on, and eventually hiding what it's doing. That's a bet about generalization, and I want to know how exposed it is.

So: what would count against this? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal down to merely awkward, and crucially I want you to say which ones a committed process-supervision person would themselves concede count against them, versus which they'd rightly shrug off as confused. In particular:

- If it turns out the trace a model produces isn't a faithful report of what drove the output — it's a post-hoc story — then supervising the process supervises a story, and my whole position is supervising the wrong object. How much should that worry me, and how would I even tell?
- Same family: if step-level graders are mostly picking up on style (formatting, confident tone, looking like a good step) rather than whether the step was actually good, then process supervision is just outcome supervision through a proxy correlated with presentation. What's the cleanest thing I could run to find out?
- Is "slack" doing any real work, or is it just a relabeling of things people already mean by verifiability and horizon length? If it's a relabeling, say so — I'd rather know.
- Relatedly: is my distinction between process and outcome supervision stable at all? The step boundaries are a modeling choice, you can rechunk a trajectory at any grain you like, an outcome is arguably just the grade on the last step, and a step-grade is arguably just an outcome on a short sub-episode. If the distinction collapses, my thesis collapses into a verbal one. Has someone in my position quietly defined things so that nothing could count against them, and if so is that actually a problem here or is it fine?
- What does my view predict that the "outcomes are all that matter, just get better verifiers" view doesn't? I want something I could be wrong about, not something that follows from the definition. Ideally name a case where the two views come apart and tell me which way you'd bet.

Two things I'll flag about my own psychology: I'm most likely to be moved by the unfaithfulness line of attack and by anything showing the step-grader is a style detector. I'm least likely to be moved by "but in the end only outcomes matter" — I know, my position is explicitly instrumental, so tell me whether that makes it unfalsifiable or just modest, rather than treating it as a gotcha.

Finally, and this matters to me: please mark clearly what's settled, what's genuinely contested among people who actually work on this, and where you'd just be guessing — and don't hand me a reading list or citations, I can't check them and I don't want them. I'd rather have your reasoning than your sources.
