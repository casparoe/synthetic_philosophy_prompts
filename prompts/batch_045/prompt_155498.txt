So I'm the undergrad rep on our university's Senate Communications Standards Working Group, and this term I'm also taking a seminar on language and power where we did two weeks on Orwell — the essay on English prose and the Newspeak appendix — which is basically why I got handed this. I have to circulate something before Thursday's meeting.

Backstory: in March the provost's office emailed the whole campus to announce that the classics and linguistics majors were being closed and three language teaching units merged. The email called this an academic portfolio realignment, said affected staff would be transitioned into alternative roles, and never once said who had decided it or how many people were losing their jobs. There was a sit-in, and the concession the administration offered was that we'd write a policy on clear language in official university communications. The draft on the table is essentially a banned-words list maintained by the communications office.

In seminar we kept coming back to a distinction that I think kills this draft but that I can't articulate well enough to survive the meeting. Orwell's worry about Newspeak is usually read as a worry about vocabulary shrinking, but the mechanism he actually describes is different: ready-made phrases assemble themselves and do your thinking for you, so a list of banned terms just gets you fresh jargon in a year while everyone feels virtuous. Our TA had us apply a test — rewrite the sentence so it names an agent, a verb, and the people affected; if you can't, the sentence is doing political work. Then there's the worry on the other side, which the comms director will definately raise: a mandated plain house style is an orthodoxy too, and Orwell's target was people defending the indefensible, not long words as such.

Can you draft the memo I'll circulate? It should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame and some way of telling afterwards whether it's working. I'm inclined to reject the banned-words list and propose something else, but argue it honestly and say so if you think the draft is salvageable. Take the strongest opposition seriously instead of just restating that Orwell disliked jargon: the comms director's position is that HR and legal constraints require vagueness about personnel matters, and that vagueness is sometimes kindness — announcing a colleague's death, for instance; the faculty rep's position is that the university shouldn't be policing how academics write. We've all done the Orwell so no citations or reading list please, and don't assume I can look anything up.

One format thing, since this goes straight into the agenda pack: continuous prose only, no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, and lead with the recommendation in your first sentence or two, then the reasoning, the way you'd write a long email.
