Over faculty lunch today two colleagues of mine spent forty minutes going at each other about whether it's ever fine to hold a moral belief purely on someone else's word. I mostly just ate my sandwich and said nothing useful, and now I've been volunteered as referee for next week. Here's the exchange more or less as it went.

M: Deferring on a moral question is defective in a way deferring about, say, the train timetable isn't. A moral judgment is supposed to be backed by an appreciation of the considerations that make it true. Take it on testimony and you get a true belief with none of the understanding attached, and then you can't act for the right reasons either.

D: You're running two claims together. Testimony plainly transmits moral knowledge — a kid who believes slavery is wrong because her teacher said so knows something, and there's nothing defective about her. Where moral deference goes badly it goes badly for ordinary reasons: the speaker isn't reliable, or the hearer had no reason to trust her. Nothing there is special to moral content.

M: The kid isn't the case I care about; she's still acquiring the concepts. Take someone who has them: no view on whether buying fast fashion is wrong, roommate says it is, he adopts it, and when you ask him why he just points at the roommate. He can't produce a single consideration himself. That alienation is the defect, and it's a defect in him, not just in his informant.

D: He may have perfectly good higher-order reasons though — the roommate has been right about adjacent questions he could check for himself, and she knows more about the supply chains then he does. And deferring can beat going with your gut when your gut is mostly a product of how you were raised. Also, "can't produce a consideration" is too strong: he can repeat the reasons she gave him, which is more than most of us can do for half our beliefs.

M: Then here's the hard case: chains. Suppose the roommate got her view by deferring, and so did everyone up the line, and there is nobody at the top who ever did any moral thinking for herself — the chain never cashes out. So deference can transmit moral justification but it can't originate it. Somewhere there have to be people who actually judge. That means judging is the real thing and deference is at best a second-best, which is all I ever claimed.

D: Fine, but that's not a fact about morality, it's a fact about testimony in general — no method is self-sufficient, and moral deference has to rest on other sources like everything else does. Your chain point shows deference needs a base, not that it's defective. And if you want to worry about structure, worry about the mirror image instead: I trust you on the fast-fashion question, you trust me, and we each take the other's confidence as confirmation. Two-person loop, no base at all.

Please go through this move by move and tell me who's right about what, not just who wins. I also want a separate verdict on the two structural cases they end on — the chain that never terminates in anyone thinking for herself, and the two-person loop — since both of them admitted those were new to them. And say what, if anything, they're both missing.
