I've somehow ended up recording epsiode 31 of a very small political-theory podcast (about 300 downloads an episode, mostly my old department and my mum) in a borrowed study room, and next week's guest is a legal historian — call her Dr. Adaeze Renner, manuscript titled *Unfinished Business* — whose whole thing is that Frederick Douglass's antislavery reading of the Constitution isn't the embarrassing late-career softening people usually treat it as, but the most coherent theory of constitutional interpretation anyone produced in the nineteenth century. Her reconstruction of it: strict construction, because the text grants no power anywhere to treat persons as property; then a purposive reading off the Preamble's commitments to justice and to securing the blessings of liberty, which she says supplies a presumption in favour of liberty whenever a clause is ambiguous; then a third move she calls aspirational originalism, where the meaning is fixed at ratification but its realization is progressive and tracks the character of the political community — which is why, on her account, federal power to override the Black Codes during Reconstruction was there all along. She also has a name for the Thirteenth Amendment, she calls it the ratchet: it locks in what the text already meant rather than changing its meaning.

What I want from you is eight to ten questions that make her actually work — not the warm-up stuff, and please nothing like "what should people read to get into this," since she'd rattle off a list of books I can't follow up on. Concretely: one on the reversal itself, since in the 1840s and 50s he was publicly on Garrison's side, treating the Constitution as a proslavery compact not worth the paper it was printed on, and by the Glasgow speech he's arguing the opposite; frame it so she has to separate a principled change of mind from one that arrived suspiciously close to his turn toward electoral politics and toward Lincoln. I'm guessing her answer is that the change was forced by having to answer Taney's opinion in the Dred Scott case — give me the follow-up that turns that into a problem rather than a vindication. One on the clauses that hurt her: the fugitive-labour clause, the three-fifths ratio, the twenty-year protection of the slave trade, the due process clause. She'll say the document never uses the word slave and that those clauses are limitations rather than grants of power, so I need the follow-up that presses whether that test is doing real work — e.g. whether the same strict method would also refuse to protect things everyone agrees are in there. One on the redundancy puzzle: if the Constitution was already antislavery, why was an abolition amendment necessary, and if her answer is the ratchet move, how is a ratchet different from admitting the meaning wasn't legally operative beforehand? And one on what I keep thinking of as the enforcement-dependence worry — Douglass himself kept saying things along the lines of a government's character following the character of the people, and that the federal arm had been too short to reach into the states — so if realization depends on who is holding power and what they'll enforce, what is the interpretive argument actually buying us, and how is this not living constitutionalism in originalist vocabulary? I'd love a follow-up where I run her method on a clause whose results she'd hate: someone using silence plus the Preamble to get a constitutional right to housing, or to strike down the entire administrative state, and asking what principle she has left to say no with.

Two more things. She has one move she falls back on in every other interview I've listened to: when you press her on something uncomfortable she says something like "that's a question about Douglass's political strategy, not about constitutional meaning." I'd like the questions ordered so that escape hatch gets harder and harder to reach for, with at least one that takes the distinction apart directly. And I want to end on what would change her mind — what she'd have to turn up in the archive, a letter, an 1850s editorial, a shift in the timeline, to give up the claim that the late position grows continuously out of the early one — plus one on how much of the argument is really Douglass's own, as opposed to him popularising the antislavery-constitution case that abolitionist lawyers in the Spooner mould had been making for a decade or more. For each question, add a line or two on what you expect her to say and what the follow-up should be, because I am genuinely not fast enough on my feet to improvise.
