I write novels, and for the past three years I have also been the English translator of a long verse novel — something over six hundred stanzas, six lines each, rhymed AABCCB, in a source language I will leave unnamed whose lines are built on a fixed syllable count that its readers hear very precisely. I deliver roughly forty stanzas a month, which means a crux I cannot settle by instinct comes up two or three times a week; I have about an hour per crux, the author reads no English and answers my questions through a friend by email, usually a week later and usually in a single sentence, and my publisher has agreed to exactly one page of endnote for the whole book. In my notebooks I have been sorting the original's formal constraints into three kinds: what a reader can hear (the rhyme, the syllable count, a refrain that returns every ninth stanza), what a reader can see on the page (the stanza shape, and an acrostic — the initial letters of the stanzas, read in order across the book, spell a line of dedication to the poet's dead brother), and what a reader can only be told about (the original's grammatical gender, which settles the addressee as feminine in a way that English nouns and pronouns do not, and settles it in a passage where the novel is carefully not settling anything). My working rule has been to keep what can be heard, translate what can only be seen, and footnote the rest, and it has now failed me badly more than once. What I want instead is a procedure I can actually run at the desk: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which it stops and tells me to keep the constraint, the point at which it stops and tells me to let the constraint go, and — importantly — the point at which it hands the decision back to a person, to me or to the author, because what is really at issue is what the book is for rather than what the original says. Please also say which distinctions have to survive being simplified for someone using this under time pressure and which can be dropped without loss, and be plain about where the procedure will misfire.

Run it on three cruxes I am facing now. First, the acrostic: in English the brother's name would have different initials, so I can preserve the name, preserve the device, or preserve the fact that a reader can discover something hidden, but not all three — and the page budget will not let me explain all of this to the reader. Second, a stanza where holding the syllable count forces an English word order that reads like a translation, while letting it go lets the line sound like something a person would think; the rhyme is easy here, the count is not. Third, the gendered address: the original fixes the addressee as grammatically feminine, and in English any noun or pronoun I choose will say something the original, on my reading, is deliberately not saying. I would rather you not soften the outcome: if the honest result of your procedure is that the rhyme scheme has to go, or that the acrostic cannot survive into English, say so and tell me what I lose by that. And I would like at least one of the three worked cases to come out in a way I will dislike, since that is the only real test of whether the procedure is deciding anything.

I am asking for this to be grounded in the argument that form can be the part of a poem that paraphrase cannot reach — the position associated with Hofstadter's 1997 book in which he drags one short Renaissance French love poem through dozens of English versions — and against the objection a critic raised at the time, that the poem in question is a charming trifle whose charm is inseparable from its elegant form, so that a translator who preserves the form at the cost of the words has preserved the wrong half. I have read both sides and neither tells me what to do on Tuesday. I am at a residency with no library and no reliable connection, so please argue the steps out rather than pointing me toward books, critics, or further reading: I need reasons, not sources. Finally, since I will be defending these choices to an author who cannot read the result and to an editor who will want certainty, please attach to each step a few words on how confident you are that it is the right instruction, and end by singling out the single claim you are least sure of — ideally the one where a working literary translator or a translation theorist would be most likely to tell me I have got it wrong.
