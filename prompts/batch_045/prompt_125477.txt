Over the past few months I've been reading Hume's essays on money, interest, and commerce in the evenings. I have no philosophy background — I build data infrastructure for a living — so I've skipped the secondary literature and instead tested his claims against cases I make up, going with my gut on each one and then asking what principle, if any, my verdicts add up to.

The verdicts:

1. A kingdom with no foreign trade, where every coin is duplicated overnight: nothing real happens, prices double, nobody is better off.
2. The same kingdom, but the money arrives as a thin trickle into the hands of farmers and merchants over thirty years: the country really is more industrious at the end, and the gain does not disappear once prices have fully adjusted.
3. A treasure fleet lands, and the court spends it on imported luxuries: no lasting gain, and domestic workshops end up worse off.
4. The windfall goes entirely to exporters, who park it abroad and never spend it at home: nothing happens inside the country at all.
5. A one-time money creation used to buy grain from abroad during a harvest failure: a real benefit — real food arrives, people live — even though the country produced nothing extra.
6. A neighbor grows much richer and more productive in every line we export: I want to say we gain too, but I keep going back and forth once their manufactures get so advanced that they undersell us everywhere.
7. After the overnight doubling in case 1, I don't think interest rates fall; and if the new money mostly lands with indebted heirs who borrow in order to consume, I think interest rises. So the rate looks to me like it tracks who wants to borrow, who is able to lend, and what habits of commerce and thrift prevail — not how much money there is.

Cases 1 and 5 are the pair I can't reconcile: if money changes nothing real, the famine purchase shouldn't work; if it can command real goods from abroad, case 1 shouldn't be a wash. My tentative principle is that money matters only through the real reallocations it causes in the interval before prices and wages have adjusted, so who receives it, what they spend it on, and how fast prices move decides everything — but I can't see how that yields the lasting gain in case 2, or whether the principle should be asymmetric, since wages and prices seem much stickier downward than upward, which would make a sudden outflow destructive in a way a sudden inflow is not.

Please state a principle, or a short list of candidate principles, precisely enough to apply. For each clause, say which case motivates it; say what it predicts for a country whose currency foreigners are eager to hold, so that the interval never seems to close; and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it forces me to give up.
