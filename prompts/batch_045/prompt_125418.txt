I work as a backend infrastructure engineer and have no formal philosophy background — just a couple of podcasts and an argument I can't stop thinking about. My team is rewriting our incident postmortem template, and we got stuck on one field, currently labelled "why the system did this." One colleague wants to write things like "the autoscaler chose to shed load because its cost model said that was cheapest"; another insists that nothing chooses anything, that given the build, the state, and the inputs the output was the only thing that could have happened, and that saying "it chose" is just a way of grading the policy rather than describing an event. I eventually recognized this as the free will dispute, but everything I can find is written in vocabulary I don't have. So I'd like a translation. Please restate, as faithfully as you can, each of the following four positions in the vocabulary I actually work in: deterministic and nondeterministic processes; reproducible runs and seeds; race conditions and flaky tests; rollbacks, hot patches, config flags, and canaries; tests as counterfactual executions of the same artifact under different inputs; code review; CODEOWNERS files and service ownership; root-cause analysis; audit logs and observability; legacy code nobody on the team fully understands; staging versus production; and the distinction between "the behavior was in the code" and "the behavior was an accident of timing." The positions: (i) being able to do otherwise is compatible with everything being determined, because "could have" is just a counterfactual — the agent would have acted differently if its reasons or aims had been different, in the way that a fully deterministic service would have kept the load if its config had told it to; (ii) no it isn't, because the agent's reasons are themselves outputs of a fixed past and fixed laws, and you can't have control over the past, so no such counterfactual is really up to the agent; (iii) so there has to be genuine indeterminism somewhere inside the decision itself, such that the very same state and inputs could issue in either outcome; (iv) the metaphysical question is beside the point, because what we are really doing with "she could have done otherwise" is deciding whether to go on treating her as someone who can be held answerable — praised, blamed, resented, thanked, excused — and that practice does not wait on the causal facts. I believe (i) is called compatibilism and (iii) libertarianism, but please don't lean on the labels; work from the descriptions.

There is a second thing I want, and it's the part I care about most. In incident review I use two different tests, and they seem to come apart in a way that maps onto this debate. The rerun test: same build, same state, same seed — does the bad thing happen again? If yes, it was "in the code" and I attribute it to the artifact. The patch test: would some small, well-motivated change to the code or config have prevented it? That is a counterfactual over nearby versions of the program, and it can come out positive even when the rerun test says the behavior was fully determined. And then there's a case I have no name for, which I'll call the orphan problem: a service nobody owns. Both tests pass, the causal story is completely clear, and yet the postmortem stalls, because there is no one to whom the outcome belongs. Please tell me which of the four positions each of my tests is really a test for, and whether the orphan problem is a fifth thing the debate has a name for or something my vocabulary invented. Then settle this for me if you can: a flaky test that fails about one run in two hundred because of a race condition. Nobody on my team thinks the failing run is more of an action, or more attributable to anything, than a deterministic failure, and we would never write "the service acted freely" in a postmortem. So does adding nondeterminism to an otherwise deterministic decider buy any of what the debate is after, or does it only buy noise? And is the property actually worth wanting a third thing instead — something like: the behavior is traceable through considerations the system itself would endorse on reflection, and revisable in light of them, i.e. legibility plus revisability, which is neither rerun-indeterminism nor mere counterfactual dependence?
