I host a once-a-month discussion evening at my kitchen table — seven of us, none with any formal training in philosophy: a nurse, two software engineers, a retired accountant, a primary-school teacher, a surveyor, and my brother-in-law, who is a lawyer and will not let a claim pass without asking what is supposed to follow from what. We take one question a month, argue it in plain language, and do no reading in between. Next month's question is whether "better than" has to be transitive, and I have been trying to build the evening around a case of my own rather than one of the famous ones, because I want the group to feel the pressure of the argument before they have any sense of what shape the settled debate takes.

The case I have is medical. A health authority has a fixed stock of an analgesic — call it 100,000 units, where one unit is roughly a day of moderate dental pain spared — and must decide how to spread it. Option one: 100 patients, 1,000 units each, i.e. a hundred people lifted out of years of severe pain. Option two: 1,000 patients, 100 units each. Option three: 10,000 patients, 10 units each. And so on, doubling the number treated and halving the depth of relief at every step, the total always fixed at 100,000, for a dozen steps, ending at something like 400,000 people each spared an afternoon of mild soreness. Nothing else varies: same drug, same cost, no risk, no identifiable individuals anyone can point to. My hope is that they will say option two really is better than option one, because the nine-hundredth unit of relief handed to one person does less good than the first hundred units handed to nine others; that they will then say the last option is plainly worse than the first; and that they will then have to face the fact that the local reasoning which carried them from one to two applies unchanged at every single step. I have also sketched a second, independent spectrum that reaches the same endpoint by trading duration against number instead of intensity against number, on the thought that anyone who wants to block the argument by denying one link cannot deny both — but I genuinely cannot tell whether that second route is doing real work or is just clutter.

What I want from you is a plan for the ninety minutes, not the explanation itself: the order the pieces have to arrive in and what depends on what; whether my spectrum is the right thing to carry the whole evening or what such an example has to have and, just as importantly, to lack; the wrong turns this particular group will take in the order they will take them — I am fairly confident the accountant will say the puzzle dissolves because the totals are identical, someone will conclude that all thirteen options are therefore equally good, and the lawyer will say we have only shown that our intuitions are untrustworthy — and the point in the sequence at which to head each one off; what to leave out and say openly that I am leaving out; and something better than my planned closing question, which was going to be "which of your commitments are you prepared to give up?", since I suspect that will only get me shrugs. One more thing: I had intended to open by establishing that "better than" must be transitive the way "taller than" is, on the grounds that there is a single underlying quantity, and to close with the moral that we should stop trusting pairwise judgments and simply maximise total units of relief. Someone on a forum I lurk on told me both of those are mistakes, and that the real way out is to say that neighbouring options are not equally good but merely "on a par" — which struck me as renaming the problem rather than answering it. If that is right, and if my planned opening and closing are indeed the wrong way in, tell me what should stand in their place.
