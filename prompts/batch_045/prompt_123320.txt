Our reading group on truth has been stuck for two weeks on an argument that one of the members, who works on the formal side, built to show that deflationism about truth can be refuted outright. He sets it up as follows. Let L be a language with negation, disjunction, its own truth predicate T, and enough syntax to name its own sentences; call a sentence of L *ordinary* if it contains no occurrence of T and no reference to itself. (1) The entire content of the concept of truth is given by two inferential rules: capture, from S infer T applied to S, and release, from T applied to S infer S. He stresses that this is all he needs: no bivalence, no full equivalence schema, no substantial property shared by all truths. (2) For any ordinary P, L contains a sentence λP that is equivalent to: either λP is not true, or P. (3) Fix an arbitrary ordinary P. Either T applied to λP is true or it is not. (4) Suppose it is not true. Then its negation, λP is not true, is true; so by disjunction introduction the whole disjunction is true; and by the definition of λP that disjunction is exactly what λP says; so λP is true, contradicting the supposition. Hence λP is true unconditionally. He means this to foreclose the reply that λP is a pathological sentence with no truth value: declaring it valueless, he says, only makes the first disjunct true and reinstates it. (5) From λP being true, release gives the disjunction λP is not true or P, and since its first disjunct is false, disjunctive syllogism gives P. (6) P was arbitrary, so every ordinary sentence is true — that snow is black, that our department is solvent, and every ordinary sentence nobody has ever uttered. (7) Since he used no conditional, he concludes that the paraconsistent replies to Curry have nothing to grab, and since he assumed no bivalence, neither do the paracomplete ones; the only premise with real content is (1), so (1) is false and truth must involve more than capture and release.

I can't accept the conclusion, and I also can't find the exit, which is the annoying part. Three things I am attached to, and I suspect I cannot keep all of them: capture and release unrestricted; classical logic, including disjunctive syllogism and excluded middle; and being able to say, truly and in the language I actually speak, that the Liar is not true — the thing that gap approaches are said to be unable to manage. I would also rather not buy my way out by banning self-reference wholesale, since I want sentences like *everything in the auditor's report is true* to keep working normally.

So I want a single diagnosis, not a survey: tell me which step fails, why it looked obligatory when it doesn't, and which premise I should actually give up if I want to pay as little as possible, given the three things I would most like to keep. Commit to one cut and give your reason for preferring it over the others rather than leaving me to choose. Please answer as exactly five numbered items — the step you reject, why it looked fine, the cut you would make, what that cut costs me, and what the best alternative cut would cost — one or two sentences each, with no preamble and no summing up.
