Last night I argued for four hours over dinner with an old friend from graduate school, a set theorist, about whether the continuum hypothesis has a determinate truth value, and we got exactly as far as we always do: she says there is one cumulative hierarchy, the powerset operation is determinate, and the independence results (Gödel's inner model, Cohen's forcing) record a limitation of our axioms rather than an indeterminacy in the subject matter, so the right response is to look for new axioms; I say the powerset operation is not pinned down by the concept of set, forcing extensions are as good as the universe you started in, and the question of how many reals there are behaves like the question of how many parallels to a given line pass through a given point before you have specified a geometry. Her own expectation is that the search for a canonical universe — as thin and well-ordered as it can be while still containing all the large cardinals we have reason to accept — will deliver, and that CH will come out true. Mine is that no candidate principle will ever command the kind of consensus large cardinals command, and that the appearance of a determinate question comes from our habit of using the definite article. We have agreed to stop and write something together instead.

Please draft the joint statement such an adversarial collaboration should produce, addressed to a reader who has taken one graduate logic course and is undecided. Define every technical term you use in a clause (forcing, inner model, large cardinal, absoluteness, and so on) rather than assuming it.

Structure it as follows.

1. A numbered section of agreed claims, eight to twelve of them, that both of us can sign and that are not vacuous. Among the things I think belong here: what the independence proofs do and do not establish; that the large-cardinal axioms set theorists actually accept are compatible both with CH and with its negation, so the independence is not an artifact of unusually weak axioms; that forcing extensions and inner models are both legitimate objects of study and neither of us thinks the other's technique is illegitimate; that much of ordinary mathematics outside set theory is insensitive to how the continuum question is settled; and that justifying an axiom by its consequences is not the same as proving it.

2. A disagreements table with four columns: the issue; the formulation she would sign; the formulation I would sign; and what would be different in mathematical practice if one of us were right. At minimum the rows should cover whether the phrase the universe of sets picks out a single structure, whether the powerset operation has a determinate output, in what sense if any CH is a meaningful question, what counts as a good reason to adopt a new axiom, and what would count as the debate being over.

3. A cruxes section: three sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of us. For each, give a test, case, or result both of us agree would bear on it, and record in advance what each of us predicts about that test and about what it would show. Two of the tests are stipulations I want both of us to answer:

- The Census. Suppose set theorists found a canonical way to survey all forcing extensions of a given universe, counting equivalent ones once, together with a well-motivated notion of almost all, and suppose the Census came back reporting that almost all of them satisfy the negation of CH. What weight would each of us give that? Would the answer differ if the Census had come back the other way, or had come back with no determinate answer at all? Answer all three versions.
- The Parallel Community. Suppose we encountered a mathematical culture that had developed set theory with an explicitly indeterminate powerset operator: it proves everything we prove in arithmetic, analysis and algebra, it treats how many reals are there as ill-posed, and it finds our dispute unintelligible. Would each of us count that as evidence that our question is defective, or merely as evidence of a different convention? Say what each of us would expect such a community's mathematics to look like in detail before the encounter, so the prediction is checkable.

The third crux is whether the two guiding heuristics — prefer the universe that is canonically minimal and definable, versus prefer the universe that is maximal in width and contains as many reals as possible — are genuinely competing programmes, orthogonal axes, or two descriptions of one aim. For this one, choose the test yourself, but it must say what each of us would conclude both if it were shown that the two heuristics provably cannot be pushed to their natural limits together, and if it were shown that they can always be jointly satisfied while the continuum question stays open.

4. A short closing list of what is not in dispute — I take it we agree that the truth of the standard axioms is not the issue, that assuming CH as a convenient hypothesis is legitimate, and that neither of us is claiming the other's mathematics is wrong.

Two further requirements. First, mark explicitly which parts of the disagreement you take to be merely verbal and which are substantive, and justify both verdicts; if some row resists neutral formulation, say so and explain why instead of papering over it. Second, do not adjudicate: the document should not tell either of us we are right, and it should avoid vocabulary that begs the question — real, intended, genuine, arbitrary — except inside a quoted formulation with a gloss attached. Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words, in the register both of us could put our names to, with no citations, no author attributions, and no reading lists.
