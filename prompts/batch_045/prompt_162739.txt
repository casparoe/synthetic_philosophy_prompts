I write the public-facing text for a genocide education centre — wall panels, statements, school materials — so several times a year I have to decide, in public, whether some event gets the word 'genocide' or something vaguer like 'mass atrocity.' I definately have gut verdicts and I want the principle behind them, plus the verdicts it tells me to drop.

Clear yeses: the Holocaust; the Armenian deportations and massacres of 1915; Rwanda in 1994; Srebrenica, even though mostly the men and boys were killed and the women driven out; the Herero and Nama under German colonial rule in southern Africa; the Khmer Rouge, even though what they went after was closer to a class or a city population than to any of the four groups the treaty lists. Also yes: the Ukrainian famine of the early 1930s; Canada's residential schools, where the stated aim was assimilation rather than death — to make the group stop existing while its members lived on; a regime that wrecks a region's water and hospitals knowing a million will die while saying out loud that the group should go. And yes to a would-be exterminator who is stopped after killing three percent of the group, because I don't think body count is what does the work.

Clear no: a famine caused by reckless policy with no group in mind; the firebombing of Japanese cities and the atomic bombs, even though civilians died in enormous numbers precisely because of who they were. I balk at the 1965-66 killings in Indonesia, which may just be my Khmer Rouge verdict applied inconsistently. And I go back and forth on the transatlantic slave trade: it was about labour, not destruction, but families were permanently sundered and the deaths were vast.

The formulation I've been playing with is that genocide is aimed destruction of a group you cannot resign from, where that means your children inherit the membership even if you convert, recant, or flee — my way of keeping the Khmer Rouge without letting in every political purge. I'm not sure it survives the residential schools, and I notice I reach for the word faster when the perpetrator was a government my own country opposed, which makes me suspect the whole list is contaminated. One case I have no verdict on: nobody is killed, but over two generations a state dismantles the group's land base, language and schools until the group is gone.

Answer as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, no introduction and nothing after the last one: (1) the principle, stated so a colleague could apply it to a case I haven't listed; (2) which of my verdicts it overturns, and which it just can't accommodate; (3) the slave trade; (4) the stopped-at-three-percent case and the two-generation, nobody-dies case; (5) whether the politics of naming — who the word obliges, who it honours, who it pushes into denial — belongs inside the principle or is a separate judgement I should be making on top of it.
