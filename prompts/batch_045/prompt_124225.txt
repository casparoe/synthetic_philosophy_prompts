I'm a science journalist — I cover AI and adjacent stuff for a living — and I'm finally writing the long piece I've been dodging for a year about "prosaic alignment": the thought that we're probably going to have to make do with aligning systems assembled from current methods (pretraining, RLHF-ish fine-tuning, a bit of interpretability bolted on afterwards) rather than waiting around for a conceptual breakthrough that may not come. Before I write it I want someone to audit where I've actually landed, because I have a nagging feeling I've accumulated a set of views that don't all belong in the same head. Here they are, roughly in the order I picked them up:

(1) What RLHF-style training installs isn't values, it's a repertoire of surface behaviour. The model learns which outputs get a thumbs-up, not to want whatever the thumbs-up was proxying for. So prosaic alignment is shallow, and I expect it to degrade exactly where it matters, i.e. off-distribution.

(2) Which means behavioural evaluation basically can't distinguish a model that shares our goals from one that's merely compliant where compliance pays. So interpretability — actually reading goals off the weights — is the only thing that could close that gap, and everything else is harm reduction.

(3) Nonetheless I think dangerous-capability evaluations are the right trigger for regulation, and I'd defend a capability-threshold rule in a policy conversation tomorrow. Behavioural evidence is clearly good enough to govern by.

(4) I dont think today's systems are running any kind of long-horizon deception, and I roll my eyes at most of the "it's scheming" anecdotes that do the rounds — but I also think it's correct to act as though they might be soon, precisely because (per 2) the tests that would tell us can't.

(5) I think the crux isn't a deep technical mystery at all, it's the incentive structure: labs in a race, no liability, ship first. Fix the race and most of what people call "the alignment problem" becomes an ordinary safety-engineering problem, the boring kind that aviation and pharma already solve badly but solve.

(6) I also think the habit of saying "the model wants X" is loose to the point of being a category error. A model is closer to a distribution over simulated characters that the prompt samples from, and there is no privileged "its" goal for us to be aligning. (I know this is the standard move for dissolving a lot of the risk talk, and I'm aware it might be doing more work in my thinking than I've noticed.)

Then a researcher I interviewed a few months ago said something I can't shake, and I want it folded in. He had a name for it — the photocopy effect, which I'm sure is not what he'd call it in print. The claim: any two behavioural audits of the same frontier model are much less like independent tests than everyone behaves as if they are, because the failure modes aren't independent draws. They're inherited from a shared pretraining distribution and, increasingly, from synthetic training data distilled out of the same small handful of teacher models. So the audits share a common cause and fail together, and the consequence he drew is that a model passing forty evaluations is closer to passing one evaluation forty times than to forty independent confirmations — and it gets worse as model families converge on each other's outputs.

So: does this package hang together?

I've changed my mind twice about where the problem is, and I've stopped trusting my own guesses, so I'm not going to tell you which one I suspect — I dont want to anchor you. What I'd like is:

- the genuine tensions, ranked by severity rather than all flagged as equally bad, with what would have to give in each case and what that would cost me;
- at least one place where two of these look like they conflict but on inspection don't — I'm fairly confident my list has one or two of those and I'd rather not amputate something I get to keep;
- where the photocopy effect bites: which of (1)–(6) it supports, which it quietly undercuts, and which it forces me to restate, since I have a feeling it cuts both ways and I can't say how;
- and if the honest answer is that one of these commitments is doing all the real work and the rest is decoration, say which.

Last thing: I'm going to put some version of this under my own byline, so please mark how confident you are in each thing you claim, and single out the one specific claim you're least sure of. I'd much rather have a hedged true sentence than a confident wrong one, and I'd rather find out now which is which.
