I host a small philosophy podcast — mostly long-form conversations, one guest, about forty-five minutes of usable time — and next month's guest is Dr. Miriam Vosk, whose new monograph *The Borrowed Eye* defends what she calls calibrated deference: the view that aesthetic testimony cannot by itself give you knowledge or justified aesthetic belief, but does so once you have an independent track record showing that your responses covary with the speaker's on a suitably large and varied sample of works you have both experienced first-hand. So she is neither a flat pessimist who says the critic's verdict can never put you in a position to know, nor a credulous optimist who thinks it usually can; the testimonial transmission of aesthetic justification is, on her account, an empirically earned entitlement rather than a default. I know the broad shape of the dialectic she is intervening in, and I have read the book, so I do not want questions that let her restate the calibrated-deference machinery or walk through her own favourite examples again. What I want is pressure applied at the joints, and in particular I want a sequence that closes off her stock escape route: whenever I raise a case where deference to a critic looks perfectly reasonable — choosing what to see, trusting a programmer's taste in a recommender, letting a teacher's verdict shape how one looks at a painting — she answers, "that is a reason to act, not a reason to believe," and the conversation moves on. I would like at least two questions built so that this maneuver gets progressively harder to deploy, ending with one where acting and believing seem to come apart in the opposite direction from the one she needs.

Here is where I would like the questions to bite, with her most likely answer anticipated in each case and a follow-up keyed specifically to that answer rather than to the question: first, on what acquaintance is actually supposed to be doing, I want her forced to disassociate the candidates — I have in mind the amnesiac critic who stood in front of the painting, formed a verdict, wrote it down, and now remembers nothing of the experience but still reports it from her notes; the hypothetical expert who never encountered the work but whose prediction of her own verdict is perfectly reliable; and the contemporary case she does not discuss, systems trained on large corpora of human criticism that predict critical and popular verdicts well without undergoing anything plausibly called an aesthetic experience. Her answer will presumably be that acquaintance is not mere causal contact and not mere reliability either, and I want the follow-up to make her say what the experience contributes that the reliable prediction lacks. Second, on calibration itself: how many shared works count, what counts as agreement on a work (a matching verdict, a matching emotional response, or matching reasons), and — the question I care most about — how anyone gets started, since on the first work you ever judge your agreement sample is empty, and if the sample has to be built by first-hand judgment, the view looks as though it needs a testimony-free bootstrap that no one actually has. I expect her to relax the requirement to something coarse-grained, and I want the follow-up to test whether a coarse-grained version still excludes the cases she wants excluded. Third, a pair of cases I have not seen handled anywhere: testimony about works one cannot in principle experience first-hand (a building known only through drawings, a score that cannot be performed, a work destroyed before one was born), and testimony from an artist about her own work ("I was aiming at serenity, and I failed") — the second one matters because it is hard to describe the artist as lacking acquaintance, yet it is also hard to see why her authority should be insulated from calibration. Fourth, I want one question on peer disagreement, since she leans on the thought that critics disagree with each other in a way scientists do not, and I would like the follow-up to press the disanalogy rather than accept it.

A few constraints on the output. Please give me eight to ten questions, in the order you would ask them, each with a one- or two-sentence sketch of the answer she is most likely to give followed by a follow-up written against that specific answer; the sequencing should make the "reason to act, not a reason to believe" reply progressively less available rather than letting her repeat it in different words. Keep each question self-contained enough that a listener who has not read the book can follow it, avoid anything that amounts to "tell us about your book" or "what got you into this topic," and make sure at least one question is the kind she cannot answer by refining her own machinery — something that would count as evidence *against* calibrated deference if it turned out a certain way. If you think one of my four pressure points is weaker than I take it to be, say so briefly and substitute something better rather than padding the list.
