My friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about whether moral worth really requires acting for the right reason, and I came away having built an argument whose conclusion neither of us accepts and in which neither of us can locate a bad step. I would like you to locate it and tell me what giving it up costs.

We were using *moral worth* in a narrow sense: the credit an action earns for the agent's will, as opposed to its being merely the right thing to do, or its being right by luck, or its being done for a reason that happens to coincide with the right one. My friend's starting point was the familiar Kantian suspicion that a person who is beneficent because she happens to have a sympathetic temperament earns less credit than one who does the same thing because it is what she owes. I was defending the opposite: that the well-tempered person is the better person, and that a theory on which she earns less credit has the wrong shape. Then I noticed that my friend's view, spelled out carefully, says something neither of us believes.

(1) An action has moral worth only if the agent performs it because it is right — the fact that it is right must be what moves her, not merely something she could cite if asked.

(2) Whether that fact is what moves her is not settled by what she says or by how she feels; it is settled by a counterfactual. She acts because it is right only if she would still act that way in a nearby case in which the action is right and none of her non-moral desires favor it. Call this the detachment condition: the action must be detachable from the desires that happen to accompany it.

(3) Detachment is naturally read as a matter of degree. The less of an action's explanation is occupied by non-moral desire, the more fully the right-making fact is the thing doing the work; and moral worth is supposed to track the work the right-making fact does. So moral worth varies inversely with how much of the action's explanation is occupied by the agent's non-moral desires.

(4) The fully virtuous agent — someone who has become the sort of person who wants to do the right thing because it is right — is precisely someone for whom acting rightly never goes against the grain. Her desires reliably accompany and favor the right action, and the better she is, the more reliably they do.

(5) So in every right action the fully virtuous agent performs, some of the explanation is occupied by non-moral desire, and the better she is, the more reliably this is true. By (3), her action therefore has less moral worth than the same action performed by someone for whom doing the right thing is a struggle; and a perfectly good agent, if there could be one, would perform no action with full moral worth at all.

(6) But moral worth is the dimension on which we credit an agent's will, and crediting the will is what distinguishes moral admiration from mere relief at how things went. It cannot be that the person we would most want to become is the person who earns least on that dimension.

(7) So one of (1) through (5) is false. I cannot see which.

Three cases I want any diagnosis to sort correctly, because my friend and I have firm and conflicting intuitions about them:

— The worth-seeker. She gives to famine relief because she knows it is the right thing to do, and also because she savors the thought that her action has moral worth; she would still have given had the second motive been absent. My friend says she has full worth and the extra enjoyment is only a blemish. I say the enjoyment of one's own worth is exactly the kind of additional thought that spoils the action. Neither of us can say what in the argument settles it.

— The depleted helper. A nurse who has spent twelve hours with dying patients; by the end her sympathy is gone and she does the next right thing purely because it is right. (3) says her last act has more worth than her earlier, warmer ones. That seems to me flatly wrong — her exhaustion is not an achievement — but I cannot say what (3) has missed.

— The paramedic with a standing policy. She rescues because it is right and because she loves the work, and she has decided that if the love ever goes she will quit, since the caring is why she is there at all. The detachment condition appears to classify her as failing, yet what explains her failing is a fact about why she shows up, not a defect in her will on the occasions when she does act.

As for what I want to keep: I want to keep (1), and I want to keep the claim that the fully virtuous agent's action has at least as much worth as anyone else's. I am willing to give up the counterfactual test, but only if you tell me what should replace it, because my friend's retort is that without some such test (1) is just a slogan. My own repair was to weaken (3) into a sufficiency condition: worth requires that the right-making fact be sufficient on its own to produce the action, not that it occupy more of the explanation than anything else. That rescues the virtuous agent, but I don't see that it gives me anything to say about the worth-seeker, and I am not sure it survives the paramedic.

So: which step do you reject, and why did that step look like a harmless way of spelling out the one before it? Tell me what rejecting it costs — what I lose by way of a test for (1), and what I must now say about the three cases. And if (3) is where the argument breaks, tell me what a condition on moral worth would have to look like in order to distinguish an accompanying desire that is a second, independent route to the action from one that is merely the shape a person's grasp of the right takes in someone like her.
