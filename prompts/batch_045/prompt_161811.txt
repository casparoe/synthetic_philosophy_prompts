Hey — I'm TAing a phil of mind module this term and I'm putting together the discussion-section handout on theories of emotion. We've covered the judgment view (emotions as, or as essentially involving, evaluative judgements/beliefs — the Nussbaum/Solomon sort of thing, where emotions have intentional objects and can be rational or irrational), the perceptual or quasi-perceptual view (emotions as experiences of evaluative properties, nonconceptual and recalcitrant in the way perception is, the Tappolet/Prinz/Döring sort of thing), and the feeling/affective view descending from James, where the emotion just is — or is essentially — the feeling of the bodily changes, so what fixes which emotion it is and how strong it is, is the interoceptive profile rather than any representational content. The example everyone always reaches for is recalcitrant emotion: the person who judges the rollercoaster or the spider to be harmless but still experiences it as fearsome. That case splits judgement from experience, and I'm bored of it, and more importantly I don't think it settles anything because the perceptual theorist is obviously going to win it.

So here's what I want. Build me a single realistic episode — one paragraph, ordinary life, one subject, no sci-fi, no brain lesions or drugs or clinical diagnoses doing the work, pitched so a first-year can hold it in their head — in which the split runs the *other* way: the subject's evaluative judgement and their evaluative experience have both fully moved on together, and what dissents is the body. Call it an affective echo if you like: the thing is over and genuinely over, she no longer judges it to be bad and no longer experiences it as bad, she'd say so sincerely and she'd be right — but the bodily signature of the old emotion keeps coming back at full strength, triggered by cues she knows perfectly well are irrelevant (a smell, a street, a time of day, a song in a shop), and she recognises it as *that* emotion rather than as generic agitation, a panic attack, caffeine jitters, tiredness or a startle. Bereavement or a breakup is my hunch for the setting but I'm not attached. Hard constraints: all three theories have to agree that something emotional is going on, so don't make it a case where anyone gets to say "that's not really an emotion"; and the state has to be full-strength, not a faint "as if" shadow, because "it's a weak residue" isn't interesting.

After the scenario, I want the demonstration: for each of the three theories in turn, what it says about whether this is an emotion, which emotion if any, and whether the state has correctness conditions or can be fitting or unfitting — and, separately, which single feature of the case is doing the work, plus what happens to the case if you remove it (e.g. if the judgement hadn't reversed, or if the evaluative experience hadn't — I want to know which one it collapses back into the boring recalcitrance case). Then give me the honest part: the best available attack from a defender of the judgement view and the best available attack from a defender of the perceptual view, since they should both have real moves here. What counts as cheating, for my purposes: don't stipulate the verdicts, and don't quietly posit a tacit or unconscious judgement, or a sub-personal evaluative perception, and then present that as a result — if your case needs one of those to work, say so explicitly and argue why it isn't just the standard move I said I was bored of. And if you don't think the spec is jointly satisfiable, tell me which constraint has to give and why rather than fudging it.
