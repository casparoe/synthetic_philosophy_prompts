On my philosophy of language module we read Hofstadter's 'Person Paper' essay, and I've got stuck on the actual practice of gender-neutral language reform rather than the satire. The house style at my uni, and of most style guides I've seen, cuts three ways: compounds where the "man" bit looks like it's doing gender work on a person get replaced (chairman to chair, policeman to police officer, spokesman to spokesperson); compounds where no person is left in the picture still often get replaced anyway (manpower to workforce, man-hours to person-hours, manhole to utility hole, man-made to artificial); and some are never touched at all (human, manual, manufacture, place names). So what's the implicit theory? What view of linguistic sexism does that pattern presuppose — something about what a word means, about what it defaults to or implicates, about etymology, about a term encoding stuff that's nobody's business (the line used for pushing "Ms." over Miss/Mrs), or about the job a form does in a paradigm where one form is unmarked and the others are built off it?

I've been trying to sort it with a three-way distinction — content (does the term actually say "male"?), default (does using or hearing it make you picture a man?), and markedness (is the male form the one everything else is a special case of?) — and my hunch is that the practice only makes sense if markedness can be objectionable all by itself, with content and default absent, but I can't tell whether that's a real third thing or just the other two rearranged. Also: Hofstadter's move of translating into the marked form and seeing if it sounds ridiculous is doing a lot of work in how people actually decide these cases, so what does using that as your test presuppose, given how easy it is to confuse unfamiliar with absurd? And is any of it consistent with the same guides insisting elsewhere that what matters is what a term communicates, not what it sounds like or where it comes from? I'd like you to separate what the practice strictly needs from what it merely sits well with, and say where it's underdetermined and several theories would license it. If the question is muddled or my three-way split is a dud, just say so plainly instead of humouring me, and mark how confident you are in each bit of the reconstruction — I'd rather have one claim you'd actually defend than five you wouldn't.
