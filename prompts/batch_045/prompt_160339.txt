Here is the half-formed material I want you to develop rather than illustrate. Three ideas I have been turning over, which I have not seen handled in quite this way:

First, what I have started calling the purity tax — the possibility that keeping one's own hands clean is not costless but is instead a cost that falls on someone else, typically the same vulnerable people whose interests motivated the refusal. If so, the clean-hands option is not the moral baseline against which dirt is measured; it is one more option with a price, and it may be the option that is easiest to feel good about.

Second, what I call laundered remainder: in a bureaucracy you can almost always get the same outcome either by choosing it or by building a process — a formula, a waitlist rule, an automated eligibility check — whose predictable output is that outcome and which nobody in particular chose. Is the agent's situation morally different in the two cases, and if it is, is the difference one the agent is entitled to rely on?

Third, a thought I am less sure of, which I will state as a conjecture: that the moral remainder in these cases is conserved rather than eliminated by delegation — that it does not evaporate when the dirty act is pushed down to a caseworker, a contractor, or a piece of software, but relocates, and specifically relocates to whoever has the least power to refuse it.

You do not need to use all three; pick whichever one or two the story can actually carry, and let the others sit. What matters is that the fiction does the philosophical work itself — that a reader who had never heard the term dirty hands would come away having felt the specific difficulty — rather than staging a debate and letting a character explain it.
