my friend Sam did his PhD in decision theory and now works in government, and for about six years I've been quietly collecting his verdicts on cases where someone has no reason to prefer either option, because he's extremely consistent and he refuses to tell me what rule he's running. Here's the track record:

1. Two piles of hay, identical, equidistant, the donkey setup: he says either is fine, there's nothing irrational about just taking one, and flipping a coin is allowed but not required.
2. Restaurant, two dishes he likes exactly equally, both familiar: same answer, order one, don't make a production out of it.
3. Two bottles of wine, he knows nothing about wine and has no evidence either way which he'd prefer: he says that's not indifference, that's ignorance, and the mistake is treating absence of evidence as a tie. Go get evidence. If you absolutely have to choose this second, he'll allow either, but he treats that as a concession to time pressure, not the real answer.
4. Two job offers, one pays much more, the other is near his family, he genuinely can't rank them and has gone round in circles: do NOT flip a coin and don't just grab one. This isn't a tie, it's the other thing, the kind of case where you decide by making yourself into someone for whom one of them is better, and picking at random would be a failure of agency rather than a permissible way out.
5. Same as 4 except one offer is a hair better on everything he cares about, well within noise: take that one. Even a sliver of a real reason settles it, no coin.
6. A sure 500 versus a fair coin flip for 1000, and he's genuinely indifferent between the risk profiles: either is permitted; he'd personally take the sure thing but says that's just taste, not rationality.

So 1, 2, 5 and 6 all fit a plain permissive rule: where the options really are equally good, pick either, coin optional. 3 and 4 fit something stricter. That's the tension I want you to work with.

Here's the case I have a standing bet with him about, and I want your prediction before I spring it on him:

And part of what he doesn't know isn't a fact about the drugs at all, it's how *he* would feel on B, which no amount of reading will settle — the only way to find out is to be on it. The choice is reversible: he can switch back, and he'll face the same decision again at his next annual review. Nothing identity-constituting or expressive is at stake, so it's not a case of type 4.
