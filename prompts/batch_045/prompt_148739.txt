You are a professional philosopher who works on applied ethics and on the methodology of moral and empirical uncertainty. Please take this slowly and reason carefully: before offering any verdict, spend a moment brainstorming four or five candidate diagnoses of why the dispute below has stalled, then argue for the one you think is doing the most work, and finally say what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a measurement project, a reframing of the question, or nothing at all.

I'm in a final-year seminar on food ethics, and I run a small discussion group attached to it — six of us, mostly philosophy and biology majors — and we have spent essentially the whole academic year going in circles on a single question. I want a diagnosis of the stall, not a verdict on the question itself, though if the right diagnosis dissolves the question I'll accept that as an answer. Assume we have already settled the things that usually stop this conversation early: animal suffering is morally significant, species membership is not what makes it significant, and the numbers on both sides are appalling. Given all that, the question is: should someone who is trying to minimize the suffering their diet causes choose beef or chicken? Two positions have hardened. The chicken camp argues in terms of individual animals: chickens are slaughtered after a few weeks and yield only a kilogram or two of meat, so a kilogram of chicken is roughly one death, while a kilogram of beef is a small fraction of a single cow's carcass; count deaths, or count days of confinement, and chicken looks dramatically worse. The beef camp argues in terms of land: ruminants need something like an order of magnitude more land and considerably more feed per kilogram of protein, and that land is worked — tilled, sown, sprayed, harvested — with its wild occupants poisoned, crushed and starved out, so a kilogram of beef plausibly causes far more wild-animal deaths than a kilogram of chicken. Both camps have spreadsheets; both concede their numbers are extrapolations from a handful of studies with enormous error bars; and neither camp has moved in twelve months.

Here is what made me think the disagreement isn't really about the numbers, and I want you to tell me whether I'm right. Suppose a research programme — call it a "displaced-animal ledger" — hands us figures we all agree on. It measures, for the actual supply chains that feed our campus, not only how many wild animals die per hectare-year of arable land and of pasture, but also how many would have lived on that land had it not been farmed; and suppose it reports both that pasture supports more small-mammal-years per hectare than the cereal monoculture that produces chicken feed, and that those small mammals' lives are on balance net negative. Even with all of that settled, I don't think we would agree — because my friend is counting deaths of animals that already exist, while the version of me that finds the wild-animal argument persuasive is implicitly counting animal-years that would or wouldn't exist at all, since habitat conversion and harvest don't merely kill the mice that are there, they prevent the mice that would have been. That looks to me like a population-ethics disagreement — person-affecting versus total versus average views — smuggled in underneath a dispute about dinner, and I suspect it is doing more work than either the empirical uncertainty or any disagreement about how much suffering matters. My professor, by contrast, says it's just premature science and we should wait for welfare biology; a friend says the whole exercise is expressive anyway, about what you're willing to endorse rather than what you cause.

So: diagnose the stall, and be specific about how each side's argument depends on the commitments you attribute to it — does the beef camp's case actually need the totalist reading, or can a person-affecting view reconstruct it? Is mine a real conceptual disagreement, an empirical one in disguise, a verbal dispute about "cause" or about what "my diet's footprint" means, or two different questions — what to buy versus what to campaign against — sharing one name? If my hypothesis is right, tell me what it would cost the beef camp to drop the wild-animal term and what it would cost the chicken camp to take it seriously.
