I would like a case analysis that runs in two directions: a real policy episode read through a specific decision-theoretic lens, and the lens in turn tested by the episode. Please answer in British English, in continuous prose with a few short headings at most, in about 900 words, and do not pad the answer with the familiar safety, waste and cost-overnight literature — assume I know it.

The episode is the divergence of German and French nuclear policy after Fukushima. Take the following as given, since I do not want the analysis to turn on your ability to recall figures: Germany's 2000 consensus between the federal government and the utilities set an exit path for the fleet; a later government extended operating lives in 2010; after March 2011 that was reversed again, eight reactors were shut at once and the end date was fixed for 2022; a government-appointed ethics commission on secure energy supply reported in 2011 that the phase-out could be completed within a decade while the transition proceeded; the last three reactors closed in April 2023, in the middle of the gas crisis that followed Russia's invasion of Ukraine. France, by contrast, kept and then re-endorsed the fleet built after the 1974 Messmer plan, which still supplies roughly two-thirds of its electricity: policy in the 2010s aimed at reducing the nuclear share towards half of generation, the state has since ordered a new generation of reactors and is seeking to run existing units well beyond their original forty-year design assumption, and in 2022 EDF took about a dozen units offline to inspect for stress-corrosion cracking, cutting its output guidance for that year to roughly 280–300 TWh.

The lens I want applied is real-options reasoning about irreversible choices under uncertainty, sharpened in four ways that are my own stipulation rather than standard doctrine. First, distinguish three decision problems that are often conflated: a retention decision (keep amortised plants running), a replacement decision (build new capacity), and a reversal decision (reopen, relicense or extend what has been retired or scheduled for retirement). These have different irreversibility structures and therefore different option values, and a country can be rational about one and careless about another. Second, treat the quasi-option value of waiting as only one side of the ledger: delay is not a neutral default, because it forgoes learning, lets supply chains and regulatory competence atrophy, and is paid for in emissions from whatever fills the gap. Third, require an explicit system boundary before any emissions accounting — territorial generation, consumption-adjusted generation, or the coupled Central European grid — and say how the verdict changes with it. Fourth, treat nuclear capacity as carrying an option on future options: not merely terawatt-hours but a standing industrial and regulatory capability, which is partly why closing a fleet and later rebuilding one are not symmetric transactions.

What I want from you: a disciplined mapping of each decision node (2000, 2010, 2011, and the 2022 outage episode) onto this frame — who the decision-maker was, what the real option set was, what was known and not known at the time, and what the payoffs looked like at two different horizons, say 2035 and 2050. Then say where the frame explains each country's behaviour and where it plainly does not: what it makes of the ethics commission's role in converting a technocratic question into a moral one, of the fact that the German decision ratified a decades-long social movement, and of the French state's dependence on a single national champion for both fleet and new build. Then turn the lens on itself: does the comparison vindicate irreversibility-and-option-value thinking as the right way to frame national fleet decisions, or does it expose the frame as misdescribing the situation — for instance because the binding constraint was never information but social licence, or because the frame presupposes a single decision-maker with a coherent objective function while these are two states with coupled grids, shared electricity trade and a common decarbonisation target? Be explicit about where the frame is silent rather than forcing it to speak, and finish by saying what evidence, short of hindsight, would have discriminated between the two paths at the time.
