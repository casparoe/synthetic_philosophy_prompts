You are a political philosopher who has published on luck egalitarianism and who has also spent years running a graduate seminar, so you know both where the literature actually presses and what makes a question work when it is spoken out loud rather than written.

I host a university podcast aimed at philosophy grad students and policy-adjacent listeners; episodes run about fifty minutes, which leaves room for roughly ten questions plus follow-ups. Next week's guest is Dr. Miriam Voss, who defends a luck-egalitarian view of distributive justice: differences in people's shares are unjust when they trace to factors outside their control, while people may properly be left to bear the costs of risks they knowingly and willingly took. She is formidably quick, and she has two stock moves I want to close off by the end of the hour: she answers the abandonment-and-shaming family of objections with "that's an objection to the currency, not the principle," and she answers the regress from constitutive luck with "the view doesn't need desert, it needs a practice." (In an earlier episode on sufficientarianism I let a guest get away with "that's just terminological" three times, and the listeners noticed, so I would rather be handed questions that make escape expensive.)

Before drafting, spend a moment brainstorming which five or six objections in the literature would genuinely be uncomfortable for someone holding this exact formulation — the distinction between brute and option luck, the classification of expensive tastes, the aggregation problem, the levelling-down worry, the suspicion that a carefully stated version collapses into some neighbouring view — and discard the ones that only look hard. Then draft ten interview questions, each spoken rather than written in register, at most two sentences, none of them of the "what first drew you to this" sort. For every question give me: the line as I should say it; the answer she is most likely to give, in one sentence; a follow-up keyed specifically to that answer; and a short note on which of her two moves the question is meant to disable. Among the pressure points I want covered, or consciously dropped with a reason: whether the morally relevant feature of an option-luck case is that the person could have declined the gamble or that the gamble was fairly priced at the time, and what the view says when every available gamble is a bad one; expensive tastes treated as a puzzle about how a taste gets classified as chosen or unchosen rather than as a debate about subsidizing champagne; whether option luck survives aggregation, given that the market return to my choice depends on everyone else's simultaneous choices, so that the value of my talents is nobody's choice in particular; whether the view has any content in a world with no luck at all, and whether a naturally arising equal distribution needs redistribution; whether the view can be stated so that it does not require levelling down; whether it is, once stated carefully, extensionally equivalent to luck-weighted prioritarianism or to welfarism under some background risk policy, and what it would tell a finance minister that those rivals do not; and one late question about what empirical or institutional finding would count as evidence against it.

Finally, mark for each question whether the point it presses is settled among specialists or is a live controversy, say where a specialist in the room would think my framing smuggles in an assumption, and don't present any contested claim — about free will, about interpersonal comparisons of welfare, about what justice is even for — as the consensus. Give me no reading lists, no bibliographic pointers and no citations: everything has to be askable and answerable live, on the strength of the ideas alone.
