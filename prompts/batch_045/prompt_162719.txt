I am trying to work out what I actually think about the special composition question: not what the available theories say, but which principle, if any, fits the verdicts I find myself giving when I go case by case. I know the familiar answers — composition never occurs, composition always occurs, composition occurs only when the parts constitute a life, only when they are bonded, only when they are in contact — and I have never been able to accept any of them whole. So I want to work upward from cases. Below are my verdicts, such as they are. Two pairs I cannot reconcile are flagged.

1. A cat. Yes: one thing, and not merely many things arranged catwise.
2. A cat together with the flea on its tail. No. The flea is a thing, the cat is a thing, the two together are not a third thing.
3. A water molecule, and a diamond crystal. Yes to both. I am not a nihilist about chemistry: there really are molecules and crystals. This already rules out the life-only answer as I understand it, though I would be interested to hear that it can be stretched to cover chemistry without ceasing to be the life-only answer.
4. A granite boulder. Yes. A pile of gravel of the same volume and the same mineral, dumped on a driveway. No. I feel the difference sharply and cannot state it.
5. A pair of shoes, one in a closet in Boston and one in a closet in Oxford. Yes: the pair is a single scattered thing, even though its two parts neither touch nor interact.
6. My left shoe together with the Eiffel Tower. No.
7. [flagged] A watch, fully disassembled, its parts laid out on a table in a chain so that each part touches the next. No. Compare 5: I said yes there with no contact at all, and no here with contact throughout. So contact is neither necessary nor sufficient for me, and I cannot say what is doing the work in either case.
8. A named hurricane: it lasts nine days, travels a thousand miles, is tracked and reported on by name, keeps its structure while replacing all of its air several times over, and is eventually said to have died. Yes, I am willing to call that one thing.
9. [flagged] A single swell crossing an ocean, likewise persisting for days and hundreds of miles through constantly changing water. No: I will not call that a thing. I have tried and failed to state a difference between this and 8 that I actually believe. Both are more or less bounded patterns of moving matter that survive complete material exchange. If you tell me the two stand or fall together, I do not know which way I would jump, and I would like you to tell me which way you think I should.
10. A thundercloud. Yes — but with fuzzy edges: for droplets near the fringe I think there is no fact of the matter whether they are parts of it. I find vagueness easy to accept about parthood and hard to accept about existence, and I do not know whether that is a stable combination.
11. A glider in a cellular automaton. Take a grid of cells, each on or off, updated by a rule under which a cell is on at a step if and only if either exactly three of its eight neighbours were on at the previous step, or it was on and exactly two or three of its eight neighbours were on; in such a world there are small patterns of on-cells that reconstitute their own shape shifted one cell diagonally every four steps and travel across the grid indefinitely. I lean yes: if I am a realist about that world at all, I want to count the glider among what there is in it. Nothing there is alive, bonded, or in contact in any ordinary sense.
12. A bronze statue and the bronze it is made of: two things, same matter, same time. A bronze cube that is also a paperweight: one thing. I know these two verdicts sit badly with each other, and I do not know whether that pushes me toward relativizing composition to a sortal or toward giving up the statue verdict.

Here is the principle I have been toying with, which I suspect does not survive the list. The xs compose a single thing at a time if and only if (a) the causal traffic among the xs is substantially denser than the causal traffic between the xs and everything else, and (b) that internal traffic is organized so as to maintain the arrangement of the xs over time — a self-maintaining causal nexum with some claim to a boundary. As I read it, it delivers 1, 3, 4, 8 and 11 and rules out 6. But the cat and the flea have plenty of causal traffic — biting, blood, heat, and the cat's behaviour changes because of the flea — and I still say no in 2; and a settled pile of gravel has enormous contact traffic and a fairly stable arrangement, so I cannot see how condition (b) fails for it while succeeding for the boulder in 4. The repair that suggests itself is to require that the internal traffic be what explains the persistence of the arrangement; my worry is that this is either life under another name or a functional notion that will drag the hurricane and the glider down with the gravel.

What I would like from you:

First, one principle, or at most two or three genuine rivals, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case you have not seen — with the case that motivates each clause, so that I can see what I would be buying and what it costs. I would rather have one sharp principle with its costs admitted than three surveyed even-handedly. Please argue from these cases and from any you introduce; I am not asking for a tour of the standard positions.

Second, verdicts on three cases I have not settled: (a) a single inference pass of a large neural network spread across a few thousand machines in several data centres, with messages passing between them, one input and one output, one computation going on, and no container, no common location and no shared material boundary; (b) a galaxy, or a gravitationally bound star cluster; (c) a clonal aspen grove, many stems sharing one root system.

Third, a plain statement of which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and what you think should give way: the verdict, the principle, or the assumption that these cases are being decided by composition at all rather than by vagueness, convention, or what we happen to bother naming. In particular: should I accept that some fusions compose only indeterminately, or can the cloud's fuzziness be located somewhere other than composition? And does the statue-and-cube pair force composition to be relativized to a sortal, and if so what fixes the relevant sortal?
