I teach a first-year seminar on contingency in history, and I am putting Tom Tykwer's Run Lola Run (1998) on the syllabus as the case study for the for-want-of-a-nail intuition: a couple of seconds lost on a stairwell, a limp, a bicycle glimpsed at the wrong moment, and the same twenty minutes produce three entirely different endings. I have been trying to sharpen that intuition into three models that the proverb blurs together, and I want to know where the film sits among them.

Amplification: the situation is genuinely chaotic in the technical sense, so a microscopic difference grows without bound and the far outcome is unpredictable in principle, not merely unknown to us. Basin-switch: the small event does not create the outcome but tips an already poised system into one of several outcomes that were there anyway; I suspect this is what the nail proverb and the old remark about Cleopatra's nose really describe. Severing: the small event adds or removes a strand the outcome depended on, so what follows is not a different determined outcome but an open one; remove the archduke and you do not get a different war, you get a different century whose shape nothing about the removal predicts.

What I would like is a reading of the three runs against those three models, and I have a worry I would rather have settled now: that Run Lola Run is not really a chaos film. The variations look less like sensitive dependence than like Lola learning to stop forcing things — in the final run she gets the money by winning at roulette rather than by robbing the bank at gunpoint, and she wins it with a scream — so the ending feels earned rather than propagated, and the butterfly reading may be something the film dangles and then quietly abandons. Press the opposite direction too, since the film's three runs are the controlled re-run no historian ever gets: does the film thereby expose counterfactual history of the for-want-of-a-nail kind as untestable, or does it simply assume the extreme sensitivity that such history would have to argue for? And say plainly where the film resists the framework altogether, including the flash-forward stills of the bystanders' futures and the blind woman who seems to know what is going on.

Please answer in three parts: (a) at most 500 words of prose giving the reading; (b) a markdown table with exactly these columns — Beat (name a specific scene or moment from any run), Best-fit model (one of Amplification, Basin-switch, Severing, none of them), What that model predicts here, What the film actually does with the beat, Fit (holds / partly holds / resists), Why (one sentence) — with four to six rows, at least two of them for beats that break or resist the framework; (c) a final one-sentence line, Verdict, stating whether the film belongs on a syllabus about chaos and contingency in history and, if so, what it should be there to teach.
