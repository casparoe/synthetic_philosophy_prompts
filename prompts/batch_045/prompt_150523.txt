I score probabilistic forecasts for a living and read epistemology on the side, and I keep bumping into a pattern that I cannot find a name for. I would like it identified, but more than that I would like it carved: what exactly it is, what it is not, and where its edges are. A bare label will not settle the thing that puzzles me.

Start from an ordinary case. Two forecasters agree that there is a 60% chance of some kind of supply shock this quarter and a 40% chance of none. Cara has no view about which kind of shock and, if pressed, splits the 60% evenly between an energy shock and a shipping shock: 0.3 / 0.3 / 0.4. Fin has evidence that an energy shock is the more likely kind and says 0.5 / 0.1 / 0.4. Score them with the ordinary Brier score, the sum of squared differences across all cells, so that a two-cell forecast of 0.6 / 0.4 gets 0.72 when the second cell comes true and 0.32 when the first does.

On the two-cell question the two of them are indistinguishable in every possible outcome. On the three-cell question they come apart, and in opposite directions depending on what happens: if the energy shock occurs Fin scores 0.42 to Cara's 0.74, if the shipping shock occurs Fin scores 1.22 to Cara's 0.74, and if nothing happens Fin scores 0.62 to Cara's 0.54. Take Fin's own numbers as the truth, so that he is perfectly calibrated. Then his expected penalty is 0.58 on the three-cell question and 0.48 on the two-cell question; Cara's is 0.66 and 0.48.

So: Fin's opinions and the world stayed exactly the same, and his expected accuracy fell by 0.1 merely because the question asked of him was finer-grained. A third forecaster who knows strictly less than Fin but is only ever asked the coarse question posts the better record than Fin does. And two forecasters who are tied at coarse grain in every single outcome can be ranked in either direction at fine grain.

What I think I have noticed is that the grain of the question list is doing normative work that nobody acknowledges. The algebra I did suggests one reason: expected Brier penalty splits into a term that depends only on how the truth is distributed over the cells and a term that measures the distance between the truth distribution and the forecast, and it is the first term — the one the forecaster has no control over — that inflates when you split a cell. But coarsening does not reliably shrink the second term either; I can make it grow by arranging that a forecaster's errors inside a cell all point the same way.

Three things it is not, I think, though I want this checked. It is not the familiar complaint that accuracy verdicts depend on your choice of scoring rule, since here the rule is held fixed and only the question list varies. It is not, I believe, just the calibration-versus-sharpness distinction, since that is usually drawn for forecasts about a fixed question, whereas here the question itself changes. And it is not the worry about how fine-grained the space of possibilities should be before you apply these measures at all, which is about metaphysics rather than about which questions you are asked. It also feels related to, but not the same as, the old observation that if you score full beliefs by counting true and false ones, the person who believes nothing comes out maximally accurate.

Does this pattern have an established concept in the accuracy-first and epistemic utility literature, or in the forecasting literature where people score predictions for a living? Which existing notion is nearest, how does the thing I have described differ from each candidate, and does anything fit it exactly? If nothing does, give me a name, a definition stated tightly enough that I can tell which cases fall under it, and at least one case that looks like mine but is not.
