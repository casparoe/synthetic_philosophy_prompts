I've been going round in circles on Singer's drowning child and I want to know what I actually think. Here are my gut verdicts, case by case:

- Shallow pond, kid drowning, I'd ruin a 200-dollar jacket: I definately have to wade in.
- Same size harm prevented, but the cost is that I'd miss the only flight to my mum's deathbed: not required, and I don't feel bad about it.
- Same harm prevented by donating 200 dollars to a charity instead of wading in: required, I think — but I don't *feel* it the same way and I can't tell if that's just weakness of will.
- Ten other people are standing at the pond, any of them could wade in, none of them do: I still have to, and their being there changes nothing.
- But: ten thousand people could each donate the 200 dollars that would save a statistical kid, none of them will, and I'd be paying alone: this one feels much weaker, like I'm being asked to cover other people's shares. I don't know why these two come apart for me.
- I left the gate open and the kid fell in: I have to, and walking on is clearly worse than in the plain pond case.
- The kid's dad pushed him in and is standing right there, perfectly able to help: my duty feels almost gone, even though the kid still dies if I walk.
- Saving him means wrecking a stranger's parked car — I'd pay them back in full, but it's their car and they're not here to agree: torn. Something says don't, something says obviously do it and settle up.
- Saving him carries a 1-in-50 chance of killing me too: I lean toward "required," and it feels insane.

Is there one principle that gets most of these, or am I just holding two that conflict — one about costs and benefits, one about whose job it is and who caused the problem? If it's two, which verdict do I give up, and what does whatever you land on say about the case I keep failing to settle: the rescue would save the kid but cost me something I've built my life around, say quitting the orchestra I've played in for twenty years, which matters enormously to me and to nobody else?
