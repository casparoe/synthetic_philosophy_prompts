Retired electrophysiologist here — forty years of sticking electrodes into visual cortex without once needing the word consciousness, and now in retirement I've drifted into philosophy of mind and I'm trying to get my own house in order before the reading group I joined starts poking at it. Roughly where I've landed:

(1) Heterophenomenology, more or less as Dennett sets it out, is the right method: the subject's utterances are data, you don't grant them any special authority, and the theory has to explain why they say what they say. (2) The hard problem is real — you can nail every discriminative capacity and function and still not have explained why any of it is accompanied by experience, and a science that only tracks reports and behavior hasn't answered the question I actually care about. (3) I do think reports are decent evidence: when a practiced psychophysical observer says the two patches look different to her, I'm inclined to treat that as a finding about her experience, and researchers who write off first-person data as noise have thrown out the phenomenon. (4) My dog is conscious and so is a three-month-old, and neither can produce anything the method would recognize as data. (5) On-the-fly introspection is definately unreliable — change blindness, split-brain confabulation, that whole literature — so no armchair self-observation in the theory.

I've also been toying with an extension, what I call a response-calibrated protocol: first measure a subject's report accuracy on stimuli you can independently verify, get a reliability coefficient per subject per task, then treat that subject's reports on the interesting stimuli as instrument readings with error bars rather than as bare verbal behavior. It feels to me like heterophenomenology done properly rather than a rival to it, but someone in the group says it quietly abandons the whole point.
