I run human-annotation projects in which raters read a short vignette and label whether some side effect of what an agent did was brought about intentionally, and my raters' splits look like the Knobe effect: in the chairman-style pair — a company head is told a new program will raise profits and will, as a side effect, harm (or help) the environment, says he does not care about the environment at all, and approves the program — the harmful version gets labeled intentional far more often than the helpful one. I need to know whether my rubric is measuring anything stable, so before I read further into the literature I want the specification. Write the desiderata that an explanation of this effect has to meet to be usable for that purpose: the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse (foreseeing versus aiming, intentionality versus blameworthiness versus responsibility, harm versus mere badness or rule-breaking, blame versus praise), and what it must say about what the data are even evidence for — the folk concept of intentional action, the psychology of attribution, or intentional action itself. Include the variants my own batches keep producing: a side effect that is merely norm-violating or distasteful rather than harmful, one the agent actively regrets but accepts anyway, one that benefits the agent personally and affects no one else, and one that is certain rather than merely likely. For each requirement give the case or consideration behind it, mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of a particular camp, and flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. Then, briefly, how the main families fare — semantic or conceptual-role accounts, deep-self accounts, and conversational or affect-based deflationary accounts — and name the single requirement that excludes the most of them.
