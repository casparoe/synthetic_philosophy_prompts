I'm putting together a long-form piece — more of a zine chapter than an academic thing — on whether individual contributions to collective harms matter: the inefficacy objection and the pile of moves people make to get around it. I've got a shortlist of replies and I need to decide which ones deserve a section of thier own and which get a paragraph and a shrug. The trouble is they all sound fine in the abstract and I can't tell which ones are actually doing any work.

So: score each reply out of 100 for how much real work it does, then sort them into tiers (A = gets its own section, B = gets a paragraph, C = mention and move on), with a sentence or two on why it lands where it does. By "doing work" I mean coverage and robustness multiplied together: how wide a range of cases the reply has something to say about, times whether it still has anything to say in the very worst case rather than quietly collapsing into hand-waving. Be brutal, and give me your own ranking, not a survey of what people usually say.

Here are the cases I want them tested against; I've tried to build the list so that it separates the replies rather than letting them all look equally good:

1. Smooth. Harm is a continuous function of the number of contributors, no thresholds anywhere, and no uncertainty about how many other people contribute. Your one act changes the total by a tiny amount you could in principle compute.
2. Lumpy. Harms only switch on at thresholds — every extra thousand units sold and the supermarket orders another batch, a species goes at N, a plant adds a shift. Usually you're nowhere near a threshold, occasionally you're right on one, and you can't tell which.
3. Sunk supply. It's already in your fridge. Bought, dead, nothing you do now propagates upstream. Eat it or bin it, the world comes out the same.
4. Screened off. Whatever you do, someone else was going to do it anyway and you know it — the neighbour who drives to the station regardless, the safe seat, the shift that gets covered either way.
5. Invisible. Nobody ever observes your choice and it leaves no trace in any record anyone else can read. So if there was a reason to act, it wasn't that anyone found out.

And the replies, under labels I made up — argue with the moves, not with my names:

(a) Expected difference. Small chance of a big difference times a big difference is an expected value that isn't negligible, and once you add up a lifetime of such acts it isn't small at all.
(b) Threshold lottery. In real cases there's nearly always a threshold somewhere in the causal chain, and there's always some probability you're sitting exactly on it; you just can't tell which act is the one that matters, so you should treat each as if it might be.
(c) Group membership. You aren't a lone atom; you're a member of a group that together produces the outcome, and members of a collectively harming group can be on the hook even when no single member makes a difference.
(d) Practice participation. No individual instance is worth anything, but the practice of voting, or of not eating animals, has enormous expected value; what's justified is joining the practice, and joining is something you do one act at a time.
(e) Expression and integrity. The question isn't what you cause, it's what your act expresses or what sort of person you're being; refusing is about not lending yourself to the thing, not about moving the needle.
(f) Universalisation. If everyone did it, disaster, so don't. (With the standard objection attached: the conditional that actually matters is "if everyone *else* did it", which is a different and much weaker premise.)
(g) No-replacement counterfactual. The right comparison isn't "does the world differ" but "does the world differ given that nobody steps into your place" — supply chains, replacement workers, the next customer in the queue. In most real cases the honest answer is that the harm gets moved rather than avoided, which relocates the whole argument from your act to where in the chain an intervention would bite.
(h) Observability amplifier. Your choice is visible to some number of people, they update a little, they're visible to others, and so on; even a tiny per-person effect gets multiplied by a conformist cascade. Which means the value of the act depends on how many people are watching — strong in public, dead in case 5.
(i) Capacity building, or option value. Even when the act does nothing to the outcome, it builds or maintains what does the work later: habits, skills, infrastructure, a coalition that can be mobilised, the fact that there's still a place on your street that sells the alternative. Treat the act as an investment that keeps a future option open rather than as a cause of this outcome.
(j) Efficacy isn't the relevant notion. Consequentialism about individual acts is the mistake being smuggled in; morality constrains what you may do, and "it made no difference" simply isn't the kind of consideration that makes an act permissible.
(k) De minimis. Below some size of expected difference there is no moral reason at all, full stop, and every reply above smuggles in a threshold-free principle that nobody has ever actually defended.

Three more things, briefly: if any two of these are secretly the same move in different words, say which and say whether collapsing them loses anything. If one of them only looks good because of a case I've left off my list, say which and name the missing case. And give me the single extra case you'd add that would shake up the ranking most, with the resulting tier changes.
