My friend — ex-ML PhD, now at some alignment-ish thing, reflexively anti-hype about anything preference-learning — left a giant substack comment about inverse reinforcement learning that I can't evaluate. As best I can reconstruct it, his take is roughly:

IRL is the problem of taking an agent's behavior and recovering the reward function that explains it, and the classic degeneracy result from the early 2000s basically kills it: for any finite batch of behavior there are infinitely many reward functions that make that behavior optimal, including the all-zeros reward, under which every policy counts as optimal. So it's underdetermined in exactly the way fitting a thousand parameters to ten data points is underdetermined — you're not learning anything, you're picking a story. The usual patch is to assume the demonstrator is Boltzmann-rational (noisily optimal: actions chosen with probability proportional to exp of their value, with a temperature knob), but that doesn't rescue it either, because the temperature is itself a free parameter, so any conclusion of the form "cold agent with reward A" can always be rewritten as "hot agent with reward B," and you still pin down nothing. Therefore, he says, the modern stuff about inferring human preferences from behavior is behavior cloning in a trenchcoat: it can only ever reproduce the behavior distribution it was trained on, it inherits all of behavior cloning's brittleness, and anyone claiming that a smart enough IRL system would figure out what humans really want is selling something. He also tossed in that this is why two reward functions differing by a potential-based shaping term can't be told apart, which he called "the same degeneracy, restated."

Two things make me suspicious, but I genuinely don't know enough to say which of us is wrong. (1) The all-zeros example feels like cheating somehow — isn't ruling it out exactly what the noisy-rationality assumption is for? Under Boltzmann rationality a constant reward should give you near-uniform flailing, not the demonstrator's actual behavior. (2) He keeps using "IRL can never distinguish caring about X from caring about looking like you care about X" as his flagship example, but that seems like precisely the case you'd test by dropping the agent into a new environment with no audience: if it still does the thing, you've learned something, and isn't that the entire point of inferring a reward rather than a policy? Though maybe I'm conflating "identified in principle" with "identified from the data we'd actually have."

So: what would someone who actually works on this accept, what would they wince at, and what is flat wrong? Is the temperature thing a real equivalence — reward identified only up to positive scaling, or up to scaling plus an additive constant? — or is he overclaiming, and does it even matter for the things people want to do with a learned reward (transfer to new environments, comparing how much two people want something, plugging it into an optimizer)? Is the shaping-invariance point really the same degeneracy as the zeros thing, or is he lumping together a shallow underdetermination that better assumptions dissolve and a deep one that survives even ideal data? Does the behavior-cloning conclusion follow from the parts of the argument that are right, or does it need a separate argument of its own? And does any of this actually license the skepticism he wants — that this whole style of value learning is definately doomed — or is the real problem somewhere else entirely (misspecified rationality models, humans not really being optimizers of anything, Goodhart-flavored trouble with what you do with a reward once you've got it)?
