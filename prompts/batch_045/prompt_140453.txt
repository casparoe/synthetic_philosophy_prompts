I am giving a paper in three weeks to a joint seminar of my own department's political philosophy group and the university's welfare economics group, and what I need from you is a Q&A preparation sheet rather than a defence of the view.

The thesis I am defending is one I have been calling ratio-limited totalism. The claim is that the utility monster does not tell against the summing of welfare as such, but only against summing over a scale on which interpersonal welfare ratios are unbounded. On my view we should maximise total welfare subject to a trimming constraint: each person's welfare enters the sum only up to a level fixed as some multiple K of the mean welfare of everyone else, and no person's welfare counts for less than the corresponding fraction below that mean. Below the cap, ordinary totalism; above it, the extra welfare simply stops counting, rather as extremely large incomes stop counting in a capped voting scheme. Because ratios of welfare levels are not well defined unless the origin of the scale is fixed, I fix that origin at a specified neutral point and argue, first, that the fixing is a substantive moral posit rather than a measurement claim, and, second, that nothing in the argument depends on knowing where the neutral point is.

The case I lean on hardest is one I construct myself, because the standard monster can be dismissed as a fantasy about one exotic being. Call it the diffuse monster. Suppose that within an ordinary population there are ten thousand people who convert goods into welfare about 1.2 times as efficiently as everyone else: a given bundle of resources reliably produces in each of them a life going modestly better than it would for anyone else. Nobody is dramatically better off, nobody is strange, and there is no single creature on whom the totalist verdict can be blamed. Total utilitarianism nevertheless says that, so long as the efficient minority has not yet hit the point of diminishing returns, resources should be transferred to them and to no one else, repeatedly, even when the rest of the population is badly off. I also consider in passing a variant I call the latent monster, whose efficiency advantage cannot be detected before the resources are transferred, and I use it to argue that the difficulty is not merely an epistemological one about identifying converters in advance.

My argument runs in four steps: that the alarming verdicts flow from unbounded ratios rather than from aggregation; that unboundedness is independently dispensable, since a totalist can accept a trimming rule without giving up the thought that benefits always count and always count equally below the cap; that the diffuse monster shows why replies that depend on the weirdness of a single alien being will not generalise; and that the resulting view is not prioritarianism, because it weights a benefit by the position of its recipient only at the cap and not at every level.

The seminar will be about twenty people and I will get roughly twenty-five minutes of questions, so I can give six or seven substantive answers and must concede or deflect the rest in a sentence. The audience breaks down roughly as follows: six philosophers of distributive justice, mostly consequentialist in sympathy, unmoved by intuitions about imaginary cases, one of them a totalist who bites every bullet and thinks the monster is undergraduate fare; four economists, one of whom works on social choice and aggregation theorems and will want to know what properties my social ordering has, whether it is continuous, whether it satisfies anything like anonymity and the Pareto principle, and whether it can be given a usable representation at all; another who works on optimal income taxation and is sceptical that interpersonal comparisons of welfare can be made in the form my view requires; three prioritarians or sufficientarians who will suspect that my cap is simply their view with an extra parameter bolted on; a couple of doctoral students with non-consequentialist sympathies; and a chair who works on the history of economic thought and will ask where the whole enterprise of interpersonal comparison came from.

What I would like from you is the objections I will actually get, ordered by a combination of how likely they are from this audience and how much damage each would do if I answered it badly. Please phrase each one the way that person would phrase it, in their own vocabulary rather than mine, and for each give me a reply of no more than about two minutes together with an honest note on where the reply is weakest. Tell me which objections have genuine force and which are merely dialectically likely, what I should concede in the first two minutes rather than be forced into later, the single reply that has to land if the paper is to survive the session, and any load-bearing step in the four-step argument that I appear not to have noticed is load-bearing. Finally, I have a private worry about the negative side of the cap: if no one's welfare may count for less than the reciprocal of the multiple, then someone in sufficiently extreme agony appears to matter less than they should, and I am not sure my trimming rule can be restricted to the positive side without collapsing back into the view I was objecting to. Tell me whether that is in fact the question I should be hoping nobody asks, or whether there is something worse waiting for me.

Please write your answer in British English.
