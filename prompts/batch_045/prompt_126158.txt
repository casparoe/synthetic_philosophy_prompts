For more than a century Bertrand's chord problem has served as the standard exhibit against the principle of indifference, and the dispute around it seems to me to have stopped moving. I would like a diagnosis of that impasse, not another rehearsal of the three answers.

The set-up is familiar enough to state quickly: an equilateral triangle is inscribed in a circle, a chord is chosen at random, and we ask for the probability that it is longer than a side of the triangle. Choosing two points uniformly on the circumference and joining them gives one third; choosing a radius uniformly and then a point uniformly along it, and drawing the perpendicular chord, gives one half; choosing a point uniformly in the disc and making it the chord's midpoint gives one quarter. Procedures now known to give every value in between have been described as well, so the multiplicity is not an artefact of three badly chosen examples. What I want to understand is why nobody converges.

Three responses are still live, and I will characterise them as I have met them. The invariancist holds that the problem, properly posed, does have a unique solution: if a definite answer is to be compatible with total ignorance, the distribution over chords must be indifferent to the position and size of the circle, since neither is given; imposing scale and translation invariance and solving the resulting equations singles out the random-radius procedure and the answer one half, on the ground that the other two procedures smuggle in information the problem does not contain. The mechanist holds that probabilities attach to chance set-ups, that the words chosen at random specify no set-up, and that the three procedures describe three different experiments each of which is a perfectly good realisation of the description; on this view there is nothing to be settled a priori, and actually tossing straws would only measure whichever apparatus one happened to build. The indeterminist agrees that nothing is settled but draws a different moral: the principle of indifference is sound, what it delivers under genuine ignorance is a set of measures rather than a single one, and the right response is to change the formalism, represent the state by an interval or a family of measures, and stop asking for a number.

A formal proposal I recently encountered makes me suspect that at least part of the standoff is a hidden mathematical question. Restrict attention to the one-parameter family of procedures in which the chord's midpoint is drawn with radial density proportional to d raised to the power alpha, alpha greater than minus one, and the chord is taken perpendicular to the radius through it; alpha equal to zero is the random-radius procedure, alpha equal to one is the random-midpoint procedure, and the resulting probability is one half raised to the power alpha plus one. Now impose a self-similarity requirement: condition the procedure on the chord's meeting the concentric disc of radius R over two, and require that the induced distribution over chords of that smaller disc be the same procedure applied to the smaller circle. I have verified by hand that alpha equal to zero and alpha equal to one both satisfy this, and my informant claims that every member of the family does, while the random-endpoint procedure does not. Please check whether that is right, and then tell me what it is worth. It looks to me like genuine but limited progress: an infinite-dimensional freedom is reduced to a single real parameter, and the parameter is not determined by the constraint.

My own hypothesis is that the word random in the problem statement is doing the work of three different questions at once: which measure is compatible with ignorance, what a chance set-up must specify before probabilities attach, and what an agent should do when no measure is singled out. On that diagnosis the debate has not stalled from difficulty but from equivocation, and the invariancist's uniqueness claim, the mechanist's appeal to apparatus, and the indeterminist's change of formalism are answers to different questions rather than rival answers to one. But I want this tested against what each side actually argues, and I want to know what it leaves unexplained. In particular: is the choice among alpha values a factual question in disguise, about which physical randomisations are natural or which are stable under perturbation? Is the invariancist's selection of a transformation group an a priori constraint or an empirical bet about the world's indifference? Do the sides disagree about which cases count as data, so that physical straw-tossing, scientific practice with maximum-entropy priors, and intuitions about idealised set-ups are being weighted in incommensurable ways? Or is the persistence of the dispute evidence that the question is ill-posed?

Given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: another formal constraint, a convergence or stability theorem about physical randomisation, a decision-theoretic argument for imprecise probabilities, an experiment, or a change of question. And if you think no progress should be expected, say why, and say what that shows about the principle of indifference rather than merely about this example.
