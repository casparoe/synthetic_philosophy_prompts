I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and I am working on a piece about habitat destruction and wild-animal suffering. Before I interview the people who actually do this work, I want to be clear about what their practice already assumes, because I suspect the argument is settled before anyone states it.

The practice is biodiversity offsetting, as it is now codified in no-net-loss and net-gain rules for development: a road, a housing estate or a data centre is permitted to destroy habitat on condition that the loss is compensated by restoring or creating habitat elsewhere, or, where that is impossible, by buying credits from a habitat bank, and in the English rule now in force by delivering a 10 per cent net gain, so that the ledger closes in credit rather than merely balanced. As I understand it, the currency is the habitat unit, arrived at roughly by multiplying the area of the habitat by a score for its ecological condition and a score for how distinctive or irreplaceable it is, with adjustments for how long the replacement will last and how likely it is to fail. The units count area, vegetation and species composition. They do not count animals, or births, or deaths, or anything about how those lives go. A field restored to a condition that will carry two hundred voles, nearly all of whom will die before they are a year old, registers as an unqualified gain.

Two things about this puzzle me. The same bodies that score nature this way spend enormous sums to save a handful of individuals of a rare species, and spend more again culling other individuals in order to protect habitat for the first ones, so individual animals are not simply invisible to them. And the ledger refuses some trades: like-for-like requirements, special protection for habitat judged irreplaceable, and the demand that any claimed gain be additional, that is, over and above what would have happened anyway. That last one is what I keep coming back to. The mechanism is scrupulously counterfactual about habitat and entirely non-counterfactual about the animals in it.

So here is what I need. Reconstruct the theory of value and welfare the practice presupposes. Does pricing nature in units of area and condition commit its users to the view that species and ecosystems matter in their own right, or to the view that wild lives are on balance worth living so that more habitat straightforwardly means more welfare, or to nothing at all beyond what is measurable and administrable? Be precise about the difference: which of these does the practice strictly require, and which does it merely fit so well with that advocates of each could still claim offsetting as their own? Where do its parts pull against one another?

Then the part I actually need for the piece. Suppose the implicit theory were replaced with a sentience-based one that took seriously the possibility that most wild lives contain more suffering than happiness. What would have to change in how a project is appraised: could restoration itself count as a harm, could culling or contraception count as a benefit, and would the offset mechanism survive in any recognisable form?
