Me and two friends have this hopeless little reading group — we meet in a pub every other Tuesday and argue about whatever one of us found on the internet that week — and a few weeks back we got completely stuck on something I think is called the toxin puzzle (attributed to someone named Kavka? I may have the name wrong). As I understood it: an eccentric billionaire hands you a vial and says, think it over tonight; if by midnight you have genuinely formed the intention to drink it tomorrow morning, I'll wire you a million dollars at midnight, whether or not you actually drink it; drinking it just gives you about a day of mild flu, nothing else. So the money is paid for the intention, not the act, and by tomorrow morning you'll have no reason whatsoever to drink. My friend Dave says it's impossible: if you intend to do something you must believe you'll do it, and you can't believe you'll do it while knowing you'll have no reason. My other friend says that's exactly backwards, that the whole job of an intention is to survive the moment your reasons change, and that if Dave were right no plan or promise could ever mean anything. We went round for two hours and got nowhere, and now its leaking into everything I think about.

Which is bad timing, because I'm best man at my mate Tom's wedding on Saturday and I still have nothing written. Tom's the one who got me into this stuff — he did a philosophy degree, works in data science now, still turns up to decision-theory reading groups for fun — and roughly half the room is his old department crowd, who will absolutely heckle me if I say something sloppy about what an intention is, or if I sneak in a prudential "your reasons will keep pointing the same way" speech, since that's the bit the puzzle seems to break. The other half is his fiancée's huge family, who have never heard any of this and mainly want to cry a bit and eat cake. Here's the actual problem: I can't do the toast built on "you'll stay together because you'll each go on having good reasons to," because that looks like exactly the thing you can't count on — the point of a vow, if it's worth anything, is that it's supposed to hold at the moment the reasons stop lining up. But I also can't do the cynical version where nobody can really promise anything, because it's a wedding. And Tom has this line he half means, that a public promise is basically a toxin-puzzle intention: the payoff is right there at the altar, in the saying of it, and everything after is the part you have no reason to do. His old supervisor will be there too, and I know he'll be watching to see whether I fall back on the Ulysses-contract reading — tying your future self up — which he thinks is cheating and Tom thinks is the only honest story we've got. I want to answer Tom without sounding like I agree with him.

So write me the toast: five minutes max, no "love isn't a feeling, it's a choice" cliché, no decision-theory term explained for its own sake, nothing the non-philosophers can't follow, and nothing I'd be embarassed to say out loud — no promising anything about Tom's future that I don't think he's in a position to promise. It needs to land for the philosophy half as actually engaging the puzzle (that you can intend what you know you'll have no reason to do, and that this is what makes a promise more than a prediction about yourself) and land for everyone else as affection rather than a lecture. Give it to me as exactly five numbered beats, each one a sentence or two, no introduction and no conclusion, so I can print it on an index card.
