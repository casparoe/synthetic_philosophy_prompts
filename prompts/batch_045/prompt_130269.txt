Hey — I'm doing one of those unstructured night reading groups (we meet in the back room of a brewery, which is not as conducive to philosophy as you'd think) and last night we got completely stuck on something, so I'm bringing it here. We'd gone through Putnam's Twin Earth setup: the duplicate planet where the clear drinkable liquid is XYZ instead of H2O, nobody can tell the difference from the inside, and the moral that Oscar and Twin Oscar can be molecule-for-molecule identical and still mean different things by 'water' because the stuff in their respective environments differs — the meaning isn't in the head. Fine, everyone more or less accepted that. Then Rowan, who'd been quiet all evening, floated a variant and it set Deniz off, and the two of them went at it for about forty minutes while the rest of us just sat there. The variant: forget a separate twin planet. Imagine our own Earth, except that starting sometime around 1900 a slow geological/industrial process begins replacing the H2O in the water cycle with XYZ at something like a third of a percent a year. Nobody ever notices a thing: the liquid always looks, tastes, smells, freezes, boils, dissolves things and keeps you alive exactly like the stuff before it. Every generation learns the word 'water' from the generation before it, each generation's chemists analyse whatever is coming out of the tap and duly record its composition, and no one ever has any sense that the word has shifted. By the 2200s the taps run pure XYZ. So: does 'water' then mean XYZ, or does it still mean H2O?

Their exchange, roughly as well as I can reconstruct it (I numbered it so its easier to talk about): (1) Rowan said XYZ, and that externalism predicts this outright — the reference is fixed by the actual stuff in the speakers' environment, and in 2200 the actual stuff is XYZ; the whole point of the original case is that the environment does the work and the head doesn't, and the historical chain of use is just the usual mechanism by which the fixing happens, not something that outranks the stuff itself. (2) Deniz said H2O: the term was introduced for H2O, every generation has been deferring to the previous one while explicitly taking itself to be talking about the same liquid its grandparents drank and the same liquid the older textbooks described, and reference is inherited rather than re-earned by each generation's causal contact. (3) Rowan then raised the error objection — on Deniz's view every sincere twenty-third-century utterance of 'the glass contains water' is false, and an entire linguistic community is systematically and undetectably mistaken about the contents of its own kitchen taps for centuries, which is exactly the kind of wholesale-error result that externalism was supposed to have gotten us out of. (4) Deniz said that isn't a cost, it's the moral: Oscar can be wrong about water, so a community can hold wildly false beliefs about what water is made of and still refer to water perfectly well; being wrong about the composition of the stuff in front of you is not the same as failing to refer to it. (5) Rowan then went for the middle of the case — at some point the taps are running a fifty-fifty mix, and he said Deniz's story has to say either that 'water' flips from one substance to the other at some particular percentage, which no one could ever pick on a principled basis, or that it refers to a mixture that nobody in the scenario ever encounters as a mixture; either way, he said, the historical-chain view can't even state its own answer at the halfway point. (6) Deniz replied that the vagueness is Rowan's problem and not hers: being H2O isn't a vague property, so a theory that manufactures borderline cases where nature has none is a theory with a faulty premise — it shows hidden microstructure can't be doing the reference-fixing, since nobody in the drift scenario is tracking microstructure at all. She added that if you actually asked the 2200 chemists what they take themselves to be talking about, they'd say 'the same liquid our ancestors called water', which is a same-stuff intention, not a whatever-happens-to-be-here-now intention.

I had to leave before they finished and I honestly can't tell who's winning — I kept flip-flopping. Could you take it apart move by move, saying for each of the six whether its right, mostly right, or wrong and why, then give your verdict on who's more right overall, and say what you think both of them are missing? One thing I couldn't work out is whether there even is a fact of the matter here, or whether the case just dissolves into separate questions depending on what you think a theory of reference is supposed to do — predict and systematise usage, explain how communication succeeds, or track what speakers take themselves to be talking about. And nobody raised this, but it's been nagging at me: what if you stipulate a single immortal speaker who lives through the whole three hundred years, never notices the change, and keeps using the word the whole time — does that change the answer, and if so, does that tell us something about whether the question is really about individual idiolects or about communities?
