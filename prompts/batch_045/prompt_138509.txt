I have no academic background in philosophy, but a friend and I have now spent about a year arguing about Kavka's toxin puzzle, and we have reached the point of talking past each other. The version we use: shortly before midnight, a billionaire credits a large sum to your account if, at midnight, you have formed the intention to drink, before noon the next day, a vial of toxin that will make you thoroughly miserable for about a day and do no lasting harm. The money is paid for the intention alone; you never have to drink. Once midnight has passed, drinking costs you a day of misery and gains you nothing. The question we keep circling is whether you can form that intention at all.

Please draft the joint statement the two of us should have produced. The two positions are, first, that intending to do something is constitutively tied to wanting to do it, or at least to being motivationally oriented toward doing it, so that in the toxin case the intention cannot be formed; and second, that intending is a matter of settling a question for yourself and letting that settlement structure your later reasoning and action, which requires no desire to succeed, so that the intention can be formed. Give each at its strongest, in wording its own side would accept rather than in the wording the other side would find easiest to reject.

Three cases of my own that I want used as the joint tests, because I think each bears on a crux. First, the escrow variant: before midnight you may irrevocably sign the money away to a cause you detest if you fail to drink, so that after midnight you have a strong reason to drink; can you then form the intention, and if the answer changes, what was doing the work in the original case? Second, the unmovable decider: an agent, human or not, whose plan-states are set by a bare commit operation with no motivational backing and which then reliably guide its action; is that state an intention? Third, the doxastic twin: you plainly cannot believe, at midnight, that you will drink; is the inability to intend the same kind of inability as the inability to believe, or a different one? Add further cases only if you think mine miss a crux.

Structure the response in these sections, in this order.

1. Agreed claims — 8 to 10 numbered claims both sides could sign, none of them trivial and none merely verbal. About 200 words.
2. Where we differ — a table with one row per disagreement: the neutral statement of the issue, the wording the first camp accepts for its own view, the wording the second camp accepts for its own view, and a note on whether the difference is one of substance or of words. About 200 words.
3. The cruxes — numbered sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side; for each, the case or argument both sides accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. About 350 words.
4. Verbal or real — a short verdict on which parts of our dispute are about the word intention rather than about the thing, and why. About 100 words.
5. Where I'd put my money — I do not want a survey of options: commit to which side has the better of each crux, with one line of reasoning for each and a rough confidence beside each verdict, then name the single claim you would abandon first if pressed. About 150 words.

Please do not cite or name papers or authors; I cannot check them, and it would not settle anything for me.
