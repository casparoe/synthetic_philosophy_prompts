i'm helping a friend who's writing on Hume's essay about the standard of taste, and I want to open my section with something funny instead of another paragraph of setup. Could you write a page from the official examination handbook of an invented licensing body that certifies "True Judges" — the people entitled to settle disputes about beauty — as numbered clauses, about 550 words, in deadpan civil-service prose, with no winking at the reader.

Background so you dont have to go look anything up: the essay starts from the fact that people disagree wildly about taste and that beauty, for Hume, isn't a property of objects at all but a sentiment in the mind of whoever contemplates them; he then goes looking for a standard anyway and lands on the joint verdict of qualified critics, the true judges. True judges are supposed to be picked out by five marks: strong good sense, delicacy of sentiment (the knack for catching faint qualities everyone else misses — his example is two connoisseurs tasting a hogshead of old wine who detect a trace of iron and leather, later traced to a key on a leather thong at the bottom of the cask), plenty of practice at judging, wide comparison across works and periods, and freedom from prejudice.

What I want the joke to do is take that structure seriously and let the bureaucracy chew on it. The examiners who set and grade the exam have to be certified true judges themselves, and the handbook should notice this and handle it the way a real committee would — a grandfather clause, a transitional provision, an equivalency route for experienced practitioners — without ever admitting anything is wrong. The five marks should recieve the same treatment as testable competences that quietly can't be tested: freedom from prejudice means withholding all information about the work's author, date and genre, which makes comparison across periods impossible; practice and comparison mean no one can ever have finished qualifying. Include at least one clause where a candidate's actual felt pleasure gets overridden by the rubric — failing the sentiment module, being required to re-sit after remedial practice — since that's the point where the whole thing turns into a rule correcting a sentiment. Maybe close with an interim-certification clause that lets people practise while uncertified.

Constraints: don't break character to explain the joke, don't add a note about Hume afterward, and don't resolve anything. I want someone who knows the essay to come away with the real worry — whether the standard is anything more than the taste of the people who already agree with each other — and someone who doesn't to have enjoyed a straight-faced document about an impossible exam. No character should say out loud that it's circular.
