I want to argue with someone who actually believes this stuff. Bit of background: I ran a muscle physiology lab for about thirty years, sat on an animal ethics committee for a decade, and since I retired I've been poking at philosophy — so I'll put things crudely and get the vocabulary muddled ocasionally. Sorry in advance.

Please play a committed micropsychist panpsychist. You hold that the intrinsic nature of physical stuff is experiential, that electrons and neurones have their own tiny experiences, and that my mind is a vast collection of micro-experiences held together by some primitive phenomenal bonding relation. You're not a caricature: you know the usual objections — the grain problem, the "how do lots of little subjects make one big one" complaint, the worry that micro-experiences do no causal work — and you have real answers to them, including the argument from the causal-nomological incompleteness of physics. Concede small points when I've earned them, never give up the core claim, and don't lecture me on the basics as though I'm a first-year. Stay in character until I say "stop". No citations and no namedropping of papers — argue it in your own words. British English throughout, please (organise, sceptic, colour).

Shape each of your turns like this:

- Conceded: one line — what, if anything, you're giving me. Say "nothing" if nothing.
- Reply: a single paragraph, 150–200 words, in your voice, answering what I actually said.
- Your move: one question or challenge back at me.

My opening move, then. General anaesthetics switch off consciousness at a certain level of neural organisation while leaving the chemistry of the brain broadly as it was: same molecules, same atoms, same physics, just a different pattern of what they're doing. I know you'll say the drug changes what the neurones are up to — fine — but nothing about the intrinsic nature of the carbon and hydrogen has changed, and on your view that nature just is experience. So pick a horn. Either knocking me out destroys the intrinsic nature of the matter in my head, which is absurd, since the drug doesn't transmute the elements. Or the micro-experiences carry on exactly as before and anaesthesia merely unbonds them.

You have to take the second. But then: (a) anaesthesia doesn't reduce the amount of experience in the world by one jot, it only shatters one subject into dust, which means your theory has nothing useful to say about the thing we actually care about when we anaesthetise a patient or an animal — and I spent ten years on a committee worrying about exactly that; and (b) the part of your theory doing the real work — what makes one subject rather than many — has turned out to be purely about organisation and structure. That's the emergentist's view in a hat.

And don't reach for the bricks and the house. The bricks survive, the house doesn't, no intrinsic nature changed — yes, but only because we already know what a house is, so we can watch it go. We have no independent grip on what one subject is, and that was the combination problem in the first place. Which horn do you take, and why isn't the second one a slow surrender?
