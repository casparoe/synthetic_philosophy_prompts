I co-host a small Tuesday-night series at the local museum (usually ten people, one bottle of wine, no projector) and next month's session is on when a legend turns into history. We're using three cases: the Trojan War, King Arthur, and the Exodus. I want to open the evening by reading a poem out loud before anyone starts laying out evidence, and I dont want one of those wise-saying poems that lands on a moral. What I have in mind is a proper sestina — six stanzas of six lines, then the three-line envoy, unrhymed, roughly ten or eleven syllables a line so it reads well out loud in a room with bad acoustics. Six end-words; I was thinking ash, layer, name, song, date, king, but swap one or two if you get better lines (keep six, and keep the rotation honest — if stanza one ends ash / layer / name / song / date / king, stanza two has to end king / ash / date / layer / song / name, and the envoy carries all six). The reason I want a sestina specifically: the form never lets the same words sit in the same order twice, and that's sort of the whole problem. Troy, Arthur and Sinai aren't three versions of one question; the same half-dozen things — a burnt layer, a name in a late source, a date, a song everyone already knows — keep getting re-arranged, and which arrangement you end up with depends on what you're willing to count as evidence in the first place.

Here's the angle I keep arguing with my friend about (she does remote sensing and thinks the Arthur problem isn't missing evidence but a missing kind of object — you don't know what sort of thing you'd even be looking for). My own view, which I want the poem to take seriously rather than balance against its opposite: a legend becomes history not when enough evidence piles up in its favour, but when it gets pinned down tightly enough that some possible find could kill it. Pinning is what turns a story into a hypothesis — and note this cuts both ways, because a pinned story that fails the test doesn't become history, it becomes a refuted claim. So Troy has crossed (there's a city, there are destruction layers, and everyone knows that the wrong layer or the wrong century would sink the identification), Arthur hasn't (as far as I remember the earliest near-contemporary source doesn't name him, and the round table and Camelot only show up centuries later — there's nothing to pin him to), and the Exodus has been pinned and is in trouble (no Egyptian record of it, no trace of a huge group crossing Sinai, and the settlement in Canaan looks more like people who were already there than arrivals). Give the three cases some order in the poem that shows that gradient, and let the envoy land the point rather than hand out a lesson. It should be accurate enough that someone who actually works on the period wouldn't wince, and still be a poem for ten people with wine.

One last thing I need: after the poem, five or six lines of prose, no sources and no footnotes (the room has no library and I'm not checking anyway), telling me (a) which line takes the biggest liberty with the evidence, and (b) which line you'd defend hardest if someone in the back who really knows the archaeology pushed back on it. Commit to the ranking — I'd rather you say which of the three has crossed the line and which hasn't, and be wrong in an interesting way, than have you tell me six times in iambic pentameter that it depends what we mean by history.
