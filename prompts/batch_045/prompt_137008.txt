I am writing a novel in which one character, a modeller who works almost entirely inside simulations built from other people's field data, is told by a rival that she has never really learned from a mistake, only from other people's. I need to know whether that charge has any teeth, so I would like the proverb learn from your mistakes taken apart.

Set out the distinct precise theses it could be asserting, ordered roughly from the platitudinous to the radical: whether the mistake must befall the learner herself or may befall only her hypothesis; whether it must actually occur or may be simulated in advance; whether what corrects her must be the world or may be inherited ready-made from other minds; and how much of the slogan's weight is descriptive rather than a claim about credit, blame or what a learner owes. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive, or false, and which kind of creature on the Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian and Gregorian scheme it presupposes — I suspect at least one reading is unavailable to a creature that does not learn at all within a lifetime, and at least one collapses into a claim about a population rather than an individual.

Then tell me which of these readings would actually support my rival's accusation, and whether the accusation survives if the modeller turns out to be a thoroughgoing Gregorian learner whose Popperian testing is delegated, so that nothing ever dies in her stead but a great deal died in someone else's.
