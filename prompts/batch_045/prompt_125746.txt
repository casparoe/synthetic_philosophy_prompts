I'm a formal-methods engineer, and most of my working life is spent in Alloy and TLA+. My routine is: write a relational specification of a system, ask the analyzer for instances within a fixed scope, read the instance diagrams it produces, and — because in my experience almost every real counterexample turns up at scope 3 or 4 — debug against those small instances and treat a specification that survives small-scope checking as probably fine. So I am fluent, without needing them explained, in the following distinctions: a specification versus an instance of it; a relation symbol being interpreted as some set of tuples drawn from a finite universe of atoms; one specification having many non-isomorphic instances; raising scope monotonically growing the set of instances; satisfiability versus truth across all instances; and the gap between what I can see from outside a specification (that every instance the analyzer will ever show me is finite, that this universe contains seven atoms) and what the specification itself asserts.

Skolem's paradox keeps crossing my feed, and my instinct is that it is something I already live with professionally and that the philosophers are dramatizing. I want that instinct tested rather than flattered. So: take the three positions below and restate each one, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually use — spec, instance, scope, interpretation, isomorphic instances, what the modeler sees from outside versus what the spec says inside.

(1) The "Skolemite" reading: no first-order specification of set theory pins down an intended interpretation, and a set's being uncountable is not an absolute property of the set but something that varies between instances of the same specification.
(2) The standard realist reply: the countable instance is not the one we meant; its membership relation is not real membership, it is not transitive, and the enumeration witnessing countability is not itself an object in the instance — so nothing has been shown to be "really" countable.
(3) The move that tries to escape by strengthening the specification language so that relation variables range over all relations rather than over whatever the instance supplies.

Then give me the residue report, and this is the part I actually want. Divide it into what translated cleanly, what translated only after you distorted it to fit, and what has no expression in my vocabulary at all, and for each item say why. Three things in particular:

First: in my world the modeler always stands outside the instance, holding the intended system in mind, and that is exactly the standpoint the realist reply relies on. Is the absence of any such outside standpoint something my vocabulary can even represent, or does the structure of my tools guarantee in advance that I will hear the realist as obviously correct and the Skolemite as confused? If it guarantees that, my instinct is worthless and I want to know it.

Second: the small-scope habit. I routinely accept that a specification's quantifiers range over less than what I naively meant them to range over, and I do not treat that as a crisis. Is that already a commitment to something structurally like Skolemite relativism, or is there a disanalogy that survives scrutiny? I have a candidate disanalogy — that I can always re-run at higher scope, and that I have an intended infinite system the instances approximate — and I want you to press on whether it does the work I am tempted to assign it.

Third: the strengthened language. My impression is that it buys less than it is advertised as buying: it constrains the instance but does not single one out, and what remains relative is the height of the universe rather than the width of its power sets. State precisely what remains relative, and say whether that leftover relativity has the same shape as the original problem or is a different problem wearing the same clothes.

Where your translation had to fudge something to make it fit, say so in as many words; do not paper over a gap by quietly importing an assumption from my framework and calling the result a translation. And give me your verdict on the residue: does what fails to translate point to something my vocabulary cannot capture, or to something less clear in the original dispute than its presentation suggests?

Format: a short paragraph of translation for each of the three positions, then the tripartite residue report, then your verdict. Hard limit of 900 words.
