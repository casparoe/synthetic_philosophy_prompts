I teach an upper-division seminar on Wittgenstein and rule-following: 14 students, one 80-minute session, no technology beyond index cards, markers, and a projector. Design a structured activity that makes students feel the force of Kripke's skeptical paradox before anyone names it.

Constraints on the design:

- The rules must be ones the students invent themselves, over an invented formalism you specify — give the alphabet and the form the rules take, and choose them so that students cannot fall back on "the abstract objects settle it," the way they can with addition.
- No lecture up front, and no mention of Kripke, quus, or the skeptical solution until the debrief.
- The divergence must come from what I'll call a shadow rule: it agrees with the inventor's rule on every case in the training sample and on every case the class could actually compute during the session, but diverges at a precisely specifiable scale beyond their reach (say, strings longer than a million glyphs). The students should still want to say it is the wrong rule even though no evidence available to them could settle the matter — "we just need more data" must be closed off as a reply.
- Small real stakes: a pair's score should depend on whether the learner's extrapolations match the inventor's.
- Include a stage where the whole class adjudicates a deviant answer. The inventor knows what she meant, the group sides with her, and students should come away having felt both why the community's verdict is satisfying and why it looks like just more behaviour.

Deliver: the exact handout wording, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps each moment onto a part of the argument — including the normativity question, what makes the deviant answer wrong rather than merely different, and what the dispositional, communal, and realist replies each cost. Also say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be frank about one feature of the real paradox the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in my own words.
