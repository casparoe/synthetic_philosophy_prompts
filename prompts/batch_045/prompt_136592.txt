I'm in a final-year philosophy of mind seminar, and next week I have to give a fifteen-minute response on Dennett's three stances — physical, design, intentional — and the account of real patterns that comes with them: the criterion, roughly as we were given it, that a pattern is real when there is a description of the data that is more efficient than the brute specification of the data, with the savings growing as the data grows rather than being some fixed one-off. A friend in the seminar claimed that on this picture anything the intentional stance can predict successfully will automatically count as a real pattern, so the compressibility test can never actually bite against belief-desire ascriptions. I think she's wrong, and I want a case that separates the two, but every example I sketch either smuggles in irrationality or degenerates into a lookup table with beliefs stapled to it. Could you build one to the following specifications?

The system and the behavior: one system, one stretch of behavior, described in a single paragraph. It should be realistic — something that exists now or could plausibly exist within a few years, no thought-experiment physics, no demons or perfect predictors. Please don't reach for the usual stock examples: no chatbot or large language model, no chess engine, no thermostat, no Clever Hans. They've been done to death in the seminar and each of them lets me cheat somewhere.

What the stances must deliver:

- The intentional stance must be genuinely successful, not vacuously so: its predictions are made in advance, over a long run of novel cases, and beat chance; every ascribed belief has to earn its keep by covering at least two distinct behaviors, so this isn't one-belief-per-action; and two independent interpreters working from the same evidence should converge on the same ascriptions, so the result isn't just interpretive slack.
- Even so, the intentional description should save only a constant factor over the raw behavioral data, so that under the growth version of the criterion the intentional pattern fails to be real. Show that bookkeeping explicitly.
- The design stance must have a compact description you can state in one sentence, must compress without bound, and must have strictly more predictive reach than the intentional stance: name a non-gerrymandered class of cases, on the system's home territory or a natural extension of it, where the design stance predicts correctly and the intentional stance predicts wrongly.
- The intentional stance's failure must not be traceable to irrationality, weakness of will, self-deception, false belief, ignorance, a performance slip, or a malfunction. Those are the escape hatches I want nailed shut.
- One sentence on why the physical stance isn't a live competitor here; don't actually run a simulation.

The point of the case is to test whether predictive success is sufficient for realness, which fixes what counts as cheating: a scenario that stipulates the verdicts, or that wins by making the design description unavailable in principle, hasn't done any work. After the scenario, walk through what each stance actually predicts about one specified next move and why, do the two pieces of compression bookkeeping, and then tell me which single feature of the case is load-bearing and what happens to the case if you remove it — I want to know which way it collapses, whether into a case where the intentional pattern is real after all or into one where neither pattern is. Finish with the strongest reply a Dennettian could give (not a straw man) and an honest verdict on whether this is a counterexample or something his view should welcome. If I've misstated the criterion, correct it in a line and build the case for the corrected version. Please write in American English and aim for something I can present in under eight hundred words.
