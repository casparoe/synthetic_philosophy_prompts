I have been trying, without success so far, to state karma as a principle of moral desert — not as folklore, not as a causal mechanism, and not as a metaphor for "what goes around comes around" — and I would like to know whether the verdicts I actually have on a set of cases add up to anything. I will list them as I have them, including the pairs I cannot reconcile, then give the principle I have been edging towards, and ask you to test it and repair it rather than replace it with something else.

1. Hartley, a financial adviser, quietly defrauds dozens of clients of their retirement savings; he is never caught and never repents. Thirty years later he loses his own savings to an unrelated investment fraud. Verdict: apt. And I suspect part of why it feels apt is the resemblance between the wrong and the loss.

2. The same man, but instead he develops a painful degenerative illness that costs him roughly as much as the fraud would have. Verdict: also apt, but noticeably less vividly so. The missing resemblance costs something I cannot defend.

3. Two drivers, both scrolling at the wheel. One kills a child who steps out; the other drives home never knowing how close he came. The first is consumed by guilt for decades — verdict: apt. The second feels nothing at all — verdict: his untroubledness is itself wrong, he owes something, even though nothing happened. Meanwhile I also believe that desert requires control, and whether the child stepped out was not up to either of them.

4. A seller who was not careless sells a house; an undetectable gas leak kills the buyer's child. Verdict: no debt at all. The will, not the harm, seems to generate it.

5. A woman resolves to poison a colleague, buys the poison, and is prevented by a chance event; no one is harmed. Verdict: a real debt, nearly as large as if she had succeeded.

6. A man cruel and violent at twenty, genuinely patient and generous by forty after a long struggle, suffers a serious illness at fifty. Verdict: I balk. I want to say the debt has been discharged by the change in him, not merely served. This is where I think karmic desert comes apart from legal retributive desert, which would still hold that he owes a sentence.

7. A child born with a painful congenital condition into grinding poverty. Verdict: I refuse the word deserved. But I also want a theory in which suffering is never merely brute, so I keep being tempted into inherited debt from a life she cannot remember. This is the case I go back and forth on.

8. Hartley's daughter loses her home in the collapse following his exposure, knowing nothing of the fraud. Verdict: not apt in the slightest — a different person. I note that this is structurally what case 7 is, if the bearer of an inherited debt is a new person with no memory of the wrong.

9. A village buried by a landslide. Verdict: not apt as collective karma; I would rather say each person's suffering is their own debt and the shared event is coincidence, though I find that strained.

10. A woman spends years caring for a disabled brother at great cost to herself and later receives an unexpected inheritance. Verdict: I do not feel desert here as I do in the suffering cases — the good outcome reads as a windfall rather than a wage. I suspect the principle is not symmetric in sign.

My ordinal judgements, for what they are worth: 4 is zero; 5 is close to 3's first verdict; 1 is somewhat more than 2; 6 is very much less than 1.

The principle I have been tending towards runs roughly as follows. A person incurs a karmic debt at the moment of a culpable act, or of a culpable resolution whether or not it is carried out. Its magnitude is fixed by the quality of will displayed, not by the harm that ensues. The debt is discharged either by suffering of comparable magnitude or by a transformation of the will that dissolves it, and not merely by its being served. It is not transferable between persons. It survives the death of the agent by attaching to a causal rather than a psychological continuation of the stream. And the suffering that discharges it is fitting rather than merely useful, though I cannot say what its fitness consists in.

What I want from you: test that principle clause by clause against the cases, tell me which clause is carrying which verdict and which verdict no clause can deliver, and repair it where it breaks. In particular, say what it commits me to on cases I have not listed — an animal trapped in a wildfire; a suffering incurred now for a wrong the same person will commit in ten years' time, and whether that is really different from inherited debt; whether the theory requires rebirth for closure at all, given that if Hartley dies serene and unrepentant at ninety his debt must be either cancelled by death, stored, or a refutation of the theory; whether the child in case 7 can ever discharge a debt she can neither repent for nor know about; and what, if anything, fixes the exchange rate between a quality of will and a quantity of suffering, given that I have only ordinal verdicts and cannot see how anyone would get cardinal ones. Finally, if there is a simpler principle that fits more of this by overturning one of my verdicts — I suspect either the resemblance verdict in case 2 or the refusal in case 7 — say which it is, what it costs me elsewhere, and whether the price is worth paying.
