I teach a graduate seminar on the moral status of nonhuman animals, and I am writing a paper on a narrow question: whether brain size — neuron number, or whatever proxy we would settle on — is morally relevant at all, and if so whether it is relevant intrinsically or only as evidence of something else. I have an argument I cannot get out of. I cannot tell whether it is valid with a premise I should simply reject, or whether something subtler breaks partway through, so tell me which it is.

(1) Moral status comes in degrees, and the degree a being has is fixed by facts about the life it can live: roughly, how much conscious experience it can have, how rich that experience is, and how much of it can go badly. Nothing about species membership, lineage, or ancestry matters in itself.

(2) Those psychological facts are realized by neural machinery, and the same facts can be realized by different quantities of machinery. Take a being whose cortex is replaced, cell by cell, by a smaller and more efficient circuit that preserves every discriminative, affective, and behavioural capacity: nothing about how its life goes for it changes.

(3) So brain size is not intrinsically morally relevant. Its relevance, if any, is purely evidential: it is a proxy for the facts in (1), and a proxy contributes nothing once we have the facts themselves.

(4) Now take the cases that actually matter: other species, infants, patients with disorders of consciousness, anencephalic newborns. In these cases we do not have the facts in (1). What we have is neuroanatomy and behaviour.

(5) A proxy has exactly as much evidential force as its correlation with the target property. Here the correlation cannot be calibrated independently, since anatomy and behaviour are the only things we could calibrate it against, and its tightness varies in ways we cannot determine across lineages.

(6) So we have no determinate route from what we can observe to the facts that fix moral status, and our confident ranking — human being above chimpanzee above carp above oyster, and the parallel ranking among patients — is unsupported.

(7) That ranking is not unsupported. So (3) is false, and the sheer quantity of neural machinery must be doing intrinsic work: more machinery, more experience, more moral status.

(8) But then: functionally idle neural tissue — added cells doing no psychological work — would raise its bearer's moral status.

(9) And suppose, as I stipulate rather than report, that healthy adult humans vary in neuron number by something like a third with no matching psychological difference. Then some human beings matter more than others, which I take to be a reductio.

(10) And quantity is a magnitude, so twice the machinery is twice as much of whatever matters. Nothing in the view settles whether that means one subject whose experience is twice as intense or two subjects each experiencing once, yet that choice decides whether the suffering of a very large number of small-brained animals outweighs the suffering of one large-brained one — and I can find no principled way to make it.

The claims I most want to keep are (1) and the first sentence of (7). I would give up (6) without regret if I could, but I cannot see how.

Which step do you reject? Explain why the step looked sound, and what rejecting it commits me to — I have to defend the choice in a footnote, so I need the cost of each alternative, not just the one you prefer. Mark your confidence in each claim you make, and flag where you are guessing. And if the dilemma as I have set it up is itself confused — if there is some third way for brain size to matter that is neither intrinsic nor merely evidential, or if my (5) is bearing weight it cannot bear — say that plainly instead of politely choosing a horn. No literature review; I want the diagnosis.
