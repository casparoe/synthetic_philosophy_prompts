I work on the infrastructure team for a product that ships a fleet of around ten million small language-model agents, all fine-tuned from a single shared base checkpoint but with different random seeds and disjoint fine-tuning data splits. In a spare-time reading group — two engineers and a product manager, no philosophers among us — we've been chewing on evidential cooperation in large worlds: the claim that in a big enough population an agent's own decision is strong evidence about what similarly-constructed agents will do, which is supposed to give even a purely self-interested agent a reason to cooperate in a one-shot dilemma. I want to know how much of that survives translation into my setting, and I want a number rather than a survey. So: suppose an agent in the fleet is matched against one randomly chosen other agent in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, knows the entire setup (shared checkpoint, independent seeds, disjoint data, no communication, no reputation, no iteration, and no causal influence of its own act on any other agent's act), and is otherwise self-interested. Give me your credence that such an agent is rationally required — not merely permitted — to cooperate, and decompose it.

At minimum I'd like terms for: (i) whether some correlation-sensitive decision rule rather than a causal one is correct in a case where the agent's causal efficacy is exactly zero; (ii) whether the correlation the argument needs actually obtains given shared pretraining but independent fine-tuning, i.e. how much of the behavioural variance across the fleet my own act can really be evidence about, given that the fine-tuning runs differ; (iii) how much of your number is instead a bet that "rationally required" has a determinate answer here at all, as opposed to being a choice of framework; (iv) whether the reason really scales with population size in the way the large-worlds argument needs when the agent doesn't know whether the fleet is ten thousand or ten billion; and (v) what happens if, before choosing, each agent is shown the choices of a random sample of a thousand other agents, which I genuinely can't tell either screens off the act–outcome correlation or just relocates it. Give a number and one line of justification per term, then show how you combine them. Since you have no way to look anything up, please don't spend the answer on papers, citations, or reading recommendations — I want your own reasoning about the case as stated.

Two more things. First, a wrinkle I made up and can't evaluate: suppose that with some small probability ε two agents in the fleet draw the same seed and end up weight-identical, and consider the limit where ε goes to zero while N·ε stays fixed at about ten, so there are expected to be a handful of exact duplicates somewhere in the fleet. Is the reason to cooperate in that limit the same kind of reason as in the ordinary case, or a different one? One sentence, and say whether it's load-bearing for your number. Second, for calibration: I'm at roughly 0.55 and my colleague who wrote the fine-tuning pipeline is at roughly 0.15; she says her number is mostly "the correlation isn't real — different seeds and disjoint data give near-independent behaviour," while I think mine is mostly "if there is any correlation at all, ten million is an enormous multiplier." Tell me which of those two diagnoses you think is right, where our disagreement actually lives, and — most importantly — which single sub-question your own final credence is most sensitive to, and what argument or measurement would move it most.
