The question, as it went out, was this. The course notes state the theorem as follows: take any voting rule that always selects a single alternative (no ties, no lotteries), that has at least three alternatives available, that is defined for every possible profile of strict rankings of those alternatives, and that is onto — every alternative is the winner at some profile. **Question 3 (25 marks).** A city budget committee has five members who must adopt one of five funding levels for a programme: $0m, $2m, $4m, $6m, $8m. Each member submits a strict ranking of the five levels. (iii) *(7 marks)* Now drop the single-peakedness assumption: suppose members may have arbitrary strict preferences over the five levels, while the rule itself is unchanged. Here is the answer I need graded, transcribed as submitted:

> **(a)** The theorem from the notes says: given at least three voters, at least three alternatives, a rule that always returns a single winner (no ties, no randomization), defined on the unrestricted domain of all strict rankings, and onto — every alternative wins somewhere — strategy-proofness forces the rule to be dictatorial, i.e. some one fixed voter's favourite alternative always wins. Contrapositively, any such rule that is not dictatorial can be manipulated: at some profile some voter does strictly better by reporting something other than her true ranking. Note that this is an existence claim, not a claim about how often manipulation is available or about how much a would-be manipulator needs to know about the other ballots.
>
> **(b)** The conditions matter. If the rule's range contains only two alternatives, majority between them with a fixed default is strategy-proof and non-dictatorial, which is why "at least three alternatives" and "onto" are both needed. The escape that matters for this question, though, is the domain: committee members' preferences are single-peaked on the funding ladder, so the profiles that can actually arise are a small subset of all rankings, the theorem is simply silent about the rule as the committee uses it, and we have to check strategy-proofness by hand.
>
> Fix the other four members' reports together with the two charter anchors: six numbers. Let L be the third-smallest and U the fourth-smallest of those six. My own report r makes a seventh number, and the median of the seven is L if r ≤ L, is r itself if L ≤ r ≤ U, and is U if r ≥ U — that is, my report clamped into the interval [L, U]. So if my ideal point lies inside [L, U], reporting it truthfully hands me exactly my ideal point and I am done. If it lies below L, then no report of mine can bring the outcome below L, so truthful reporting is optimal; and similarly if it lies above U. The rule is therefore strategy-proof.
>
> Notice that this argument nowhere uses single-peakedness. Whatever my preferences over the five levels happen to be, the effect of my report is to pull the outcome toward the level I report and never past it, so submitting anything other than my favourite level can only move the outcome further away from the level I like best. The rule would remain strategy-proof even if members had entirely arbitrary preferences. In fact we do not even need the domain restriction to escape the theorem: because the charter anchors sit at the two ends of the ladder, the extreme levels $0m and $8m can never be the median of the seven numbers, so the rule is not onto either, and the hypotheses of Gibbard–Satterthwaite fail twice over.
>
> **(c)** So the committee is safe, and not by luck. Gibbard–Satterthwaite is a genuine impossibility result, but it has loopholes, and the charter median rule falls through two of them at once: it is used on a restricted domain, and it cannot select the extremes of the ladder. Any member who tried to game it could only push the outcome toward whatever level she claims to want.
