I sit on the allocations committee of a small global-health funder that describes itself as "effective altruism adjacent," and over the last two grant cycles I've become convinced of the measurability critique: that the movement's prioritization is systematically distorted toward interventions whose effects can be stated as a cost per unit of outcome, and away from interventions and causes whose effects are diffuse, long-lagged, contested, or systemic — and that this is a distortion rather than merely a reflection of what we happen to know.

In arguing with colleagues I've come to rely on two distinctions I don't think are standard, and I'd like them stress-tested alongside the critique itself. Call the first the conversion problem: measurement is not a passive readout of a cause's tractability but an intervention on its epistemic status, because the same resources that estimate an intervention's effect also make that intervention more estimable. So the pool of supposedly unmeasurable causes is not stable under investigation, and any test of the critique that works by going out and measuring an allegedly unmeasurable cause destroys the very thing it was meant to test. My colleagues reply with what I'll call the evidential symmetry argument: for an expected-value maximizer, ignorance about an intervention should widen the interval around its value, not lower the mean, so unmeasurability as such cannot demote a cause — and hence a genuine measurability bias has to be explained by something other than mere uncertainty.

Tell me what would count against the critique. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward and sorted by what each would show: that the actual allocations are defensible, that the critique misdescribes the mechanism, or that the critique has been qualified into unfalsifiability. Please cover the following.

- What defenders of cost-effectiveness-driven prioritization would themselves concede as counting against the critique, as opposed to what critics allege about them, and where such a concession should stop.
- Whether the conversion problem protects the critique or indicts it, and whether the evidential symmetry argument really shifts the burden the way my colleagues assume. In particular, assess whether risk aversion over moral uncertainty, the option value of learning, selection effects in who is drawn to quantifiable work, institutional incentives to produce reportable numbers, or asymmetric priors can each independently do the work the critique needs — and say which of them the critique is actually committed to.
- Which observable facts would discriminate. What allocation pattern, what kind of internal deliberation, what episode in the movement's funding history would be decisive rather than merely suggestive? I would personally weight a revealed-preference test most heavily: a documented case in which a quantifiable intervention was funded over an unquantifiable one that the same decision-makers privately judged better.
- An honest verdict on how exposed the critique is, and on whether, if it does turn out to be insulated, that is a defect here — or whether insulation is simply the normal condition of a claim that functions as a standing suspicion about a research and funding culture rather than as a testable hypothesis about it.
