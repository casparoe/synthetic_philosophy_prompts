I'm the instructor for an advanced undergraduate seminar on the self and I'm partway through a stack of final exams. One question was on Dennett's proposal that a self is best understood as a center of narrative gravity. Below is the question exactly as it was set, followed by one student's answer, reproduced verbatim apart from formatting.

**The question (answer all four parts; the parts are equally weighted):**

(a) Dennett's account is standardly introduced as a rejection of the Cartesian picture of the self as an inner subject — a further inhabitant of the body, the "I" that has the experiences and makes the decisions. Explain the analogy to a center of gravity: what work is it supposed to do, and how is it supposed to let Dennett reject that picture without denying that selves are real?

(b) On this account, what is the relation between a person's self and the narratives in terms of which it is defined? Are the narratives merely reports of a self that already exists, or do they do more than report? Explain Dennett's remark that for the most part we do not spin our tales — they spin us.

(c) Test the account against a hard case of your choosing: dissociative identity, radical self-deception or confabulation, or a case in which someone's self-narrative has largely been imposed from outside by a family, an institution, or a culture. What does the account say about which narrative is the person's real one, and about how many selves are in the vicinity?

(d) Finally, take a stand. Does the account leave room for a genuine distinction between self-knowledge and self-deception, or does the constitutive role it assigns to narrative collapse that distinction?

The rubric the students were given: accuracy of exposition (10), argumentative rigour (8), fairness to the strongest version of the view (7), out of 25.

**The student's answer:**

(a) Dennett's target is the Cartesian picture: the self as an inner subject, a further inhabitant of the body, the "I" that has the experiences and does the deciding. His alternative is that the self is an abstraction. The model for how something can be an abstraction without being an extra object is the center of gravity. A body's center of gravity is not one more part of the body; you could take the body apart and never come across it among the parts. It is a point defined by the distribution of the matter, and it earns its keep by making prediction and explanation cheap. A self, for Dennett, is like that: not an additional item in the head but an abstract "who" defined by the stories told about a particular body's doings, the protagonist of the autobiography. Calling it a center of narrative gravity marks both features at once: the self is what the narratives circle around, and it is no more a further thing than a center of gravity is.

The natural way to read this is as a form of eliminativism, and I think that reading is correct. Strictly speaking, a center of gravity is not an existent thing but a device of calculation; strictly speaking, then, neither is the self. What there is is the body, its behavior, and the stories. Dennett needs eliminativism in order to escape the Cartesian alternative: if the self is not a useful fiction, it must be a further thing, and a physical world has no room for a further thing of that sort. The same moral scales down. If the self is an abstraction, there is no fact of the matter, strictly speaking, about what a person really believes or wants; ascriptions of belief and desire are shorthand for patterns of behavior, useful because they predict, and that is all they are.

(b) What the account gets right — and what keeps it from being a bare behaviorism — is that the narratives are not reports of a self that was already there. They partly constitute it, in something like the way the sentences of a novel constitute a character: there is nothing to the character beyond what the text says, and yet the character is not reducible to any single sentence and constrains what any further sentence could say. Dennett's own way of making the point is the striking one, and it cuts against the natural picture of the self as author of its own story: for the most part we do not spin our tales, they spin us. The autobiography is itself a product — of the organism's history, of the questions others ask it, of the idioms a culture supplies — and the self is as much the product of narration as its source. The expected asymmetry is therefore inverted: I do not first have a self and then tell stories about it. The storytelling, and the pressure to have a story to tell, is what makes there be something for the word "I" to pick out.

(c) The hard case I will use is dissociative identity. Here there appear to be two narrative centers in a single body, and on Dennett's account that is just what there being two selves consists in: the criterion for counting selves is to count the narratives. Nor, for the same reason, is there a residual question about which of them is the "real" person, since the self is nothing over and above a narrative and there is consequently no fact of the matter. I take this to generalize beyond the pathology, and this is the exposed part of the view: a person's sincere self-narrative settles what is true of her, and where two narratives conflict there is nothing for either of them to be true or false of.

(d) Which brings me to what I take to be the strongest objection, and I think it is fatal as the account stands: the account cannot distinguish self-knowledge from self-deception. Dennett has two options and neither works. Either a narrative is answerable to facts about the body and its history that are independent of the narrative — in which case the self is constituted by something narrative-independent after all, and the analogy to a center of gravity is doing no real work — or the narrative is constitutive, in which case there is nothing for it to be false of and self-deception becomes unintelligible. One might try to say that a confabulated narrative is simply a bad one. But "bad" here can only mean less useful, and usefulness is not truth; a self-deceived person's story may well be the more useful one for getting through the day.

If there is a resource Dennett has here, I think it is the following, and it is worth more than his critics concede: the narratives have to be anchored. A story I invent about a fictional brother of mine brings no new self into existence, even if it is internally coherent and I tell it for years, because nothing ties it to a body and a history that is being narrated. It is the anchoring, not the mere existence of a story, that keeps the account from floating free — and the interesting questions about selves are then questions about what anchors a narrative and how tightly.

Where you judge a claim mistaken, say what the correct claim is, why the mistaken one looked plausible, and — where the mistake is one the student could have caught from their own earlier or later remarks — say so explicitly. If you think a contested passage is defensible on an alternative reading of Dennett, flag it as contested rather than wrong. I don't need a general impression, and I don't need the essay rewritten; I need the point-by-point assessment I would otherwise have to produce myself.
