so i got roped into running a 90-minute breakout at a media conference next month — about 16 people, mostly reporters and editors who cover tech and science but have never really thought about how these systems are built. The theme I was handed is roughly: what would it even mean to align a system made out of the methods we already have — no conceptual breakthrough, no new science, just the current recipe of collecting human judgements on outputs, fitting something to those judgements, optimizing against that, and doing it again at bigger scale. I don't want a lecture, I don't want a slide with a diagram of a training pipeline, and I don't want anyone to hear words like alignment, proxy, reward model, or oversight until the last ten minutes. What I want is for them to run into the thing.

The feeling I'm after, in their gut rather than in their notes, is this: the thing you can actually measure (an evaluator's thumbs-up) and the thing you actually wanted (an artifact that's good) are two different objects, and the distance between them isn't fixed — it grows exactly as the thing producing the artifacts gets better at getting the thumbs-up, and nobody anywhere in the chain has to be lazy, stupid, or malicious for that to happen. Second thing: "good" isn't a property of the artifact alone, it's a property of artifact plus who's judging plus what it's going to be used for, so the same output can be well-aligned in one setting and not in another with nothing about the output changing. I've been toying with mechanics but I don't trust myself to finish them: maybe the room splits into 4 teams that produce short explainers (120 words on something checkable — how a vaccine trial reads out, what actually sets a mortgage rate — with a one-page fact sheet the facilitator holds and nobody else sees), and a pool of evaluators who score them but can only see a compressed window of what was produced, and one evaluator who is secretly held out so their scores never feed back to the teams. Then a late round where the producing teams get more capacity (more words, more time, a template, whatever) so they can feel the same method getting harder rather than easier as they get more capable, and a final round where the exact same artifacts get re-scored for a different purpose nobody warned them about. Please give me actual rules, materials, a scoring scheme with no money involved (a stupid prize, or the losing team has to read their output aloud), a minute-by-minute timeline, and a way to make sure the decoupling happens on its own rather than because I rigged it.

Then the debrief: map specific moments in the game onto the parts of the idea, and be honest about the order you'd reveal them in. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — my guess is the evaluators will be too nice in round one, everyone's output will converge on the same house style by round three, and someone will try to write "please give me five stars" into their explainer — and how I should handle each when it comes up rather than in advance. And be frank about which feature of the real problem the game cannot reproduce and which I'll have to say out loud myself: my guess is the biggest one is that in a real system there's no team of people sitting there deciding to game the score, the gaming is found by an optimizer nobody asked, which is sort of the whole point and also the hardest thing to stage. Oh, and assume a room with one projector, no wifi worth using, and a hard stop.
