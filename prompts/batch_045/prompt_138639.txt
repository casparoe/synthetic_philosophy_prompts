I'm in the final year of a PhD in historical musicology, working on the reception of post-Kantian aesthetics in nineteenth-century instrumental criticism, and I've been asked to set and mark one of the three essay questions on the summative paper for our MA option 'Music and Philosophy, 1800–1900'. The convenor moderates whatever I write and a second marker has to be able to apply the standard, so both the question and the criteria need to be explicit. The paper is closed-book: ninety minutes, one essay of about 1,500 words, marked out of 20.

My worry is that the obvious Schopenhauer question — why he ranks music above the other arts — has stopped discriminating. Every candidate arrives able to write that music, unlike the other arts, is not a copy of the Platonic Ideas but a copy of the will itself, and then to reproduce the remark that the world could be called embodied music just as well as embodied will. I want a question that makes that answer the *beginning* of the difficulty rather than the end of it. Four pressure points, in particular, should be built into it.

First, the anomaly. Art is officially the cognition of the Ideas — the will's adequate objectification at some grade — by a subject temporarily released from the service of the will. Music presents no Idea. So either music is not an art by his own definition but something of adjacent value, or the definition is incomplete, or the notion of an immediate copy is doing more work than it can. A candidate should notice that calling the copy immediate does not by itself help: a copy, immediate or not, is still a representation, and representation belongs to the side of the principle of sufficient reason, not to the will in itself.

Second, the symmetry problem. If music copies the will and the world is the will's objectification, then music and world are coordinate copies of one original. Yet in the second volume's supplement on the metaphysics of music he maps the musical texture onto grades of the world's structure — the deep bass onto the crudest, most inorganic grades, the upper voices and the singing principal melody onto the higher grades culminating in human self-consciousness. That mapping looks like a description of music as a copy of the world's structure after all, which is to say of the Ideas. Candidates should have to say whether he is entitled to the mapping as an allegory, or whether it quietly reinstates what the official doctrine denies — and, relatedly, what an ungraded copy of a will that is not in time could even be, given that melody is essentially temporal.

Third, the problem of the effect, which I care about most. Aesthetic experience is supposed to still the will: the subject becomes a will-less, painless knower. But music is said to move us precisely by expressing the movements of the will — striving, frustration, satisfaction — and to do so while giving us the satisfaction without the suffering that ordinarily buys it. That looks less like quieting the will than like gratifying it at no cost, which on his own ethical psychology would be an inducement to go on willing rather than a step towards resignation. The question should force candidates to say whether music is therefore an internal embarrassment for the pessimism, or whether the merely 'as if' character of musical emotion — the fact that it puts us in relation to no object of desire — is enough to make it innocent.

Fourth, detachability and specificity. Does the metaphysics yield any verdict about kinds of music — imitative or pictorial music, music subordinated to words, the priority of melody over mere harmonic or rhythmic effect — or are those detachable matters of taste that happen to be bound to it? And, more sharply: if music copies a will that is one and undifferentiated, and expresses only joy, sorrow and striving in general rather than the particular joy of this event, what account can he give of the individuality of a work, and of why we attend to the structure of this symphony rather than that one?

Two constraints. Because candidates have no text in front of them, the question must refer to the doctrines in paraphrase and must not reward verbatim recall; and for the same reason I'd rather the model answer paraphrased than quoted, since I would have to check any quotation against the translation we set and I have no wish to. The question should also be answerable from Schopenhauer alone — the seminar has read Hanslick and the early Nietzsche, but I don't want name-dropping to earn marks; if you think a move drawn from either should earn credit, say so explicitly in the scheme. It should take a definite position rather than invite a survey.

Please give me, in this order and at roughly these lengths:

**The question** (about 200 words): the item exactly as it would appear on the paper, including any stipulation or definition candidates are given.

**Why the question is set this way** (about 150 words): which of the pressure points it puts in the candidate's way, and what the recitation answer misses.

**Model answer** (about 1,000 words): continuous prose, as a first-class candidate would write it, committed to a position and stating the strongest version of the side it rejects.

**Marking scheme** (about 500 words, totalling 20 marks): itemised; for each item, the move required, the marks, and one line on how candidates characteristically fail it.

**Borderline and failing answers** (about 150 words): what separates a first from a high upper second, and what pulls an otherwise competent answer down into the third-class range.
