My flatmate did a philosophy BA and now writes HR training material, and she left a folder of her old lecture handouts on the kitchen table; I read the one on the philosophy of emotion while waiting for the kettle and I cant tell whats solid in it. It says, roughly: "There are three theories. The feeling theory, from William James, says an emotion just is your awareness of your own body — fear is nothing over and above your heart racing and your muscles tightening. The judgment theory, from the Stoics by way of modern cognitivists like Nussbaum and Solomon, says an emotion is a judgment that something is good or dangerous, so emotions are the kind of thing that can be true or false and that you can be argued out of. The perception theory, which is where most people working on this are now, says emotions are perceptions of value — seeing that the cliff is dangerous, but with the affect built into the seeing — and because perceptions aren't beliefs, they don't have to line up with what you believe, which is also why nobody can really blame you for how you feel. The judgment theory is dead anyway, killed off by recalcitrant emotion: people who know perfectly well that flying is safe are still scared on the plane, and if fear were a judgment they'd simply stop being afraid once they judged otherwise. Appraisal theory is just the psychology version of the judgment theory — same claim, more experiments." I have two hunches about this and no confidence in either: that the perception view is only the judgment view with the vocabulary swapped in order to dodge the objections, and that the handout is too quick to bury the judgment theory, since a cognitivist can always say the nervous flyer has some half-formed or habitual judgment she doesn't fully own.

Two cases keep bugging me, and I want to know whether they do the work the handout thinks recalcitrant emotion does. First, the cold rescuer: an ER nurse who sees a patient crashing, correctly and sincerely judges in that moment that this is dire and that everything must go right in the next sixty seconds, does everything right, and afterwards says "I knew how bad it was" — but reports no feeling at all, no racing heart, no dread, no affective colour, and isn't dissociating or burnt out; she just isn't moved. Second, the leftover anger: I'm furious at a friend, then I see a recording that settles it — it plainly wasn't her — I fully believe that and say so out loud, and the anger still sits there for about ten minutes and then drains away with nothing else changing. The first case is the one nobody ever mentions and it worries me more, because the mismatch runs the other way: judgment present, emotion absent. I also keep coming back to watching a film scene for the second time: you know exactly what's coming, your assessment of what's happening hasn't changed, and yet you feel far less.

So: is the handout fair to the views it's summarizing? Please don't give me a bibliography or things to look up — I'm not going to chase references from here, I want it reasoned out. What I care about is whether the "perception" language is doing any real work or is just a relabeling, whether the recalcitrant-emotion argument actually refutes the judgment theory or is just a hard problem with standard replies, where appraisal theory really sits relative to the judgment theory, whether my two cases (and the repeat-viewing one) push anywhere the recalcitrant case doesn't, and whether there's anything in the handout that's straightforwardly right and worth keeping.
