For the past two years I have kept a running log of my supervisor's verdicts on standing-to-blame cases. She is a responsibility theorist, I am writing a chapter on hypocrisy and standing, and I wanted a record of her judgments that is independent of whatever I end up arguing, so at the end of supervision I have been putting one or two cases to her and writing down the answer before I have time to argue with it. The verdicts I have are these: a husband who is having an affair blames his brother for having one — no standing, she said he was in no position; a woman who shoplifted a chocolate bar at fourteen blames a colleague for systematic expense-account fraud — standing, the earlier act is irrelevantly small; a manager who privately opposed a plan to withhold emissions data but signed off on it anyway blames her CEO for lying to regulators — no standing, or at any rate her blame is defective in whatever way matters here; a colleague who watched a junior researcher being publicly humiliated and said nothing afterwards blames the person who did the humiliating — standing, and she insisted that failing to intervene is not the same as being implicated; a man who once revealed a confidence under a credible threat blames a colleague for gossiping — standing, because his own act was excused and so there is nothing for him to be inconsistent about; a man who cheated on a grant application a decade ago, confessed, was sanctioned, and has been scrupulous since blames a colleague for minor CV inflation — she wavered and then said standing, on the grounds that what matters is whether he is now willing to be judged by the norm he is invoking; and a woman who blames her sister's employer for harassing her sister, when the sister has declined to complain — standing, since you can speak for someone who is not in a position to speak for herself.

A real case has now come up in my department and I want to know what she will say before I put it to her. Miriam, a postdoc here, has no car, has not flown for leisure in nine years, and has moved her savings out of unscreened index funds; in a department meeting she publicly blamed, first, a senior colleague, Professor Ames, for flying to a conference in Sydney on a trip Ames did not choose — she was assigned to represent the department on a hiring panel — and, second, the department's governing body as a body, for refusing to cap conference travel and for the university's investment policy. Two features make this unlike anything on my list. The first is that Miriam's own pension sits in the university's default scheme, which holds the very index funds she has divested from personally; switching to the screened option is a one-page form she has not filled in, and privately she admits she has not got round to it. She is therefore a beneficiary of, and in some weak causal sense a contributor to, the investment policy she is condemning, while having never done anything remotely like it herself and plainly not being committed to exempting herself from the norm she invokes. The second is that part of the blame is addressed to a collective for a diffuse harm in which there is no individual victim who could complain and no individual member who controls the outcome, and part of it to a person who was not in a position to have acted otherwise — so the case probes whatever she thinks vicarious standing to blame requires when there is no one on whose behalf she is speaking, and whatever she thinks blame of a collective inherits from blame of a member. My own suspicion is that her verdicts are best explained by one forward-looking rule about the blamer's relation to the norm rather than by either a simple no-better-than condition or a complicity condition, and I have been trying to keep apart three things I think she may be running together: standing, meaning the authority to address the blame at all; the aptness of what the blame says; and whether the blaming is itself a fault in the blamer, since it seems to me that a hypocrite's blame can be apt and authorized and still be something he ought not to do.

Predict her verdict on each half of the case, the individual half and the collective half, and tell me which single rule best explains the whole run of verdicts, including why she said no in the emissions case and yes in the bystander case and why she wavered in the reformed-cheater case. Commit to one rule and one verdict, with your reasons: do not set out the candidate rules with their respective predictions and leave me to choose between them, though do tell me which runner-up your prediction is most at risk of being confused with, and whether you think the beneficiary-but-not-agent feature or the collective-address feature is doing the real work in your answer. Then give me the one further case — the smallest variation on Miriam's, I would guess — that would most cleanly separate the rule you have picked from that runner-up, say what her answer to it would have to be for you to conclude you picked the wrong rule, and tell me how confident you are, in rough terms, that she answers as you predict. If you think my three-way distinction between standing, aptness and fault-in-the-blaming does no work on this material, or that the individual/collective split matters less than I am making of it, say that plainly rather than accommodating me.
