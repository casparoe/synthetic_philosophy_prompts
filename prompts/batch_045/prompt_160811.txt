I have been reading about consciousness on rationalist blogs and forums for well over a decade, and the family of views I keep circling back to — despite knowing all the standard objections — is the higher-order one: the idea that what makes a mental state conscious is that the system is, in the right way, aware of itself as being in that state. I am not asking for a literature review, a reading list, or citations. I want you to reason from the cases, and I would rather you steelman the higher-order approach than tell me it is unfalsifiable.

What I want is a principle. Below are my gut verdicts. They are not the verdicts of any position I have read; they are just what I find myself wanting to say, and I suspect some of them are mutually inconsistent. Tell me whether one account of the required higher-order state returns all of them, and if it cannot, which ones I should retract.

1. A normal awake adult human: conscious, obviously. A human in deep dreamless sleep, or during an absence seizure: not conscious of anything.
2. A newborn infant: yes, there is something it is like to be it, though the phenomenology is thin and unstructured. This makes me think the higher-order state cannot require possessing the concept of a mental state.
3. A dog: yes. A crow: yes. A honeybee: probably yes, but my confidence drops sharply, and I do not know what I would say about a nematode.
4. A blindsight subject forced to guess about stimuli in the scotoma and guessing correctly well above chance: no visual experience of those stimuli. I feel this is a point in favour of higher-order theories, not against them.
5. A subject shown a grid of letters for a fraction of a second who can report only three or four of them: my honest lean is that there was something it was like for the unreported letters too, briefly, before the trace decayed. I know this sits badly with 4, and I want you to say which of the two has to go.
6. A boiler with a sensor that registers the boiler's internal state and drives a display and a shutoff valve, where the displayed value reliably covaries with that state: not conscious. Whatever a higher-order theory requires, it is not merely reliable second-order information.
7. *The Orphan Monitor.* Take a system with rich first-order representational states and add a module that forms accurate, noninferential higher-order states about them — states with roughly the content *I am now in a state of this kind* — but arrange things so that the module's outputs are written to a log and influence nothing else: no action, no report, no learning, no further processing. My lean is that this changes nothing about whether the system is conscious; causal isolation makes the addition inert rather than constitutive.
8. *The Bootstrapper.* A large neural system whose second-order labels are randomly initialised and then shaped purely by reinforcement until they reliably track its first-order states; the labels have no ground beyond that covariation, and the system does nothing with them except emit them as tokens. My lean is no. But this troubles me, because reliable tracking is close to the only thing a higher-order theory officially demands, so I suspect I am smuggling in a further condition and not admitting it to myself.
9. *The Mirror Buffer.* A higher-order state that does not take the first-order state itself as its object, but takes as its object a faithful structural duplicate of it that has been copied into a buffer one step downstream. Everything else is as in the ordinary human case. I have almost no intuition here; my weak lean is that the mediation does not matter, since the copy is type-identical in the respects that count.
10. Phantom limb pain in a patient with no limb and thus, presumably, no first-order pain state in the relevant receptor population: yes, there is something unpleasant it is like for them. This seems to me to pressure the requirement that the higher-order state have a veridical first-order target.
11. Lucid versus non-lucid dreaming: both conscious; lucidity does not add phenomenality, only reflexive self-knowledge. So whatever the higher-order state buys, it is not a matter of degree in the way I would have naively expected.
12. The case I oscillate on. A human — or an emulation of one — whose first-order sensory and affective processing is left intact while the higher-order or global-broadcast machinery is selectively disabled, so that the system still discriminates, still forms beliefs and desires, and still acts appropriately, but nothing in it is aware of any of it. Half the time I say: then nothing is conscious for it, and that is exactly what a higher-order theory predicts. The other half I say: you cannot extinguish what it is like to be a subject by disabling a broadcast mechanism, so if the first-order states are intact the phenomenology is intact and my whole framing is wrong.

Note that 6 and 7 pull in opposite directions: the boiler's second-order state is causally efficacious and does not make it conscious, while the orphan monitor's is causally inert and does not make its system conscious either. So causal role cannot be the discriminator by itself, and I do not know what the real one is.

Here is what I want, concretely. State a principle precise enough that I could apply it to a new architecture without asking you again: what kind of higher-order state is required, what relation it must bear to the first-order state, what causal or functional role it must play, what it must be embedded in for there to be a subject at all, and whether the resulting property comes in degrees. For each clause, name the case above that forces it, and say what the principle predicts for two cases I have not listed. Then tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot recover — I would rather lose a verdict than keep a principle that only works because it is vague. If you think my list runs together two different things, such as state consciousness and creature consciousness, or phenomenality and reportability, say so and redistribute the verdicts across the distinction rather than smoothing over it. And if the honest answer is that no higher-order account fits and that the pattern actually pushes toward a first-order or self-representational view, say that too, and tell me which verdicts I would have to give up to get there.
