I'm in a small philosophy-of-perception reading group — I come from psycholinguistics, so I'm mostly here for the empirical side of things — and we've now spent three sessions on whether perception is cognitively penetrable and we're going in circles. The positions themselves aren't mysterious: one side says that belief, desire, fear, expertise and expectation can change what you literally see, and they point to the old New Look work on value and need, to effects of expertise on ambiguous figures, to threatening stimuli popping out for frightened subjects, and to the shape of predictive-coding models where high-level priors are simply part of the computation. The other side says every one of those effects lands on attention, on memory, on response bias, or on judgement rather than on seeing; that the early visual system is informationally encapsulated in Fodor's sense; that knowing the two lines in the Müller-Lyer figure are equal doesn't stop them from looking unequal; and that the big review Firestone and Scholl did a few years back showed that nearly every published demonstration confounds a change in what's seen with a change in how the task was set up. Both sides then cite the same dozen studies and come to opposite conclusions about them, and nobody has moved an inch in three weeks.

The distinction we invented to try to get some traction was that maybe there are three theses getting run together: (1) a cognitive state changes the *processing* — it alters the computation the perceptual system runs; (2) it changes the *content* — the experience represents something it wouldn't otherwise have represented; (3) it changes the *phenomenology* — things literally look different to the subject. These could in principle come apart: attention might change how something looks without changing what it represents, and a prior might shift content in a Bayesian model while the system stays architecturally encapsulated. My hypothesis is that the debate stalls because different people are defending different items on that list and there is no agreed test for any of them. Someone else in the group has a rival hypothesis: that this is a proxy war about whether perceptual experience is rationally assessable and can serve as a source of non-inferential justification, which is why people who'd otherwise not care care so much. And hovering over both is the worry I associate with Stokes, that penetration is either trivial — any top-down influence on what gets selected counts — or vanishingly hard to establish — only literal changes in content count — so that the interesting middle just isn't there.

So my actual question: which of those diagnoses is right, if any, and what kind of disagreement is this — verbal, methodological (a fight about what would count as evidence), or genuinely substantive about architecture and content? And separately: could an experiment settle it? The design I sketched to strip out the usual confounds is to put two stimuli on screen simultaneously and have subjects just pick which one looks more X — no memory, no yes/no, no judgement about a single distal object — while we manipulate a high-level belief about one of them (what category it belongs to, whether it's valuable, whether it's threatening), and we measure the forced-choice pattern alongside a signal-detection split of bias versus sensitivity, plus a reaction-time version of the same task, ideally crossmodally so that an effect confined to vision tells us something about where the influence enters. Is there any outcome of that experiment that both camps would accept as settling the question, or does the disagreement sit deeper than any experiment reaches? And if it does sit deeper, what would actually count as progress here — a distinction, a formal result, an operational criterion, a change of question, or nothing?
