The question she set:

An s-risk ("suffering risk") is a risk of an outcome involving astronomical amounts of suffering. The standard argument for prioritising s-risk reduction is multiplicative: expected disvalue = probability × number of sufferers × suffering per sufferer; the middle factor may be astronomically large, so even at tiny probabilities the expected disvalue swamps anything we could do near-term.

We discussed three replies:

(a) Cluelessness: our uncertainty about knock-on effects over long timescales is deep enough that we can't confidently assign even a sign to the net effect of an intervention, let alone a magnitude — so the expectation is indeterminately uncertain rather than large.
(b) Measure scepticism: the number of future minds isn't a well-defined quantity to multiply, since it depends on arbitrary choices about the boundaries of a mind, which substrates count, and which merely possible minds get counted — so the calculation is ill-formed.
(c) Proportional deflation (she made this one up for the pset, I don't think it's in the literature): grant the magnitude. But the probability that an intervention like ours actually makes a difference to a process falls off roughly like 1/N, where N is the number of minds involved: the bigger the stakes, the likelier it is that the levers are held by whoever runs that future, and the more agents there are competing for them. So the product doesn't blow up, and the astronomical part of the argument deflates itself.

Task: in about 300 words, reconstruct the proportional-deflation reply in your own words and assess whether it defeats the conclusion that s-risk reduction dominates other priorities. If you think it only partly works, say what it leaves untouched.

One student's answer:

The proportional-deflation reply concedes the magnitude and attacks the probability. The idea is that influence doesn't scale with stakes: if some future process involves N minds, then either the decisions that shape it are made by agents very distant from us in time and causal reach, or they are contestable — and the number of agents trying to contest them grows with N. Either way our share of the causal influence is about 1/N, so the expected disvalue we can avert is N × (1/N) = 1. In other words, an s-risk threatening 10^30 sufferers is, expected-value-wise, just equivalent to one person suffering with certainty, which is why the astronomical numbers can't do the work the s-risk people want them to do. That also dissolves the Pascal-style fanaticism worry, since nothing here depends on multiplying by a huge number.

Notice that this is really the same point the measure sceptics are making: the huge numbers in these expected-value calculations aren't quantities we can actually get a grip on, so building priorities out of them is a mistake. Both replies are ways of saying we should be humble about very large numbers in cost-effectiveness reasoning, rather than claims about what the future is actually like.

So the reply does defeat the dominance claim: if it holds, s-risk reduction has at most ordinary-scale expected value and cannot beat near-term work, where our influence isn't diluted. What it doesn't show is that s-risk reduction has positive expected value at all — for that you'd still want to check tractability and neglectedness, and whether there's any concrete intervention on offer.
