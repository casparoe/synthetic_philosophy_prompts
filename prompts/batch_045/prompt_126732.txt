Here's the question I set (I used a made-up app so they couldn't just recite the urn example from lecture):

Two new ramen shops, Kuro and Shiro, open on the same block. They're identical in every respect except that one of them is better, and before anyone has any information each is equally likely to be the better one. A review app shows each new customer, in order, the full sequence of the previous customers' choices. Every customer gets a private signal about which shop is better that is correct 60% of the time, signals are independent given which shop is better, and every customer picks so as to maximise expected payoff given everything available to them. (a) Explain why a "cascade" can start here, in what sense the resulting herding is rational, and why the crowd can nonetheless end up locked onto the wrong shop. Be explicit about what later customers' choices do and do not convey to the people behind them. (b) The app's designer proposes replacing the sequence with two big running tallies — "Kuro 327, Shiro 41" — on the grounds that the size of the crowd tells you how much evidence is standing behind it. Does a bigger, more lopsided tally mean the crowd is more likely to be right? (c) Now suppose the app also displays how long each customer sat on the choice screen before tapping, and everyone — customers included — knows that a long pause tends to mean the customer's private signal pointed at the other shop (it's a noisy but informative indicator, not a perfect one). Call this the latency channel. Does the cascade still block information? And what happens to the herding itself?

And here's the answer, verbatim (student #14):

(a) A cascade starts as soon as two people in a row pick the same shop. At that point the public information contained in those two picks outweighs any single person's own 60%-accurate signal, so the Bayesian thing to do from then on is to ignore your own signal and go with the crowd. That's the sense in which the herding is rational: nobody is being a sheep, everybody is responding to evidence, and you also can't really be blamed for a bad bowl of ramen when everyone else walked into the same place. The catch is that once you're in that regime your own choice no longer reveals anything about your signal, so the private information of everyone after the first couple of customers never gets into the public pool at all. The information is blocked, not aggregated. That's why the whole crowd can pile onto the wrong shop, and why the chance of that happening is set by the luck of the first few people rather than improving as the crowd grows. It's also fragile and path-dependent in the way we discussed: reorder the first three customers and the crowd can lock onto the opposite shop. Finally, this is purely observational learning — it has nothing to do with the shops getting better as more people go to them, and nothing to do with anyone being rewarded or punished for conforming.

(b) The designer is basically right. Each additional person who shows up in the tally is another independent draw from the same signal distribution, so the law of large numbers kicks in: the larger and more lopsided the tally, the more independent evidence has accumulated behind the leading shop, and by the time you're a few hundred customers in, the probability that the crowd has settled on the wrong shop is negligible. The count is just a compressed summary of how much private information the crowd has managed to pool.

(c) The latency channel unblocks the flow. If a long pause means your signal pointed the other way, then a customer's tap plus their hesitation time hands their private signal back to everybody behind them, so information keeps arriving and the public belief keeps updating instead of freezing. The blockage that makes cascades pathological is therefore gone, and with everyone's signals effectively public the group ends up pooling them the way a jury pools what its members know. And since a cascade just is a situation where people have stopped being able to learn from each other, the cascade itself dissolves: once customers can see that the people ahead of them were mostly hesitating, they'll start trusting their own signals again and the crowd will spread out across the two shops instead of piling onto one.
