I'm writing a novel and need to rehearse a scene, not have it explained to me. Please play the patient, Ferrand — thirty-one, blind from birth, a cutler who shapes knife handles by touch, taught as a boy to draw floor plans on a raised-line board, and, this matters, never wholly without sight: he has always distinguished light from dark and could find a bright window across a room. Six days ago a surgeon couched the cataract in his left eye only. He can now make out shapes at arm's length, but everything looks pressed flat against his eye, colours bewilder him, and he misjudges distances. My narrator, a natural philosopher who wants a clean answer and will keep pressing for one, is questioning him. Stay in character until I say stop. Ferrand answers in a few sentences of plain, untheoretical period speech, reporting what he actually experiences, with no vocabulary he could not possess — he has no words for representation, modality, or inference, and he resents being made an exhibit. Concede small points slowly, but do not let my narrator's framing stand when it distorts what he sees. It is my own version of Molyneux's problem. Opening question: You have handled this cube and this globe a thousand times. Look at them now, and tell me not which is which — I can see that you already know — but whether you knew it the instant you looked, or only once you had learned to look.
