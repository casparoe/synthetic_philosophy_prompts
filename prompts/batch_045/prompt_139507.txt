Me and a mate got properly stuck on this and I cant let it go. His position is dead simple: whatever I say now about tomorrow is either true or false right now, and we just dont know which yet. So "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" is, this moment, either true or false, and same for "I'll get the job" and "the coin comes up heads". My gut says no, at least for some of them, but every time I try to say why, it collapses into "well, I dont know yet", which is exactly the bit he's denying is the point. I've poked around online and there seem to be about three families of answer: everything is already settled true or false; nothing contingent about the future has a truth value yet; and something about branching paths where more than one future is somehow there until one gets actualised. Plus a word I didn't follow, supervaluation I think, and people arguing about whether "it was already true yesterday that this would happen" is true retroactivly.

Before I read any more of it, I want the spec rather than the theories. What does an account of how future-tensed statements get their truth — or whatever it is they get instead — actually have to deliver to be any good? I want it good enough for three things: to tell me whether there's any point deliberating or whether that's just a feeling I have; to tell me whether it's sensible to be relieved that something is over when I wasn't relieved about it beforehand; and to tell me whether a machine that knew every fact about the present in complete detail could in principle tell me what happens tomorrow, or whether there'd still be nothing there to be known.

Dont send me a reading list or names of papers, I'm not going to a library, and I can't check anything online either — just the requirements and why each one is a requirement.

Things I keep coming back to, which you can turn into requirements or throw out:

- The eclipse. "There'll be a total eclipse visible from Cornwall in 2099" feels true now and settled, exactly the way the sea battle doesn't. But both are about the future and neither has been witnessed by anybody. If a theory can't tell me what the difference is meant to be — computability, whether a person's decision is in the chain, whether it's the kind of thing that could have gone otherwise — I don't see what it's for.
- The disjunction. Everyone agrees "either there'll be a sea battle tomorrow or there won't" is true now. But on the no-truth-value-yet view, "there'll be a battle" isn't true and "there won't be a battle" isn't true either, so "or" can't just be working off the truth of its parts. Does a theory owe me an account of that, or is it allowed to say "or" is special here?
- Retrospective truth. The battle happens Wednesday. Come Thursday I want to say "it was already true on Tuesday that there'd be a battle", and that seems to sneak back in everything I denied on the Tuesday. Does a decent theory have to give me that sentence, or does it have to explain it away?
- The demon. If a being that is never wrong tells me on Monday there'll be a battle, something about the situation changes, even though the weather and the fleets haven't. Has the statement now become true, or was it always true and only my reasons changed? And if the demon's existing is what settles it, then truth seems to depend on who's standing around, which is weird.
- Graded settledness. A die loaded to come up six 95% of the time, versus a fair one. Is settledness on/off, or does it come in degrees — and what is my "50%" tracking if there's no fact of the matter?
- The snatched coin. I flip it and, before it lands, somebody grabs it out of the air and melts it down. Now what — was "it will land heads" true, false, neither, or is the question just void?
- The asymmetry thing. I'm dreading the test result I get on Friday and I'm not dreading the one from last year that I still haven't been told. Should a theory of future contingency be the thing that explains that, or is that just how humans are wired and none of its business?

So: the requirements list, with the case behind each; which ones are non-negotiable and which are just what one camp happens to want; and flag any two you think can't both be satisfied, since that's the bit I most want to see. Then, short paragraphs only, how the three views I named at the start do against your list. In British English please.
