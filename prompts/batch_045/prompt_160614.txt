I direct a graduate reading group on the justification of induction, and I have been trying to state precisely what the no-free-lunch results do and do not establish about Hume's problem. I have ended up with the argument below. I believe each step as I have stated it, and I reject the conclusion. My suspicion is that the argument proves considerably more than the problem of induction, and that this excess is the clue to where I have gone wrong. I would like a diagnosis rather than a survey of the literature: identify the step that fails, explain why the step looked safe, and price the alternatives for me.

The setting: a finite input space, a binary output space, a fixed training sample of labeled points, and accuracy scored on the points not in the sample. Let F be the class of all total functions from inputs to outputs, equipped with the uniform distribution.

(1) No-free-lunch equality. For any two learning algorithms A and B, the average over the uniform distribution on F of A's off-sample accuracy equals the corresponding average for B.

(2) Mirror construction. Given a deterministic method M, define M* as the method that, on the same training sample, outputs at each unsampled point the complement of what M outputs there. For every target function f and every unsampled point, exactly one of M and M* is correct, so the two methods' accuracies on f sum to one. Averaging over F and applying (1), each has uniform-average accuracy one-half.

(3) Every inductive method is an algorithm in this sense, a map from data to predictions: enumerative induction, curve-fitting, Bayesian conditioning on any prior you like, inference ordered by simplicity.

(4) Therefore, averaged over the uniform prior, enumerative induction and its counter-inductive twin — predict that the observed pattern inverts — are exactly equally reliable, and both sit exactly at chance. The same holds for every method and its own mirror.

(5) Hume's question is a demand for a reason to prefer induction to counter-induction, and it is part of that demand that the reason not presuppose the answer, that it not help itself to the assumption that we inhabit a world of the inductive-friendly sort. But (4) tells us that the only world-independent comparison available, the average over all possible worlds, is a tie. So the problem of induction is not unsolved; it is unsolvable in the only form in which Hume's challenge can be met. Call that demand prior-free vindication.

Two extensions make me doubt the argument, neither of which I intended.

(6a) Breadth. Nothing in (1) through (4) turns on the future resembling the past. Every rule taking any body of experience E to any contingent hypothesis H has a mirror, and the two tie under the uniform prior. So no experience is ever evidence for anything beyond itself, and perception, memory, and testimony go down together with induction. I did not set out to defend global skepticism about learning from experience.

(6b) Height. The reasoning applies as well to policies for choosing methods. Any policy pi for selecting a method is itself a map from experience to a decision, and pi ties with the policy that adopts the mirror of whatever pi selects. Hence the policy of accepting (5) and ceasing to rely on induction ties with the policy of doing the opposite of whatever that policy recommends. The argument cannot so much as recommend suspended judgment; it consumes itself.

(7) A further worry about content. The partition of methods into those that preserve the observed pattern and those that invert it is not invariant under a relabeling of the output space: swap the labels on the inputs beyond some cutoff and one and the same algorithm counts as inductive under the original coding and counter-inductive under the new one. If the distinction Hume cared about is coding-relative in this way, I do not see what (5) is even a claim about.

I want to keep (1) through (4): (2) reads as arithmetic and (1) as a symmetry fact. I also want to keep the following: some experiences are evidence for some contingent hypotheses, and some methods do better than chance in the world I actually occupy. So which step do I give up, and what does each candidate cost? Is the culprit the inference from (4) to (5); the requirement that justification be prior-free; the extrapolation to (6a) and (6b); the use of the uniform prior in (1) itself; or the assumption in (7) that the induction/counter-induction contrast is well defined prior to a choice of coding? And if I place the weight on the uniform prior, do I then owe an account of what makes my actual prior superior to a uniform one, given that the uniform prior is the standard representation of ignorance?
