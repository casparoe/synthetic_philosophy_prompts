I write distributed-systems code and have no philosophy training, but a friend who works on formal verification mentioned Molinism to me and I have been turning it over in the only vocabulary I have: possible worlds as a state space, counterfactuals of freedom as the transition relation, creation as a single irrevocable commit whose downstream states are never recomputed, prophecy as a read of a value that has not yet been written. Before I go further I want the specification rather than a survey of who held what. Please write the desiderata that any account of how exhaustive divine foreknowledge, creaturely freedom of the kind that requires being able to do otherwise, and real providential control over history can coexist would have to satisfy, with the purpose of the theory fixed as follows: it should let a finite agent coherently treat history as both genuinely open and genuinely planned, so that petitionary prayer, detailed prophecy, and full moral responsibility for one's own acts can all be true together. For each requirement, give the motivating case or consideration, and say whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp. I especially want you to separate the demands that really follow from the problem from the ones that are just the prejudices of a particular tradition, such as that God must be outside time, that God must not be the author of sin, or that grace must be resistible.

Four things I keep tripping over, and I do not know whether to promote them to the list or discard them as artifacts of thinking like a programmer. First, the granularity of circumstances: a claim about what someone would freely do in circumstances C seems to change its truth value depending on how finely C is specified, since describing C down to mood and neural detail makes the outcome look necessitated while describing it coarsely makes it look unsettled, so a theory apparently owes me either a principled resolution for C or an explanation of why none is needed. Second, whether the counterfactual is about this very individual or about any qualitative duplicate of them: on the first reading, God's control is really control over which individuals exist and where they are placed, and no one's will is ever guided; on the second, it is unclear how a generalization about a type grounds knowledge of an actual person. Third, whether the menu of feasible worlds can be settled without counterfactuals about God's own future free responses, such as whether to answer a prayer or intervene, which look self-referential if they are included and leave the menu underdetermined if they are not. Fourth, whether foreknowledge has to be closed under the free acts of reporting, transmitting, and interpreting, since foreknowledge of a free event does not yet make detailed prophecy reliable once the prophet's utterance and the hearer's reading of it are themselves undetermined links in the chain. Tell me which of these are genuine desiderata, which are artifacts of my programming-shaped intuitions, which existing requirement on your list already covers them, and add the desiderata I have missed that you would rank above mine.

The part I care most about is whether the list is even satisfiable: identify any subset of requirements that cannot jointly be met, since if the specification is internally inconsistent that is itself the answer to my question, and say which single requirement does the most work in excluding familiar candidates. Only after that, and briefly, score the pictures I have run into: middle knowledge itself, the view on which God is timeless and takes in the whole timeline at once, the openness view on which the future is partly unsettled even for God, and any compatibilist dissolution you think belongs on the list. A few sentences or a small table each is plenty, with the criteria doing the real work and the scorecard treated as an appendix. Please argue from the structure of the problem rather than from what any particular theologian or school maintained; I have no way to check citations or bibliographies and I do not want a reading list.
