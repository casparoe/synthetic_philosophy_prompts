Next tuesday I'm presenting to the research committee at a big global-health funder: mostly health economists who do everything in DALYs with a positive discount rate, the director who is a treasury-style skeptic about anything he can't put a number on, and one sympathetic philosopher who will not actually defend me. My thesis is that human extinction is bad in a way that isn't just the deaths and isn't just the discounted future QALYs either — that a real chunk of what makes anything we're doing now worth doing is hosted by people who will come after us, so extinction also retroactively shrinks the point of everything happening before it. I'm leaning on Scheffler's afterlife conjecture but trying to push it toward something a funding committee could act on.

I'm opening with two cases and I want the Q&A built around them. Case one, the "one-day world": nobody dies early, nobody suffers, but some neurological change means no one can form or care about any project reaching more than about a day ahead. Case two, the archive world: humans die out, but a complete record of everything we made and cared about survives and is genuinely studied and valued by non-human successors for millennia. My line is that case one is nearly as bad as extinction and case two is much less bad than it — which I read as showing that what matters is continued forward-looking valuing, not the species as such and not welfare.

Can you write me the prep sheet? The eight or so objections I'll actually get from this audience rather than from philosophers, ordered by how much damage they'd do, phrased the way a health economist or a treasury skeptic would put them — discounting, double counting with future lives, the Parfit-style point about people who'd never otherwise exist, "if nobody knows it's coming then nothing is lost", "unmeasurable so ignore it" — each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is softest. And include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
