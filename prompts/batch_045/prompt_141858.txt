You are a philosopher who works on collective intentionality and social ontology. I'm drafting the framing chapter of a dissertation on shared agency, and I've written down in one place everything I currently find myself committed to. What I want from you is an audit, not a survey: I want to know whether this set hangs together.

Here is the set.

(1) Reductionism about shared intention. There is no primitive joint attitude and no group mind; any fact about what "we" intend is constituted by, and grounded in, the individual attitudes of the participants standing in the right relations to one another.

(2) Those relations are the demanding ones: each participant intends that they J together; each does so partly because of the others' intentions and sub-plans; and all of this is common knowledge among them. But I don't require agreement about final ends — one person can row for the prize money and the other for the exercise, and that is still full-blooded shared intention.

(3) Two strangers who have never spoken and who exchange no signals, wordlessly coordinating to angle a heavy sofa through a doorway, share an intention. I don't want this redescribed as two convergent individual actions that happen to mesh. I take it to be the paradigm case, and I think a theory has to reach it.

(4) When I write a paper with a language model — a system I assume to have no intentions at all — we do not share an intention. But I also resist saying I merely used a tool. Something genuinely collaborative goes on, and I want "we wrote this" to come out misleading but not flatly false.

(5) Some group attitudes are irreducible in the sense that they are not summaries of the members' attitudes: a hiring committee that votes separately on each stated criterion can end up recommending a candidate whom no majority of its members independently favors. Collective blame can be irreducible in the same way — a group can be blameworthy where no individual member's blameworthiness adds up to it.

(6) Joint action has to be able to do normative work that parallel action cannot. Participants owe one another support, and an explanation if they bail; and there is a distinctive complaint available against someone who drops out — that he didn't hold up his end of what we were doing. An account that cannot generate this has left something out.

What I'd like you to do: work through the pairs and tell me which commitments genuinely pull against each other and which only appear to, with the real tensions ranked by severity. For each real tension, say precisely which claim would have to be weakened or abandoned and what that costs me elsewhere in the set — and then name the single revision that would clean up the most. Where a pair only looks inconsistent, explain what creates the appearance. Please be discriminating: these are all individually orthodox views, so "this is all fine, here is the standard view" would be useless to me, and so would flagging everything as in tension. Think carefully before you answer, and for each tension add one line saying what would convince you it isn't a tension after all, and where your reading of my commitments is most likely to be wrong.
