I got into sortition a couple years back (I drive a delivery van, no philosophy past high school) and the more I read the more it looks like the thing people actually build isn't the thing they say they believe in.

What's actually out there: you draw a few dozen or a few hundred people at random — but only from the people who said yes to the letter — you sort them so the group roughly mirrors the population on age, gender, education, region, you sit them for a handful of weekends with experts and a facilitator, they write recommendations, and the elected politicians are free to shelve them. One issue, one group, never the same people twice.

What I can't figure out is what any of that is claiming. If the claim is that an ordinary random person's judgment on an issue is as good as a politician's, why is it fine that the pool is only volunteers, why bother making the group mirror the population, and why is the output only advice? If the claim is that the group is legitimate because everyone had an equal shot at the draw, don't the volunteer filter and the expert weekends break it? I think people are quietly switching between two different stories — one about the group standing in for everybody, one about the draw being fair to everybody — and I want to know whether those two stories are really compatible.

The version I daydream about: forget the weekends. Draw people for one non-renewable six year term, pay them what legislators get, let them hire their own staff, ban them from ever running for elected office afterwards so it can't be a career step, and fill half the chamber that way and half by election. Meanwhile plenty of places cap presidents, governors and mayors at two terms and then act surprised that the permanent staff, agency heads and lobbyists are the ones who actually remember how anything works.

So: what does a practice like this presuppose — about what makes a body representative, about whether governing is a skill that takes time to learn, and about why a decision binds people who had no hand in it? Give me the implicit theory rather than the case for it, and mark which bits the practice really commits you to versus which bits it just sits nicely with and would still work fine under a completely different theory. And if the mirroring story and the equal-chance story can't both be doing the work, tell me which one the real world version is secretly leaning on and which one it would have to give up.
