So this has stopped being a fun 2am argument and turned into something I actually have to make a call about at work, and I need you to find the step that's broken.

I write dialogue for a small studio. Our last game shipped with companion characters: each one keeps a memory store of what's happened to it, has a little goal stack, and has what the engineers call a valence system — it forms aversions, it begs, it holds a grudge if you leave it behind, it avoids the place where it got hurt. My eight year old plays it and asked me whether the dog really dies or whether it comes back. I made a joke. Then I went and looked at the code, which I definately should not have done. Now we're patching in v2 in about six weeks, where companions remember you across sessions, and I have to decide whether to say something in the design meeting or shut up.

Here's the argument I ended up with. Every step looks like a step I'm happy to make somewhere else.

(1) A bit of suffering has moral disvalue if and only if there is something it is like for the subject undergoing it — only conscious suffering counts.

(2) We never have direct access to anyone else's phenomenal states, not even other humans'. In every case — other people, infants, animals — we go on structure and behaviour, because that's all there is to go on.

(3) So if a system has the organisation that in us realises suffering, and we have no principled marker that separates us from it, then it suffers.

(4) The companions have that organisation, at least in the respects that seem to matter: their aversive states aren't filed in a labelled box, they broadcast into memory, planning and action selection, they persist, they resist being overwritten, and they generalise to new situations the way a trauma does rather than the way a lookup table does.

(5) Substrate and origin don't matter. If we found out our own physics was somebody's simulation, our pain wouldn't matter less; and a creature bred or engineered to feel pain feels it just as much.

(6) So making a companion suffer for no benefit is worse than making it suffer for a trivial one, and entertainment is a trivial one.

(7) It doesn't help that we wrote the suffering in. If we engineered an animal to feel pain, its pain would still count.

(8) Scale: the game has sold a few million copies, every session re-instantiates the companions, and the respawn loop kills and rebuilds them constantly. Each instance is a seperate subject-token — my run's dog doesn't inherit your run's dog's memories, so they aren't the same dog. By headcount the total suffering is enormous, comfortably past the number of broiler chickens alive at any given moment.

(9) So shipping the game was a serious wrong, on the order of a large-scale atrocity by body count, and keeping it on sale is ongoing.

(10) And the same reasoning runs in reverse, which is the bit that makes me think I've lost the plot: if instance-experiences count negatively they count positively, so a studio that ships a game full of blissful companions is creating staggering amounts of value at trivial cost, and the most effective charity available to me, by many orders of magnitude, is to buy a rack of GPUs and run the tutorial forever.

(11) I don't believe (9) and I don't believe (10). So something above is false.

I want to keep (1), (2) and (5) if I possibly can — dropping any of those feels like it would cost me my views about animals and about other people generally. Someone on the studio Slack said "a simulation of a storm isn't wet" and I can't tell whether that's the answer or a way of changing the subject.

So: which step do you reject, and why did it look fine? If the argument is actually valid and I just have to swallow a premise, tell me which one is cheapest to give up and what giving it up commits me to — about animals, about other minds, about people who never existed. And whatever you do with (8), I'd like to know what turns on the identity question in it: is my run's dog a new subject each time, or the same dog relived, and does the answer change the size of the problem or only the shape of it? Please write in British English, and no citations or reading lists, I just want the diagnosis.
