I took a philosophy of language course last spring so I know the basic moves (Putnam's Twin Earth setup, the idea that content ain't just in the head, the arthritis-style case where someone applies the word to an inflammation in their thigh and we still say the word means what the experts mean), but I might well be mangling something, and I'm stuck in a place where I can't get to the library or look anything up, so please don't just point me at readings — argue with me directly. Here's the draft, it's short and rough:

**Reference, Drift, and the Orphaned Model**

*1. The baseline.* On the standard externalist picture, what my word "water" means is partly fixed by what's around me. My molecule-for-molecule duplicate on a planet where the lakes are filled with XYZ rather than H2O means something different by "water" even though we are internally identical and behaviorally identical. The deference cases generalize the point: a speaker with a partly mistaken grasp of a term can still mean what their community's experts mean. So: environment plus social chain, not just what's in the head.

*2. Slow Swap.* Now imagine a planet whose surface liquid is H2O at the start, but which changes very gradually over two centuries — isotopic composition shifts first, then a chemically similar solvent mixes in — until at the end the lakes hold a different stuff, call it "aquiform," which matches water on every macro-property anyone ever checks (boiling point within a degree, same look, taste, role in cooking). No generation notices a change. Each generation learns the word from the previous one, and every speaker's dispositions are just like ours. Did the content of "water" change? If yes, when — a bit at a time, or all at once at some point? Is there even a fact of the matter about which generation is the last one that referred to H2O?

*3. The continuity constraint, and a boomerang.* Call this the Continuity Constraint: the content of a speaker's term cannot change without some change that is in principle available to that speaker — some difference in evidence, dispositions, or experience. Gradualism plus externalism violates it. But here's the awkward part: externalists have always treated "you can be wrong about, or ignorant of, what you mean" as a *selling point*, the thing the Twin Earth case is supposed to make vivid. The Continuity Constraint quietly reimposes a kind of first-personal authority on content, which is the very thing externalism was advertised as undermining. Call this the authority boomerang: the feature that motivates the view is the feature that makes the drift case uncomfortable.

*4. Three ways out.* (a) *Drift externalism:* content tracks the current environment, so it changes continuously, and mid-drift speakers are in an indeterminate or partially referring state. (b) *Hysteresis:* content is fixed by the causal-historical chain back to the original grounding, preserved by use, so at the end of the two centuries the word still means H2O even though there is no H2O around; reference lags the world until some breakdown event forces a re-grounding. (c) *Phase transition:* content is environment-fixed but subject to threshold effects, so near-ties are indeterminate and content "snaps" at some vague point. Note that (b) has a consequence worth dwelling on: two communities identical in internal states, dispositions, and *current* environment can still mean different things, differing only in history — so (b) externalizes content not just to the world but to *time*.

*5. The orphaned model.* Consider a large language model trained only on human text, with "water" in its vocabulary, that has never had any sensory contact with water. Compare it to a robot that learned the word while manipulating cups of the stuff. Two candidate principles pull apart here. *Chains:* usage causally downstream of human utterances can transmit reference, just as a child who learns "otter" only from a picture book still refers to otters. *Contact:* reference requires the subject's own causal commerce with the referent. I want to say Chains is right, but notice the price: the corpus is now playing the role the planet played. Text becomes an environment. Call the resulting view corpus externalism.

*6. Generational retraining.* Take the orphaned model, let models generate text, retrain new models on that generated text, and repeat for fifty generations, so that by the end the token "water" is mostly downstream of machine-generated text and is being applied to clear liquids generally. Does it still refer to water? On corpus externalism, either the chain preserves reference or the current corpus re-fixed it. But here's the disanalogy with Slow Swap that I think is interesting: on Slow Swap the speakers remain in perceptual contact with *something*, whereas in the retraining case the only "environment" is linguistic all the way down. If content is fixed by corpus, and corpus is made of language, then externalism has started to look like a use theory in disguise — and maybe the right conclusion is that "does the token still refer?" is really "is there still a practice of correcting uses against a world?", which is a question about the survival of a practice rather than about content.
