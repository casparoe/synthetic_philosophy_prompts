I teach a second-year course that's half decision theory and half philosophy of probability — about 22 students, one 80-minute slot, tables that seat four, and no budget for real money, so stakes have to be chocolate or a couple of points of course credit. Next week I've got a session on the two-envelope set-up (two envelopes, one contains twice what the other contains, you get one, you may look inside, you may then swap) and I really don't want to do what I did last year, which was write the switching argument on the board and then spend forty minutes explaining the flaw. It didn't land: they could recite the objection afterwards and the argument still *felt* right to them, and I think that's because nothing in the session ever cost them anything.

So: design me a game they actually play. Envelopes in pairs, each pair of students gets one envelope each, they can peek at their own, then decide whether to trade, and I want them to run into the problem in their own choices and in the class's aggregated results rather than in anything I say — I'd rather the words "paradox" and "expected value" don't come out of my mouth until the debrief. Two things I want built in. First, the version where both members of a pair peek and both run the argument: each concludes that trading is better for them, and they trade with each other, which shouldn't be possible, and I want that collision staged so the whole room sees it happen at the same time. Second, one of my students last year proposed a randomized rule — pick some threshold from a very spread-out distribution before looking, then switch if and only if what you see is below it — and claimed this beats both always-switching and always-keeping. I couldn't tell whether he was right or whether he'd just moved the problem somewhere else, and I'd like the activity to have room for that rule, or for you to tell me it doesn't belong. Relatedly: I once tried generating the amounts from something extremely spread out and the running averages just kept lurching around and never settled down, which I *think* is a real feature of the maths and not me mucking up the spreadsheet, but I'm not certain, so if that matters for how I should build the envelopes, say so.

What I need concretely: the rules, what goes in the envelopes and how I should generate the numbers, the payoff scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline for 80 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto specific bits of the reasoning — including which premise of the switching argument each moment is supposed to put pressure on. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong: I expect some students to refuse to open their envelope at all (can that be useful instead of a problem?), some to treat it as a mind-reading contest against their partner, and at least one to work out that my supply of chocolate is finite and reason backwards from that. And be honest about which part of the difficulty the game can't reproduce at all and which parts I'll just have to supply myself in the debrief — in particular, I suspect the aggregate data is going to show switching is roughly break-even rather than better, and I want to know what I'm supposed to do with that when it happens.
