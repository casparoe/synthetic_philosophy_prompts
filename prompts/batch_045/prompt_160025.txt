I want a careful comparison of two influential accounts of what makes an action a joint action rather than a set of parallel individual actions.

Account A, the individualist interlocking-attitudes account associated with Michael Bratman: roughly, we jointly J when each participant intends that we J, each intends to do their own part by way of the others doing theirs, the sub-plans mesh, and this whole structure is common knowledge among the participants and sustains mutual responsiveness both in execution and in the working out of sub-plans. Everything in the analysis is individual attitudes plus relations among them; there is no irreducible we and no normative bond beyond what Bratman himself is happy to derive, for instance an obligation to do one's part and to let the others know if one is dropping out.

Account B, the joint-commitment or plural-subject account associated with Margaret Gilbert: people act together only if they have jointly committed to the activity as a body, which constitutes them as a plural subject. The commitment generates obligations to the others and a standing entitlement to rebuke nonperformance, and it is not analyzable into individual intentions plus common knowledge; the normative bond is primitive and requires some expression of joint willingness among the parties.

If the labels are unfamiliar, work from the characterizations. Nothing below depends on getting the exegesis of any particular author right, and I do not want citations or a reading list.

The comparison I want is driven by a class of cases I will call scaffolded collective action, or SCA. A coordinating mechanism — a routing service, a matching platform, a protocol — assigns each of many participants an individual instruction, computing each assignment from a model of what the other participants will do. The participants know the mechanism exists and that it is coordinating others; each is disposed to follow revised instructions when the mechanism issues them; and their parts are genuinely interdependent, so that what each does helps determine what the others are told to do. But the participants never communicate, cannot identify one another, and would not recognize one another afterward. No one holds a plan for the overall outcome, and the overall outcome is not something any participant aims at.

A concrete instance: several hundred drivers each follow turn-by-turn instructions from the same navigation service, whose objective function distributes traffic across the road network. Because each driver's route depends on the routes the service expects the others to take, they collectively keep a bridge from jamming, and they collectively dump the overflow onto a residential street near a school. There are two variants I want kept in view: the pure SCA case above, and a scaffolded-with-human-coordinator case in which the mechanism is replaced by a person who knows everyone's assignments, holds a plan for the overall outcome, and intends the participants to comply, while the participants still never encounter or identify one another.

Please address the following.

1. Verdict. State precisely how each account classifies the pure SCA case: a genuine joint action, merely parallel individual actions that happen to be causally and counterfactually interdependent, or a case for which the account has no vocabulary. For each account, identify the exact condition that does the excluding.

2. Degree or kind. Is the failure a matter of degree — too little mutual responsiveness, attenuated common knowledge, no expression of joint willingness, no one with standing to rebuke — or is there a categorical element missing? Say which, and say whether the human-coordinator variant changes the answer. In particular: does the second variant generate a joint action between the coordinator and each participant while generating none among the participants themselves, and is that asymmetry a problem for either account?

3. The ownerless goal. In SCA there is a system-level goal, represented in the mechanism's objective function, together with system-level success and failure conditions, and no participant represents or aims at it. Does either account have the resources to attribute a goal to the collective rather than to its members, and what does each account lose if it cannot?

4. The rebuke-shaped reactions. People respond to SCA failures with something very close to blame of a group: platform bans, congestion charges, angry talk about what the crowd or the drivers did. Is that evidence against both accounts, or can the appearance of collective responsibility be explained without any joint action at all, for instance by causal contribution to a harm, or by responsibility attached to an institutional role?

5. The cost of accommodation. What is the minimum each account would have to add in order to count SCA as joint action at all — one extra condition, a new primitive, or the deletion of a requirement such as mutual recognition? Which addition is more expensive, in the sense that it costs the account more of what made it attractive to begin with: reductive parsimony in the case of Account A, or the tight link between acting together and being obligated and rebukable in the case of Account B?

6. Paradigm-dependence. Are SCA cases genuinely borderline, revealing that the standard examples in this literature — two people walking together, singing a duet, painting a house together — are doing unearned work by quietly smuggling in face-to-face mutual awareness? Or are they degenerate cases whose exclusion is a feature of both accounts rather than a defect? Take a position and defend it.
