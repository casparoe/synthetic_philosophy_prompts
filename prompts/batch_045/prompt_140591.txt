Write a satirical white paper of roughly 800–900 words, in American English, presented as a leaked internal strategy document from a management consultancy called Paley Partners, addressed to prospective university and hospital clients. The firm sells a program for removing comprehension from organizations, and it should be entirely sincere about it: deadpan, in the flat register of real consulting prose (headings, bullet points, an efficiency metric, a case study), never winking, never using scare quotes, never tipping the reader that this is a joke. The name of the firm is the only overt Paley joke; do not explain it.

The intellectual target is a style of argument rather than a person. Paley Partners has taken the Darwinian and Turing-style strange inversions — the move by which competence apparently requiring a comprehending designer is shown to arise from a blind, algorithmic, comprehension-free process, so that what looked like prior understanding turns out to be a late and optional gloss — and applied it as an all-purpose management solvent: if competence can come first and comprehension is optional, then comprehension is a cost center, and no deliverable needs anyone to understand it. Exaggerate that real feature; do not invent a different one. A reader who knows the inversion literature should feel that the satire is aimed at something they actually recognize.

Give the document six short numbered sections with headings, followed by a client FAQ of four exchanges. Borrow or develop the following machinery, which should be described in the same deadpan technical voice as everything else:

1. The Comprehension Tax, measured as explanatory latency — the delay between a unit's producing an output and its producing an acceptable account of that output. The firm treats explanatory latency as a cost to be minimized, but it treats a latency of exactly zero as a red flag: a system that can say in real time why it did what it did is one where someone has quietly smuggled comprehension back into the pipeline.
2. The Turing Clause: a process is certified competent if its outputs pass an outcome audit; questions about whether any person involved understands the process are ruled out of scope on the grounds that understanding was never in the specification.
3. A distinction between Type-I and Type-II comprehension-free competence. Type-I simply cannot say what it is doing. Type-II produces a fluent account of what it is doing, calibrated to whatever the auditor expects, where that account is itself a product of the same comprehension-free selection as the behavior it describes. The firm sells Type-II as compliance-grade comprehension and charges considerably more for it.
4. A hiring and workflow procedure the firm calls selection without a selector: an evolutionary search over candidate behaviors with no job descriptions and no interviews, since a job description is a design document, and design documents are precisely what the inversion removed the need for.
5. The Paley Layer: a bolted-on explanatory module, maintained only for regulators and litigation, stipulated in the contract to have no causal role in the outputs it describes — an ornament, not an organ.
6. The self-applying case: the firm's own white paper was itself produced comprehension-free, selected rather than written, and it says so without embarrassment.

Pick one high-stakes deployment — hospital triage, air-traffic rostering, benefits eligibility, or similar — and use it for the worked case study throughout, rather than flitting between examples. The final FAQ exchange should be the reductio: asked what happens when something goes badly wrong and nobody can say why, the firm's answer should be, in substance, that the outcome audit is simply run again on a larger population. Follow the logic calmly to that point and let the reader do the recoiling; do not add a paragraph that tells them to recoil.

Constraints: no real person, living or dead, should be named, quoted, or parodied, and no real text should be quoted or attributed; the target is a pattern of argument. The narrator may never say anything like of course we understand what we are doing — internal consistency is part of the brief.

After the document, add a short note of no more than 150 words, in your own voice rather than the firm's, flagging where the parody leans on a contested reading — for instance whether the Darwinian and Turing inversions really license the same move, or whether comprehension really is dispensable in the specific place the firm dispenses with it, namely accountability for a bad outcome — and saying what would change your mind: what a defender of the inversions could point to that would convince you the piece attacks a strawman rather than the view.
