The case is Bill Clinton and Monica Lewinsky in the late 1990s. Take these as stipulated — I dont want to litigate the history:

- the televised denial that he had had sexual relations with that woman;
- the Paula Jones deposition, where he answered under a stipulated technical definition of "sexual relations" that arguably didn't cover what had actually happened, and where he also denied there was an improper relationship;
- the grand-jury exchange where he leaned hard on the difference between present and past tense;
- the aftermath: the House impeached him on perjury and obstruction counts, the Senate acquitted, and the entire public fight was conducted in the currency of literal truth and falsity — definitions, tenses, what counts as a statement.

Four things I want in the answer.

1. A taxonomy, built from the bottom up rather than borrowed. Don't use the literature's labels; give me the categories of things a speaker can do with a true sentence. My first pass: (a) assert something false; (b) assert something true while knowing the hearer will draw a false conclusion; (c) pick words that are true only under a definition or reading you know the hearer isn't using; (d) exploit a conversational convention — tense, quantity, relevance — so that what you say is true and what you convey isn't; (e) say nothing, or answer a different question. Sort the items above into these, tell me if the taxonomy is wrong or has a gap, and tell me whether (c) and (d) really are different categories — I suspect they collapse into each other and I can't tell whether that's a bug.

2. The part I actually care about. Here's an argument I keep half-believing and want taken apart. Call it the discounting point: a hearer who already knows the speaker is a politician discounts his denials in advance, so hears "I did not" as something closer to "who knows." If that's right, the false belief produced by the evasive true statement is weaker than the one a flat lie would have produced, and it reaches fewer people — the ones who had already written him off aren't misled at all. So if the wrong of misleading is inherited from the wrong of lying (same aimed-at false belief, same downstream harm), the case comes out as: the evasion here was a bit less bad than a lie would have been. But that yields the backwards result that the worse your reputation for honesty, the cheaper it is to mislead you — the habitual liar gets a discount on the moral ledger for being known as one. So: is the discounting point any good? If it fails, does it fail because the wrong of misleading was never inherited from harm, or because I've got the size or the composition of the audience wrong, or somewhere else? Be explicit about where you think the wrong is located: in what the speaker tries to get the hearer to believe, in what the hearer ends up believing, in the speaker's willingness to exploit the convention, or somewhere I haven't listed.
