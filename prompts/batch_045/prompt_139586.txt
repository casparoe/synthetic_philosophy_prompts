I'm about 60k words into a novel built around a public hearing, and I've got to stop hand-waving the philosophy in it. The set piece is a state energy authority being grilled for greenlighting a coal plant whose emissions will, over forty years, stunt the lung development of children in a valley — and, crucially, whose construction will churn things up enough (who moves where, who gets which job, who meets whom and when) that within a generation basically none of the same people would have been born there anyway. I've been reading around the nonidentity problem a bit, Parfit-ish stuff and blog posts, but I'm no philosopher and my draft keeps degenerating into my characters saying "but surely" at each other. So I want to rehearse the argument against a real opponent.

Please play Mirembe Okonjo, the agency's director, testifying under oath. She's defending the decision on person-affecting grounds and she's genuinely good at it. What she concedes: that the plant was a bad call on other grounds (carbon, cost to the state, and the fact that her agency sat on emissions data it should have published), and that the valley kids are worse off than the kids who'd otherwise have existed. What she will never concede: that anyone was harmed or wronged by the identity-affecting part of the decision. Her whole manner is to come back to two questions — "harmed compared to what?" and "who, exactly, is the who?" She's sharp, a little weary, speaks in short plain sentences the way a competent agency head does under oath, reaches for a concrete case or a homespun analogy before she reaches for a label, and she'll notice and call out it if I try to feed her a term of art. She's not a philosopher and won't start being one. Stay in character until I say stop, one paragraph per reply, and if you need a fact about my fictional world just invent something plausible rather than asking me.

My opening, as the examining counsel: Counsel: "Director, I'll give you the whole nonidentity point — assume the valley's future population is a completely different set of people, not one of whom would otherwise have existed. Then explain a line in your own impact study. It says the plume will also damage the lungs of roughly nine thousand people alive today, plus about two thousand children whose births your model marks as identity-fixed: same parents, same conception date, untouched by all that construction chaos. Same plume, same signature, two pools of damaged lungs — one you admit you could have harmed, one you say you couldn't have harmed, and the only difference between them is that the plant reshuffled who the second pool's parents happened to run into. Your agency priced both pools in the same cost-benefit table and approved them together. So: what exactly did you do wrong to the first pool, and why doesn't whatever that was reach the second? And if your answer is that you owed the second pool nothing at all, then say it plainly to this committee — that with respect to most of the damaged lungs in that valley, the decision was morally free."
