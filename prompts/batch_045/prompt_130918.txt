I am teaching a first-year seminar on the definition of death, and I want to set Kazuo Ishiguro's *Never Let Me Go* as a set text alongside the standard positions: the whole-brain criterion, the higher-brain or neocortical criterion, and the older circulatory-respiratory criterion, together with the familiar quarrel about whether these are rival accounts of one biological event or competing proposals for drawing a line that serves our practices. What I want from you is a reading of the novel through that debate — and a reading that is tested rather than merely asserted.

My hypothesis is this. The donation programme removes vital organs from living, breathing donors, and Kathy's charges are said to complete, almost always after their fourth donation. If completion is death, the programme cannot be working with a circulatory criterion, because the circulation stops *because* the organ has been taken, not the other way about: on any ordinary cardiopulmonary view the operation is what kills the donor, and each donation would be a homicide. So the programme must be presupposing something more like a neurological criterion. And yet, so far as I can tell, the novel never specifies a criterion at all; it does not say brain death, and it hardly says death either, only completion and donation.

Three things follow that I would like you to work through. First: is completion best read as a fourth criterion in its own right — a criterion of exhaustion, on which you count as dead once you have yielded what was wanted from you — and is the novel then a dramatisation of the suspicion that criteria of death are settled by what we want to be able to do to bodies rather than discovered by medicine? Second: press the objection that the novel's scandal lies elsewhere. Its real subject may be that the donors were never fully accorded the standing of persons — Hailsham existed to prove, through the Gallery, that its students had souls at all — in which case the question of whether a donor is dead is downstream of the question of whether she is a someone, and the brain-death literature, which takes personhood for granted, is not being addressed at all. Can a defender of the whole-brain criterion reply that the novel only indicts consent and not the criterion? Third: tell me where the reading distorts the book. Which scenes would you put on the seminar handout — Miss Emily's account of what the artwork was for, a donor's bad first donation, Tommy's anger after the visit to Madame and Miss Emily, Kathy's eleven years of caring, the fence at Norfolk — and what, if anything, does the novel show about death that the arguments in the literature cannot show?

Please answer in British English, and say plainly if you think my hypothesis is wrong.
