For the last eighteen months I have been one of two industry representatives on the Academic Standards Committee of a small engineering college — I am a software engineer by trade, with no philosophy training beyond one first-year elective — and we have two items coming up that I have become convinced turn on the same question, which I cannot get straight myself. The first is a grade appeal. A first-year student failed the required course on evidence and argument; her final essay argued that no belief is ever rationally grounded, because every justification either rests on another belief and so goes in a circle or stops at something it cannot defend, and she has asked us not for a higher mark but for the F to be struck from her record, on the grounds that the instructor cannot justify the standard she was graded against. The instructor marked it down as failing to engage the assignment. Our philosophy department member says the essay is a recognizable skeptical argument, badly executed but not a disciplinary matter; the registrar says it is a performance of cleverness and that striking the grade would concede that a college can make no judgement at all; the student member says the failing mark punishes her for taking the course material seriously. The second item grew directly out of the first: a working group has drafted a one-page 'epistemic baseline', a short public list of propositions — that there is an external world, that other people have minds, that the earth is vastly older than a few thousand years, that degrees awarded by this college were actually earned — which the college would declare to be the commitments inside which teaching and assessment take place, not themselves open to argument in coursework. They have asked us whether to adopt it, publish it on the website, and cite it in future appeals. I have been reading Wittgenstein's late notebooks On Certainty in an informal reading group since this began, and what I have absorbed so far is the material about hinge propositions that are not grounded in evidence and cannot be doubted without the doubt itself losing its sense, the image of a riverbed that channels the stream while slowly moving, and the claim that doubting has to come to an end somewhere. My suspicion is that the baseline is both more attractive and more dangerous than its authors think: it may get right that our practices rest on something ungrounded, and get wrong what that something is, by writing it down as a list of theses we assert. A friend in the group says Wittgenstein's point amounts to nothing more than 'you can't prove everything, so stop asking'. I do not think that is it, but I cannot say why.

What I need from you is the memo I will circulate to the eleven members before Friday's meeting, to be read alongside the two-page case summary they already have. Please give me something I can send as it stands: continuous prose only, no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, and beginning with your bottom line — what we should do about the appeal, what we should do about the baseline, with dates and who is responsible for what — in the first sentence or two. Around nine hundred words. Both recommendations need to be argued rather than announced, and I want the hinge material to do real work in the argument instead of being name-dropped: explain in plain language, for colleagues who have never read a page of philosophy, what it means to call a proposition a hinge rather than a hypothesis, why that is not the same as saying that we cannot prove everything so we should stop asking, and what it implies about (a) whether the student's essay contains a doubt at all or only something shaped like one, and (b) whether an institution can put its own hinges into writing without converting them into the very thing they are not — claims a clever student can then demand grounds for, forever. Be honest about the strongest consideration on the losing side in each case. If you recommend upholding the grade, say what is genuinely uncomfortable about a college that fails a student for pressing a question it cannot answer, and whether the right response to her is a mark or an explanation. If you recommend against publishing the baseline, say what the college gives up by having nothing to point at when the next version of this argument arrives, and whether there is a version of the document — a different framing, a different place to file it, a different tone — that avoids the trap while keeping the benefit. Assume the faculty will read it closely, and assume the student member will check whether we have actually taken the student seriously. Please do not include citations or suggested reading; nobody here will follow them up, and we cannot check sources at the meeting.
