I'm a software engineer with no philosophy background (unless a phil 101 elective I barely remember counts) and for the last year or so I've been building a procurement-transparency tool for a public agency — we ingest tender and payment records and flag transactions for auditors to look at. The annoying part is that to write the flagging rules I have to actually say what counts as a shady transaction, and everyone on the team is using "corruption" to mean something slightly different. So I tried writing down what I actually believe and I'd like to know whether the set hangs together, because I have a feeling it definately doesn't but I can't locate the problem.

Here's roughly where I've landed: (1) corruption is basically a public role being used for private benefit — I think of it as someone betraying the rules of their office, or treating one person better than the rules allow. (2) The small-scale stuff that gets called gift-giving elsewhere — you bring the clerk or the inspector something around a holiday, the doctor gets a box of sweets, everyone does it and refusing would be a genuine insult — I don't think that's corruption; I think people from low-corruption countries mislabel ordinary manners as graft. (3) But clientelism — a politician handing out jobs, school places, contracts or a sack of rice to the people who supported them — is corruption, even in places where it's locally accepted as normal and even in the cases where it arguably gets resources to poor people faster than the official system would. (4) The legal version rich countries run — big campaign donations, lobbying access, the revolving door between a regulator and the industry it regulates — is also corruption, even though nothing illegal happens and no rule of anybody's office gets violated. (5) What makes corruption bad is the unfairness: some people get access and others don't, and decisions get made for private reasons. I don't buy the grease-the-wheels argument, but I also don't think "it costs a couple of GDP points" is the main case against it. (6) The international league tables of perceived corruption, and the Western reform advice that comes attached to them, are culturally loaded and I don't think you can just export them wholesale — but I'd be uncomfortable saying they're only measuring a Western hangup, because I think they track something real. (7) Last one: I want to say corruption is about public officials specifically, so a private company cooking its books is fraud rather than corruption — and yet I do think a procurement officer at a private hospital taking a cut from a supplier is doing something corrupt.

So: which of these are genuinely in tension with which? I keep suspecting 5 and 6 are the problem, but that might just be because they sound contradictory, and I honestly can't tell which one I'd give up. Please rank the tensions by how bad they are instead of listing them all as equally serious, and for each one say what exactly would have to give and what dropping it would cost me — and if any of the pairs only look like they clash and actually dissolve once you're careful, tell me that too, and why. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most of the mess. Plain reasoning please, I don't need citations or reading lists, I just need to know whether my own view is coherent before I encode it in a rules engine.
