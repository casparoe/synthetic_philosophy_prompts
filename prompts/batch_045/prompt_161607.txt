Here is the situation. I'm a senior at a fairly large public high school, and this year our student council put a proposal to a school-wide referendum: eliminate weighted GPAs. It failed, 57 to 43, with about 71 percent turnout. The losing side then ran a campaign claiming that the "real student body" had wanted it and that the council, the AP cohort, and the college counseling office had rigged the framing to protect their own advantages. In our Current Issues elective we have been arguing about that campaign all semester, and two of my friends have hardened into positions that I now think are the same positions the grown-up argument is stuck in.

Ana's view: any appeal to what the students actually want, directed against a body that is ignoring them, is just democracy working properly. Legitimacy flows one way, from the people to their institutions; institutions are only proxies, and a proxy that stops tracking the people can be revoked. Populism, on this telling, is democracy with the training wheels off, and talking about "the will of the people" is just talking about democratic authority in plain language.

Marcus's view: that is precisely the danger. "The will of the people" asserts a single unified will where there are only roughly eighteen hundred students with partly conflicting interests. The move from "many people want many different things" to "the people want one thing" is a fiction, and once you accept it you acquire a license to declare whoever disagrees to be not really part of the people — which is exactly what my classmates did when they wrote off the AP votes as corrupt.

The frustrating part is that we all agree on the facts. Turnout was 71 percent. The margin was 57 to 43. The council did control the ballot wording. AP students did vote against at higher rates. Nobody has moved since September, and every exchange now just replays the first one.

My own hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than flatter, is that this isn't a factual disagreement or even a straightforward value disagreement, but a disagreement about what kind of thing the will of the people is: something that exists antecedently, can be discovered, and can be betrayed (Ana), versus something that comes into existence only as the output of a procedure, so that there is nothing there to betray (Marcus). If that's right the dispute is partly verbal — but only partly, because the two pictures license very different institutional conclusions and very different judgments about who is entitled to speak for whom.

To probe this I invented something I call the Oracle test: imagine a device that infallibly displays the content of the people's will on some question, and imagine that its readout disagrees with the result of a scrupulously fair election on that question. My guess is that Ana would say the election was therefore wrong and Marcus would say the machine must be tracking something other than what he means by a will — and that if both react that way, the disagreement was never empirical. Tell me whether that test is actually diagnostic or whether it secretly assumes the answer, and run it yourself on my referendum case either way.
