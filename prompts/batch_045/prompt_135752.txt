hey — I'm in a third-year philosophy of biology elective (mostly bio majors, a few phil minors, nobody who's read much of the literature) and our professor keeps leaning on one analogy and I can't tell how much of the view it's actually carrying.

The analogy: a trait is like a tool in a workshop. Its function is the job it was *made* to do, not whatever you happen to be using it for this afternoon. A screwdriver used to pry open a paint tin doesn't thereby acquire the function of opening paint tins; and a screwdriver with a snapped tip still has the function of driving screws, it's just broken. She's using this to defend the etiological / selected-effects account against the causal-role account, and she draws three things out of it:

1. dysfunction is real — a heart that can't pump still has the function of pumping blood, because what it's supposed to do ≠ what it does
2. not every effect is a function — the heart makes thumping noises, that's a causal contribution, not its function
3. functions can be updated — she calls it the "modern history" variant: once selection starts maintaining the trait for B rather than A, B becomes the function, so the etiological account isn't stuck with the original selective history forever

My suspicion is that the analogy is smuggling in a designer. For artifacts there's a fact about what the maker intended and usually a blueprint; for a trait there's no agent, no foresight, and selection typically maintains a trait for a messy bundle of effects at once, plus drift. Also: under the analogy, who is the maker in case 3 — is she quietly allowing the designer to redesign the tool mid-lecture? And I made up my own test case that I think breaks it: trait T was originally selected for effect A; the environment then shifts so A stops mattering for fitness, but T has a side effect B that now does all the work of keeping T in the population. Does T have function B in the first generation, or only after selection has had time to act, or never? And what about a trait that got fixed by drift and only later turned out to be useful — no selection history at all?
