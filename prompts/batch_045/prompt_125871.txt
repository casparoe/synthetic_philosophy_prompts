I hold, tentatively, a version of the disposition answer to Parfit's hitchhiker. In the case as Parfit gives it in *Reasons and Persons* (1984) — the driver takes the stranded hitchhiker to town on condition that she pays him once they arrive, at which point paying serves no further purpose of hers, so a perfect predictor leaves her in the desert — I accept that the self-interest theory of rationality is self-defeating in the relevant sense, and I draw the standard consequence: that the proper object of rational assessment is not the individual act but the standing disposition or policy, and that an agent can have decisive reason to acquire a disposition whose exercise she will have no reason to perform. I also accept the bolder formulation, that rationality can require being disposed to act against one's reasons — that "rational irrationality" is a real phenomenon and not a rhetorical flourish. What I want from you is a map of what would count against this, and I want you to press hardest on the places where I suspect the position is either trivial or unstable.

The worry I keep returning to is a dilemma about how the recommendation is meant to work. Either acquiring the disposition maximises expected utility at the level of policies, in which case there is no irrationality anywhere and the case only shows that assessment must be done over policies rather than acts — a point no sophisticated consequentialist ever denied, and one that does not need a desert melodrama to establish. Or the disposition genuinely does fail to maximise expected utility, in which case rationality is being said to require acting against expected utility, and I need to be told why that is not simply incoherent, and how a defender distinguishes it from superstition. If there is a stable middle position, I would like it stated precisely; if there is not, I would rather be told that.

I have also been turning over some variants of my own, and I want you to tell me which of them has real bite and which rests on a confusion of mine.

First, the sealed tell. Before offering the ride, the driver reads some involuntary, unalterable physiological marker that reliably indicates whether the hitchhiker will pay; the reading is now fixed, and nothing she does in town can affect it, nor can she deceive him, since he is a perfect predictor. The marker indicated that she would pay, which is why she was picked up. She is now in town, and paying costs her something and gains her nothing. Does the disposition view still prescribe paying? If it does, the prescription seems to reduce to "act as the person who would have paid would act," a rule with no rationale in her present or future prospects; if it does not, then the view appears to lose its grip in precisely the case that motivated it, and its real lesson may be about being predictably a certain way rather than about being disposed in any sense worth calling rational.

Second, the grain problem. Any disposition that a predictor can detect must be fairly coarse, but coarse dispositions misfire: consider the driver who drops her forty miles short of town after all, still thereby saving her life, and then demands the full agreed sum; or the driver who, having picked her up, names a price equal to everything she owns. A disposition fine-grained enough to withhold payment in those cases seems to become undetectable by any realistic predictor, and so worthless in the original case. Is this a genuine dilemma — detectability trade off against fidelity — or is it merely a design problem that a well-specified disposition solves?

Third, the crowded desert. Many one-off encounters, many drivers, each predicting with, say, sixty per cent accuracy, where the ride is worth far more than the payment. Here the disposition really does pay, but only through a population-level mechanism, and the same benefit seems available to a much cheaper trait: merely looking like someone who pays. So does the argument, honestly followed, recommend the disposition or the appearance of it — reputation management, or even deceiving the predictor where one can — and is there anything in the view that explains why the appearance is not strictly preferable to the reality? Relatedly, where a commitment device is available, handing over collateral or signing an enforceable agreement seems to dominate the disposition route entirely. How much of the phenomenon survives once commitment devices are on the table, and how much once the predictor is imperfect?

Fourth, acquirability. I am not sure I can acquire a disposition by deciding to, and I am not sure the hitchhiker can either. If dispositions are not voluntarily acquirable at the moment they matter, then "acquire the disposition" is not a rational requirement on action at all but advice to parents, institutions and younger selves, and the view quietly changes subject from individual rationality to social technology.

I also want the empirical exposure of the view made explicit. Gauthier, in *Morals by Agreement*, argues for a related disposition of constrained maximisation, and his account relies on agents being what he calls translucent, their dispositions imperfectly detectable by others; that assumption has been criticised as unrealistic, and some of the cues people actually seem to read are superficial rather than dispositional. What observations — from behavioural economics, from work on reputation and signalling, or from anywhere else — would in fact count against the disposition view? How accurate would prediction have to be, and how cheap detection, for the argument to get off the ground, and what would it take to show that we are not in that regime?

Please rank the defeaters you identify from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say whether a committed defender of the disposition view would concede it as counting against them or would dismiss it as resting on a rival framing. Tell me which of my four variants you think is load-bearing and which I should abandon. And give me your honest assessment of whether the position has been qualified into something unfalsifiable — whether any behaviour can be redescribed as rational at some level of policy — and if it has, whether that is a defect in a normative thesis of this kind. I should say what would move me most: a demonstration that the dilemma I opened with has no stable middle, or that the view's force depends on an empirical assumption about detectability that is simply false in the environments we actually face. I am not asking for a literature survey; argue directly from the cases.
