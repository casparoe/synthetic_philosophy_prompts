I write fiction, and at a writers' residency this spring I found myself in an argument that I have been unable to settle, conducted almost entirely in the terms of an analogy.

The manuscript I am working on turns on a character who does something indefensible and who, given the person she has become by the time she does it, could not have brought herself to do otherwise. My colleague's answer was that moral responsibility is like authorship. A novelist is answerable for her book even though, being the novelist she is, she could have written no other ending: what makes the book hers is that she wrote it, not that she could have written otherwise. And he extended the same point to the kind of case now standard in the literature — an agent who acts for her own reasons while a counterfactual intervener stands ready, someone who would have brought it about by quite different means that she do exactly what she in fact does if she had shown any sign of doing otherwise, and who therefore never intervenes at all. That intervener, he said, is simply an editor holding a standing instruction to rewrite the final chapter; the instruction is never executed, the chapter is mine, and nothing more needs to be said.

He is using the analogy to dispatch the principle of alternative possibilities — the claim that an agent is morally responsible for what she does only if she could have done otherwise — and to answer the worry that in such a case the agent's responsibility is inherited from the machinery waiting in the wings rather than from the agent herself.

I would like the analogy audited rather than admired or waved away.

First, the mapping made explicit. A table would be ideal: what, in the source domain, corresponds to the agent, to the action, to the alternatives the principle demands, to the intervener, to the agent's settled character, to the moment of decision, and to the audience that praises and blames — and, equally important, what on either side is left with no counterpart at all. In particular I want to know what authorship is being read as: causal production, something like ownership or proprietorship, or something like avowal and wholehearted endorsement. My suspicion is that the analogy slides among these three and that at most one of them can carry his conclusion.

Then the places where it breaks, sorted by whether they matter. I would rather have three disanalogies adjudicated than eight listed. The first: a novelist is a substance distinct from her product, related to it as cause to artifact, across a process extended in time and open to revision. If the analogy imports that structure into the agent, does it not smuggle in precisely the picture the literature is supposed to have given up — an inner author who stands behind the action, surveys it, and makes it hers? (There is a further wrinkle peculiar to my case: my character is at once the thing I author and, within the fiction, an agent with alternatives of her own. So which of the two is supposed to be me, and why does the analogy seem to need both?) The second: a novelist's inability to write otherwise is often the very thing that intensifies criticism rather than excusing it — we call her formulaic, say she wrote to a pattern, repeat herself. If that is right, has my colleague shown only something about how we assess works, while the question about blame has quietly changed the subject from a person to a product? The third: I want the two versions of the editor separated. In one, she waits to see how I actually draft the ending and overrides me only if I draft it differently. In the other, she reads my early chapters and steps in at the first sign that the plot is heading elsewhere, so that my ending is guaranteed well before I reach it. A philosopher friend assures me these come apart for reasons that have nothing to do with my colleague's analogy; if she is right, I want to know which of the two the analogy was modelling, whether it can model both, and what happens to his argument if it can only model one.

Finally, the verdict. Does the conclusion — that responsibility requires no alternative possibilities — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole way? And if the analogy cannot bear it, is there a better comparison available for what it was reaching at, or is the honest answer that authorship is not the clarifying notion here but the obscure one?
