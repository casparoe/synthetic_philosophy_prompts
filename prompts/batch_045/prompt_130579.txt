My friend Priya and I got into a three hour fight at a diner last night about rent control and I woke up still annoyed, so I want you to tell me what kind of fight it actually was. Setup: our city council just passed a pretty strict rent cap, something like inflation minus a point, with no vacancy decontrol, she's thrilled, I'm uneasy, and we definately went in circles. The maddening part is that we agree on basically every fact. Neither of us disputes the usual empirical picture: sitting tenants get a real transfer, turnover and mobility drop, the uncontrolled segment gets more expensive, the overall literature comes out negative. We even agreed the econ side is overconfident and that everyone cites the folklore about an "optimal" minimum wage as if it were a constant of nature. And I already conceded that "economists are nearly unanimous" is worthless as an argument to her, because she thinks the efficiency frame begs the question. We also fought about the symmetry thing: she supports a big minimum wage hike, I do too, and she pointed out that a price floor on labour and a price ceiling on housing are the same kind of object, so if "it distorts the market" settles one it settles the other. I said something about monopsony power in labour markets, which I don't think did the work I wanted it to.

So what's left, and why can't either of us move? My hypothesis, which I want you to sharpen or kill, is that we're not disagreeing about economics at all — we're scoring the same policy against different baselines and counting different people. I compare rent control to the efficient frontier, or to a vouchers-plus-zoning package that would actually help, and she compares it to what this council will plausibly do otherwise, which is nothing. I count the people who would have moved here and can't find an apartment; she counts the named tenants on her block who get evicted in March. Underneath, that looks like a disagreement about the standing of merely statistical future people, plus a baseline problem in how you evaluate a policy a real political body could pass. Then she has one more move I can't answer: that a home isn't wage income, that loosing your apartment isn't like missing out on a raise, and that markets in the thing people actually live in are morally a different animal from markets in everything else. Half of me thinks that's just her having a stronger gut feeling about the non-fungibility of home, and half of me thinks it's load bearing.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict on rent control. Substantive disagreement, verbal disagreement, or two people answering different questions under one heading — and if the last, what exactly are the two questions? Is there a hidden empirical question doing the work, something about mobility elasticities or about how much incidence lands on people who aren't residents yet, that we could in principle settle? Is the real disagreement in population ethics, about whether possible future tenants count and how much? Is it about whether home is a different kind of good? Or is it the baseline problem all the way down? Rank those by how much weight they're actually carrying and tell me which ones are decoration. Then, given whatever you diagnose, say honestly what would count as progress here: a number somebody could look up, a distinction that dissolves the argument, some city that paired a rent cap with something else, or just the conclusion that this bottoms out in bedrock and we should drop it. Two constraints: she's going to read this, so don't just tell me I'm right and she's confused. And please don't re-litigate whether rent control reduces supply or rehash the econ 101 case — we both stipulate the empirical literature and she'll check out the moment it turns into a lecture.
