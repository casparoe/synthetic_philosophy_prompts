I co-convene a small reading group that sits across a machine-learning lab and a philosophy department, and the session I am organizing next week is the one on whether reinforcement learning agents can suffer. For three meetings the engineers and the philosophers have been talking past each other, and what finally made the disagreement concrete was a technical observation rather than a philosophical one: a well-trained agent that finds itself in an unavoidably bad state has a large negative value there but almost no prediction error left, while an agent early in training is full of error and surprise and has barely any value estimate worth the name. So depending on which quantity you point at — the reward that arrives, the learned value function, the temporal-difference error, the advantage that reweights the update — the amount of suffering in the system goes up or down, peaks during learning or only once learning is finished, and vanishes precisely when the agent has become most competent. Two further pressures should be somewhere in the background: if valence is nothing but the functional role of marking a state as to-be-avoided and driving learning away from it, then something in these systems already occupies that role (and the slogan that reward is enough to specify any goal starts to look like a claim about mind rather than about objectives); but if valence requires more than the role, then we have no test we could run, only a standoff about which we should be careful.

What I would like is a sestina whose six end-words are reward, value, error, gradient, pain, and silence — in that order in the first stanza and following the classical rotation thereafter, with a three-line envoi that brings all six back. The form should be doing real work: because the same six words keep returning in new company, technical in one stanza and experiential in the next, the reader should lose track of which frame was the literal one, and the envoi should make clear what the question turns on without settling it. I would rather the poem not be written in the first person of the agent, since whether there is a first person there is exactly what is in dispute; a voice that slides between a designer's logbook and something harder to place suits the material better.

Please keep the technical content honest enough that someone who has actually implemented a policy-gradient method would not wince: reward is not the value function, the value function is not the prediction error, the error is not the advantage, and the gradient is what changes the policy; the reward channel is typically switched off at deployment, so whatever persists of the agent's evaluative structure is not a stream of numbers arriving from outside. And please do not let it become either a sermon about the digital minds we are tormenting or a shrug about how it is all just floating-point arithmetic — several people in the room hold each of those views, and I would like the poem to be a place both of them can stand. Begin with the poem itself, with no preface or commentary; after it, add only a brief note — a sentence or two per item — marking where each of the candidate correlates shows up, so that I can point at specific lines when the discussion starts.
