I'm a rising senior at a large public high school, and I'm applying for a six-week summer research fellowship that my school runs with a nearby university: ten students get a small stipend, a faculty mentor, and a desk, and the application is a two-page proposal read by a committee of three teachers — one biology, one history, and the teacher who coaches our Ethics Bowl team — so it can't lean on vocabulary that only philosophy majors would recognize. The committee says explicitly that it wants the question and the plan, not a reading list, so please don't include citations or suggested sources; I'll fill those in later with my mentor.

I got into this after an argument with my uncle, who raises beef cattle, and have been reading about it on my own since. What frustrates me is that the arguments I keep encountering for eating chicken rather than beef, or plants rather than either, seem to me to be adding up incommensurable things without admitting it, and my half-formed idea is that the confusion comes from there being three separate ledgers that people slide between: a deaths-caused ledger, which counts the animals killed in producing the food; a lives-created ledger, which counts the animals who exist at all only because someone will eat them; and a lives-prevented ledger, which counts the wild animals whose existence is blocked when land is turned over to crops or pasture. That third ledger is what makes this a question about wild animal suffering and not just about farmed animals, because if the typical wild life contains more suffering than happiness, then converting habitat to cropland may prevent an enormous amount of suffering, and the sign of the whole comparison could flip. There is also, I think, a separate currency problem underneath the ledger problem: if the harm is measured in deaths, chicken looks far worse than beef per calorie, since it takes many more birds to make the same amount of food, but if the harm is measured in days of suffering, the ranking may reverse, because the cattle live so much longer, and field deaths from crop cultivation may then dominate both. My uncle's own argument was essentially that if nobody ate beef these animals would never have existed at all, and I want to know how much work that point can really do.

What I need is the proposal itself, not a defense of any answer. Please state the question so that it has a determinate answer rather than being a request for a general discussion, explain why the positions one can arrive at by common sense — count deaths per calorie; count suffering per animal; treat the existence point as settling it — leave the question open, and set out the method week by week for six weeks at roughly fifteen hours a week with no possibility of collecting data, including why a conceptual project makes genuine progress here rather than just restating what everyone already believes. Say why this approach is better than the obvious alternatives, such as assembling published estimates, surveying what people actually eat, or arguing directly for one diet. Give the possible conclusions and what would decide between them, and give an honest failure condition: my worry is that the result will collapse into it depends on which currency you prefer, so I want the proposal to say in advance what would make the outcome more informative than that. Finally, tell me honestly what part of this I should cut to make it feasible for someone with no graduate training, and what a deliverable would look like that would actually help a person — or a school cafeteria — deciding what to serve.
