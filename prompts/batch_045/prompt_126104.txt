You are a philosopher of science with a working knowledge of the history of economics, and you have thought hard about how long-run predictive theories survive — or fail to survive — missed deadlines. Before you answer, do some quiet brainstorming: list the candidate correspondences between the source and target domains, decide for each of them whether it is doing real argumentative work or just decoration, and ask yourself what would have to be true for the verdict "right but early" to amount to more than a politeness extended to a famous dead author. Then give me a considered answer on the merits. Please do not hand me a bibliography or a reading list; I want the argument, not citations, and I have no library access where I'm writing from.

Here is the situation. A reading group I belong to works through classic social theories and asks of each one whether it was ever actually refuted. Last session was Malthus. One member defends him as follows. She says that predicting a Malthusian check is like predicting an earthquake on a known fault: the theory says stress accumulates and must eventually be released, it does not — and in principle cannot — say on which Tuesday, so the mere fact that the rupture has not yet arrived is not evidence against the model. On that basis she concludes that the two centuries of rising living standards since the Essay are not a refutation of Malthus, only a delay, and that we should still expect a Malthusian limit to bite. A second member countered with the opposite picture: Malthus is like the boy who cried wolf, or like a stopped clock, and the passage of time is itself the evidence. Both of them went away thinking they had won.

I want an audit of the earthquake analogy, and a verdict on what it can carry.

Give me the mapping explicitly — a table of correspondences is fine. What plays the role of the fault, of the stress, of the measurable build-up, of the rupture, of the interseismic quiet, of the seismologist's date-free forecast? Then take the mapping apart. I am especially interested in the disanalogies, and in which of them matter for the use my friend is making of the analogy rather than merely being true differences. Three of my own worries, which you should feel free to rank, discard, or replace:

1. In seismology the stress variable is measurable independently of the outcome — you can watch strain accumulate and thereby confirm the mechanism while the quake is still pending. I raised this and she replied that population growth and resource drawdown are exactly such variables. Is that reply good? Note that if the mechanism predicts observable build-up, then a very long quiet period in which the predicted build-up does not occur is not neutral evidence, and I'd like to know what a hazard-rate style argument does to her position: as the quiet period lengthens under a supposedly rising hazard, at what point does non-occurrence become disconfirmation rather than patience?

2. The mechanism matters as much as the timing. Malthus's own arithmetic claim about food production looks to me not merely mistimed but wrong in a way a delay cannot fix, and the twentieth-century escape from the trap came via sources of yield growth the theory gave no handle on, plus a fertility transition in which rising income produced fewer children rather than more mouths. If the mechanism was overridden rather than postponed, does the earthquake picture misdescribe this as a longer interseismic period when it is really a change of regime — a fault that was, so to speak, decommissioned?

3. The target keeps moving. Contemporary invocations of Malthus rarely mean grain; they mean some mix of resources, sinks, and ecological throughput. Call this "retrospective capture": every later episode with a Malthusian look to it is claimed as a confirmation, while the two centuries of non-confirmation are not counted against, and the content of the theory shifts just enough to keep the correspondence intact. I'd like you to test it with what I've been calling the re-dating test: if a tradition's response to every missed deadline is to move the deadline while leaving both the mechanism and its confidence intact, what should we conclude about the status of the original prediction? Does the earthquake analogy help a defender here, or does it hide the equivocation?

Then pull the strands together. Distinguish for me, carefully, the things the verdict "right but early" could mean: that the mechanism is real, still operative, and only the date was wrong; that the mechanism was real for the world the theory was built to describe but has since been superseded; and that the mechanism was never the mechanism, with later apparent vindications being different processes producing superficially similar outcomes. Say which of these the earthquake analogy is able to express and which it collapses together. Ask the closing question too, which I think is the one that decides the matter: is there any observation the defender would accept as showing Malthus was not early but wrong — and if not, is the analogy supplying a genuine epistemic defense or merely a social licence to stop keeping score?

One more thing, if you have room: apply the same audit, briefly, to the parallel move made on behalf of the 1960s population-bomb literature — the writer who predicted mass famine within a couple of decades and lost a wager with an economist over commodity prices, whose defenders now say he was right about the mechanism and wrong only about the date. Does your verdict transfer, or does the second case come apart from the first in a way that shows something about the analogy?

Finally, if the earthquake is the wrong picture, tell me what would be better, and what that better analogy would license that this one does not.
