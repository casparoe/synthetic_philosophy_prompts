The topic is whether generations are real kinds — Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z and the rest — and here's my half-formed idea. As far as I can tell there are three live accounts: the deflationary one, on which a generation is just an arbitrary slice of birth year and the real patterns are age effects and period effects in disguise; the causal one, on which shared exposure to a formative environment produces genuinely shared dispositions, which is what people mean when they talk about cohort effects in the data; and the looping one, on which the labels partly make the kind — being told you are a Millennial, and being caricatured by the next one along, feeds back into how you act and self-describe. My suspicion is that the second and third are usually run as rivals but are better seen as operating at different grains, and that the crux is something nobody in the philosophy discussion seems to take seriously enough: the age-period-cohort identification problem. Cohort is a linear function of period and age, so you cannot pull a separate cohort effect out of the data without importing some extra substantive assumption, and that assumption looks an awful lot like the philosophical thesis wearing a lab coat. Which would mean the move where people say the data show cohort effects is quietly begging the question.

What I want from the taxonomy side is the question of grain. Instead of asking whether generations are real, ask whether there is any resolution at which the boundaries are forced — real discontinuities or thresholds in the adoption of practices, say a childhood with or without ubiquitous networked screens — rather than smooth clinal variation with boundaries drawn wherever a writer wants them. And where there are several rival plausible boundaries, treat the case the way a taxonomist treats a species complex: real enough in the middle, conventional at the edge. There's also the type-specimen problem: the ok-boomer style caricature anchors the name the way a physical specimen anchors a binomial, and I think it drags the extension of the term around, which is why people born near a boundary often refuse the label.
