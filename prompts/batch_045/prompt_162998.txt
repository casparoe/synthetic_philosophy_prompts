I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, working on an internal developer-tools platform. I need a decision tree for a design doc that a mixed audience will read (engineers, one ML researcher, a couple of skeptical staff engineers), so please keep it in engineer's language: define any term of art in half a sentence. Please don't give me citations, paper titles, or a reading list — I can't check them and don't need them; for any named approach, one clause of description is enough, and if you're unsure whether something has actually been tried, say so rather than asserting it.

The setting: we have a model that has ingested our monorepo, its commit history, and most of our CI logs. We ask it in natural language whether a proposed patch will break a test suite that has not yet been run, and it answers in prose. Sometimes the prose answer disagrees with what we can verify hours later, and a colleague suspects the model tracks the answer internally in a way its output doesn't reflect. Half the team thinks that is an anthropomorphic description of a next-token predictor; the other half thinks the activations contain something like a verdict. I don't want to settle that by argument — I want a tree that sorts us. The root should be the one commitment we all share: we want the truth about a specific question about the codebase, and right now we cannot cheaply check it.

Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of known techniques. Give me ten to fourteen branch nodes, each phrased as a question a teammate could answer without already knowing which endpoint it leads to — for example: whether we can ever obtain ground truth for the questions we care about; whether that truth arrives only after the decision has to be made; whether we need a verdict on one fixed yes/no question or open-ended answers about the code; whether we have white-box access or only an inference API; whether we are willing to train a reporter on trusted labels or need something that works with none; whether we think the model would mislead us if it knew it was being evaluated; whether the domain is one where the model is plausibly more capable than the people supervising it. Then say which combinations of answers are available in principle, which are empty, and for each empty one whether that is because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored.

Then place the real approaches in that space: better prompting plus external verification and monitoring of the model's reasoning; defer the question and verify later; supervised probes trained on activations; unsupervised consistency-style extractors that use only unlabeled activations and logical constraints; debate or adversarial amplification between models; training-time changes to the objective or incentives; mechanistic interpretability that reads an answer off a circuit; and the skeptic's option that there is no latent verdict to extract and the model should be treated as simulating one or another answerer. Add at least one leaf for an approach you think is underexplored — my own hunch is to stop treating the model as a witness and use it only as a learned index over a deterministic checker, retrieving what matters and letting the compiler or the test runner have the last word; use your own judgment if you think that collapses into an existing leaf. Six to nine leaves total. For each leaf: one line on what you would actually build, and one line on its most awkward commitment. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot really separate, rather than inventing a distinction.

Finally, be explicit about confidence. Separate what people who work on this genuinely agree on from what is a live dispute, and mark anywhere my framing of the problem presupposes something contested — in particular whether there is a single latent fact of the matter to extract, and whether a probe that performs well on yes/no benchmark questions tells us anything about a question nobody can check. For calibration: I half-remember a result where an unsupervised consistency-based probe beat zero-shot accuracy on yes/no datasets by only a few points and stayed accurate when models were prompted to answer incorrectly. Tell me whether that is roughly right, how much weight it deserves, and what the result does not establish. Do not average disagreements into a consensus that isn't there, and do not present a contested view as the settled one.
