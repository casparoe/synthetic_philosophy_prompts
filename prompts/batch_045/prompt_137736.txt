I organise a monthly applied-rationality meetup in a borrowed seminar room: about fifteen adults, ninety minutes, mostly working programmers plus three cognitive-science grad students, a physician, and two people who have read some Dennett and will arrive with opinions ready. Projector and index cards available; no budget, no special equipment.

For the next session I want to stage the argument that qualia are an incoherent posit rather than argue it. I have watched that argument get rehearsed in comment threads for years and it never moves anyone, because it is always delivered as a lecture about a word. So: design an activity in which the group runs into the problem themselves, using real choices and small real stakes, and only afterwards discovers what they were arguing about.

The idea I want the design to turn on is what I call the settlement-aptness constraint. A property is something you can genuinely stake anything on only if a third party could in principle determine, after the fact, whether it obtained. The posit is standardly a bundle of four properties: it is ineffable, intrinsic, private, and immediately apprehensible in consciousness by its subject. Now give participants a private property of this kind and make them adjudicate claims about it. To settle a claim they must produce a public criterion, and each of the three adjudication tasks pulls that criterion somewhere different. Re-identifying the property over time within one subject makes the criterion answer to changes in that subject's own discriminative machinery, which makes the property relational rather than intrinsic. Comparing it across two subjects makes the criterion third-person checkable, which costs it its privacy. Settling a claim about a silent swap — where the subject is the only possible witness — makes first-person report authoritative and unfalsifiable, which costs it the status of a fact anyone could ever have bet on. I want participants to walk into all three walls in succession, in that order, and to build the criteria themselves.

Constraints on the design:

1. No framing lecture. The words qualia, phenomenal, inverted spectrum, zombie, and Mary must not appear on any handout or in the facilitator's mouth before the debrief. The group should coin its own term for the private property and discover that they have to police what their own term means.
2. At least one round must use a real perceptual manipulation that is cheap and reliable with a projector and index cards — colour afterimages, an image with no red pixels that reliably looks red, a shadow-checker display, adaptation to a coloured field — so that something about the experience demonstrably changes while every publicly checkable fact stays fixed.
3. Small stakes only: chips, chocolate, or the right to leave early. No money.
4. Three rounds of roughly twenty minutes each and a twenty-five to thirty minute debrief, with a minute-by-minute timeline and a note on what the facilitator does during each silence.
5. Include the actual text of the contracts or ledger cards participants fill in, the scoring rule, and an explicit rule for what happens if the group refuses to settle a contract or dissolves into metadebate.
6. Make the collapse visible as a number. Ideally the session ends with a tally on the board showing that the group's own settlement rule either clears a case everyone agrees it should fail or fails a case everyone agrees it should pass.
7. The debrief should map each round onto one of the four properties, say which of the four is load-bearing — the one a defender should surrender first, and what surrendering it costs — and then handle the three escape routes as participants will actually raise them rather than as a taxonomy from the literature: retreat to a functional or relational property, retreat to the authority of first-person report, and retreat to an ineffable property known only by acquaintance.
8. Give at least four things that will predictably go wrong the first time this is run, with a specific repair for each. I expect at least: one participant who names the game's point in the first ten minutes and starts narrating it; one who dissolves every contract by declaring the matter unverifiable; one who proposes settling by neural measurement and has to be shown what that rule would let through; and one for whom the whole thing collapses into the problem of other minds.
9. Be frank about which part of the claim the activity cannot deliver. It can produce the conceptual squeeze, but it cannot produce the metaphysical conclusion, and it does not touch the knowledge argument or the further question of why there is experience at all. Say plainly what the facilitator will have to supply in words, and why no arrangement of roles, incentives, or payoffs could supply it instead.
