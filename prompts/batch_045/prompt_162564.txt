I'm TAing a third-year Philosophy of Mind course this term — well, running most of the seminars — about 18 students, a mixed crowd: roughly half philosophy majors and half cognitive science majors who want everything to come with an operational definition. I've got one 80-minute slot and I want to spend it on the qualia material, but as an activity *before* the reading: they haven't met Dennett's Quining Qualia chapter or the dancing-qualia reply yet, and I'd rather not say either "qualia" or "Dennett" out loud until the game is over. The thing I want them to walk into is the Chase-and-Sanborn-flavored claim — not that alleged qualia are unverifiable, or private in the sense that we can't know about them from outside, but that the posit itself is incoherent: the item is supposed to be intrinsic, ineffable, private and directly apprehensible, yet every way of pinning down *which* one you've got (similarity structure, discriminability, what you'd say, what you'd do next) is relational, and once the relations are stripped away there's no identity condition left for it. Materials: I can print decks of colour swatches (paint-chip cards, 10-12 per deck), I have index cards and a projector, no budget for prizes, and at least one student in the room has a colour vision deficiency.

So: design me a multi-round game where they run into that themselves. What I have in mind — change it if you have something better — is a deck-based silent-swap structure: each subject gets a deck plus a legend of colour names, and different subjects' decks are hue-rotations of one another with their legends rotated to match, so that every discrimination, similarity ranking, naming and preference comes out identical even though what's in front of their eyes differs. Rotate students through roles: Subject, Intervener (swaps decks between rounds and secretly logs what they actually did), and Auditor (sees only the paper trail and has to call which rounds involved a swap). Subjects keep a short private ledger after each round: one sentence that would let *them* tell later whether their experience changed, and one sentence that would let a *partner* tell. Then build in specific round types: a global swap (a full automorphism of the deck, undetectable); a *partial* swap where only some swatches rotate and the similarity structure inside the deck breaks, so it is detectable — the debrief point being that the only swaps that survive are the symmetries of the quality space, so the space of possible dances is fixed by the relational structure, which is the bit that's supposed to make "intrinsic" wobble; a round-trip round where the Intervener swaps and immediately swaps back, so the class has to answer whether it danced twice or zero times, and whether anyone, even in principle, could have a fact about the number; a false-disclosure round where the Intervener tells half the room they were swapped on some round and tells nobody the truth, so the "fact" arrives through a channel that changes nothing anyone can do; and a handover round where each subject writes down, without mentioning other experiences, stimuli, or behaviour, the property they are directly aware of, and a partner has to decide whether they have the same one. Give me a payoff scheme with no money involved where asserting "there was a change" is something you have to pay for and staying silent is cheap, but where silence isn't obviously the dominant strategy: I really don't want it to look like I rigged it toward the eliminativist answer, because half the room will spot that and discount the whole exercise.

Then give me a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto specific features of the incoherence claim — which round does which piece of work — plus what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover in the room. My guesses: someone saying the swap is undetectable only because I stipulated it, so it's a trick; people conflating the swap with memory erasure; the cog-sci half simply defining "difference" as discriminability and refusing the question; the Intervener being accused of cheating, or of changing the stimulus rather than the experience; and the colour-vision-deficient student being the odd one out (if it's cleaner to run the whole thing on a non-colour dimension, say which one and how). Finally, be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce and that I'll therefore have to say out loud: my guess is that it can't produce the first-personal seeming that survives every failed cash-out, and that swapping cards and relabelling legends isn't really a case where experience changes while everything functional stays fixed. Tell me how to handle that moment, because it's exactly where the strongest realist in the room will say the whole exercise missed the point.
