I'm a principal engineer responsible for a payments system whose oldest components predate everyone currently employed on it; I've no philosophy beyond a first-year course, and the vocabulary I actually think in is that of legacy-system maintenance — dead code, orphaned invariants, feature flags, staged rollout, blast radius, reversibility, incident review, tech-debt registers. Please restate, in that vocabulary and as faithfully as you can, two arguments my colleagues invoke as though they were one: Chesterton's fence — that one should not remove an arrangement before understanding what it is for — and Burke's broader defence of inherited institutions, including the suggestion that a long-established arrangement carries an authority that any individual's reasoning about it lacks.

Keep the two apart as you translate. I want it to come out whether Chesterton's principle is a deferral rule (establish the purpose; remove the thing if there turns out to be none) or a burden-shifting norm (whoever proposes removal must justify it even once the original purpose is known to be spent), and whether Burke is committed to something stronger than either — that persistence across generations is itself evidence of fitness, and that an institution contains more reasoning than any single mind can reconstruct.

Then give me the residue report, and put two complications from my own work to both arguments rather than treating them as decoration. The first is my distinction between vestigial code, which nothing depends on, and an orphaned invariant, which is enforced but whose rationale no one can recover: I have watched a team remove an orphaned invariant, pass every test, run incident-free for months, and then lose money on a rare combination of conditions nobody had reconstructed. The second is that a design can survive twenty years in one of our systems for reasons that have nothing to do with fitness — lock-in, switching costs, the departure of whoever understood it — so persistence looks like ambiguous evidence at best.

Tell me what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all — I suspect something around the trans-generational subject Burke invokes, around attachment as a motive distinct from understanding, and around the kind of authority he contrasts with individual reasoning — and then whether that residue is a gap in how we talk about legacy systems or a sign that the conservative claim was never as clear as it sounds.

Structure the answer in four sections, in this order: "Translation" (roughly 350 words, Chesterton and Burke separately, in my terms); "One argument or two" (roughly 200 words, on whether they come apart under translation); "Residue" (roughly 250 words, engaging both of my complications); "Verdict" (roughly 100 words, on whether the residue indicts my vocabulary or the argument).
