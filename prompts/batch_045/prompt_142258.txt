You are a professional philosopher who works on the epistemology of testimony and expert disagreement, and who has spent the last couple of years following the literature on AI safety via debate. You also write very good light prose. Please think carefully before you start drafting: spend a paragraph or two brainstorming which single real feature of the debate proposal you are going to press on, and which concrete mechanism will carry the joke, so that the piece has a spine rather than being a series of gags.

The piece I want: a satirical internal evaluation document from a fictional frontier AI lab, written after a trial run of a debate-based safety protocol. Call it something like a "Safety Case Addendum" or a "Post-Trial Evaluation Note". Roughly 900 words, formatted with numbered or headed sections (Executive Summary, Methodology, Results, Judge Rationale, Recommendations) and an appendix containing a short excerpt of the debate transcript together with the judge's written justification.

The voice is the deadpan assurance register of a corporate safety case: passive constructions, bullet points, a confident summary at the top, and the genuinely damning material buried in an appendix nobody in the organisation is expected to read. The document must sincerely believe that the trial was a success. No winking, no italicised sarcasm, no "ironically", no aside that breaks character: the reader should work out that the trial failed from the document's own evidence.

The target is not a person but a practice and an assumption behind it: the practice of validating debate-style protocols by measuring whether the human judge picks the answer the lab already knows to be correct, and the assumption that a weaker judge can be trusted to adjudicate between stronger debaters because truth has a structural advantage — the honest side only has to point at one broken step, while the false side has to keep a whole edifice standing. The humour has to come from real features of that setup, exaggerated but not invented. In particular I want you to get the following right and make jokes out of them:

- the honest debater's asymmetric burden: it must locate and explain the single mistaken step in a long chain, while the dishonest debater only has to produce the chain;
- the fact that the only signal actually available to the judge is which debater seemed more like someone who knew, and the report's need to fall back on that signal while describing itself as measuring truth;
- scoring rules that punish hedging, so that "I cannot locate the error, but I am not convinced" is recorded as a loss for the honest side;
- recursion: assertions supported by assertions supported by assertions, with the judge terminating the recursion by trusting whichever debater was more willing to keep going;
- a remedy in the Recommendations section — longer permitted transcripts, deeper citation chains, a second judge who adjudicates the first judge's comprehension — that plainly makes the original problem worse while being presented as an improvement.

To keep this from collapsing into pastiche, here are some specifics to build the piece around. The lab and the model get invented names. The protocol is called "Certainty-Weighted Adjudication" and awards the win to the debater whose closing statement is more confident, defended in the report on the grounds that expressed uncertainty is itself evidence of an incomplete model of the question. The subject of the trial debate is concrete and technical: whether a particular loss spike late in training was caused by a data-ordering bug or by a genuine capability jump, with the honest debater arguing for the boring explanation. The judge is a lab engineer with twelve minutes and a competing deadline. Include a small table of "Judge Comprehension Self-Reports" in which comprehension is self-rated at four out of five while correct recall of the decisive claim is zero out of three, and let the report treat the first number as the headline finding. The judge's rationale in the appendix should contain at least one sentence that is strictly speaking true and yet makes plain that the judge did not follow the argument at all.
