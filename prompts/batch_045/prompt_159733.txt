i'm TAing an applied ethics seminar and our killing vs letting die week is coming up. Most of it is the usual stuff — Rachels' Smith-and-Jones bathtub pair, the doctrine of doing and allowing, whether the bare difference between doing something and merely letting it happen can carry moral weight, and then the cases where it seems not to: withdrawing treatment, triage, refusing to donate. I'd like to put Kazuo Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go on the reading list beside it, but I want a reading of the novel that does real philosophical work rather than functioning as mood music for a week of thought experiments.

The thing I keep coming back to: nobody in that book kills anybody. The guardians raise the students, the carers look after donors through the operations, the recipients take the organs, and every one of them can describe what they do in the language of allowing — no individual ever performs the action that ends a life. Yet the entire institution exists in order that those deaths happen, and happen more or less on schedule. I've started calling this distributed allowing, and I want to know whether it's a genuine problem for the doctrine of doing and allowing or just a fancy name for a lot of ordinary omissions stacked next to each other. The distinction is supposed to track something about the relation between a particular agent's will and a particular death; does it have anything to say when the relevant agent is a system and no one ever has the death up to them?

Second wrinkle I want your view on: the donations are the reason the clones exist at all. Kathy isn't there unless the system is, so the baseline that letting-die cases normally rely on — that the person would otherwise have gone on living — isn't available. Is that a real difficulty for saying she's harmed, or a red herring that the doing/allowing question doesn't actually need?

So: read the novel in these terms. Which scenes would you point a seminar at (the Gallery, the deferral rumour, Kathy's years as a carer, the last conversation with Miss Emily and Madame), where does the lens genuinely illuminate a character's situation, and — importantly — where does the book resist the reading and a honest critic has to say so? Does the novel have any interest in the distinction at all, given how little resentment Kathy shows, or is it doing something the distinction can't see? And what, if anything, does it add that the bathtub cases can't?
