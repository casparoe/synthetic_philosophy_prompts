I am writing a novel in which a retired actuary, defending a lifetime of cautious decisions at a family dinner, argues that the preferences everyone cites as the Allais paradox are perfectly rational, and that the defect, if there is one, lies in decision theory rather than in her. I want the speech to sound shrewd rather than merely stubborn, which means I need to know exactly where it fails. As she sets it out:

(1) Expected utility theory has never required risk neutrality. It requires only that a person's ranking of gambles maximize the expectation of some utility function defined over outcomes.

(2) The Allais pattern — preferring a sure million to the gamble that trades one chance in a hundred of losing it for a ten percent shot at five million, while reversing that preference when no sure thing is on the table — contradicts the independence axiom only if the outcomes are taken to be bare sums of money.

(3) But an outcome, for the purposes of the theory, is whatever the agent cares about, and a complete description of one includes the agent's situation within the choice: whether she declined a certainty, whether a prize that was within her grasp will now have to be lived without, and so on.

(4) So the preferences are consistent with the theory after all. She is not choosing between a million and a gamble; she is choosing between a million taken and a gamble that, should it fail, leaves her with the knowledge that the million was hers to refuse. Her utility function is simply defined over those thicker outcomes, and no axiom is violated.

(5) Nor is there anything special about this case: for any pattern of choices among gambles whatsoever, one can find a sufficiently fine-grained description of the outcomes on which that pattern maximizes expected utility.

(6) Hence either the Allais preferences are no counterexample to expected utility theory, or the theory — and the independence axiom in particular — constrains nothing and is empty.

(7) Since the axiom plainly is not empty, and since her preferences are not unreasonable, something above is wrong.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I want the step named, an account of why it looked sound, and the price of each of the alternatives — in particular, what has to be held fixed, and fixed independently of the agent's preferences, for independence to be a substantive constraint rather than a tautology: the outcome space, the probabilities, the order in which each is specified. She can afford to lose almost any step but (4), since the speech is at bottom an insistence that what she was choosing between was never merely sums of money. If (4) is where the rot is, tell me what she would have to concede about herself in conceding it.
