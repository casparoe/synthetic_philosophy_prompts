I am assembling the 'map of positions' section for a graduate reading group on a single question: whether, and in what sense, the sciences carve nature at its joints. The group is half philosophers and half working scientists, and it meets without library or web access, so nothing in your answer should require looking something up and I do not want citations or suggestions for further reading. What I want from you is a decision tree that sorts a person into a named position by what they are willing to say about cases, not by which label they prefer.

My own sense is that the joints metaphor bundles at least four claims that usually travel together but should not, and I want your branch questions to prise them apart: (i) independence, that the structure is there apart from our interests, practices and classificatory devices; (ii) tracking, that some of our actual scientific classifications latch onto that structure, and that their success is explained by their doing so; (iii) uniqueness, that at a given domain and grain there is one best carving rather than many equally good cross-cutting ones; (iv) fertility, that membership in the kind licenses induction and supports counterfactuals, and that this is either constitutive of kindhood or merely good evidence for (i) and (ii). I also want one branch, somewhere in the tree, on grain: whether the same objects can be genuinely carved at several scales, so that a chemical, a cellular and a population-level classification are all correct rather than rivals.

The cases. Please anchor every question in one or two of these, and add at most two cases of your own if you think the tree needs them.

K1. Samples picked out by an ordinary procedure and traded, weighed and worked for centuries; all of them later turn out to share a single microstructural property, discovered long after the practice stabilized, and the inductions ordinary users relied on still mostly held.

K2. Two materials with different compositions and different crystal structures that have long been grouped under one name; the grouping supports some inductions and frustrates others.

K3. Two populations that look nearly identical, live together and interbreed freely in the laboratory, but turn out on whole-genome evidence to have exchanged no genes for a very long time; a third population that looks quite different turns out to be nested inside one of them.

K4. Samples classified as one chemical kind by a governing microstructural parameter; above a threshold of ambient pressure their behaviour diverges sharply, forming different compounds and differing in a property as basic as conductivity, while at ordinary pressures they are indistinguishable.

K5. A stretch of DNA that, depending on where transcription begins, yields two different products, and that also contains a sequence required for regulating a distant gene; several subfields each use the same term to pick out something different here, and each picks out something that supports the inductions its own subfield cares about.

K6. A diagnostic category with good inter-rater reliability and substantial heritability, no known mechanism, and no symptom shared by all its members; it predicts response to one intervention better than any alternative category does, but does not predict course or outcome.

Constraints on the instrument.

1. Give the tree as an indented plain-text list of branch questions with the leaves at the ends: seven branch questions, no more than twelve leaves, at least six of them occupied.
2. Build it from the logical space first. Show the combinations of answers your questions allow, then locate the actual positions in that space, then mark the unoccupied cells and say for each whether nobody is there because the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.
3. No question may name a position, an author, or a famous thought experiment by its nickname; and I do not want the three stock questions everyone asks, of the form 'are kinds mind-independent?', 'are there natural kinds?', 'is the classification merely conventional?', since they sort nobody. If you want a case of a duplicate that matches K1 in every observable respect but differs microstructurally, describe it in a sentence.
4. Each leaf must be a positive doctrine, not a refusal; no leaf may be defined only by what it denies. Among the leaves I expect microstructural essentialism of the Kripke–Putnam sort, homeostatic property cluster realism in Boyd's vein, promiscuous or pluralist realism associated with Dupré, an eliminativism about natural kinds of the sort Ereshefsky has defended for biology, a conventionalist or interest-relative pragmatism, a Millikan-style historical or etiological view, a Hacking-style looping or interactive account, a Lewis-style view on which naturalness comes in degrees, and a Khalidi-style causal-network view. Attach names to leaves only where you are confident they belong, and flag any label that different authors use differently.
5. For each occupied leaf, state which of K1 through K6 its holder is committed to counting as a real natural kind and which they must treat as a classification that merely serves us. This is what makes the leaves testable against each other.
6. Add a short answer key: the route through the tree to each leaf, plus one or two sentences that a holder of that leaf would endorse and that a holder of a neighbouring leaf would not. Note explicitly which of your questions are kind-general and which must be re-answered for each case: if the instrument turns out to have to be run separately for chemistry, for species, and for psychiatry, say so and say what that domain-relativity shows.

Two further things. First, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot really separate, and say whether the fault is in your questions or in the positions being genuinely hard to tell apart. Second, separate what is settled from what is contested: mark each placement of a named position at a leaf as uncontroversial or as itself disputed among people who work on this, say where a label is used ambiguously rather than silently picking one usage, and say explicitly where you are extrapolating beyond the literature. If one of my cases is underdescribed, say what further detail would flip the answer and which leaf it would move someone between. Do not present a contested reading as the consensus, and do not smooth over disagreements by describing them as merely verbal unless you can say what substantive question would remain if the labels were fixed.
