Hi — I need something I can actually hand to my assessors, not an essay.

I run eligibility for our county Home Support Grant (attendant care hours plus a small monthly equipment budget). Our statute qualifies someone who is "substantially unable to manage personal care without assistance." Assessors are community nurses who visit for about ninety minutes and fill in a twenty-item function form, each item 0–3, we total it, and the cut-off is 9. Nobody can tell me where 9 came from; I inherited it. We do roughly 400 assessments a month, I have to issue a written decision inside ten working days, and about a fifth of denials get appealed to a state hearing officer who wants reasons in writing and who will ask, in nearly every hearing, why the person in the file she is holding got the grant and my client did not. County counsel reads anything that looks like a policy change. My partner did a philosophy PhD, and when I complained about all this she said, oh, that's just vagueness, and there are two standard accounts: the epistemicist one where there really is a sharp line and we simply don't know where it is, and the supervaluationist one where there isn't a line until somebody draws one, and a claim only counts as definitely true if it comes out true on every acceptable way of drawing it. That sounded like it should change what I do. I cannot get from there to a form my nurses can fill in, and I don't want a procedure that is secretly my gut wearing a lab coat.

So write me the procedure. Specifically:

A numbered set of steps in plain language, in the order a reviewer runs them, no theory vocabulary, runnable in about fifteen minutes by someone with a two-page form and no philosophy. For each step say what counts as a sufficient answer and what happens when the reviewer can't get one. Mark where the procedure stops with a hard no on a gate that isn't about vagueness at all (residency, income cap, safety), and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts — my read is that how much weight the county's budget gets, and whether a daughter living forty minutes away counts as available assistance, are both value calls that should not be buried inside a scoring rule, but tell me if I'm wrong about which ones they are.

Then the part I actually care about. I want to see the two versions of the borderline step written out side by side and then told which one to put in the manual: (a) a robustness version, where we keep what I've started calling the sharpening book — the range of total scores that any reviewer or hearing officer has accepted as an admissible place to put the line, currently 7 through 12 — and we only act when every number in the book agrees; and (b) an expected-value version, where we treat the book as our uncertainty about a real line, put weights on the numbers, weigh the cost of a false grant against the cost of a false denial (a denied client who falls and ends up in hospital costs us more than the grant, which is the sort of thing my board will listen to), and spend assessment time narrowing the weights. Where do the two versions give different answers on the same file, and which one should I defend to counsel?

Two more things I need handled. First: the statute has several fuzzy bits — substantially, unable, personal care, without assistance — and I don't know whether we are supposed to sharpen each one separately or jointly. If someone is clearly unable without assistance but manages fine with two hundred dollars of grab rails, does that fall out differently depending on how we do it? That feels like it matters a lot and I've never seen it discussed. Second: how do we maintain the book? Who decides that 6 or 13 is even an admissible number, given that we can't appeal to something non-vague to settle it? And is a past grant evidence about where the line is, or an act of drawing it? That distinction decides what happens next, and it decides the actual sentence I write to the hearing officer.

Then run it on three real-ish files:

1. Total 14, well clear of every number in the book, but she manages fine as long as she has her shower chair and grab rails — a one-off equipment purchase. Her nurse scored what she can do unassisted. Does "without assistance" cover equipment, or is that a vague term nobody has ever sharpened, and what does the procedure do with a term that has no book at all?

2. Marta, 81, total 8, lives alone, early dementia, daughter forty minutes away and about to cut her hours, nurse notes "will need supervision within a year." Every number in the book is 7 to 12, so 8 is inside the gap. I have a feeling the honest procedure tells me to deny her, I would hate to sign that, and I want to see the argument happen rather than have it smoothed over.

3. Total 6, and last March we granted to someone at 6 in what the advocate says are identical circumstances, and she has already cited that file. Do I add 6 to the book? If I do, what stops it being 5 next month and 4 the month after? And what is the sentence in the decision letter explaining last March?

Finish with a short paragraph on what the procedure cannot do and which steps are judgment calls dressed up as steps, then — as the very last line, nothing after it — the three dispositions in exactly this shape: \answer{1=grant, 2=deny, 3=escalate}, with each slot filled by one of grant, deny, or escalate. Keep any theory appendix to a page; my nurses won't read more and neither will the hearing officer.
