I'm writing a piece on whether populism's appeal to 'the will of the people' is constitutively anti-expert or only contingently anti-expert, and I need a case built to some rather awkward specifications.

Please construct a scenario, realistic and at municipal or institutional scale — nothing I couldn't find in a mid-sized city, no sci-fi, no idealised assemblies — and short enough to state in one paragraph, in which the alignment everyone expects comes out inverted. The self-described populist, invoking the will of the people against unelected experts, should end up defending a policy that is a merely nominal proxy for what the people actually will, while the technocrat who says to defer to the experts is the one who best tracks it. The inversion has to fall out of the structure of the case rather than be stipulated by it, and it should be clear from the setup that (a) the people's substantive end is one thing and their belief about which policy achieves it is another, and (b) the experts' disagreement with them is purely instrumental — a dispute about the efficacy of means, not about whose values should win — and is conceded by both sides to be correct. So the case must not secretly turn on the experts knowing the people's values better than the people do, and must not lean on voter ignorance, disinformation, a badly worded ballot, low turnout, or a rigged consultation. Call the two views in play the say-so reading (the will of the people is whatever the people in fact demand) and the end-tracking reading (it is what they would demand, given what is true about available means); your scenario should put them on opposite sides of one decision, with the populist on the say-so side and the technocrat on the end-tracking side.

After the scenario, give me: what each actor says; which reading each is implicitly committed to and why they can't easily swap; the single feature of the case doing the work; and what happens if that feature is removed — I expect we collapse back into an ordinary populism-versus-technocracy standoff, but tell me whether that's right.

Two further things. The strongest populist reply I can anticipate is that the people's will includes the will that they be the ones who decide, so overriding the policy they named is disenfranchisement even when the experts are right about means — in which case the say-so reading isn't a fallback but the core of the matter, and the whole framing begs the question. Either close that off in the design and say how, or argue that it can't be closed off and say what that shows about my original question. And finally, be honest with me about whether all these constraints are jointly satisfiable; I'd much rather be told that two of them can't coexist than be handed a scenario that quietly cheats by sneaking in one of the excluded features.
