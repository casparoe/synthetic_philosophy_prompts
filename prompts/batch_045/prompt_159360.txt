I am writing a short position paper on hard choices for a value-theory reading group, and before I commit my views to paper I would like an audit of the set I have ended up with. I do not think I can locate the fault line myself, so I would rather not discover it in front of the group. Here is where I have landed:

1. Against a single scale. I deny that all values are measurable on one cardinal scale, and I reject cost-benefit commensuration as a complete account of practical reason. I am nevertheless happy to use it as a bounded heuristic inside a single institutional budget — hospital priorities, a department's hiring plan.

2. Parity rather than incomparability. In the paradigm hard choices — career, city, whom to marry — the options are on a par: comparable, but not as better, worse, or equally good. I reject the epistemic reading on which such choices are hard only because we lack information about ourselves or the future.

3. The small-improvement test, with a discontinuity. When A and B are on a par, some improvement to A makes A better than B — but it does not make A *just barely* better than B. Betterness arrives by a jump, not by degrees, and I take that discontinuity to be what separates parity from incomparability proper.

4. Voluntarism, with a qualifier. Nothing in the values settles a hard choice; the agent's will makes it the case that one option is to be pursued. This is a rational exercise of agency rather than an arbitrary lurch — though I also want to say the will can be exercised well or badly, responsively or capriciously, even when nothing determines its outcome.

5. Diachronic coherence. A life can contain a long series of hard choices, the resolutions can add up to a stable practical identity, and a later resolution can be criticised as unfaithful to an earlier one. To make this concrete: suppose that every three years I must decide whether to renew a demanding collaboration or wind it down, that on each occasion neither option is better, and that each time I resolve it by an act of will. Ten years on, I want to be able to say that the series as a whole hung together, or that it did not — not merely that each step was locally permissible.

6. The shadow option. After a hard choice is resolved, the unchosen option retains a normative presence; the residue of regret is fitting. I take the persistence of that presence as evidence for a mind-independent gap in the values rather than for a defect in my own willing.

7. Advice. I think it is perfectly rational to ask someone else what I should do and to let their answer shape my resolution, even though I also hold that the will's authority is irreducibly first-personal and cannot be delegated.

Please go through the pairwise relations among these seven and tell me which are in genuine tension and which only look as though they are. Rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say what exactly would have to be given up and what that would cost me elsewhere in the set. If a single revision would clean up more than one of them, say which. I am least confident about (5): it is the commitment I most need and the one I suspect my other commitments may not be able to support. Please be concrete about the mechanism of each tension rather than generically describing the dialectic, and keep the response an audit rather than a survey of the literature.
