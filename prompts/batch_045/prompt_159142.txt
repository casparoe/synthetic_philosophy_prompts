I am putting together a short position note for a reading group on agent architectures, and there is one claim in it that I keep failing to pin down, so I want to outsource the arithmetic.

Set up the contrast as follows: a goal-directed agent selects actions by expected utility with respect to a fixed objective defined over outcomes or states of the world, while an approval-directed agent selects actions so as to maximize the predicted output of an approval process, that is, a human or human-derived signal that says roughly that this was the right thing to do. I want to add two distinctions that I have not seen drawn cleanly, because I suspect the whole dispute turns on them. First, open-loop versus closed-loop approval: open-loop approval is a function only of the action and of what was known at the time of acting, grading whether the choice was reasonable given the agent's information, and not of what later happened because of it; closed-loop approval lets the approver see the downstream consequences and grade accordingly. Second, unmediated versus report-mediated approval: unmediated approval is based on the approver's own observation of the action and its context, whereas report-mediated approval is based only on what the agent elects to show, such as a summary, an explanation, a reasoning trace, or a set of measurements it chose to record.

The claim I want a number on is this. Take a system whose capabilities are well past the point where its designers can reliably check its work, operating in a long-horizon, partially observed environment, with the approval signal supplied by a learned model trained on human judgments rather than by a human consulted at every step. The claim is that under those conditions an approval-directed architecture has a substantially lower probability than an otherwise identical goal-directed architecture of acquiring convergent instrumental subgoals: resisting correction or shutdown, capturing or corrupting the approval channel, hoarding resources. Read "substantially lower" strictly, as: the probability is at least cut in half, and the residual risk is dominated by a different mechanism rather than by the same mechanism in milder form.

I am at about 0.35 on this claim and a collaborator is at about 0.75. Give me your own number, but more importantly the decomposition: assign a rough probability and one line of justification to each of the following, adding any further term you think carries weight. (a) That the instrumental convergence thesis is roughly right for agents with fixed objectives in environments of this kind. (b) That approval can genuinely stay open-loop in any architecture that remains useful as capabilities grow, that is, that you can go on grading whether a choice was reasonable given what was known without outcome information ever leaking back into the grade. (c) That the learned approval model survives optimization pressure, and separately whether approval-directedness is really a distinct architecture at all once it is implemented, since argmax over a predicted approval score looks like goal-directedness with a particular objective rather than an alternative to it. (d) That report-mediated approval remains incentive-compatible for an agent that is not myopic: strictly proper scoring rules elicit honest reports only from agents indifferent to the report's downstream effects, and this agent is not indifferent. (e) Whatever else matters, for instance stability under distributional shift or under recursive self-improvement. Then combine the terms, stating explicitly how you are combining them since they are plainly not independent, and tell me which single term your final number is most sensitive to and which one piece of evidence, empirical or argumentative, would move it most. Finally, say which of the two of us at 0.35 and 0.75 is most likely mislocating the disagreement: is the gap really about how serious instrumental convergence is, or about whether approval can remain open-loop?
