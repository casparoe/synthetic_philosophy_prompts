Two colleagues in my department's perception reading group spent most of last week's session arguing over a circulated preprint, and since I chaired the session I've been asked to say who had the better of it. I couldn't, and I'd rather not fake it.

The paper reports four things. (i) Participants were trained over roughly eight sessions, with corrective feedback, to sort a set of very similar desaturated hues into two novel categories; the boundary was placed arbitrarily within the physical colour space. (ii) After training they show the usual categorical-perception signature: pairs straddling the learned boundary are discriminated better than pairs equally far apart in colour space but falling inside one learned category — and they show it on a task where category membership is irrelevant, since they are only asked whether two successive patches are the same. (iii) The scalp-recorded response that distinguishes the two kinds of pairs appears inside the first 120 ms or so after stimulus onset, over occipital sites. (iv) The same pairs presented at 25–30° eccentricity, where no training was given and fine hue discrimination is poor, show no signature at all. And the result that split the room: when trained participants were moved into a block in which both sides of the boundary were mapped to the same response key and feedback was withdrawn, the signature collapsed within a single block, and reappeared when the original mapping was restored.

Their exchange, more or less as it happened:

(1) Nadia: This is cognitive penetration, and a strong case of it, because it meets the conditions the skeptics themselves have insisted on. The effect is acquired, so it depends on something the subject has learned rather than on the stimulus; it shows up early enough to be inside the processing that fixes perceptual content rather than in a later judgment; and it is automatic, in that the subject is not being asked about categories and the boundary intrudes anyway.

(2) Gil: It is perceptual learning, which is not the same thing. Even people who hold that early vision is informationally encapsulated allow that experience can retune early visual mechanisms. What is in dispute is whether an occurrent cognitive state — a belief, a goal, a desire — can alter, on a given occasion, the function that early vision computes. A rewired visual system is not a penetrated one; it is a different visual system. If your argument works, then the fact that a week in distorting goggles changes what you see counts as cognitive penetration, and nobody thinks that is what is at issue.

(3) Nadia: The last manipulation is there precisely to break that reply. The signature switches off and on again within about ten minutes, with the same retinae, the same hours of training and the same stimuli. Retuning does not behave like that. What changes is what the subject has been told, i.e. a cognitive state, and it changes processing early enough to matter. So this is online, state-dependent influence, not rewiring.

(4) Gil: What the subject is told changes the task, and task set works through attention. Attention is exactly what the encapsulation line said early vision is not subject to, in the sense that matters: attention selects which inputs a fixed early process operates on, it does not change the operation itself. So the collapse is predicted by my view too. And notice the bind you are now in. If attention counts as penetration, your thesis becomes trivial, since no one denies that attention is guided by beliefs and goals; if it does not, the manipulation shows nothing.

(5) Nadia: Two replies. First, the eccentricity result tells against a pure task-set story: the effect is confined to the region of the visual field where the learning could have occurred at all, which is what you would expect if the visual system itself has changed, and not what you would expect from a response bias applied uniformly to every stimulus. Second, "attention changes the selection but not the function" is stipulation dressed as architecture; there are familiar cases in which attention alters apparent contrast, apparent spatial frequency, apparent duration, and if appearance changes then something downstream of selection has been altered too.

(6) Gil: Those cases are contested, and several of them are plausibly response-level; but set them aside, because there is a more basic problem. Nothing in this data set picks out "the function computed by early vision" as opposed to "what the subject ends up responding to." Not latency, since early components can inherit late feedback; not automaticity, since a task-irrelevant effect can still be post-perceptual; not field specificity, since that is equally predicted by a spatially specific change in decision criteria. So this experimental program has no operational grip on the philosophical thesis. That does not refute the thesis; it means this kind of evidence cannot adjudicate it either way.

(7) Nadia: Then the thesis has been insulated rather than supported. If every candidate case can be reinterpreted downstream, the claim that early vision is penetrable is unfalsifiable in practice, and that should count against it. And the stakes are not merely taxonomic: if any of these effects are genuine penetration, then what a subject sees is partly fixed by what she believes, and experience can no longer play the epistemological role it is supposed to play — supplying warrant that is not itself inherited from belief. A position that survives by rendering every case inconclusive is in trouble, not in good shape.

(8) Gil: The epistemic conclusion is narrower than you are making it. Penetration by belief would be circular in the way you describe, but a change produced by training is a new disposition that can be perfectly reliable, and penetration by mood or desire need not connect experience to belief at all — so most of what this paper might show leaves the epistemology untouched. As for falsifiability: the claim is a modal one about what architecture permits. Demanding a decisive empirical falsifier for it misfires, in the way it would misfire for a claim about what is computationally possible.

(9) Nadia: Your central distinction between an occurrent cognitive state and a stored change in the system assumes that cognition is not stored in the visual system. But whether the learned boundary lives in a belief box or in the weights of early visual processing is an empirical and architectural question, and it is one your view needs, not one it is entitled to assume.

Please adjudicate this move by move. For each of the nine moves, say whether it is clearly right, right but doing less work than its speaker thinks, genuinely contested, or mistaken — and why. Then give a verdict on who is more right overall, and say what both of them are missing: in particular, which parts of this dispute are empirical, which are questions about how the thesis should be individuated, and which turn on what the thesis is supposed to be for. If you think the argument has reached a point where no available evidence could settle it, say so and say what that implies about how the question ought to be posed instead.
