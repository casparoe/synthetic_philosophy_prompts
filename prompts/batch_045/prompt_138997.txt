I'm writing a short paper for my epistemology seminar and I want to check one move before I build the rest of the argument on top of it. The view — I've been calling it externalist hinge theory, though I suspect someone has already used that name for something else — is that what makes a proposition a hinge for a person isn't its role in our practice of giving and asking for reasons but a causal-historical fact about how they came by it: you aquired it from other people as a child, you have never checked it, and it has never seriously been up for review. The nice consequence is that this turns hinge-hood into a plain empirical question, so a careful interview study could tell us which propositions are hinges for a given community, and since a hinge is by definition something that cannot be rationally doubted, that study would also tell us what is genuinely indubitable for them. My friend raises two objections I can't shake: first, that this just makes hinges into Descartes's foundations with the testimony of my parents standing in for clear and distinct ideas; second, that Wittgenstein would never grant that a survey could turn up a hinge. Is she right about either one? And if the second is right, what does settle which propositions are hinges, if not how we came by them?
