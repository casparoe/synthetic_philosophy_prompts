I sit on the appeals panel of a large persistent virtual world. Our remit is not to enforce the platform's terms of service but to decide, in disputed cases, whether a player's conduct did another player a real harm, as opposed to merely altering entries in a database; our only sanction is a public finding plus suspension, and we try hard to be consistent with our own findings.

Our findings so far:

Deleting another player's character, built up over four years of play, permanently and irreversibly: real harm.

Looting a rare item that its owner had saved up for months to buy from the platform's own market: real harm.

Reproducing another player's celebrated castle block for block in another region, against her express wish and to her considerable annoyance: not a real harm.

Body-blocking a player so that he could not reach a one-off, never-repeated event that he had learned of ten minutes earlier and had made no preparations for: real harm.

Destroying a memorial garden that other players built for a member of the community who died, none of whose relatives have ever entered the world: real harm.

Deleting, contents and all, the account of a player who had stopped playing years earlier, never returned and never found out: not a real harm.

Damaging an item that a player had received minutes earlier through a server bug, had never used and had been about to discard: not a real harm.

The case now before us: a player maintained, for three years, a hidden garden inside an instanced pocket of the world that no other player can enter and that she has never shown to anyone or mentioned in public. She assembled it in about ten minutes using an in-game building tool, but she visits it for an hour on most days and says it is the only thing that keeps her playing. Another player found an exploit, got into the instance and deleted it; the platform cannot restore it, its code says nothing about instanced content, and the community has no settled expectation either way, since until now nobody knew such pockets could be entered. She found out and was distraught.

Was a real harm done? I would like three things from you. First, set out the two or three rules about what makes a virtual object, or a virtual harm, real that fit all seven earlier findings about equally well, and say what each of them predicts here. Second, give your best guess at what we should find if we want to be consistent with ourselves, identifying which earlier finding is the closest analogue. Third, since I suspect our findings underdetermine this case, say what principle we would be committing ourselves to with each verdict, and which further case would best separate the surviving rules.
