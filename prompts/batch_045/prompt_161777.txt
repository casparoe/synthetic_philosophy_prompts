I'm on the hiring committee at the small company where I work and we just bought a resume-screening tool from a vendor. Their whole pitch in the demo was that the model is "blind to race and gender" — they strip those fields before training, so, their words, the model can't be biased. I'm the one who has to explain this to the rest of the commitee next week and I have a bad feeling about it.

I've been reading a bit about the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness — the ones where you can't satisfy all the natural fairness criteria at once once the groups differ in base rates — and what I want to bring to the meeting is a list of the different precise things "blind to race" could be claiming, with a verdict on each. The readings I can half-see: the attribute isn't in the input columns; the score is statistically independent of race; no function of the remaining features predicts race much better than chance; the score would have been the same if this applicant's race had been different, even holding fixed things like school, postcode and employment gaps that are themselves downstream of race; the model is blind but the human reading the score isn't; the training target is clean — i.e. the historical "who got hired" labels it learns from aren't themselves race-affected; and blindness as a moral/legal claim that nobody decided "because of" race.

Sharpen those, add the ones I'm missing, and for each say whether it's trivial, whether it's substantive, whether it's actually achievable and checkable, and whether it gets the vendor the conclusion they want. Which readings run straight into the impossibility results, and which pairs of readings are quietly incompatible with each other in a way I should point out? And is there a reading under which deleting the column makes outcomes worse for the groups it's meant to protect? I'd rather bring a menu than freeze on one reading and argue the wrong one.
