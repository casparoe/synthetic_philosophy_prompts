I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for well over a decade, and there the socialist calculation debate is almost always invoked in one direction only — as a settled refutation, compressed into a slogan about prices carrying information that no planner could ever gather. I'd like to stress-test that compression against a concrete case rather than argue about it in the abstract. Please answer in British English, and please don't give me references, a reading list, or citations: I want the argument, not the sources.

The case is the semiconductor shortage of 2020–2023. Take the following as given rather than as claims to check: as car demand collapsed in early 2020, vehicle makers cancelled foundry bookings that had been committed months ahead, while demand for PCs, consoles and other consumer electronics rose sharply; capacity had already been allocated, so those cancelled slots went elsewhere and were not easy to reclaim. Lead times for cheap microcontrollers went from weeks to many months. Assembly plants making relatively low-margin parts were idled for want of a few dollars' worth of silicon. Spot and grey-market prices for some parts rose by multiples of list price. Buyers responded by ordering the same parts from several distributors at once, so that order books came to overstate real demand and then collapsed. Scarce capacity was allocated not by any open market but through a mixture of internal committees inside suppliers, long-term contractual claims, customer tiering, and informal or explicit auction-like arrangements at the margin. Governments eventually answered with large subsidy programmes for domestic fabrication.

I want two things done with this.

First, stop treating "the calculation argument" as one claim. As I read it, at least five separable theses get bundled together in the usual telling, and I want them kept apart: (a) the accounting thesis — that without markets in capital goods and factors of production there is no common, cardinal unit in which heterogeneous inputs can be compared, so a plan cannot be checked for waste even in principle; (b) the epistemic thesis — that the relevant knowledge is dispersed, local, partly inarticulable and constantly changing, and that prices transmit a drastically compressed summary of it; (c) the discovery or adaptation thesis — that the hard problem is not computing an allocation at a moment but tracking and discovering change, which is a different task from solving a static optimisation; (d) the market-simulation reply associated with Lange, in which a planning board announces parametric prices, instructs managers to follow cost-minimising rules, and iterates by trial and error, with the advertised bonuses that externalities are internalised and the income distribution is chosen; and (e) the incentive-and-selection thesis — residual claimancy, loss-bearing, bankruptcy, and entry and exit as a filter on plans, which is arguably a different argument from any of the others. Correct me if the taxonomy is wrong or if some of these collapse into each other.

Second, map the case onto those theses and report what breaks. The mapping is not obvious, and that is most of what I am interested in. There is no central planning board here: the nearest analogues to a planner are the internal capacity-allocation processes of a foundry and of a vehicle manufacturer, and both are hierarchical allocations of a shared scarce resource to competing internal uses, conducted with little or no internal price. Those processes did not visibly collapse; the shortage was absorbed by a mixture of internal allocation, long-term contracting, queueing, tiering and price spikes at the margin. So: who is the "planner" for the purposes of each thesis, what are the options each actor actually faced, and what would count as evidence for or against each thesis in this episode? And then the reciprocal question, which I care about more: does the case confirm the calculation argument, refute it, or fall into a region the argument was never about?

Let me add one idea of my own that I want you to use and, if it is wrong, to break. Call it the embedded-planning thesis: hierarchical allocation succeeds only when it is nested inside an external price system that supplies it with two things it cannot generate itself — a unit of account and set of opportunity costs, and an external selection mechanism, since the outputs of the internal plan are ultimately graded by a market the plan does not control. On this reading the shortage shows planning working, but working parasitically: internal allocation decisions tracked external spot and grey-market prices, and suppliers' allocation choices were disciplined by their own profits and by the credible threat that customers would redesign around them or dual-source. If that is right, then the calculation argument has quietly been converted from a claim about computation or about information into a claim about nesting and discipline, and it says considerably less than rationalists usually suppose about very large islands of planning — including, possibly, a hypothetical fully socialised economy that retained some external benchmark. Tell me whether the case supports this restatement, whether it is really just the incentive-and-selection thesis in disguise, and what would have to be true for it to be false.

Two further wrinkles I want handled rather than smoothed over. One: the double-ordering episode looks to me like a control-theoretic failure — delayed feedback plus a gain above one producing oscillation in the order book, the familiar bullwhip pattern — rather than a failure of information aggregation or of calculation. Is that a genuine blind spot in the classical argument, or does the discovery thesis already cover it under another name? Two: allocation in this case was allocation by willingness to pay, which is exactly what the theory predicts, and a great deal of public anger was directed at that fact — ventilator and medical-device makers competing with consumer electronics for the same parts. Say plainly whether the theory has anything to say about that dimension, or whether legitimacy and need are simply outside its scope, and note if the case therefore gives any support to the distributional and externality half of the market-socialist reply.

Throughout, I would rather you said "this thesis is silent here" than stretched the case to fit it, and I would like you to flag explicitly which of the five theses the episode genuinely discriminates between and which it leaves untouched.
