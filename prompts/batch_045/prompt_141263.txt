My friend Marit is writing a dissertation on natural kinds and keeps a running list she calls the joints list — candidate kinds from the sciences, each marked in or out, updated after every reading group. I've got a small bet with her about what she'll do with five cases I'm springing on her next week, so what I want is predictions, not a tutorial.

Her verdicts so far.

In: gold (the element, not the colour), water, mass, charge. Tiger, but only as a lineage — her line is that there history does the work an essence was supposed to do. And, suprisingly, carburetor, which she deffends on the grounds that carburetors are wildly various but the job they do is real.

Refused: any general verdict on species. She's a pluralist about species concepts but insists tiger is in.

Out: jade. Weed (her note: the cluster is real, we just picked it out because it annoys us). The green-before-2050 / blue-after style of predicate. Raven-or-writing-desk. Money (too much of it is in the head). Schizophrenia as the diagnostic manual picks it out — a committee's compromise with several different mechanisms behind it. And planet in the official sense, with the clearing-the-neighbourhood clause; that's the verdict she's wobblest on, she's said twice she might flip it.

The five cases:

1. Systems in the same universality class. Near a continuous phase transition, systems with completely different microphysics — liquid helium, a ferromagnet at its Curie point, and, if a preprint I half-read is to be believed, a flock of starlings right at the onset of collective motion — share a set of critical exponents that depend only on the dimension of space and the symmetry of the order parameter. Those shared exponents let you predict quantitative details of systems nobody has built yet. No shared microstructure, no common ancestry, no mechanism holding the cluster together.

2. Systems that implement an optimal recursive estimator. A modelling group reports that a moth's auditory interneuron, a bacterium's chemotaxis network, and the inventory controller in a warehouse all approximate the same recursive estimator to within noise, and that one set of error-covariance equations predicts the behaviour of all three better than any mechanism-specific model does. These systems have never been in causal contact with each other.

3. Kinds defined by an absence: the knockout mouse line, or more generally lacks gene product G. Defined by something not being there, and yet among the most projectible kinds in the life sciences — thousands of reproducible generalizations, stable across labs.

4. Invasive species, in the sense agencies actually use it: non-native, introduced by humans, and causing harm. Note that the harm clause is doing real work and that the members have nothing in common by descent.

5. Short one: reptile in the old paraphyletic sense — snakes, lizards, turtles, crocodilians, birds excluded.

What I want from you: for each case, her verdict, the rule that generates it, and the rules that fit her old verdicts just as well but come apart here. I care less about which philosophy of natural kinds is true than about what she is actually running — including whether it's one rule across all the sciences or a different one for chemistry, biology and the human stuff, and which of her existing verdicts is the load-bearing one she'd have to give up if I pushed her. Also: money is out but carburetor is in, and I don't think she's noticed how hard that pair is; say what has to be true for both to stand. Finally, if you were designing a sixth case purely to split the two rules that survive, what would it be?
