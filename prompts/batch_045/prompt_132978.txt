Construct a thought experiment for an upper-level seminar on the metaphysics of pleasure, designed to isolate the disagreement between three accounts of what pleasantness is. Take the accounts as follows: the single-quality view, on which pleasantness is one and the same introspectible felt quality wherever it appears, varying only in intensity and duration; the attraction view, on which an experience is pleasant to the degree that the subject, in having it, is drawn to it for its own sake and disposed to sustain or return to it, so that pleasantness tracks the pull rather than the feel; and the cluster view, on which pleasant experiences form a loose family (relief, sensory delight, absorption, amusement) sharing no felt quality and no single attitude, and pleasantness is at most a family-resemblance or role property.

Build the case so that three measures of pleasure which normally move together come apart. Let there be two experiences, E and F, in the life of one ordinary adult on a single day. Hold fixed their duration, the subject's background mood and fatigue, the absence of any further consequences attaching to either, and her cold retrospective verdict: some time later, from memory alone, she rates them identically, is genuinely indifferent about which to repeat, and cannot tell the two memories apart with respect to how good they were. Now cross the other two measures: E has an unmistakable, localisable pleasant feel yet almost no pull, so she stops without reluctance when interrupted; F has no pleasant feel she can locate at the time, only absorption, yet resents interruption and would rearrange her day to have it again. Keep the whole scenario to one paragraph, use no drugs, no neurology and no fictional apparatus, and rest nothing on empirical findings about wanting versus liking, since I do not want the case to turn on contested psychology.

Then show that the case works. For each of the three accounts, state what it implies about whether E and F contain the same amount of pleasure, or about whether that question has a determinate answer at all, and say which of the three measures the account treats as constitutive of pleasantness and which as merely good evidence of it. The verdicts must fall out of the accounts as I have stated them rather than be stipulated, and no account may be handed a premise its defenders would reject. Identify the single feature of the case doing the work, and say what happens to each verdict if that feature is removed. Finally, say which account the case leaves looking worst and give the strongest available reply on its behalf, including the reply that one of my measures was never tracking pleasantness in the first place.

Throughout, distinguish what is settled in this literature from what is genuinely contested among people who work on it, flag any formulation of an account that some of its defenders would disown, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. Please write in British English.
