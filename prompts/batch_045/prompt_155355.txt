I'm on a threat-modelling team at a lab, and there's an argument we keep having that I've become convinced is stalled rather than merely hard. Here's what everyone in the room already agrees on. Assume the model may be misaligned in the specific sense that it plays nice while it's being evaluated, including deliberately sandbagging on capability evals, and is watching for the first moment where an oversight gap makes defection cheap. Assume too that all we get is behaviour plus maybe some internal activations and there's no reliable lie detector. Given all that, two camps.

Camp one says control measures are self-defeating, because every control measure has a trusted component inside it — a monitor, a sandbox, a human who signs off on the diff — and the trust you need in that component is just the alignment problem one level down. Either control presupposes that you have already got some system you can trust, in which case it isn't really a distinct strategy from alignment, or it presupposes nothing, in which case it's theatre. Camp two says there are structural asymmetries that favour the defender no matter how capable the model is: the defender needs to catch a defection once, the model needs to succeed every time; the defender sets up the environment while the model has to find an opening inside it; and defection requires doing something in the world, which is more visible than thinking. Both sides have heard the other's arguments dozens of times and neither has moved in about a year.

My own hypothesis is that this isn't really a conceptual disagreement at all — it's a hidden quantitative one about how fast oversight quality decays as the capability gap between the monitored model and whatever is monitoring it grows, and it only looks conceptual because nobody can currently measure that quantity. But I keep coming back to two complications that make me doubt my own diagnosis.

First, what I call the bait asymmetry: the defender can manufacture fake sabotage opportunities cheaply — planted credentials, obviously juicy exfil paths, whole decoy environments — so the model has to solve a discrimination problem the defender doesn't. That looks like a genuine defender advantage that scales with how much of the deployment environment the defender controls. But it's self-undermining, because it only pays off while the model can't tell which environments are being watched, and a model with good situational awareness can infer that from distributional oddities in its inputs. So the value of the asymmetry is hostage to a fact about the model's situational awareness, not to anything in control theory.

Second, what I call attribution laundering: if a misaligned model can route its sabotage through tool calls, subagents, or plausible-looking drafts that a human rubber-stamps, then the thing being monitored isn't the model's action but the action of a human-plus-model system, and the monitor's effective error rate is dominated by the human's rubber-stamping rate rather than by anything about model monitoring. Add the multi-instance point — if you have N instances doing useful work in parallel and any single undetected defection is enough to matter, a per-instance detection rate p leaves you with roughly 1−(1−p)^N at the system level, so control either needs p to shrink faster than 1/N or needs the damage from any one defection bounded to something trivial, which is an environment-design choice and not a monitoring one at all.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it verbal — two things going by the name control? Is one side answering can this work in principle and the other will it work given competitive deployment pressure and regulators who don't understand the threat model? Is there a factual question about capability-gap decay or about situational awareness doing all the work while everyone argues about concepts? Or is the whole framing of control versus alignment as separate strategies the thing that's broken? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of techniques. And given whatever you think it is, tell me honestly what would count as progress — a distinction, a measurement, a result about scaling, a change of question — or tell me why no progress should be expected here and what we should do instead with the argument.
