I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy — I work mostly on formal epistemology and philosophy of probability — and I've been asked to run a one-off 75-minute session for a mixed reading group: about 14 people, mostly philosophy grad students plus a few from CS, and no shared background beyond intro logic and a bit of probability. The topic is logical uncertainty: uncertainty about claims that logic or arithmetic has already settled, and the logical-induction-flavoured idea that what makes a believer good isn't that it started from the right prior but that no computable trading strategy could make unbounded money off it.

I want a game, not a lecture. Design a prediction market they actually play in the room: participants trade shares on sentences whose truth values are fixed by math but that nobody in the room can settle by heart. No real money — points, or something like "winner picks next week's reading."

What I want them to run into, in this order, before anyone names anything:

1. That they have genuine, graded, non-0.5 uncertainty about things that are already decided by logic.
2. That the coherence constraints (price of P plus price of not-P equals a share; price of P plus price of "if P then Q" at least the price of Q) are things they get *pushed* into by losing points, not axioms they're handed.
3. That sitting at 0.5 on everything is not the safe neutral option — a player who just does the damn computation will take their money. Being uncertain is only permitted for as long as you can't compute your way out.
4. That a market can stop losing money without anybody having the "right" prior — lots of different price paths are equally unexploitable. The no-exploitation ideal is a structural, ratcheting virtue; it doesn't tell you what to believe.

Concretely, please give me:

- The sentences: 6–8 candidates that are (a) settled by finite computation, (b) not knowable from memory by a numerate adult, (c) actually settleable within the session if someone uses a laptop or ten minutes with pencil and paper. Tell me how each one gets settled at reveal time, and flag any you think someone in the room might just happen to know.
- Roles and rules, including a rotating "exploiter" role: a participant with their own bankroll whose only allowed moves are combination trades against incoherent posted prices (buying P and not-P when they sum above a share, buying P and "if P then Q" while selling Q, and so on) plus straight bets against prices whenever they personally can compute the truth. I want an exploit ledger on the board so the group can stare at their own pattern of losses and work out the rule that's bleeding them.
- Materials, and a payoff scheme with no money changing hands.
- The one-page rules handout as the participants would actually read it — around 150 words — since I can't explain anything up front and they shouldn't hear "Dutch book," "coherence," or "logical induction" until the debrief.
- A minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes, including how reveal rounds are staged so there's no last-mover free lunch.
- The debrief: map specific moments and specific rows of the ledger onto the four points above, and say which of the four the game establishes best.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time: someone who happens to know a fact, prices frozen because nobody wants to trade first, the exploiter turning into a market maker, confusion about the conditional being material implication, someone betting their whole stack on a hunch, the CS students computing everything in two minutes and ruining round one — and what I do about each.
- An honest note on what the game cannot reproduce and I'll have to just say out loud: the computability and time-budget constraints on traders, the fact that the criterion is asymptotic, and the fact that all the guarantees are relative to how strong the revealer is, so that anything the revealer never settles can be priced arbitrarily without anyone being able to exploit it.

Finish the whole thing with a single line \answer{...} containing a short phrase — under twelve words — for the one thing you most want them walking out having felt rather than been told.
