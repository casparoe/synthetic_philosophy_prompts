I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar titled Chance, Law and Prediction: 18 students, mixed majors (philosophy, physics, economics, one or two from engineering), one 80-minute session, a projector and a whiteboard, no laptops, and no budget beyond printed sheets and index cards. Half the class has no mathematics past high-school algebra, and I would like the activity to work for them too.

I want a single session's activity that makes students feel, rather than being told, the gap between saying that a system runs according to deterministic laws and saying that anyone can predict what it will do next — and I want them to run into the gap through their own choices and their own losses, not by watching a simulation on my screen.

Here is what I want them to end up having seen for themselves:

1. A system can be fully law-governed and still defeat any forecaster whose knowledge of the present is stated to finitely many digits.
2. The loss of foresight is a tradeoff rather than a cliff: each additional step of forecast lead time costs a fixed multiplicative factor of precision in the starting value, so no attainable measurement buys unlimited foresight — while better instrumentation is not worthless either, it merely buys lead time at a terrible exchange rate.
3. Possessing the true rule is not the same as possessing the prediction. I want one student, playing an ideally-informed predictor who is handed the exact updating rule and the starting value to six decimal places plus a calculator, to fail anyway, in front of everyone.
4. There is a further question that a finite-precision stretch of data cannot settle: whether the irregular series they are looking at came from a deterministic rule at all. I would like a phase in which two black boxes emit series that look statistically alike — one generated by a deterministic rule, one by coins, dice, or a random-number app — and the students stake points on which is which.

Please design the activity accordingly. I need: the rules; the materials (what I print in advance); a wagering or scoring scheme that requires no real money, is keepable on index cards, and punishes overconfident bidding — something in the spirit of a scoring rule that charges you for the confidence you did not earn; a minute-by-minute timeline; and the reveal, meaning the moment the two-line rule that generated all the data goes on the board next to a table showing two runs from starting values that differ only in the eighth decimal place.

Then the debrief. Map specific moments in the activity onto each of the four points above, and say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. My worries include: that students will conclude the system simply is random; that they will think a faster computer or more data would fix everything; that they will discount the whole thing because the data came from my laptop; that the ideally-informed student's failure will be read as personal carelessness rather than as the point; and that someone will announce that determinism is therefore unfalsifiable, which I do not want to endorse either. Give me a response to each.

Finally, be frank about what the activity cannot deliver. My sense is that at least three things have to be supplied by me in so many words: that the unpredictability on display is an artifact of the precision and tolerance I chose rather than a discovery about the world; that the black-box phase illustrates underdetermination at a fixed resolution and does not show that deterministic structure is undetectable in principle, since there are techniques for finding structure in longer and cleaner series, and I want a prepared answer for a sharp student who raises this; and that nothing done in the room bears on whether the actual world is deterministic or on what any of it implies for free will. Tell me which moves I must make by lecture, and where.

No lecture up front, and the word for sensitive dependence should not be spoken until the debrief. Close with a short written exit ticket I can use to check which of the four points actually landed.
