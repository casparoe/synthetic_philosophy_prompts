Dear Colleague,

I am rewriting the moral psychology half of our second-year ethics module, and I should be grateful for your judgement on a point that I had taken to be settled, but which now seems to me considerably less straightforward than I had assumed.

I had understood the situationist literature — the obedience studies, the Good Samaritan studies, the work on incidental mood and helping, and the use Harman and Doris make of it — to have established something quite strong: that what an agent does on a particular occasion is determined almost entirely by features of the situation, and that the ascription of stable traits of character is a systematic error, the projection of an imaginary unity onto conduct that is really cued by local circumstances. If that is right, it seems to me that the honest course is to stop presenting the excellences of character as a normative ideal, and to teach instead a kind of situational ethics: identify the variables that actually move conduct, and then arrange our environments so that people behave decently.

A junior colleague resists this. She says that I am running together two claims which the careful literature keeps apart — the claim that conduct is sensitive to situational variables, and the claim that traits of character do not exist — and she proposes a "restricted" virtue ethics in their place: a virtue is not a global trait but a localised reliability, a stable disposition to act well in situations of a precisely specified type, keyed to exactly the variables the social psychologists manipulate. Such dispositions, she argues, are precisely what the experiments cannot exclude; so the situationist critique has no purchase upon them, and the whole dispute turns out to be merely verbal — a disagreement about how broad a disposition has to be before it merits the name of character.

I find this attractive, but I suspect that it may be a retreat. Which of us has the better of the argument? Specifically: is situational sensitivity in the data any evidence at all against the existence of traits, given that a disposition is by its very nature something whose manifestation depends upon conditions? If the critique succeeds, does it tell against the normative claims of virtue ethics, or only against our ordinary descriptive claims about who possesses the virtues? And does my colleague's proposal really answer the critique, or does it purchase immunity by surrendering what was distinctive — the thought that a virtue is worth having for its own sake, that possessing one makes an agent's conduct intelligible, that the virtues hang together?
