i'm a pharmacy tech and i got into an argument with a coworker about whether painkillers actually take the pain away or just make you stop caring about it, which sent me down a rabbit hole and now i've built myself an argument that i'm pretty sure is broken somewhere but i can't see where. Setting it up: i ended up reading about a condition called pain asymbolia (i think thats the spelling) where people can tell you exactly where a pinprick is, how strong it is on the usual 0-10 scale, and whether it's stabbing or burning, and they'll still yank their hand away, but they say it doesn't bother them, they aren't upset by it, they don't want it to stop, and they don't ask for anything. Here's what i built out of that:

(1) Pain has two separable pieces: a sensory piece (where it is, what quality it has, how intense you'd rate it) and an "affective" piece, the bit that's a matter of how much it bothers you / how awful it is to be in it.
(2) Asymbolia shows the two really can come apart in real people: full sensory piece, no affective piece. And whatever exactly the asymbolic person has, it isn't bad for them — there's nothing there that needs fixing, and giving them something for it would have no point.
(3) So all of a pain's badness lives in the affective piece, and the sensory piece contributes exactly zero. On this picture an asymbolic 9/10 is less bad than an ordinary 2/10, even though the first one has way more of the sensory stuff.
(4) Now: what is that affective piece? The only way i can cash it out is as motivation — the pain bothers you just in case, while you're in it, you want it to stop and you're inclined to do something to end it. Fittingly, the asymbolic person has none of that. So: how bad a pain is = how strongly you want it gone. Call it the "want it gone" theory.
(5) But nothing in that theory cares where the wanting comes from. So take two people with the identical sensation, same intensity, same everything on the sensory side, where one of them has some outside reason to want it gone — say she's been told that if she's still in pain in five minutes she'll say something that'll wreck her marriage. By (4) her pain is now much worse than the other person's, even though the two feel exactly the same. Flip it around and give someone a reason to want the pain to keep going while it feels identical, and by (4) the pain stops being bad, or turns positive.
(6) And here's a made-up case i keep chewing on, call it mirror asymbolia: everything the asymbolic person is missing, this person has, and everything they have, she's missing. No location, no stabbing or burning, no number on the scale, nothing to describe — but she's miserable and begs you to make it stop. If (3) and (4) are right then what she's got is exactly as bad as ordinary pain and we owe her the same urgent treatment, but a painkiller has nothing to work on, since by hypothesis there's no sensation there at all, only the wanting. That can't be the right way to describe what's wrong with her.
(7) So something in (1)-(4) is off. (2) and (3) feel like they just fall out of the actual asymbolia cases and (1) feels like plain common sense, so i'd rather drop (4) — except i have no idea what the affective piece is supposed to be if it isn't a desire for the pain to stop, and if i say "it's a feeling," i don't see how a feeling is the kind of thing that makes a state bad for you rather than just being one more thing you're sensing.

Which step do you think i should give up, and what does giving it up cost me? And if your answer is (4): what is the affective piece, really, and how would i ever tell whether a drug or a brain lesion took away the piece rather than just taking away the wanting — because if there's no way to tell from the outside, then the "want it gone" theory and the theory that denies it aren't actually two different theories, and i'm stuck. (A friend said pain isn't a sensation at all but more like an order your body issues, roughly "get rid of this," and another said the sensory piece isn't irrelevant, just not enough on its own — i don't know if either of those helps, but feel free to use them if they do.)
