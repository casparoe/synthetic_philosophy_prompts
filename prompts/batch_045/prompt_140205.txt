I'm supposed to be writing a 3,000-word essay for my final-year philosophy of action course, and I've got it into my head that free will might be best understood negatively, as the absence of sphexishness — the word from the digger wasp Sphex who, as the story goes, paralyses a cricket, drags it to her burrow, leaves it on the doorstep while she pops inside to check the nest over, comes back out and hauls it in, and if you nudge the cricket a few inches while she's in there she'll drag it back to the threshold, go in and inspect again, come back out, and so on, apparently without end. My supervisor says I'm using "sphexish" as a sneer and that I ought to say what a theory of sphexishness would actually have to deliver before I try to hang an essay on it. Fine. So: write me the specification. The job I want the notion to do is specific — it has to be the sort of thing such that being non-sphexish is at least a necessary condition on acting freely, and I dont need it to be sufficient, and I don't need it to settle anything about determinism. Given that job, what does an adequate account have to get right?

I want the requirements, each with the case or worry behind it, and I want you to sort them into the ones that are genuine adequacy conditions on any account meant to do this job and the ones that are just the preferences of one camp dressed up as requirements. Things I think belong on the list somewhere, but you decide what's really load-bearing and add what I've missed: the difference between a behaviour being rigid and it being reliable (the wasp's routine works fine in the environment that shaped it, and my flatmate's insistence on re-running the same washing-machine cycle is annoying but not a failure of agency); the difference between an agent that can't do otherwise and one that could but for whom the alternative isn't worth the computation; rigidity at one timescale being flexibility at another (learning, meta-learning, the fact that a habit can be both the thing that frees you up and the thing that traps you); the difference between the routine being fixed and the agent being fixed about which routine to run; and above all the worry I keep circling, that sphexishness can't just be predictability, because on most views a perfectly rational agent responding perfectly to the reasons is the most predictable thing in the world and we don't want to call her a wasp. I also want a verdict on a couple of specific suspicions of mine. First: that "sphexishness has to be a property of the mechanism, not of the environment" and "sphexishness has to track whether the behaviour still serves the point of the routine in the actual situation" are quietly incompatible, because whether a fixed routine is rigid or merely well-calibrated seems to depend on the distribution of situations the system was shaped for. Second: that the requirement which actually does the most work in ruling accounts out is the one about intervention spaces — you only ever get evidence of sphexishness by perturbing something, and which perturbations you allow decides the verdict in advance, so unless an account says something principled about the space of interventions it's rigged. Flag any other pair you think can't be jointly met; that's the part of the answer I care about most, since if the list is itself impossible that's an essay in its own right.

Then — and I mean a paragraph, not a scorecard, I'll do the marking up myself — how these three rough ways of cutting the space do against the list: sphexishness as insensitivity to the goal-relevant counterfactuals (would the agent still have got what it was after if things had been slightly different?); sphexishness as cheapness, i.e. the routine running on a very short leash of inputs and computation; sphexishness as the absence of anything internal that models the situation, so the system is stuck reacting to cues rather than to what the cues indicate.
