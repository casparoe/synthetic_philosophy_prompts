I am writing the final paper for an advanced undergraduate seminar on philosophical logic and epistemology. One question is to be on Lewis Carroll's 1895 *Mind* dialogue in which the Tortoise accepts the premises of a simple modus ponens argument and the corresponding hypotheticals, yet refuses to accept the conclusion, thereby generating a regress of ever longer conditionals. I need the question itself, a model answer, and a marking scheme, and I need them built so that the question discriminates rather than invites recapitulation.

The failure mode I want to design against is the single-paragraph answer that says premises are propositions while rules of inference are rules, so the Tortoise's demand rests on a category mistake and the regress never gets started. That move is worth some credit, but on its own it should land a student in the middle of the second-class band, not the first. So the question has to go somewhere that move does not reach.

Three cruxes, at minimum, should be unavoidable:

First, the relocated regress. Achilles abandons conditionals and instead offers the Tortoise a rule: from A and if-A-then-Z, infer Z. The Tortoise replies that he can see what the rule says without seeing why he should follow it, and asks for a reason. Students should have to say whether the premise/rule distinction answers this demand or merely relocates it, and they should have to distinguish the circularity involved in arguing for the rule by using it from the circularity involved in adding the rule's own statement to the premise set. A top answer will say what kind of non-question-begging support a rule could in principle receive and why that support looks different from support for a proposition.

Second, a stipulated agent. Define in the question an agent who is 'conditionally saturated': he accepts every true conditional you present, including the one linking his total evidence to the conclusion, but never performs the transition to the conclusion. Students should have to say whether such an agent is irrational, in what the defect consists if it is one, and whether the defect is a defect of justification or of something else — agency, inference, or the application of a rule to a case. This is meant to isolate the difference between the regress of justification and the regress of application.

Third, a generalization test. Have students reconstruct the Tortoise's style of demand against a norm that is not deductive — a probabilistic coherence or updating norm, say — and then ask them whether the demand generalizes. If it does, is that a reductio of the demand, a reason to think the problem was never specifically about deduction, or an interesting result about which norms can be self-enforcing? The question should force a verdict here rather than permitting the comfortable conclusion that the matter is unsettled.

Requirements for the deliverable:

The question text must be self-contained — any symbolism or invented notion defined inside it, no named author or work required to answer it, nothing that would require a student to look anything up. It may be lettered into parts or posed as one continuous question, whichever makes the marking cleaner.

The model answer should be roughly 700 to 900 words, in continuous prose, and should take a definite position on each crux rather than surveying options and suspending judgment.

The marking scheme should total 25, broken into five named must-hit points with a mark allocation each, and should include for each point a one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong. It should also state explicitly, in two or three sentences, what separates a first-class answer from an upper-second answer and what caps a purely recapitulative answer.
