hi — i watched a youtube video about Fitchs paradox of knowability for my philosophy club thing and i want to know how much of it i should actually repeat out loud. Roughly what the guy says is:

Anti-realists are stuck with the knowability principle: every truth could in principle be known. So take some truth nobody happens to know — like, whether the number of grains of sand on a certain beach at noon today is even. Call it p. Then 'p and nobody knows p' is a truth as well, so the principle says it has to be knowable too. But it cant be: the second you come to know it youve come to know p, so it stops being true that nobody knows p. The sentence destroys itself in the act of being known. Since you can do that with any p, the only way to keep the principle is to say there are no unknown truths at all, i.e. everything true is already known by somebody, which is obviously nuts. So the principle dies and anti-realism about truth goes down with it. And the usual fix people try — restricting the principle to truths that dont mention knowledge — is just cheating, because given any truth p you can always build 'p and nobody ever knows p' and run the whole argument again, so the restriction doesnt actually save you.

Two things bugged me. He never says what 'in principle' means — knowable by a human given unlimited time, or by some possible creature in some possible world — and that feels like it might be where the real disagreement is? And my friend says the anti-realist can just drop the principle and admit some truths are unknowable, so at worst the paradox kills one version of their view, not anti realism as such. Also I dont get how the example even gets going when we dont know whether the grains-of-sand thing is unknown.

So: which parts of that would someone who actually works on this accept, which are the sneaky bits, and is my friend right that it only refutes one formulation? And is the 'you can always rerun it' move fair to the people who restrict the principle, or is it more of a debating point?
