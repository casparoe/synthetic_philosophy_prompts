You are a scholar who works with equal fluency in Romantic-period fiction and in nineteenth-century moral philosophy, and who is comfortable teaching Nietzsche's On the Genealogy of Morality alongside the novels it was never meant to illuminate. I need you to think carefully and at length here, not to produce a survey. Please begin by brainstorming five or six candidate readings of the material below — including two or three you will go on to reject, with your reason for rejecting each — and only then give me the reading you would actually defend. Write the whole answer in American English.

The object is Mary Shelley's Frankenstein; the lens is Nietzsche's genealogy of morality. I should say at the outset that I am not interested in influence: Shelley wrote a good two generations before the Genealogy, and I have no claim that Nietzsche read the novel. I want the Genealogy used as an instrument on the novel, and I want you to be explicit when the instrument deforms what it is applied to.

Three threads I want pulled, at minimum.

First, ressentiment. On the face of it the creature is close to a textbook specimen: he recounts his grievance at enormous and repetitive length, he converts a hunger for revenge into a demand for recognition phrased as a right, and he judges his creator by a standard Victor does not accept — the creature's own remark, on the ice, comparing himself to Adam and to the fallen angel, is a small act of revaluation. But his grievance is also true, and he knows exactly how he came to have it, and he says so to Victor's face. Does that break the diagnosis, or is Diagnostic reach independent of truth on Nietzsche's own terms?

Second, bad conscience. Nietzsche's mechanism is that reactive force, dammed up by a social wall so that it cannot discharge outward, turns inward and becomes a sickness of the self against itself. The creature is dammed up in the most literal possible way — every human being he approaches drives him off — and yet the obstruction has an author, and that author is Victor. Nietzsche's story locates the cause inside the sufferer. Does deploying it here quietly exculpate the person who actually did the damming, and does the novel itself register that objection?

Third, Victor. Read his project as reactive rather than noble: it begins in his mother's death and is aimed at mortality itself, which is close to what Nietzsche elsewhere calls revenge against time. Then read his promise to build a second creature — and his destruction of that creature mid-construction — through the second essay's account of the sovereign individual who has earned the right to promise and the memory of the will that makes promising possible. Victor is a promiser who cannot keep a promise and a creator who will not own his creation. What does the novel thereby claim about responsibility, and would Nietzsche concede it?

I also want you to test a distinction I have been toying with, and tell me whether it survives. Call a grievance origin-transparent if the person holding it can know in full detail how it arose — in fear, in envy, in an inability to forget a hurt — and still feel its full normative force, and origin-opaque if it survives only so long as its holder does not know its own history. My reading of Nietzsche is that he needs moralized, reactive grievances to be origin-opaque; that is what separates ressentiment from justified anger. My worry is that Frankenstein supplies a counterexample, since the creature is the most origin-transparent avenger in the literature. Either show me that he is not origin-transparent after all, or show me that Nietzsche is committed to something weaker than I have claimed, or grant the counterexample and say what it costs the genealogy.

Two constraints on the honest-critic side. Say plainly where the novel resists this lens — I suspect the absence of anything like a priestly caste, a herd, or an organized ascetic ideal is the main structural mismatch, and I want your view on whether the creature's self-banishment at the end is or is not a will to nothingness. And say what the novel adds to the philosophical debate that the arguments alone do not: my own hunch is that it gives us a genealogy spoken in the first person by the reactive subject himself, and an injustice produced not by a social structure but by a single act of creation followed by abandonment, for which nineteenth-century moral psychology has no vocabulary. Tell me if that hunch is worth anything.

Do not quote the novel at length; I have the text in front of me, and anything you need to refer to can be described.
