hey — I'm a journalist on the science/ideas beat, mostly explainers, and I've been chewing on something that I realised is basically the regress problem showing up in my day job. Every time I write "X happens because Y", someone — a reader, my editor, me at 1am — comes back with "ok, but why Y?", and as far as I can tell I've only got three moves: keep going until I hit something I stubbonly declare bedrock ("look, electrons just have negative charge, that's where the digging stops"); quietly loop back around so the last "because" restates the first one in different words, which I can feel is cheating even when I get away with it; or go on forever, which obviously doesn't fit in 900 words.

I recently stumbled on the third option being defended for real — infinitism, the view that being justified just *is* standing in an endless, non-repeating chain of reasons, where what matters isn't that the chain terminates but that the next link is there for you when someone asks. I find it weirdly attractive, but I genuinely can't tell whether its just flattering the way I already work or whether it commits me to something I'm not doing.

So: what are the most important implications of taking infinitism seriously for how I should actually write and edit explainers? Please don't give me the textbook run-through of foundationalism vs coherentism vs infinitism — I've got the gist, skip it. I want three or four concrete implications I could act on this week, each tied to something I'd really be writing about (say a piece on how GLP-1 drugs work, or why the newest telescope keeps finding galaxies that "shouldn't" exist yet). Things I'm especially curious about, though don't feel boxed in:

- If every "because" only has to be continuable-on-demand rather than anchored in something final, then the place I stop is never a fact about the world but always a fact about my reader. Do I owe them a marker of that — saying which question I'm answering instead of implying I've answered the ultimate one?
- Infinitism bans repeating links in the chain. I suspect a lot of bad popular science is circular in exactly that way (the molecule makes you feel full because it acts on the brain circuits that make you feel full). How should that constraint change the way I read my own drafts?
- The objection I keep seeing is the finite-minds one: nobody can hold infinitely many reasons in their head, so no actual person is ever justified. Does the infinitist's "available rather than actually held" move really defuse that, or does it smuggle a foundational stopping-point back in through the side door? And whichever way it goes, what should it mean for how much authority I claim over a topic I've reported for three weeks?
- Is there anything in this for the distinction between stopping because you've hit bottom and stopping because the next answer would be a *different story*? My hunch is that distinction does more work in my job than the whole debate does, and I'd like to know if the epistemology backs me up or tells me I'm rationalising.

Be concrete and a bit opinionated — I'd much rather have an argument I can fight with than a balanced overview.
