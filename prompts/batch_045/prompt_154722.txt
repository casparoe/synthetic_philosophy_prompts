hey — I need something for the media-ethics reading group I help run, and I'd rather it be a piece of writing than a handout.

Write an internal house style guide — the sort of thing that lives on a newsroom wiki — for a small regional outlet that has just adopted one hard rule: nobody may publish a statement they themselves believe to be false. Everything else is permitted. Voice is deadpan, bureaucratic, faintly proud of itself; it should read like it was drafted by someone who genuinely believes the rule is working. About 600-700 words, 8-10 numbered rules with short worked examples.

The comedy has to come out of the philosophy rather than out of punchlines. The guide should build a real craft out of exactly the gap between lying and misleading — sentences that are strictly true and are nevertheless meant to leave the reader with a false belief — and it needs to get the mechanics right: answering the question you weren't asked without marking that you've changed it; exploiting quantity/scalar implicatures by understating; scope ambiguity and neg-raising; the literally-true denial; disclosure that is technically complete but arranged so nobody finishes it; "we haven't been told" constructions that trade on a true claim about your own epistemic state; and letting an implicature do the work and then refusing to either confirm or deny it. Someone who actually works on assertion and implicature should find the techniques correctly described, not just see the word implicature dropped in.

Two things I want built in. First, invent a compliance device for the outlet — I was picturing a literal-truth audit that a line has to clear before it runs, with a numeric threshold, plus a rule for what the subeditor is supposed to do when a sentence passes the audit and still obviously misleads. Second, I want the guide to escalate: the early rules should be ones most people would defend outright, and by the end the craft should be indistinguishable from lying in every respect except the single one the rule tracks. Finish on the guide tangling itself up: it needs some rule about whether you're allowed to deny an implicature you created yourself, and whichever way it goes it can't stay consistent with its own earlier rules. Let the last item be an appendix or footnote where the contradiction just sits there — no one steps in to fix it.

Don't let the guide editorialise about whether any of this is wrong. No winking, no "readers may feel" asides; a reader should be able to tell what's being ironised from how straight it plays it.

After the piece, a short note — five or six lines at most — on which reading of the lying/misleading debate the guide is quietly presuming (i.e. what the reductio is even meant to show), how confident that reading is, and the one move in the piece you're least sure is right.
