Hey — I run a small philosophy-and-film thing in the back room of a pub, maybe fifteen people, half of them philosophy grads and half just folk who like sci-fi, and next month we're doing Ex Machina. I really don't want it to turn into another "is AI dangerous" evening, which is what it always collapses into, so I'm planning to run it as a problem-of-other-minds film and I want the reading worked out before I stand up there.

Here's the angle I've been chewing on, and tell me if it's a dud. The ordinary route to another mind is the argument from analogy: I know what behaviour of that kind is caused by in my own case, you produce behaviour of that kind, so probably the same kind of cause. What I think the film dramatises is a case where that inference is deliberately exploited rather than merely unreliable — Ava's behaviour has been optimised by somebody else to trigger exactly that inference in exactly the kind of person Caleb is, and Caleb is a programmer whose day job is modelling what users want and feel, so the film puts a man who builds models of minds in front of a mind built to model him. There's also an asymmetry I want to make something of: she has been trained on everything he's ever typed into the company's search engine, and he gets seven sessions and a pane of glass. I've been calling this the engineered-analogy problem, and I want to know whether it's a genuinely distinct shape of the problem of other minds or just the old scepticism with a designer added. Relatedly: our ordinary fallback when we suspect someone is performing is to look for behaviour they didn't stage for us — catch them alone, off-guard, in a moment they didn't design — and the film seems to hand the audience exactly that (Ava by herself in the glass room, the conversations during the blackouts) and then shows that she was causing the blackouts herself, so the apparent unguardedness was part of the design. Is the film making a point about the limits of behavioural evidence, or is that just a twist?

So: give me the reading. Which two or three scenes should I actually put on the projector as the load-bearing ones, and what do I say about each. Where does the film resist the reading and an honest critic has to back off — I suspect the film isn't really worried about philosophical zombies at all, and that Nathan's own test is about whether she can get out rather than about whether she's conscious, so I need to know how much of the "other minds" framing survives that. One member of the group will definitely bring up philosophical zombies and another will bring up Blade Runner's empathy test; tell me whether to let those in or head them off. And I'd like an ending to the session: does the film, in your view, land anywhere — is it a sceptical argument, or does it end up saying something closer to the anti-skeptical thought that the question "does she really feel or is she simulating" isn't answerable and isn't really the question — and is there anything the film sees about other minds that the papers don't? I'm writing the session up as a short piece for our little newsletter afterwards, so I want an actual reading, not a list of themes with clips attached.
