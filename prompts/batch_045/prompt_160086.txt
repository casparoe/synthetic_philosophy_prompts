Hi — I work in health policy (mostly boring reimbursement stuff) and I keep running into the same odd pattern, and I want to know what it commits us to philosophically.

Here's the practice. Whether something counts as a disease — for insurance coverage, clinical guidelines, diagnostic manuals — gets settled by committees, and the committees change their minds. Homosexuality was taken out of the psychiatric diagnostic manual decades ago by a vote. When a guideline body lowers the blood-pressure threshold, millions of people acquire hypertension overnight with nothing changing in their bodies. At the same time, the profession talks as if disease were a plain biological fact: you can't vote a bacterium into existence, and clinicians insist that asymptomatic conditions — an early cancer found on a scan, blood pressure you cannot feel, a gene variant picked up by accident — are diseases even when the patient feels fine and functions fine. But then, in the same psychiatric manual, nearly every diagnosis also requires clinically significant distress or impairment, so the identical state, with the same biology, counts as a disorder in one person and not in another depending on how she feels about it and what her job demands of her.

So: what view of health and disease does that package of practices presuppose? Is it quietly a value-laden view (dysfunction plus harm, or dysfunction plus disvalue) even though practicioners talk like value-free naturalists? Or can a purely biostatistical, function-and-normal-functioning account make sense of the distress clause and of classification by committee? And are the parts consistent with each other, or does the package presuppose naturalism in one place and normativism in another? I want you to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and to tell me where it is underdetermined — where either theory would license the same behavior.

Three specifics I want taken on. First, the reference class problem: if disease is departure from species-typical functioning, typical at what age? A 75-year-old's arteries and joints are typical for 75-year-olds and atypical for 25-year-olds, and whichever reference class you pick, something you'd want to call a disease flips sides. The naturalist seems to owe us a principled answer there, and my guess is that the choice is doing hidden value work. Second, an asymmetry I've noticed and can't quite cash out: taking something off the disease list gets celebrated as progress, while adding new pre-disease categories — pre-diabetes, pre-hypertension, mild cognitive impairment, anything labeled a risk factor — gets attacked as medicalizing normal life, even though both moves are committee decisions of the same kind. Is that asymmetry principled on either theory, or is it just about which direction of error we dread more? Third, what would actually have to change under each rival: if disease is strictly value-free biological dysfunction, how should thresholds be set and who should set them; if it is essentially normative, what should those committees be doing that they currently aren't?

One more thing, since I'm going to quote this to colleagues: mark clearly which of what you say is settled among people who actually work on these questions and which is genuinely contested, and don't smooth a live dispute into consensus. If you attribute a view to a particular theorist, say who, and flag it when you're extrapolating past what they argued. And please write it in American English.
