I host a fortnightly podcast on philosophy of mind and action, and next week's guest is a philosopher who has spent years defending a broadly intentionalist account of self-deception: the claim that genuine self-deception requires the agent to intend — at least in some non-occurrent, standing sense — to bring it about that she comes to believe, or goes on believing, something she takes her own evidence to speak against, and that the feat is possible because our cognitive lives are partitioned, whether temporally, attentionally, or sub-personally. I do not want an episode in which he simply restates the view, so I need eight to ten questions, each paired with a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give, and I should be grateful if you would write them in British English.

Please build the sequence so that it presses, first, the two standard puzzles: the one about getting into the state (how can an intention to make oneself believe something be formed and executed without being self-defeating, given that in forming it one seems already to know exactly what one is up to?) and the one about what the state itself is supposed to be (simultaneous contrary beliefs, a belief alongside a suppressed awareness, or something weaker than belief altogether?). Anticipate that he will reach for partitioning, for divided or sub-personal states, or for the contrast between occurrent and dispositional belief, and give me the follow-up that forces him to say what partitioning is actually doing and why it is not just a redescription of the puzzle. Then include one question about the deflationary rival — that most of what we call self-deception is really motivated irrationality, biased evidence-gathering, selective attention and wishful thinking, none of which needs an intention to believe against the evidence — with a follow-up on what exactly is lost if that rival is right, and whether the dispute is partly verbal, a quarrel about how much of the phenomenon each theory gets to count. Add one question on whether the acquired attitude must be false, and on 'twisted' cases in which a person engineers a true belief, or merely engineers a downward shift in credence well short of belief, by means she herself regards as unreliable.

The second half of the episode should take him somewhere he is unlikely to have a stock answer. My research group has been chewing over three cases, and I want a question on each, each with a follow-up for either way he might answer. First, 'delegated' or 'environmental' self-deception: someone who never manipulates her own attention at all, but deliberately builds and then trusts an information filter — a curated feed, a hired analyst, a journaling app that surfaces only certain entries — with the aim of ending up believing something, so that no single mental state of hers ever aims at the false belief. Second, 'role-occupancy' self-deception: someone who never intends to believe the proposition, but intends to become the kind of person — the kind of colleague, parent, or believer — for whom believing it comes naturally, on the thought that identity precedes conviction. Third, collective self-deception: a laboratory or a family that sustains a comfortable falsehood through distributed avoidance, where no individual member ever forms the relevant intention. For each, ask him to say plainly whether his account counts it as self-deception: if he says yes, ask what has become of the intention condition; if he says no, ask whether his theory is then characterising something considerably rarer than the phenomenon we were interested in to begin with. Close with one question about what empirical or first-personal evidence could count against his account — what he would expect to find, and not find, were he wrong — and, if there is room, one on whether any of this has practical upshot for how a person ought to go about changing their own mind.
