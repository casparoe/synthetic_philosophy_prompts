I'm in the eleventh grade, and for an independent project I'm trying to build what my supervisor calls a decision procedure: a short field guide that my class could actually use to rule on anything anyone hands us, and say whether it is alive. The purpose is fixed in advance. I do not need the guide for cats, oak trees, or the bacteria on a petri dish in the lab cupboard, because everyone already agrees about those. I need it for the cases where nobody agrees: things assembled in a lab, samples a rover might bring back, self-copying computer programs, industrial chemical processes, and whatever else turns up that nobody has seen before. Before I read any more of the proposed definitions of life, I'd like the specification that such a definition is supposed to meet.

So please write out the desiderata for an account of what it is to be living, given that purpose: the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, the results from biology and chemistry it is not allowed to contradict, and the features it must have as a test rather than as a slogan. For each requirement give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are really preferences of one camp dressed up as requirements. I care much more about the list and about why each item is on it than about any scoring of existing theories.

The cases I keep coming back to, and which I think any account has to say something about, are these: an individual that is plainly alive but can never have offspring of its own; a seed or spore in which essentially no chemistry happens for centuries or millennia and which then starts up again; a forest that is one individual spread over many acres, linked underground, while also shedding parts that live on their own; a computer worm on a school network that copies itself, mutates, and sheds functions nobody needs; a factory process that consumes fuel, grows, and produces waste, but produces nothing that inherits anything from it; a laboratory cell that runs its own chemistry but can only reproduce by sending instructions to a machine it neither built nor can repair.

The distinctions I don't want collapsed are: being alive versus being a part of something alive; being alive versus being a product of something alive; whether a thing is alive at all versus how much it counts or how alive it is; reproducing versus merely being copied by something else; and having a function or a purpose versus merely happening to be useful to something.

I've also made up three cases for the project, and I'd like them treated as real constraints on the account rather than as jokes. The Rental: a creature that maintains itself and reproduces, but every component of it, including its outer boundary and the machinery that copies its instructions, is manufactured by a different organism living inside it; separated from that partner it does nothing at all. The Library: a creature whose young develop into the right body only if raised within a structure the parent builds, where the shape of that structure rather than the parent's genetic material carries most of the information about the body's layout, where the shape is rebuilt slightly differently each generation, and where those differences are passed on. The Twin Ladder: a lineage that must alternate, one generation multiplying by splitting and the next only by exchanging material with another individual, where neither method alone can carry the line more than one step. For each of the three, tell me what a candidate definition is committed to saying, and whether you think that is the right thing to say.

Two further things I specifically want. First: I suspect that two requirements people usually demand are in tension, namely that the account must not simply be reverse-engineered from a list of the organisms we already call alive, and that it must of course agree with us about those organisms. If that tension is real, make it precise and tell me what has to be given up; if I'm just muddling two different things, say so and explain the difference. Second: tell me which single requirement, of the ones you defend, excludes the largest number of the definitions currently on offer, including the popular style that defines life as something like self-maintaining chemistry that can evolve by natural selection. Say which way of thinking it rules out and why that is a point in the requirement's favour rather than a reason to weaken it.

Finally, on how I'd like you to answer: please commit. Give me the list you would actually stand behind, argue for it, and where you have to make a judgment call, make it and say why rather than laying out the options and leaving me to choose. If you had to drop one desideratum from your own list, say which and why, and say which part of your answer you take to be most open to reasonable disagreement. A binary verdict may or may not be what the guide should output; if your own desiderata settle that question, say which way they settle it.
