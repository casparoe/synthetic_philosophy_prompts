For my sixth-form creative-writing portfolio I need a short story — roughly 1,200 words, and in British English, please — that stages a disagreement about forgetting rather than explaining one.

The institution I have invented is called the Shedding. Once a decade, the residents of a small town vote by secret ballot for one episode from the town's own history to be placed in active oblivion: struck from the school syllabus and the local archive, omitted from the remembrance-day reading, and — the part I care most about — something that everyone who still remembers it has promised one another never to mention again. Children are not told that anything has been taken out. Its defenders say this is neither denial nor censorship: the episode is named aloud once, at a ceremony, and then deliberately let go of, the way a family might decide to stop rehearsing an old injury. It is not forgiveness either — nobody claims the wrong has been atoned for or excused. The town also keeps a sealed ledger listing everything it has shed, which is where my protagonist finds this year's entry.

Two complications should carry the story. First: the episode chosen this year is one in which a single family in the town was badly wronged, and their granddaughter argues that a community owes the people it has wronged a duty of remembrance that no majority can vote away — that forgetting on their behalf is a second injury, and that the duty is owed to them, not merely to whatever the living now find useful. Her opponents reply that keeping it present is not loyalty but a way of pinning her family into the role of victims, and that forgetting can itself be a form of care. Second: the rules require the consent of the last surviving witness, who is now in the advanced stages of dementia and can no longer recall the episode, though she used to speak of it constantly — so the story has to feel its way around whether a duty to remember can be owed to someone who can no longer remember, and whether the town would be remembering for her or merely about her.

Please avoid the obvious statue-being-pulled-down set-up: the dispute should concern a forgetting that everyone involved has agreed to, not contested public symbols. I would also rather the ending turned on a concrete act than a speech — I had in mind whether she tells her younger brother, who under the rules is not meant to know that anything was ever removed, and thereby manufactures a memory where there was none. No moralising narrator, no tidy resolution, and no character who is simply right: I want the reader left unsure whose claim on the past is the stronger one.
