I work as a software engineer, and a few of us keep a long-running thread — half reading group, half argument — on the Fermi paradox. Last week someone ended a stretch of back-and-forth by saying that absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, meaning, as far as I could tell: we have surveyed a laughably small slice of the relevant space, so the fact that we have heard nothing carries no information, and therefore there is no paradox to explain, only a data deficit. Nobody pushed back on it, partly because the line sounds like common sense, and I want to know whether it actually does the work it was being asked to do.

So I would like a menu of the distinct precise claims that sentence could be expressing when it is used about the silence. I want the whole range, from the platitudinous to the contentious to the plainly false: a claim about what our surveys have and have not covered; a claim about the direction and size of a Bayesian update; a claim about whether an absence can ever support an existential conclusion; a claim about where the burden of proof sits in a disagreement of this shape. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or wrong, and — the part I actually care about — whether it would support the move that nothing here needs explaining, or only the weaker move that nothing here is proven.

Two wrinkles I would like you to engage with rather than steer around.

First: the paradox is itself built out of an absence, so the claim that the evidence is absent and the claim that the evidence would be absent even if the hypothesis were true pull in opposite directions, and it is not obvious to me which one the slogan is asserting. It seems to me that two different silences are being run together. One is a sampling deficit: our coverage of sky, frequency band, duty cycle and elapsed time is minuscule, so a null result is unsurprising either way. The other is a legibility failure: the civilization is there, but its activity is not the kind of thing our search would register — if, say, technologically mature transmitters compress their output toward the thermodynamic noise floor and become indistinguishable from background, then detectability occupies a window in a civilization's history rather than tracking its lifetime, and the expected number of detectable civilizations depends on how long each spends being loud rather than on how long it lasts. A galaxy can then be crowded and still look empty. Is that distinction one the slogan is able to express, and does the conclusion that there is no paradox require collapsing it?

Second: on the Bayesian reading, silence is informative only relative to a rival hypothesis, and the sign of the update flips depending on which hypothesis predicts loudness. My sense is that the silence is strong evidence against something quite specific — not against the existence of civilizations, but against the existence of long-lived, expansive civilizations that leak waste power at scales we would notice — and that this is exactly what makes the situation a paradox rather than a shrug. If that is right, then the one reading of the slogan that would genuinely defuse the argument, namely that the silence is evidentially inert here, is also the least defensible one. Tell me if I have that backwards, and if so, where.

Please keep the apparatus light: I have no formal philosophy background, so a likelihood ratio is fine and a formal treatment is not. I am not asking for citations or a reading list — I want the distinctions sharpened, not sources. And if you can say which reading my colleague was most likely reaching for, given what the line was being used to do in that exchange, that would be the most useful part of the answer.
