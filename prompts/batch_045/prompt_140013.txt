I have been asked to give the eulogy for my grandfather, who died two weeks ago at eighty-four, and I have just come from an argument about him with two friends that ran until well past midnight and that I do not think either side won.

The facts, since you will need them. He spent thirty-four years in the civil service of our state, the last eleven of them as director of the office that ran the automated eligibility system for disability benefits. During his tenure the system was recalibrated in a way that, combined with a change in how it counted unreported income, raised the rate at which people were terminated for supposed ineligibility. Internal audits, which he read and initialled, put the erroneous terminations in the tens of thousands over about four years. He chose phased remediation rather than suspension of the tool, in memos written entirely in the vocabulary of program integrity, payment accuracy, and exposure. He never met a recipient of the benefits. He was, by any professional measure, good at his job — careful, punctilious, non-partisan, trusted by legislatures of both parties — and considered himself a man who did the job rather than the politics. To me he was generous, funny, patient, and the person who taught me to tie flies. When I asked him about the audits once, about a year before he died, he said that the office administered the rulebook the legislature wrote, that if the rulebook produced outcomes people disliked the remedy lay with the legislature, and that an administrator who substituted his own judgment for the law was not a good administrator but a rogue one.

The argument was about whether Hannah Arendt's notion of the banality of evil has anything to do with him, and about what follows if it does. My friend A says the whole invocation is grotesque: the idea was meant to describe a man who administered the murder of millions, and applying it to a benefits director who broke no law is an inflation that damages both cases. My friend B says the scale is beside the point, because what is being described is a structure of action rather than a magnitude of harm: the wrong located not in a cruel motive but in a failure of judgment; the agent who is scrupulous about the job and never once thinks about the people the job acts on; the official vocabulary that converts persons into cases; and, above all, the exculpation of the rules and of obedience, which B insists Arendt meant to name as the very thing to be judged and not as an excuse. On B's reading the monstrousness belonged to the deeds and the ordinariness to the doer, and the point was to hold the two together rather than let the second cancel the first.

Where we stalled is what I have been calling the problem of the well-run office, and it is the thing I want your speech to think about. My grandfather did think, and think hard, about error rates and legal exposure and throughput; the material was on his desk. What he never did was think about the people at the other end of the numbers. A says that this is not a special kind of failure at all but simply what it is to be a person — attentive to some things, inattentive to others — and that unless it is more than that, it is hard to see why it should make him worse rather than merely imperfect. I could not answer that, and I do not want the eulogy to pretend that I can.

So I need a eulogy, six to eight minutes, in my voice, for a room of about a hundred and fifty people: family, neighbours, perhaps two dozen of his former colleagues, his brother who has said privately that the subject is out of bounds at a funeral and will walk out if this turns into an indictment, two of his former subordinates who went on the record about the office and are sitting in the second row, and my cousin, who teaches philosophy and will hear every soft move I make. It has to be speakable by a granddaughter who loved him and who also thinks something went badly wrong; it has to be truthful enough that the two people in the second row do not feel erased and restrained enough that his brother stays in his seat, without achieving that by splitting the difference into mush. It should not settle whether what he did was evil — I do not want his funeral to be the place that gets decided — but it must not proceed as though the question were not there.

Three formal constraints, which are the whole difficulty. It must not name Arendt, and must not use the words evil, banality, cog, or system, or any sentence of the shape he was just doing his job: I will not turn the funeral into a seminar, and I will not hand my cousin a vocabulary to argue with. It must not close by appealing to his private decency as though that settled the public question — you may mention the fishing and the patience, but not as a verdict — and it must not console itself with the move that he was a man of his time, or that who among us has not. Whatever judgment the speech contains has to be carried by its shape and its particulars rather than by its terms. Where you need a specific memory, leave me a bracketed slot; three at most.

After the text, add four or five sentences naming the single decision in it that a rigorous Arendtian would be most likely to reject, and why you made it anyway.
