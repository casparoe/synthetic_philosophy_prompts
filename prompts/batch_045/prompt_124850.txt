i run a small local EA-adjacent reading group and we just did a session on the measurability critique, and I'd rather close it with a poem than with a summary. My own version of the worry, which I half made up and want the poem to actually carry: it isnt just that hard-to-measure goods get ignored, its a ratchet — once you rank interventions by cost per unit of good, anything that spends on unmeasurable goods scores badly, so money flows away from it, so the evidence base gets thinner, so it scores worse next time around, and the whole region of value kinda shrinks a little every time you look at it. I've been calling it Goodhart's shadow but if you have a better name use it.

Form: a pantoum, maybe five or six quatrains — lines 2 and 4 of each stanza become lines 1 and 3 of the next, and the last stanza reaches back to the opening lines. The repeats are the whole point: a line that first shows up flat and spreadsheet-y, about dollars and cost-effectiveness and counterfactuals, should come back later in a context where it lands on something in the speaker's life that never had a price — a friendship let lapse, a parent's last year, the decade itself — and mean something different, with no announce-y "and then I realised" moment. Let the looping structure be the ratchet: the ending should feel like we're back at the start but further down, not like a resolution.

Voice: someone who spent about ten years earning to give and is doing the accounting on it. Accuracy bar — no cold-utilitarian-robot stuff. The speaker should plainly still think the money did enormous real good and shouldnt be doing much self-flagellation; the critique is of the measurement machinery and the feedback loop, not of the motives or of caring about strangers. Someone who actually buys the EA critique should read it and not think you're attacking a cartoon. Keep it under 30 lines and dont give me a tidy moral in the last stanza.
