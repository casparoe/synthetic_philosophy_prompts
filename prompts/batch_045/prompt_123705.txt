I'm retired now — thirty-odd years in atmospheric chemistry, mostly satellite retrievals, nothing philosophical about it — and for the last year or two I've been sitting in on a graduate seminar in moral philosophy at the local university, which is how I ended up puzzling over something in health policy. As I understand it, a lot of publicly funded health systems ration by ranking treatments according to cost per quality-adjusted life year: they attach a weight between zero and one to each health state, they get those weights by asking large numbers of ordinary people to imagine living in the state or to trade off years of life against being more disabled, and then they fund treatments whose cost per QALY gained falls below some threshold (the English system has an actual number, which I can never rember) and decline treatments above it, whatever the disease. Yet the very same systems will spend enormous sums to rescue an identifiable person — a trapped miner, a child named in the newspapers who needs a drug costing many multiples of the threshold per QALY — and often run special, more generous rules for end-of-life treatments and rare diseases. The gap between those two halves is what I keep getting stuck on.

So, first: what does the cost-per-QALY half actually presuppose? On the face of it, it needs utilities that are not merely orderings within one person's life but cardinal and cut from the same cloth across people, so that one patient's 0.4 of a year in moderately bad health can be set against another patient's 0.7 in pretty good health and then summed over a population. But someone in the seminar said the whole apparatus can be rebuilt without any cross-person comparison at all, on something like a Harsanyi line: the planner imagines herself behind a veil of ignorance, not knowing which patient she'll turn out to be, and the machinery of expected utility then delivers a sum anyway, so that what's really doing the work is one person's intrapersonal preferences under uncertainty plus an impartiality assumption. I'd like to know which of these the practice strictly requires and which it merely sits comfortably with, and whether the survey method for the weights — asking the healthy to imagine being ill, then averaging — is doing real philosophical work or just papering over the fact that we can't get inside anybody's head.

The thing I most want to bounce off you, though, is a scheme I sketched in the margins of my notes, which I've been calling the same-person rule. The thought is to do rationing while never comparing one person's welfare with another's at all: given two ways of spending a fixed budget, compare each person only with themselves under the alternative — is Aisha better off under plan A than she would have been under plan B, is Ben, is Chidi — and then just count, choosing A if more people come out ahead, or perhaps refusing to move unless nobody comes out behind. Nobody ever has to say that Aisha's gain is bigger than Ben's, only that there is a gain and whose side it falls on. Does that really avoid interpersonal comparisons, or do they creep back in somewhere — in the intrapersonal judgement that Aisha is better off in one health state than another, or in the choice to count heads rather than magnitudes, or in what you do when the counting cycles? And where would a system run on the same-person rule diverge from one run on cost-per-QALY, and would those divergences be a bug or a feature? Last thing: does the rescue-the-named-child half of current practice presuppose something inconsistent with the cost-per-QALY half — that aggregation is fine in the abstract but breaks down when there's a face, or that proximity and certainty carry weight of their own — or is it just politics bolted onto a theory, and what would a consistent version of either view have to do about it?
