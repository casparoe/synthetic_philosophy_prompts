His verdicts so far:

- a single electron: yes, but the tiniest possible flicker, closer to nothing than to anything
- a rock: no. There's no "what it's like to be the rock," even though the quarks inside it have their flickers. He says they just don't add up.
- a human: one subject, obviously
- a beehive: no hive-subject, just 50,000 bee-subjects, even though the hive solves problems no single bee can
- the same hive, but you wire the bees so each one feels exactly what its neighbours feel, all the way through: he says that DOES give you one subject. One, not 50,000.
- two people hooked to a machine that lets them share one sense (each sees the other's visual field as well as their own): still two subjects. Sharing some experiences doesn't merge them.
- two identical brain emulations running side by side on two laptops, in exactly the same states, chips not conscious: two subjects, not one. "being identical isn't being the same one."
- a normal emulation of your brain on a laptop, chips not conscious: one subject, and it has experiences like yours
- someone under general anaesthesia or in deep dreamless sleep: zero subjects for that stretch, but same person before and after
- a patient who had the corpus callosum cut: still one subject, not two — I think because the brainstem stuff is still shared, that's roughly what he said

New case, the one I actually care about: instead of running the emulation on a laptop, you build it out of a billion tiny robots. But the robots don't share experiences with each other. They just pass signals along, and each one keeps having its own separate bee-sized experience the whole time.

How many subjects are in the box? Is there a subject in there whose experience is like mine? Does he say yes, no, or "one, and it's literally made out of the bee ones"? And here's the one I keep failing to get a number out of him on: what if you swap one robot out for an unconscious dummy chip that does the same job? Does anything change about the big experience, or not?

The bit I can't work out is which rule he's running. As far as I can tell there are at least three that fit his old verdicts: (1) subjects only fuse when their experiences literally get shared or merged into the same one, so a swarm that never shares never fuses — that seems to predict no human-level subject at all, a box that talks like me with nothing it's like to be it; (2) there's a subject wherever the causal organization is the right kind, whatever the parts are up to — that seems to predict one human-like subject, and leaves you with the weird question of what happened to the bee ones; (3) the big subject just is the little ones arranged right, so its experience is composed of theirs — which predicts one human-like subject, but also that swapping in a dummy chip should change or break something.
