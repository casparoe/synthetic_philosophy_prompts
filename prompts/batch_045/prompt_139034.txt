I sit on the classification panel of an independent democracy-monitoring institute. A formal "democratic backsliding" determination from us triggers a public report and suspends the country's participation in our peer-exchange program. The case before us is the Republic of Marcea.

Over eighteen months the governing coalition — elected with 52 percent of seats under rules it inherited — has: (i) replaced at-large appointment of the state audit office's board with staggered twelve-year terms and filled every vacancy in the first round; (ii) required organizations receiving more than a fifth of their funding from abroad to register and label their publications; (iii) switched from open-list to closed-list proportional representation; (iv) enlarged the constitutional court from nine to thirteen seats; and (v) placed the public broadcaster under a council appointed by a two-thirds parliamentary vote. The opposition calls this a coup by checklist. The government notes, accurately, that each of the five measures has a live analogue in a democracy we have never flagged, and that the electoral system it replaced was designed by the party that governed for the previous twenty-two years. As enlarged, the constitutional court is scheduled to review (iii) and (v) next year.

Two colleagues want to issue the determination now. Two want to dismiss the complaint outright: they argue that "backsliding" has become a term of abuse for governments one dislikes, that our verdict will simply ratify the priors of whoever sits on the panel, and that if our test cannot separate Marcea from a country with closed lists and a foreign-funding registry, then we have no test.

Draft the decision memo I will circulate, in under 900 words. It should recommend a concrete disposition with a timeline, including whether to wait for the court's review. It should state the decision rule you are applying clearly enough that a differently composed panel could apply it to another country without knowing our politics, and it should address honestly the strongest version of the objection you reject — including the question of whether a body like ours should be making these judgments at all. Close by saying what evidence, if it emerged, would change your recommendation.
