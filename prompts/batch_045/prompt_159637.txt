You are a professional philosopher of science who has taught Goodman's new riddle of induction many times and who is good at building cases for students. Please think this through carefully: brainstorm two or three candidate settings before you commit to one, and tell me in a line or two which candidates you rejected and why.

I need one thought experiment for my second-year philosophy of science seminar, and I need it to run in the opposite direction from the one students always meet. In the familiar version, the grue-shaped predicate is the gerrymandered one, the plain predicate is the projectible one, and the explanation offered is entrenchment: the plain predicate has been projected successfully countless times, the grue-shaped one never. I want a case where the grue-shaped hypothesis is the one a competent inquirer should project and the plain, entrenched, syntactically simpler one fails — and I want that verdict generated by the set-up rather than stipulated. The point I am trying to make in class is that entrenchment and causal structure can come apart, so that a grue-shaped hypothesis can carry a warrant that is not merely an artifact of how we happen to talk.

Specifications. (1) One ordinary, non-science-fictional setting — a lab, clinic, warehouse, farm, workshop, archive. One paragraph, no more than about 150 words. No demons, no oracles, no perfect predictors, no exotic physics, nobody stipulated to be irrational or deaf to evidence. (2) The case must contain a kind K of things, an observable property φ, a different observable property ψ, and a time t. Every K examined before t has been φ. Two hypotheses must fit the data exactly and diverge only about Ks examined after t: H-plain, which says that all Ks are φ, and H-grue, which says that Ks examined before t are φ and Ks examined after t are ψ. H-grue has to be the one it is correct to project. (3) Cheats to avoid: do not make the community speak the grue-shaped predicate as a basic vocabulary item, which is Goodman's own symmetry move; do not stipulate a law, a memo, or an announcement stating that the switch happens at t; do not make H-plain gerrymandered by convention or definition. H-plain should look, to the inquirers inside the case, like the natural, simple, well-entrenched description of what is going on — and it should in fact be more entrenched, in their actual past practice of projection, than anything on the grue side. (4) What I think must do the work, and what I would like you to use unless you have something better, is a mechanism I have started calling index-inheritance: the grue-shaped predicate's time-index is not a bare calendar date but a proxy for an unobserved, causally relevant variable — age, batch, provenance, storage history, position in a stack — so that the disjunction tracks a real underlying difference instead of being gerrymandered. If your case works by some other mechanism, name it and say what it is.

Then show that the case meets the specification. State, in this order: (a) what Goodman's entrenchment criterion says, and why it comes out for H-plain; (b) what a simplicity or structural criterion says — the projectible predicate is the one not defined by reference to a particular time — and why it too comes out for H-plain; (c) what a causal-structure or natural-kind criterion says — project the predicate whose instances share an underlying causally efficacious property, the sort of property that would support counterfactuals and interventions — and why it comes out for the grue side. After that, give your own all-things-considered verdict about what the inquirers should predict, and say whether it agrees with (c).

Finish with three short notes, a sentence or two each. First: which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to the three verdicts if you remove it — I expect the case to collapse back into an ordinary Goodman case, but tell me honestly if it does not. Second: whether (c) really endorses the grue-shaped predicate itself or only a nearby predicate indexed by the shadow variable, and what it means for the case if the endorsement is only of the neighbour. Third: the strongest thing an entrenchment theorist could say to defuse the example. My own guess is something like this: the grue-shaped predicate is still not what anyone projects, because once the inquirers possess the batch concept they project with that instead, so the case is really about concept acquisition rather than projectibility. Give me your own version, and say whether you think it works.

Please write it up as something I could photocopy and hand out: the scenario first, then the walkthrough of the three criteria, then the three notes. Aim for roughly 600 to 700 words in total, and do not pad the scenario with atmosphere — it has to survive being read by a hostile student who is looking for the trick.
