I'm teaching a unit on the concept of liberty in an upper-division political philosophy seminar, and I'm assigning Margaret Atwood's *The Handmaid's Tale* alongside the theory rather than merely as a change of pace. We will already have worked through Berlin's distinction between negative and positive liberty and the neo-republican revival of freedom as non-domination, understood as a status: not being subject to another's arbitrary will, even when that will happens not to be exercised. What I want from you is a reading of the novel through all three lenses, for a handout I'm writing. Please don't quote from the novel; I have it in front of me, and I'll supply the passages myself.

The scene I keep returning to is the Red Center lecture in which the Aunts tell the Handmaids that there is more than one kind of freedom: that the world before gave them freedom *to*, and Gilead gives them freedom *from*, and that they shouldn't underrate it. I want to know how much genuine theory is in that speech. Is it a recognizable Berlinian claim — a regime that secures negative liberty for some by narrowing the options of others, plus a positive-liberty story about being released from one's own corrupted desires in order to serve a real collective purpose? Does the novel let any of it have force, given how it renders the pre-Gilead world Offred remembers and the things she says she no longer has to fear? Or does it intend the speech as bad faith through and through — and if so, can the novel explain why without simply assuming that negative liberty is the whole of freedom?

Second, apply non-domination to three cases and tell me where it fits and where it grinds: a Handmaid placed in a comparatively benign household versus one in a brutal one; the Ceremony, and the related practice of making the Handmaids participate in the Salvagings and Particicutions, so that they are made instruments of the will that dominates them; and the women with real but precarious power, such as Serena Joy or the Aunts themselves. I'm especially interested in whether non-domination needs an identifiable dominator, since the power over Offred has no single author — it is distributed among the Commander, the Aunts, the Eyes, the doctrine, and the regime's demographic need.

Third, I want to try out an idea of my own, and I want you to test it rather than endorse it. Take the Commander's unsanctioned Scrabble games and the night out at Jezebel's. Offred gets what she wants, no one interferes with her, and the power over her is being exercised well within the regime's own rules. On both the negative and the republican accounts these episodes come out looking considerably freer than the novel insists they are. What the novel seems to be tracking is that her agency exists only on sufferance, at another person's continuing pleasure, with no standing on her part to contest or renegotiate the terms — call it licensed agency. Is this already captured by non-domination, because the power is arbitrary in the relevant sense? Is it a genuinely distinct fourth thing, about the dependence of one's practical reasoning on someone else's ongoing permission? Or is it just the old republican point about dependence restated, in which case the novel adds nothing to the argument?

Finally, be honest about where the reading fails. The novel's framing epilogue — the future academic conference at which a scholar turns a woman's recorded testimony into material for a paper, jokes about her name, and worries mainly about authenticating the recording — satirizes exactly the sort of reading I am asking you to produce. Take that seriously: say what it is about this book that resists being used as a philosophical illustration, and what a liberty-theoretic reading distorts.

Close with the two or three scenes you would put on a syllabus for this purpose, and with a verdict on what, if anything, the novel shows about freedom that the arguments alone don't.
