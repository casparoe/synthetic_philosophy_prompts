For the past year, since retiring from a career in evolutionary biology, I have been reading philosophy on my own, and much of what I find in moral and political theory turns out to be formal results I already knew under other names. I have been trying to get clear about the structure that goes under the name of Moloch: arrangements in which each party's individually sensible move makes everyone worse off, where the move is not folly or malice but something no one can unilaterally stop making.

I recently re-watched Kubrick's Dr. Strangelove and it struck me as a nearly pure instance of it. The bombers that cannot be recalled, the standing expectation that the other side will strike second, and above all the Soviet doomsday machine, disclosed to the American President mid-film precisely as a device designed to end the arms race by making retaliation automatic and therefore credible. That machine is what I want help with. It seems to me at once the solution to the trap and its completion: it removes the commitment problem that drives the race, and it removes every last opportunity for anyone to decline to carry the race through to its end.

So I would like a reading of the film that takes that seriously, on two questions. First, is the doomsday machine best understood as an escape from the multipolar trap — a credible commitment that makes the race unnecessary — or as the trap's final form, a world in which no agent retains the power to choose peace? Second, and I would rather have the uncomfortable answer: does the film actually dramatize a multipolar trap at all, or does it locate the catastrophe in one man's private delusion plus bad luck, in which case the Moloch reading flatters the film and the honest critic has to say where it resists.

I would also value your view on a parallel I keep drawing and mistrusting. In my field we modelled the same formal structure as a Red Queen arms race: competing lineages invest more and more in offence and defence, each gain is cancelled by the other side's matching gain, and everyone ends up paying a higher cost to hold the same relative position. Does that analogy survive contact with this story, or does it smuggle something in — a background stability, or the absence of anyone who actively prefers the outcome being produced — that the film contradicts? This is for a reading notebook rather than for publication, so please do not summarize the plot or survey deterrence theory; I want the argument about this machine and this story.
