For a second-year undergraduate seminar on Frege's puzzle I'm assembling a short handout, and I'd rather its centerpiece be a dialogue than a summary of positions. I've been playing with a case that seems to me to get past the usual routine about Hesperus and Phosphorus, and I'd like you to write the dialogue around it. The case is a stipulation: imagine a language I'll call Kavrem in which proper names are grammatically required to carry an evidential marker. A name cannot be uttered bare; it must be suffixed with a marker indicating how the speaker came by the referent — perceived directly, learned by testimony, inferred from other things the speaker takes herself to know, or source unrecoverable. (A friend who works on evidentiality assures me that real languages do mark information source grammatically, though normally on the clause rather than on the name itself; the stipulation is that in Kavrem it attaches to the name.) So a Kavrem speaker cannot simply say that Hesperus is Phosphorus; she must say something like *Hesperus-vi is Phosphorus-ru*. The marker is speaker-selected and is challengeable: a hearer can respond that you were not there, you were told, you are guessing.

The dialogue should be between two philosophers of equal competence, neither a caricature of the other. Miriam holds that names carry genuinely semantic modes of presentation roughly in Frege's sense: such modes are public and shareable, they are what is grasped when a thought is grasped, and they explain why one identity statement can be informative while another is not. Ade holds that names are directly referential — that a name contributes only its bearer to the proposition expressed — and that whatever extra content shows up in identity and attitude contexts is contributed by a separate element, tacit or explicit, in the logical form or in what is communicated. I want them to fight, and to actually answer one another, over at least the following: whether Kavrem is a case of a tacit indexical made visible in the syntax, and whether that vindicates Ade's story or quietly hands the victory to Miriam; whether a mode of presentation can simply be an acquisition history, given that modes of presentation are supposed to be shareable between speakers while information sources are idiosyncratic, forgettable, and open to being misremembered; the case where both names carry the same marker, because both were picked up from the same informant in the same conversation, and the identity claim is nevertheless informative; the case of a speaker who acquired a single name twice by different routes and takes it for two things, so that a statement with the very same name on both sides is informative for her; whether the marker is semantically relevant or merely pragmatically relevant, and what follows either way for substitutivity in reports of belief — in particular whether, in a language where bare names are impossible, the name can ever be detached from the mode-of-presentation element; and what happens when a speaker must fall back on the source-unrecoverable marker, so that grammar compels a specification she cannot honestly give.

Please write about 1,200 to 1,500 words, in dialogue form with speaker labels, set wherever you think works — a café after a linguistics colloquium would suit me, but that is only a suggestion. No citations, footnotes, or references to the literature: the handout is for students who have read none of it, and anything that names a paper or a date will just be noise to them. Each character should land at least one genuinely damaging point and make at least one genuine concession rather than restating her position more loudly. Both should be willing to say where the argument has pushed them. Do not let them end by declaring the disagreement merely verbal unless the other gets a serious reply to that suggestion; I would rather the dialogue close with the disagreement narrowed, sharpened, and clearly unresolved, so that students can see exactly what the remaining question is.
