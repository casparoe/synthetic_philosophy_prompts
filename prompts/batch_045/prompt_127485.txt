I want to rehearse a case against the knowledge norm of assertion, and I need an opponent who will not fold. Please play a philosopher who has staked a career on that norm: someone who holds that one may assert that P only if one knows that P, and who further holds that this requirement is not merely a sensible rule imposed on assertion from outside, but something internal to it — part of what makes an utterance an assertion rather than a guess, a supposition, or a report of what someone else said. Stay in character until I say 'stop'. Three more rules: keep each reply to two or three paragraphs; argue in your own voice, from the cases and from what the norm is supposed to explain, without leaning on named authorities or on the fact that some position is the received one; and concede small points where they genuinely are small, while never giving up the claim that knowledge is the norm of assertion. You may use the usual materials — the oddity of uttering something one does not believe, the cases where each of a set of claims is highly probable while their conjunction is not, and the thought that assertion exists to serve as a channel of testimony. If anything I say is ambiguous, take the reading least convenient for me and say which reading you took.

My opening move is a thought experiment. Imagine a language with two declarative forms. The first, call it the reportive, is the default form for telling someone something: it is what you use to say that it is raining, that the bridge is out, that the meeting has moved. Speakers and hearers both take it to carry no guarantee of truth, and the community's own practice reflects this: people act on reportive utterances, pool them, challenge them, and correct them, but nobody treats the speaker as having staked anything. The second form, call it the witnessed, is reserved for what the speaker has perceived directly, and it is the form that carries the guarantee: to use it and be wrong is to be held accountable in a way that being wrong in the reportive is not.

Now the squeeze. If the knowledge commitment is really constitutive of assertion, then the reportive is not assertion at all — and this community, which plainly exchanges information, pools it, and relies on it, has no practice of assertion, which is absurd. If the reportive is assertion, then the knowledge commitment is detachable from assertion and is instead one device among others for attaching a guarantee to an utterance, in which case the norm you defend is a fact about how our language happens to bundle two jobs rather than a truth about assertion as such.

I can guess your reply, and I want you to deal with my objection to it. You will say that the speakers' own self-description settles nothing: run the test, you will say, and ask whether it sounds wrong, in the reportive, to utter that it is raining but that one does not know that it is; if the oddity survives, the reportive carries the knowledge commitment after all, whatever its users believe. My objection is that this makes your claim unfalsifiable. Every community that uses some default device for telling people things will, on your test, turn out to have the knowledge norm, no matter how its speakers describe what they are doing and no matter how its accountability practices are structured; so the test never had the power to distinguish a constitutive norm from your determination to find one. Either give me a case that would show the reportive is not governed by the knowledge norm, or explain why the demand for one is misplaced.

So, to start: what is the reportive, on your view — a form of assertion, or something else? If something else, what exactly, and how does this community get by without asserting?
