ok sorry this is long but our philosophy elective did Hume on miracles last week and I can't stop turning it over against something else I read about, so here goes.

Roughly what I took Hume to be saying: a miracle is a violation of a law of nature, our evidence for those laws is justUniform repeated experience, and a sensible person proportions belief to the evidence — so no testimony is ever strong enough to make it reasonable to believe a miracle happened, beause the proof against it is as strong as any experience can get. Plus people who report miracles tend to be credulous or to have a stake in the story, and every religion has its own miracle reports so they cancel each other out. My teacher kept saying Hume isn't claiming miracles are impossible, he's claiming the evidence for one can never be good enough. Fine.

Here's the other thing. The Catholic Church has this formal process for declaring someone a saint, and the way it was explained to me it goes like this: a healing gets proposed, a panel of doctors looks at the medical records and decides only whether medicine can account for it — apparently they're told to ignore the religious side entirely, like who the person prayed to. Then a seperate group of theologians decides whether it really was a miracle granted through that person's intercession. And you need one verified miracle to get the person to the first stage where they can be venerated locally, but a second one, occurring after that, before they can be declared a saint for everybody — unless they were martyred. There's also a rule I half-remember that a miracle can't be accepted as confirming a teaching the Church has already rejected, so the teaching gets settled first and the miracle is judged against it.

What I can't work out is what any of that presupposes.

The two-stage split makes it look like a miracle is just an event nature can't produce, identifiable with zero religious information — which sounds like it buys Hume's own definition. But then why two? If one miracle is enough to show God publicly endorsed this person, why isn't it enough? Is the doubling secretly a concession to Hume, or is it that the standard of proof goes up because the stakes go up (local vs universal veneration), which would make it some kind of threshold thing? And doesn't that no-false-doctrine rule mean the miracle does no independent work at all — if you already know the teaching is true you don't need it, and if you don't know that yet you're not allowed to use it? Or is it evidence for a different claim, that this person is in heaven and is holy, rather than for the religion?

Also: the doctors are supposed to look for healings that are instant, total and permanent. Is "medicine can't explain this" just an argument from ignorance, gap equals God? Or does the instant-and-permanent part do real work, and that's the bit that only looks Humean?

I'm not asking for sources or whether any of these healings actually happened. Assume the process is roughly as I described and tell me: what view of evidence and testimony does it only make sense under, which parts strictly require that view and which parts would sit fine with a bunch of different views, and where it contradicts itself.
