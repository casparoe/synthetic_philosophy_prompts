ok so I've been reading this insect sentience debate on LessWrong and the EA Forum on and off for years and I keep feeling like I'm making the same basic error somewhere in the middle of it and I want it pinned down.

Here's the setup as I understand it. The population numbers are astronomic — whatever the real figure is, call it quintillions-ish alive at any one time, heavily dominated by ants and termites — and the probability that any given one of them has morally relevant experiences is small, or unknown, or somewhere between 'basically no' and 'obviously yes' depending on who's talking. The naive move is to multiply: expected moral weight = N × p × w. N is huge, so even a p of 0.01 hands you a number that dwarfs every other cause, and then everyone accuses everyone else of fanaticism and the thread dies.

What I actually want explained is what's going on underneath that multiplication, because I think there are three different pictures hiding in it and I can't keep them straight:

(1) 'Many independent draws': whether a given insect matters is its own little coin flip, or varies by species or by individual, so across 10^18 insects the total is tightly concentrated — the law of large numbers basically hands you N × p × w plus a rounding error, and there's no gamble here at all, just an average.

(2) 'One switch': sentience is a single fact about a kind of neural architecture, so it's true of all of them or none of them. Now the total isn't concentrated at all, it's a bimodal nothing-or-everything lottery with deranged variance.

(3) What I suspect is actually right: it's a dial, not a switch — degrees of moral weight — and in that version almost all of the expected value lives in the extreme upper tail of your prior over the dial, so your conclusion gets set by a region of your own probability distribution you have essentially no introspective access to.

So: is that trichotomy the right way to carve it, and is (3) really where all the action is? Why does the difference between (1) and (2) vanish completely under straight expected value but change everything under any rule that cares about variance or that won't bet the whole farm on one unresolved hypothesis? I've got a half-baked rule I've been calling 'median-world budgeting' — plan as if the median hypothesis is true, then spend separately on finding out which world you're actually in — which seems to give sensible answers in (1) and obvious nonsense in (2), and I want to know whether that's a bug in my rule or a fact about the situation. And relatedly: how should I think about value of information here? Does the whole thing mostly collapse into 'go run the decisive experiment first', and does VOI scale differently across the three pictures?

Give me the gut-level version with just enough arithmetic that I can check it — I don't need proofs, but I do want to be able to tell when I'm double-counting the correlation or when a number is doing all the work.
