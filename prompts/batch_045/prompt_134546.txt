ok so here's roughly where I am and I'd like someone to tell me if it's a package or a pile. About four months ago I got moved off the growth team at work onto a small group that's supposed to be about "user value" rather than engagement (mid-size consumer app, I'll keep it vague), and because I'm the only person on it who'll read anything methodological I've somehow become the one who has to defend the sentence "this change makes users better off" in review. I've been reading about revealed preference on the side to get my head straight. My partner did a PhD in econ and keeps saying I'm doing welfare economics without a welfare concept, which I think is unfair, but I can't quite articulate why. So, my commitments:

1. Utility isn't a feeling or a quantity in the head. It's just a numbering of someone's choices that satisfies the consistency conditions. That's all it is, and it's all I can ever observe.

2. We still need to compare welfare across people. I'm not giving that up — otherwise I can't say anything about who our changes hurt and the whole cost-benefit thing we do collapses into just counting.

3. Our practical yardstick is willingness to pay in money, with distributional weights: a dollar of benefit counts for more when it lands on a low-income user. I know the weights aren't "in the data."

4. Preferences can be wrong. People pick things that are bad for them — impulse stuff, features they don't understand, habits they say they want to break — and I'm fine with friction, cooling-off periods and defaults that protect them from that.

5. What I'm not fine with is nudges that leave the options alone and just change how they're described or framed. Those feel like tricks even when they help, and I don't think they count as making anyone better off.

6. Choice overload is real, not a slogan: we cut a 40-item menu down to 8 once and users did measurably better on our own welfare-ish metric afterwards. So we curate, pretty aggressively.

7. When users violate the consistency conditions in our A/B tests — cycles, flipping when we rephrase a description — I treat it as noise and look at the aggregate, and I take the aggregate as evidence about what's better for them.

Is this coherent? What I want is an audit, not a lecture. Go through the pairs and tell me which are in real tension and which only look like they are, then rank the real ones by how bad they are for me specifically. For each one say what I'd have to give up and what that costs — i.e. which of my practical conclusions stops being defensible if I give it up — and if something has to go, tell me which one and why that one rather than its neighbour, don't leave me a menu. Three things I especially can't see my way through: whether 4 and 5 are different in principal or whether I just have an aesthetic preference for one kind of interference; whether 6 is really in conflict with 1 or whether I'm confusing myself about what the options even are; and whether the "it's just noise" move in 7 is already smuggling in more than 1 allows, given that the choices I'm measuring are the output of a menu I curated. Please argue from what my own commitments force — don't just tell me the literature disagrees or point me at a reading list.
