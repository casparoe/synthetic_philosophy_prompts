Here's my problem. I'm 71, retired a few years back from a plant physiology lab (35 years of membrane transport, mostly), and I've drifted into a philosophy reading group that meets at the library on Tuesdays — six of us, an ex-chemist, a retired GP, a couple of others. We've spent the last month on the old equality-of-what question and we're going in circles, because everyone has picked a label they like — Miriam is the capabilities person, Dave keeps saying resources, and I've come to think they're answering different questions without noticing. So I want a quiz that sorts us by what we actually say about cases instead of by which word we've glued to ourselves. Could you write it for me? Ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key that maps answer-patterns onto the real positions: welfarist ones (both level-of-welfare and welfare-opportunity versions), the resourist / hypothetical-auction-and-insurance view, capability views, the responsibility-sensitive luck-egalitarian variants of the above, and the non-egalitarian escape hatches (sufficientarian, prioritarian, and the plain do-nobody-owes-anybody-equality option). Nothing may ask anything as blunt as do you think equality is about welfare or resources — every question should be answerable by someone who has never heard the labels. We do it on paper with pencil, so I need a fixed set of ten with a scoring key, not a branching flowchart.

A few cases I definately want in, because that's where our group actually came apart: someone whose basal metabolic rate is 40% above the population mean, so they need far more calories for the same activity; a person who acquired a genuinely expensive taste involuntarily, as a side effect of a medication they had to take, next to someone who cultivated the same taste on purpose to look refined; someone with a severe phobia who could in principle turn up to the town council meeting but never will; a cheap-to-please person who is very content on very little sitting next to a miserable person who needs a great deal; someone whose one real aim is positional — to have more than the neighbour, or to be the best trumpet in the band; and two lives with the same total welfare, one lived fast and intensively to fifty and one mildly to a hundred, so I can see whether people are equalising a total or a rate. Where you can, work in the time dimension too: whether the thing being equalised is measured over whole lives or at time-slices, e.g. a front-loaded life with a miserable old age versus a back-loaded one.

One more thing that bugs me, and I'd rather it went in the notes after the key than into the questions themselves. In the lab you would never compare readings across two instruments before you'd fixed a zero and a unit, and nobody in this debate seems worried about who fixes them. Call it the calibration problem: comparing welfare across people looks like it needs a common zero and a common unit, and I can't see where those come from — and the capability people look like they have the same trouble the moment you ask how one central capability is weighted against another. If the calibration problem bites hard on some of these views and barely at all on others, say which and why; and if there's a standard move that dissolves it, explain it in a way a retired lab person would find satisfying rather than hand-wavy. Also please mark any combination of answers that nobody seems to actually defend, with a line on whether its incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored, and flag any two leaves your quiz can't really seperate — I'd much rather know where the instrument is blunt than have it paper over a difference that's really there.
