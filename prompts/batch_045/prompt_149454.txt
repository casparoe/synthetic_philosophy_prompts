I am a philosopher of science who has spent four years on the interpretive review panel of a mid-sized regional museum — the small committee that signs off on every wall text, object label, and audio-guide script before it is installed. Most of our work is unglamorous checking of dates, findspots, and provenance. The recurring difficulty, and the reason I am writing, is a different class of claim: the ones that run from objects to minds and to institutions. That this building was a temple. That these vessels served ritual feasting. That the uniformity of these weights shows a central authority enforcing a standard. That the figurines show a cult of a mother goddess. Nothing in the assemblage entails any of these; each is an inference across several links, and each is exactly the sort of sentence a visitor remembers, a journalist repeats, and a nationalist or colonial narrative later leans on.

I would like a procedure the panel can actually run. Its output is not a verdict on the archaeology but a decision about wording under constraint: we meet for ninety minutes, we handle five or six labels at a sitting, we usually have no regional specialist in the room, and the final sentence has to be plain enough for a twelve-year-old and honest enough for a curator to defend.

What I want from you is the operationalization, not a survey of theory. Some of the materials I would expect you to work with, so that we are starting from the same place: the old ladder-of-inference picture, on which reconstructing subsistence is easy and reconstructing ritual and belief is hardest, because the further up you go the more the objects underdetermine what you want to say; the point that processual and post-processual writers agree on despite everything else — that an assemblage is not a passive residue of behaviour but the product of depositional and post-depositional processes that must themselves be theorized, and that objects are meaningfully constituted rather than transparent traces; and the familiar demand that the static object be linked to the dynamic activity that produced it by some independently supported bridge, not by intuition.

The procedure should contain, at least: a taxonomy of claim types fixed before evidence is considered — occurrence of an activity, recurrent practice, rule-governed institution, representational or symbolic content, propositional belief — because the warrant required changes sharply across these; a requirement that the chain be warranted link by link, object to producing event to repeated practice to norm to meaning, with no borrowing of warrant across links and an explicit audit of where a draft label has quietly escalated from one to the next; a calibration condition, so that an inference form is used only where it has at least once been checked against a case whose answer is known independently, by documentary, ethnographic, or experimental means, and is labelled as uncalibrated when it has not; a symmetry or modern-analogue test, in which the same inference is run over a documented modern assemblage of similar kind — a household's refuse, a church hall's cupboards, a club's storage — and is refused if it returns a false reading there; a residual-category check, since symbolic and ritual readings are so often just the bin for whatever has no functional explanation yet; for institutional claims specifically, a short list of markers, of which more than one must be present — visible enforcement or sanction, differential access by role, durability across a generation, costliness not reducible to individual advantage, persistence under changed material conditions; a ceiling on belief attribution, since saying what people held to be true requires representational evidence of its own, a readable iconographic convention, a text, or a comparative anchor, and should fail without one; a hard stop where findspot and association are unreliable, which for us includes most of the old collections; a graded confidence vocabulary tied to the gates passed, so that the label says shows, or strongly suggests, or is consistent with, or is one of several readings, rather than leaving the assertive force to the curator's pen; and clearly marked hand-off points, where what is left is a question of values or of who has authority to speak, and the panel must stop treating it as an inference.

Two things I want stated rather than finessed. First, direction of error: false claims about past people's beliefs and institutions do present-day damage, and I want the procedure to force us to say which error we are readier to make, while being explicit that this choice is not itself epistemic. Second, the hand-off to non-archaeological evidence, particularly the testimony of descendant communities, which I want described precisely enough that it becomes neither deference nor dismissal.

Then run it on three of our cases. One is institutional: a Bronze Age urban assemblage of the Indus type, with hundreds of small cubical stone weights from many sites conforming to a single system of ratios, and stamped seals in the same deposits, where the draft label says a central authority enforced a uniform standard of measurement; tell me what survives and at what grade. One is a belief claim and will be unwelcome: our most reproduced text, written by our senior curator and unchanged for twenty years, accompanies a case of bronze weapons and tools recovered from river gravels and peat, and says they were offerings thrown into the water for a deity; I expect your procedure to force a rewrite, and I want to know precisely which gate blocks the sentence and what the strongest defensible replacement wording is. The third is a hand-off: a delegation from a descendant community asks us to describe a set of carved wooden objects, collected in the 1890s with almost no documentation, as the regalia of a hereditary society; the objects license some inferences and not the institutional one, and the community's own transmission is evidence of another kind. Show me the procedure stopping here rather than grinding the claim down.

Finally, be explicit about what would make you withdraw the procedure rather than refine it. Tell me which gate is most likely to be applied mechanically and produce absurdity, which step is really a judgment call wearing procedural dress, what a site would concretely have to yield to upgrade each claim you have blocked, and whether, if you are honest, the upshot of your own procedure is that a museum should mostly stop attributing beliefs and institutions on its labels. I would rather have that conclusion, stated plainly, than a procedure that lets us keep saying everything in a whisper.
