I'm a second-year neurocritical care fellow, and I somehow let myself get talked into giving the 40-minute talk at our hospital's Death Determination and Donation Policy Committee next month, where we're supposed to decide whether to start doing the newer retrieval technique — the one where, after death is declared on circulatory grounds, they reopen the chest and restart circulation to the abdomen while clamping off the vessels going to the head, so the kidneys and liver stay oxygenated until they're taken out. I care about this more than I expected to, for two cases. One: a 34-year-old with a catastrophic bleed, family decided to withdraw and consented to donation, and I stood there for the five minutes after the monitor flatlined while one of our senior ICU nurses muttered that we were all just waiting for him to die — in the same unit I've run codes where we got a pulse back after way longer than five minutes, and everyone knows it, we just call it "irreversible" because we've already decided not to restart. Two: a young woman declared dead by neurological criteria whose family refused donation because she was warm and pink and her chest was rising on the vent and they said she looked like she was sleeping, which is a thing no amount of explaining the brainstem to people fixes.

Here's what I actually want to argue, so you know what's getting attacked: that brain death and circulatory death are not two tests for one biological event, they're two thresholds we've built for two different jobs (one for deciding when it's OK to stop supporting a body, one for deciding when it's OK to take organs), that the statute's word "irreversible" is doing quiet work it can't really do in the circulatory case — what we mean is permanent, given that we've decided not to reverse it — and that we'd be more honest, and honestly more trustworthy, if we grounded retrieval in the authorization the person gave rather than in the claim that the donor is dead in the same sense a decapitated person is dead. I am absolutely not about to say "let's take organs from people who aren't dead," and if my argument sounds like that I've blown it.

So I need a Q&A prep sheet, and I need it for this room specifically, not a generic one. In the room: the abdominal transplant surgeon who runs the retrieval program and thinks in terms of the dead donor rule and public trust; our ICU nurse manager, who has to staff the cases and is the one who'll ask what any of this changes at 3am; the palliative medicine attending, who mostly thinks we already talk out of both sides of our mouths and will want to know what I'd say to a family; the hospital's in-house counsel, who will point out that our state adopted the 1981 uniform act, that it says "irreversible," and that the recent attempt to revise that act got abandoned as unworkable; a Catholic bioethicist on our ethics consult service; our family support coordinator, who sits with people through this; and the ICU medical director chairing it, who is sympathetic but hates anything that sounds like philosophy for its own sake. Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll land, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it, each with a reply I could actually deliver in about a minute, a note on what I should concede versus hold the line on, and a note on where my reply is weakest. Two specific things I'm dreading: the bioethicist is going to agree with part of my argument for reasons I badly don't want — there are Catholic writers who argue brain death isn't death at all, and if I let that alliance stand I'll get pushed somewhere I won't go — so tell me how to take the agreement without owning where it leads. And give me the one question I'm hoping nobody asks, and what I'd say if they do.
