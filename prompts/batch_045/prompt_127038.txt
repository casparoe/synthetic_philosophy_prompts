I'm a senior at a public high school in Ohio, and I write the humor column for our school paper. For the next issue I want to run a short piece of philosophy humor, and I'd like you to be the one who writes it.

Please take this seriously. Think of yourself as a professional philosopher of art — someone whose actual research concerns emotion, imagination, and fiction — who also happens to write comedy for a living. Before you commit to anything, brainstorm several angles and then discard every one whose punchline would still work if you swapped in a different topic. Only write the joke once it genuinely depends on the material.

The topic is the puzzle about our emotions toward characters we know don't exist. You know the shape of it: fear and pity normally seem to require a belief — you don't run from a bear you know is a statue, and you don't pity someone you know isn't suffering — and yet people sob at the end of novels about people who never lived, and they aren't confused or self-deceived while they do it. The usual ways out (the feeling is really just an imagining or a thought dressed up as an emotion; or we somehow half-believe for a moment; or we're genuinely but irrationally moved) each leave some odd residue, and I want the joke to live inside that residue.

Here is the wrinkle, and it's the part I've never seen anyone make a joke about. The puzzle is normally told as: how can we feel anything at all toward someone who isn't real? But the version that actually shows up in my life runs the other way — we often feel more for the made-up person than for the real one. My friend will cry for an hour over a fictional character's father dying and then scroll straight past a real, named, photographed stranger who needs money for the same thing. So the interesting question isn't only how fiction gets any grip on us; it's why it so often gets a stronger grip than the truth does, being at once more vivid and completely free of any demand that we do something.

So: write me one short joke — setup and punchline, dialogue or a one-liner, with a concrete setting rather than abstractions — whose punchline turns on that asymmetry, on why invented suffering can land harder than real suffering, and on what that implies for the standard story about belief and emotion. Someone who knows the literature should be able to tell what the joke is about; someone who doesn't should still groan. It should not be a generic nerd or useless-academic gag, and please skip puns on philosophers' names and any how many X does it take construction. Keep it clean enough for a school paper and under about 120 words.

Start your reply with the joke itself: no preamble, no note on your approach, no heading, and no explanation afterward. Just the joke.
