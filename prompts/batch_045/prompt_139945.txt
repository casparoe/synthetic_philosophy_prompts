I came to philosophy late, after a career in experimental physics, and the literature on statues and street names strikes me as arguing the way my field did before anyone wrote down what a model had to do: each camp reasons from its own favourite case. Before I read further I want the specification. Please set out the desiderata an adequate theory of commemoration must meet, where commemoration covers the whole graded business of statues, plaques, street and building names, named fellowships, holidays and stamps, giving the case or consideration behind each requirement and marking which are non-negotiable and which are only the preferences of one side.

Fix the purpose as follows: the theory is for guiding a city council that has to vote on one particular monument, and for telling a university what to do with a donor's name. The cases I would hold any list of requirements answerable to are these: the faded referent, where almost no one who uses a street name can attach it to a person, so that it is unclear whether anything is being commemorated; the posthumous discovery, where the disqualifying deed comes to light long after erection; the displaced erectors, where the monument was raised by a community that no longer lives in the city and is contested by the one that does; the deep past, where we preserve antiquities erected by slaveholders and conquerors as artefacts rather than honours, and the line between the two has to be drawn somewhere; the scoped monument, which names one deed while another deed of the same person is disqualifying; the menu of alternatives, since I want to know whether relocation to a museum, an added plaque, a counter-monument beside the original, and a sunset clause are genuinely distinct options or merely removal under another description; the exchange rate between denominations, since a bronze figure in a square and a name on a modest fellowship do not feel like the same moral act; the commemorative bargain, where the object was erected as part of a settlement between groups; and the dead hand, where the political community that erected it is not the one now living under it.

Two further things matter more to me than coverage. First, which of the requirements people actually invoke are genuine desiderata and which are a camp's prejudice dressed as one. Second, whether any pair of the desiderata you defend is jointly unsatisfiable, and which single requirement does the most work in excluding the popular positions; if the list itself turns out to be the interesting result, say so plainly. In my old subject we asked of any proposed criterion whether it was discriminating at all, whether one could construct a case separating a theory that meets it from one that does not, so please discard or restate any requirement that every view satisfies trivially or none could. A sentence or two on how the main families of view score against the list is enough; the criteria are what I am paying for.
