I'm putting together a short handout for a reading group on confirmation theory — we spent last session on the raven paradox (the one Hempel made famous) and I went in thinking I'd settled it years ago and came out unable to say what I actually believe — so I want to get my own verdicts down before I write anything up. Here is where my gut lands, case by case. (1) The first black raven I ever saw confirmed that all ravens are black. (2) The thousandth black raven in the same county confirms it, but barely. (3) A black raven found in a remote valley no ornithologist has visited confirms noticeably more than (2), about as much as (1) did. (4) A red apple in my kitchen confirms it, by a hair; I accept the equivalence condition (logically equivalent hypotheses are confirmed by exactly the same evidence), I accept that an instance of a generalization confirms that generalization, and I've decided to swallow the unpalatable conclusion rather than block the argument. (5) A white shoe pulled at random from my closet does nothing at all for it, and repeating that five hundred times still does nothing. (6) An exhaustive sweep of every non-black thing on a small island, finding no ravens among them, would confirm it strongly. (7) The black ravens I had already seen before I ever formulated the hypothesis do confirm it now, even though learning them again couldn't move my confidence. (8) Confirmation shouldn't depend on the order the evidence arrives in, or on when I happened to think of the hypothesis — though (2) and (3) make me nervous about this. (9) A black raven does not confirm "either all ravens are black or my kitchen tap drips." (10) A raven that looks black only because someone spray-painted it does not confirm anything.

The principle I keep half-believing, and which I suspect is what commits me to some of the above, is this: evidence confirms a hypothesis just in case the evidence would be more likely if the hypothesis were true than if it were false, and the degree of confirmation is just the ratio of those likelihoods — nothing else, no separate relevance or severity requirement. I'd like that tested against my verdicts and repaired where it fails. Please state the repaired principle precisely, clause by clause, telling me which of my verdicts motivates each clause; tell me which verdicts it cannot deliver and which of those I should give up; rank the required revisions by how much they cost me; and tell me what the result says about the case I genuinely go back and forth on — a bird I take for a raven and note to be black, which a better ornithologist later identifies as a lookalike species from the same genus.
