hey — I'm reworking the free will section of my intro course for the fall and I keep going in circles on the sphex wasp, so I want a second opinion before I commit to anything.

Background: my colleague teaches the same material and has a handout on "sphexishness" that he swears by, and I heard basically the same thing on a philosophy podcast a few weeks ago, so I take it this is the standard popular version. Here's roughly what the podcast guest said — I'm reconstructing it from memory, so the wording is mine but I think the content is faithful:

"The digger wasp Sphex is the classic case of a creature with no flexibility whatsoever. She stings and paralyzes a cricket, drags it to her burrow, leaves it on the threshold, goes inside to check the nest, comes back out and hauls the cricket in. But if you move the cricket a few inches while she's inside, she comes out, drags it back to the threshold, goes in to check again — and will keep doing that as many times as you like, indefinitely. She's a tiny automaton, a zombie running a fixed action pattern, with no capacity to reconsider anything. Dennett invented the word 'sphexish' for exactly this, and his positive view is the mirror image of it: human freedom just is flexibility — we have vastly more behavioural options and back-up routines, so we can't get trapped in a loop like hers. The awkward coda is that entomologists now think the wasp story is probably exaggerated or even invented, and that's a real problem, because if the anecdote goes then the whole 'free will is flexibility' picture goes with it, and compatibilism loses its main account of what freedom amounts to."

I've told students for years that the story is a rhetorical flourish, and I half remember Dennett himself conceding somewhere that she only repeats the routine a couple of times rather than forever — I genuinely cant remember where I got that, so tell me if I'm confabulating. But I also think my colleague is wrong that killing the anecdote kills the view, and I can't say why in one clean sentence.

So: how fair is that paragraph? Go through it and tell me which bits someone who Actually works on this would accept, which are overstatements or useful classroom simplifications, and which are just wrong. Things I especially want your read on:

- is "indefinitely / as many times as you like" doing work it shouldn't be, and does it matter if the real number is small but nonzero?
- is sphexishness supposed to be a global verdict on a creature (no flexibility at all, zombie, automaton) or something narrower and more local? And does it matter that the loop only shows up because the experimenter rigs up a situation the wasp would never meet?
- is "free will is flexibility" a fair compression, or does it lose something load-bearing (the fact that it comes in degrees, the role of reasons / self-control / evitability, whatever's actually central)?
- and is the coda fair — is the anecdote load-bearing for the view or just decoration, and would a defender of the view be bothered by losing it?

The bit I actually need most, though. I want to use a variant case in lecture and I'm not sure I've built it right. Suppose we found a related wasp that does the same thing, but with two twists: (a) she repeats the inspection a variable number of times, where the number of repetitions is fixed at the outset by a cue — say, how far she had to drag the prey — and never gets revised once set; and (b) if she hits that limit she switches to a totally different routine, abandons the burrow and digs a new one, but the switch itself is triggered by a simple internal count rather than by anything like taking stock of the situation. Call the first one "depth-limited sphexishness" if it helps. My question: does either twist get her out from under the charge, or have we just pushed the rigidity up a level — a rigid rule about when to be flexible?
