My co-author and I are drafting a paper on whether everyday conventions — queueing, tipping, jaywalking — generate obligations that are moral rather than merely conventional, and on what a visitor from a place with the opposite convention owes. Before we survey the live accounts, we want an adequacy specification: what a theory of conventional obligation would have to deliver before it is worth arguing about at all. Please write it in British English.

Give me the desiderata as a table with columns: desideratum | motivating case | verdict (non-negotiable, or a preference of one camp) | what it rules out. Each row should state, in one sentence, the case that makes the requirement bite, and each requirement should be tight enough that a theory could clearly fail it.

The pressures we want covered, plus any we have missed:

- Three verdicts must stay separable: that a violation is permissible and owes no one an apology; that it breaches a real obligation, but one owed only to the other participants; and that it is wrong in a way any affected party may complain about.
- The cases: jaywalking on an empty road where the risk is purely statistical; queue-jumping in a way that delays nobody; not tipping where the worker's wage is set on the assumption that customers do tip; a visitor from a no-tipping country; a convention whose own participants routinely exempt visitors; a convention that is locally contested or recently imported; and a demand for compliance made by a bystander who is not himself complying.
- Distinctions a theory may not collapse: the existence of a convention versus its having moral force; what participants owe one another versus what non-participants may demand; the wrong of non-compliance versus the wrong of free-riding; what an action signals in that setting versus its material effects; and the convention itself versus the distributive outcome it currently produces.
- One wrinkle we think is underexplored: some conventions appear to do their work only against a background of partial non-compliance or of foreign non-participation, so an export test — would the visitor's home convention still serve its point if everyone here adopted it? — can come apart from the question of what this visitor owes today. Say what a theory has to do with that.
- Flag any pair of desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied; that is the result we most want.

After the table, return a JSON object with fields: desiderata (an array of objects with keys id, statement, motivating_case, status, excludes), unsatisfiable_pairs (an array of objects with keys ids and why), most_excluding (an object with keys id and why, naming the single desideratum that knocks out the most candidate accounts), and underspecified (an array of strings naming questions the existing literature seems not to ask).
