I teach political philosophy, and I am drafting a paper on educational authority. My students keep treating the dispute over schooling as a dispute about choice, and I have become convinced that the real disagreement is upstream of that, in what the state thinks it is doing when it compels and funds schooling. So I want to reconstruct the implicit theory of the institutional package we actually have. Please take the following description as stipulated: I am not asking for a survey of jurisdictions or for case law, and I am happy to grant that it fits most U.S. states only roughly.

Compulsory-attendance law makes parents liable, sometimes criminally, if a child of a certain age is not, during school hours, either in a school or receiving instruction elsewhere. The state therefore insists that something we might call being schooled take place, and enforces that insistence against parents. But the content of what happens during those hours is regulated in a way that varies inversely with how closely the state is involved in providing it.

If the instruction happens at home, the same state typically imposes almost nothing on what is taught. There is no approval of curriculum, no requirement that the teacher know the subject, in some places no assessment of any kind. A parent with no qualifications may teach a child that the earth is a few thousand years old, or nothing of modern science at all, so long as the child is minimally occupied with instruction and the ordinary law of abuse and neglect is not triggered. And note that neglect enforcement is triggered by harm, not by educational deficiency: the state will intervene for a starving homeschooled child, but not, in practice, for one who cannot read.

If the instruction happens in a private school, the state wants attendance records, health and safety compliance, and sometimes a loose showing that instruction is comparable to public instruction. It does not require that the school admit all applicants, teach a comprehensive curriculum, or conduct itself as a secular institution. In most places it will also decline to fund it.

If the instruction happens in a district school, the state regulates content heavily: standards, mandated curricula, assessments, teacher certification. Yet here, and only here, parents get a partial veto over exposure. In many states a parent may withdraw a child from particular units on conscience grounds, with sex education the standard case, though not from the curriculum as a whole, and not from the assessment regime attached to it.

Finally, public money attaches to the seat a child occupies in a district school. Remove the child, and the money stays behind.

The configuration I cannot reconstruct has four limbs. The state compels the fact of schooling but not its content. It permits exit from the institution while refusing to follow the child with the funding. It grants parents a right against exposure to particular material inside the public system, while granting them, outside it, an almost unlimited right to determine content. And the deregulation of homeschooling is commonly defended on the ground that parents love their children and are therefore reliable agents of their interests, which sits badly with a public-school regime that treats parental conscience as a threat to the child's education whenever the two come apart.

So: what theory of the child's interests, of parental authority, and of the state's stake in education does this package presuppose? More specifically, I would like the following distinguished.

First, does the package presuppose that what the state may secure is custodial and socializing, rather than cognitive? On that reading, the state's interest is discharged by the child's being somewhere supervised, in the company of peers and of views other than the family's, and the state has no business in what anyone learns. Does the compulsory-attendance limb actually require that reading, or is it also available to a view on which the state is entitled to secure real learning and is simply failing to?

Second, does the asymmetry between the exposure veto and the content discretion presuppose a distinction between encounter and mastery, such that the state's interest is in the child's having encountered the range of reasonable views, an interest that a conscientious objection can defeat, while the question of what the child actually comes to know and be able to do is nobody's constitutional business? If so, is that a coherent place to stop, given that a withdrawal right exercised unit by unit can empty the curriculum while satisfying the exposure interest on paper?

Third, does the practice presuppose a conception of parental authority as a right of exit rather than a right of shaping, and is that conception stable, or does the homeschooling limb quietly concede the right of shaping in the one sector where the state's exposure interest is least well served?

I would also like to know which of these the practice strictly requires, which it merely fits well with, and where it is simply underdetermined, so that several quite different theories would license it. And then: what would have to change? Which of the four limbs becomes indefensible if the operative view were replaced by a child's-rights view on which an education is owed to the child as a precondition of her future autonomy, or by a civic-republican view on which the state's interest is in the formation of future citizens, or by a thoroughgoing parental-authority view on which education is an aspect of childrearing? If one of those views makes more of the package coherent than the others do, I want to know which, and what the residual incoherence is.
