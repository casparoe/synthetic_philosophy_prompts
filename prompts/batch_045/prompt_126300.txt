So I'm one of the volunteer mods on a history wiki (nothing fancy, a few thousand articles) and we run something we call the Fringe Claims Desk: every claim about the past that isn't in the textbooks gets exactly one of three tags — pseudohistory, live controversy, or settled. Nearly all our precedents were set by Roz, who's away till March, and I've got one to tag and I honestly cant tell which way she'd jump. Her rulings so far:

- Atlantis as a real Bronze Age empire in the Atlantic, wiped out in a single day → pseudohistory
- ancient astronauts behind the pyramids and the Nazca lines → pseudohistory
- Fomenko's New Chronology, i.e. that everything recorded before about 1600 is a reconstruction stitched together out of duplicated accounts, with a global Great Tartaria empire behind it → pseudohistory
- the Tartaria mud-flood business, buried ground-floor windows and all → pseudohistory
- a comet impact at the start of the Younger Dryas → live controversy
- the claim that some Aboriginal Australian oral traditions preserve accurate memories of coastlines drowned at the end of the last ice age → live controversy, on the grounds that researchers who work with those communities take it seriously and it makes testable predictions about submerged sites
- a retired Oxford engineering professor's claim that Britain's stone circles were all laid out using a single standard unit of length he inferred from hundreds of measurements and called the megalithic yard → pseudohistory
- Norse voyages to Newfoundland before 1492 → settled, with a note Roz put in the style guide saying we'd have tagged this pseudohistory in 1900 and been wrong for seventy years

The new one: a retired hydrographic surveyor with no academic post, who publishes on his own site and in a couple of low-ranking journals, argues that a submerged stone mole off the coast at a site I'll call Kalbiyah isn't the Ptolemaic harbour structure it's always been taken for but a Middle Bronze Age one, roughly 1,300 years older — and, more than that, that this whole class of coastal installation along that coast has been systematically misdated, because the regional ceramic sequence it's dated by rests on a circular assumption. Three things make me want to be fair to him: he dug out the 1930s excavation notebooks and showed that three pottery lots were logged under the wrong trench; he said in advance which result from his new sediment cores would make him abandon the whole idea; and he already shifted his date by sixty years after someone pointed out a mixing effect. Three things make me want to bin it: three specialist reviews have rejected it in fairly brutal terms, the one emeritus professor of maritime archaeology who's commented has said only that the notebooks point deserves a proper re-excavation, and his fieldwork is now part-funded by a nationalist movement that puts his mole on T-shirts — which I know is a genetic fallacy to hold against him, but it does make me suspect motivated reasoning wearing a lab coat.

What I'd like is: 1) the first rule that fits all eight of Roz's verdicts, and what it predicts for the mole, 2) a second, genuinely different rule that also fits all eight, and what it predicts, 3) a third if you've got one, including what it would say about the T-shirt money and the nationalist funding, 4) your best guess at the tag, the closest of the eight precedents, and your single strongest reason, 5) the one extra hypothetical case that would best seperate whichever rules are still standing. Exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no intro and no summing-up at the end, and in British English please — my flatmate already says I write like an American and I'd rather not make it worse.
