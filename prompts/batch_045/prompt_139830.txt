I recently ran into the whole fink/mask business about dispositions and I think I have the shape of it. As I understand the setup: you want to say a glass is fragile in virtue of what it would do if it were struck, and then two annoying cases show up. One is where the disposition disappears right when the stimulus arrives — some wired-up contraption that renders the glass non-fragile the instant you hit it, so the conditional says "not fragile" while your gut says "fragile, we just never get to see it." The other is where the disposition is still fully there but something else blocks the manifestation, like an antidote already in the bloodstream so the poison never does its work. Fink and mask. Fine. What I can't tell is whether this is a distinction that actually does work or just clever bookkeeping, so I want to watch it try to do work on a case I know something about: Y2K.

Facts as I have them, so we're not arguing about history (if I've got something wrong, correct it in one line and carry on with the corrected version): huge amounts of software, mostly old COBOL on mainframes but also embedded gear, stored years as two digits, so "00" was ambiguous and date arithmetic and comparisons would go wrong at the rollover. Nobody had anything like a complete inventory of what was affected or where. The fixes I know of came in roughly four flavors: expand the date fields and reformat the data; leave the two digits in place but add logic mapping them onto a sliding window of years; leave the legacy code alone and put a wrapper or bridge in front of it that translates dates on the way in and out; or retire/replace the system outright. January 1 2000 arrived and more or less nothing happened, and ever since, the popular verdict is that the whole thing was hype.
