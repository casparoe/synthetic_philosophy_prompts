I teach high-school physics, so I'm used to being the family's designated person for insisting that we really can know things. That role got harder at a dinner a few weeks ago when my brother-in-law announced that the moon landings were staged and that, come to it, none of us is in a position to *know* anything about historical events we weren't present for. Rather than argue, I went and started reading about Wittgenstein's *On Certainty* — the notebooks from the last eighteen months of his life, published in 1969 — because I remembered it as the text that takes seriously the idea that some things must be exempt from doubt. I also found a long blog post by someone who has plainly worked through the secondary literature, and who summarizes the resulting position, which he calls hinge epistemology, roughly as follows.

Wittgenstein's target is Descartes, who thought he could doubt everything at once and then rebuild knowledge on the single certainty of his own existence. Wittgenstein's reply is that a doubt only makes sense against a background of things that are not in doubt: to call one proposition into question you have to hold others steady, so a doubt that doubted everything would not be a doubt at all. The propositions that do this anchoring work are the hinges — that the earth has existed for a very long time, that I have two hands, that this is the name everyone calls me — and they are not supported by evidence and could not be, since they are what make the giving and asking for evidence possible in the first place. That is also why they cannot be justified: any justification would have to proceed inside the game the hinge itself makes possible, so demanding one is like demanding a proof of the rules of chess. Hinges are the things we are objectively certain of, the ones no sane person would seriously call into question; but since nothing grounds them, they are not really beliefs at all and function more like rules of a game, or like the bed of the river along which inquiry flows rather than one more current within it. This is the point at which Wittgenstein improves on Moore: Moore was right to stop somewhere, but wrong to describe his stopping point as knowledge, because knowledge-attributions belong to the language game that hinges make possible, and a hinge is not the kind of thing that can be known. The upshot is that the problem of radical skepticism dissolves rather than being solved: the skeptic's doubt is not a doubt but noise, so there is nothing to refute. It also has an uncomfortable consequence for disagreement, since hinges vary between cultures and forms of life, and what stands fast for us is simply our hinge system; when you meet someone whose hinges differ, argument is powerless, and all you can do is describe your system, try to convert them, or shrug.

The passage convinced me while I was reading it, and it matches what I half-remember from an undergraduate epistemology course, but the final sentence is what made me suspicious, because it seems to imply that my brother-in-law and I are just running different hinge systems and that nothing more can be said than that. So I'd like an audit rather than an introduction. Which sentences would someone who works on hinge epistemology accept as stated, which are forgivable simplifications that a popularizer is entitled to, which are live interpretive disputes in the literature that the passage quietly settles by fiat, and which would they flatly reject? I am especially unsure about three moves: the inference from hinges make justification possible to hinges cannot be justified, the description of hinges as rules rather than beliefs, and the slide from hinges are not rationally grounded to hinge systems are all equally good. Finally, do the two conclusions actually follow from whatever survives scrutiny — that radical skepticism dissolves rather than needing an answer, and that reasoned argument with someone whose hinges differ from yours is therefore pointless?
