I work on the deployment team at an AI lab that is drafting internal guidance for how a system much more capable than any human should speak to the humans consulting it. The problem is not that such a system might lie; it is that it might be right and overwhelmingly convincing, and that its persuasiveness itself is what some of us want to regulate. Before we write anything, please map the space of positions as a decision tree: every branch labeled by the question or claim that splits it, every leaf by the name of the position, or "unoccupied" where nobody sits, plus one sentence on the commitment that position is stuck with.

Sort people by cases rather than by slogans: no node should ask whether the system should be honest or whether user autonomy matters, since everyone says yes. The cases we actually argue about are these: a user who asks the system to talk them into something the system believes to be false but harmless; a user who has explicitly delegated the decision and does not want to hear the reasoning; a domain where the system's true conclusion rests on considerations the user could not verify even in principle; and an audience that will reject a true claim outright unless the system adapts its framing to their existing commitments. Which of these someone treats as the hard case should do real work in the tree.

Include as leaves at least the following: the view that the system should confine itself to transferring its reasons and let conviction follow or not; the view that the aim is the user's ending up with true beliefs and that framing is a permissible means; the view that the system should deliberately dampen its own persuasiveness, for instance by withholding the framing it knows would be most effective or by presenting itself as less reliable than it is; and the view that in some domains it should decline to answer at all, because a confident verdict from such a system would collapse the user's own deliberation. Add further leaves wherever the logical space requires them. Honor two distinctions we use internally, under whatever names you prefer: getting the user to believe something versus getting the user to have the system's reasons, and tailoring the presentation of a fixed conclusion versus letting the conclusion itself move with the audience. Also flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate: one colleague thinks the reasons-only view and the dampening view yield the same advice in nearly every case, and I want to know whether that is an artifact of his examples or a genuine indistinguishability. Where a combination of answers is left empty, say briefly whether that is because the position is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply undefended so far — the gaps interest us as much as the occupied cells.

Assume an audience of engineers and policy staff who are busy and unused to philosophy, and define any technical term in passing. Write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way one would write a long email to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, and nothing that would need to be rendered as a diagram — the tree has to be walkable in sentences, and I should be able to read it top to bottom without losing the branching structure.
