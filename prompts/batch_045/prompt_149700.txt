Two people at my local rationalist meetup got into an argument about whether Dutch book arguments actually establish anything, and I genuinely couldn't tell who had the better of it.

(1) Nadia: if your credences violate the probability axioms, there is a finite set of bets, each of which your own credences rank as fair or favorable, that together lose money for you in every possible outcome; so incoherent credences are rationally defective, and that is the whole case for probabilism.

(2) Toby: that shows exploitability, not irrationality. A bookie who already knows how things will turn out can offer me odds I think are fair and take my money with certainty, and no one thinks that reveals a flaw in my credences. So the argument must be secretly assuming the bookie has no informational advantage; and once you make that explicit, what is left is only the claim that I shouldn't accept a package that is bad in expectation by my own lights, which is close to circular.

(3) Nadia: no. In the theorem the book is constructed from nothing but my own quoted fair prices and loses for me in every state; the bookie needs to know my prices, not the world. The load-bearing assumptions are different ones: stakes denominated in a unit whose value doesn't vary with the outcome, costless trading, and willingness to bet either side at your fair price.

(4) Toby: and that first assumption fails exactly where it matters. With diminishing marginal utility of money, or when you're betting on a financial crash, on hyperinflation, or on whether you'll be alive next year, a package that is a sure loss in dollars can be a gain in utility, and a perfectly coherent agent can be booked in dollars.

(5) Nadia: then run the argument in utility units, or restrict to stakes small enough that utility is locally linear in money.

(6) Toby: you can't just swap in utils — expected-utility representation already takes probabilities as an input, so that route is a different argument with heavier commitments, and it begs the question if it's offered as a defense of probabilism. And the small-stakes move only buys approximate additivity and approximate conditionalization, minus an epsilon, whereas what Bayesian epistemology wants is the exact norms; the gap between approximate and exact is the entire content of the conclusion.

(7) Nadia: the diachronic version has the same structure; it doesn't require that I plan in advance to keep betting, only that at each later time my then-current credences sanction the bet I'm then offered.

(8) Toby: then it's a proof about agents who never learn to decline. An agent who precommits to refusing any bet after the first is immune, and nothing in the argument shows that policy is irrational — so conditionalization gets no support here either.

Please go through this move by move: who is right about which point, which moves are doing real work and which are verbal, and what both of them are leaving out. There is also one wrinkle they never reached, which I'd like weighed: the theorem assumes the bookie can offer unbounded stakes and enforce collection in every state. If stakes are capped, every bet carries a fixed transaction cost, and the agent can default in her worst state, then whether you can be booked at all depends on how large your incoherence is relative to those frictions — you are exploitable only above a threshold. Does that help Toby, or does it just show that the argument is an idealization of the ordinary scientific kind, no worse than frictionless planes?
