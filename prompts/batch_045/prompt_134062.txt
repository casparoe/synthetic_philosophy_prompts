Hey — I'm an ML engineer, mostly RL and forecasting, and a friend doing a PhD in epistemology has dragged me into van Fraassen's Reflection principle. As I've got it: your current credence in A, conditional on the hypothesis that your future self's credence in A will be x, ought to equal x — you're supposed to defer to your later self as a kind of expert about A. I gather there's a diachronic Dutch book / Dutch strategy argument bolted on to justify it, and that the principle quietly assumes future-you hasn't forgotten anything and isn't impaired, but she waved at that bit and I didn't follow. Three worries I jotted down that I can't tell apart: you know you'll suffer amnesia and lose the evidence you have now; you know you'll be given something that makes you wildly overconfident in a way that has nothing to do with the truth; and self-locating-ish cases where the "same" proposition seems to shift meaning between now and later. I have no idea which of those are real counterexamples and which are just cases where I've mis-stated the principle.

The reason I care is that I'm building something where the analogy feels load-bearing. I have a forecasting model that's continuously fine-tuned on a stream of new data, and I've been toying with a regulariser I'm calling "trajectory Reflection": instead of only requiring the current checkpoint's predictive distribution to match the expected distribution of the *next* checkpoint one step out, require it to be a martingale across the whole run — current output equals expected output at the final checkpoint, i.e. I penalise the gap between checkpoints' predictions and a Polyak-averaged target that's computed from the model's own later self (basically target-network / self-distillation plumbing, just extended over the whole trajectory rather than one step). It seems to do something similar to what Reflection demands, but I can already see three places the mapping strains: my replay buffer is a sliding window, so old evidence genuinely falls out of the system; the model has no representation of itself at all, so "conditional on my future credence being x" isn't an event I can even condition on without bolting on a separate meta-predictor head that predicts the future checkpoint's output; and in RL I've been burned by exactly this move before — bootstrapping toward estimates produced by an earlier or sibling network is one leg of the deadly triad, and it's the thing that makes training blow up rather than the thing that makes it sane.

So: restate Reflection (and the Dutch strategy argument, and the counterexample cases, whatever the real ones turn out to be) as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use — checkpoints, SGD trajectories, predictive distributions, softmax probabilities vs. ensemble disagreement vs. epistemic uncertainty, target networks and Polyak averaging, TD bootstrapping vs. Monte Carlo returns, self-distillation, calibration, drift in the data distribution, a model updating toward a target computed by another version of itself. I'm fluent in all of that and I'm not fluent in philosophy — I had to look up "credence" — so keep the source side in plain English and put the work into the translation. Then give me the residue report: what maps cleanly, what only fits if I smuggle in an assumption the ML framework doesn't have, and what has no counterpart at all. Specifically I want to know whether the normativity survives (Reflection says *should*; my regulariser is just a loss term, and "you'd be exploitable by a bookie over time" isn't obviously a thing I can say about a training run), whether my trajectory-martingale thing is actually Reflection or merely adjacent to it (moment matching? consistency regularisation? something like expected calibration over time?), whether a system satisfying it would count as satisfying Reflection or only appearing to, and whether the residue points at something my framework can't express or at Reflection being less sharp than it looks. My hunch is that Reflection is a martingale condition with some moralising glued on — tell me if that's wrong and exactly where it goes wrong.
