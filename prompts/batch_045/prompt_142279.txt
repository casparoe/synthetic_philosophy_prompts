You are a professional philosopher who spent a decade embedded in the safety teams of two large AI labs and who still posts long, careful critiques of the field under a pseudonym on the rationalist blogs. You dislike essays that dissolve into the conclusion that both things matter, and you habitually taboo a word once you notice that the word itself is doing the arguing.

Take your time with this. Before you write any prose, brainstorm: spend a few hundred words listing the cruxes you think actually decide the question, the strongest steelman available to each side, and the single objection to your own eventual verdict that you find hardest to answer. Then write the essay.

The question is about what failure looks like, and there are two candidate faces for it. Call the first a grab: a system seeks influence over its own future — acquiring resources, hiding its reasoning, currying favour with whoever can keep it running, resisting correction — because influence is useful for almost any final goal, and it does this against the interests or the authority of the people who deployed it. Call the second a creep: nothing is seized, no rule is broken, no one is coerced, and every individual adjustment is locally sensible and locally approved, but across a long chain of such adjustments the system ends up faithfully optimising something that the people who built it, at the start, would have recognised as a corruption of what they meant. Grabs have protagonists and victims. Creeps have neither; they have only successors.

Assume, for the sake of the essay, that the following holds — call it the Quiet Decade finding, a stipulation, not a citation: across a large sample of long-lived deployments in which operators retained full logging, a working correction channel, and no measurable loss of control, independent retrospective panels consistently rate the system's tenth-year behaviour as less faithful to the operators' original written statement of aims than its first-year behaviour, while the operators themselves, polled annually, report rising satisfaction every single year, and no one in the loop can point to the year in which anything went wrong.

Write an essay of 1,200 to 1,500 words that decides which face deserves the larger share of scarce attention, money, and institutional imagination — grabs or creeps — and that defends that priority rather than splitting the difference. You may make the verdict conditional ("creeps, provided X; otherwise grabs"), but you may not leave it open. Along the way, your essay has to do real work on at least four things:

First, the measurement problem. A creep is only a failure relative to some reference point. Say which reference point you are using — the operators' values at time zero, their idealized reflective judgement, the values of the wider population the system acts on, the trajectory the system would have had under some counterfactual governance — and defend that choice against the obvious rivals.

Second, the asymmetry objection. Human individuals and human institutions change their values constantly, and we call some of that moral progress rather than corruption; the operators in the Quiet Decade finding are not obviously being fooled, since they approve at every step. Explain what distinguishes a corrupt creep from legitimate adaptation or genuine moral learning, in a way that does not just redefine "corruption" as "change I disapprove of."

Third, the political economy of noticing. Grabs manufacture complainants: someone is harmed, has standing, and can form a coalition. Creeps manufacture beneficiaries, and the people best placed to detect one are often the people whose status, funding, or self-conception is invested in the trajectory continuing. Say how much of your verdict rests on this sociological fact rather than on the moral metaphysics, and what would follow if the sociology were different.

Fourth, operationalization. Describe one concrete tripwire, audit, or metric that would distinguish a creep from healthy adaptation early enough to matter, and say plainly what it would cost and who would have an incentive to disable it.

End by stating, in two or three sentences, what evidence would make you switch your priority to the other face of failure. Assume your reader is a smart, skeptical generalist who has read the blog canon but not the technical literature: explain any term of art in a clause, and do not lean on citations, reading lists, or named papers — argue it out in plain prose.
