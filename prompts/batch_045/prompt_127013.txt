I am assembling a diagnostic exercise for a graduate seminar on vagueness, and I want the deliverable to be a classification instrument rather than a summary: a decision tree, or equivalently an eight-question sorting quiz with a scoring key, that leads a student from verdicts on concrete cases to a named position. The seminar is roughly half philosophy and half computer science and mathematics. Everyone is fluent in classical first-order logic, possible-worlds and many-valued semantics, computability, and basic order theory, but almost nobody knows the vagueness literature. So: gloss every term of art in a line, and build the tree out of the structure of the problem rather than out of what is conventionally said about it. I do not want reading suggestions; I want the instrument.

The leaves must include at least epistemicism, supervaluationism (with global and local variants split into separate cells only if they genuinely occupy different ones), subvaluationism, degree-functional or fuzzy semantics, contextualism including scorekeeping or dynamic variants, and ontic or worldly indeterminacy. You may add at most two further leaves if you think the space has room — a degree-supervaluationist hybrid and some form of nihilism or error theory seem the obvious candidates — but say why you are adding them and what question separates them from their siblings.

The work is in the branch questions, and I want the tree derived from the logical space first, with positions located inside it afterwards, not reverse-engineered from a list of names. The nodes I currently take to be load-bearing are these, and I would rather be corrected than humoured: first, whether a vague predicate has a perfectly sharp extension at all; second, if the slack is not located in the extension, whether it appears as a truth-value gap, a truth-value glut, a continuum of degrees of truth, a content that the context has not yet fixed, or an indeterminacy in the object itself; third, whether it is determinate which cases are borderline, that is, what each view can say about higher-order vagueness; fourth, how the Sorites is blocked. On the last point my understanding is that supervaluationism preserves classical consequence and abandons the tolerance premises, while subvaluationism keeps the tolerance premises and blocks the argument by giving up something like the unrestricted validity of modus ponens. If that is backwards or too crude, say so and put the branch where it actually falls. Every node must be phrased as a claim or case a student can answer without knowing which leaf it leads to, and no node may ask which theory the student finds attractive.

Three cases must be used as nodes, because they seem to me to do the discriminating work that the standard heap and bald-man series does not. First, a predicate whose extension is by construction perfectly sharp but whose boundary is provably uncomputable: something like flurg, which applies to a number exactly when that number is below a cut-off given by a Busy-Beaver-style function, so that no finite investigation, however long, could ever locate the boundary. Does a sharp boundary plus guaranteed ignorance of it collapse into indeterminacy, and which views can say that it does not? Second, a matched pair: a case where the relevant magnitude is perfectly determinate but the location of the threshold in the semantics is obscure, against a case where it is the magnitude or the object itself that looks indeterminate, such as the spatial extent of a cloud or the membership of a slowly disintegrating artifact. Third, a case of cooperative sorting: two competent speakers whose thresholds differ slightly must produce one shared verdict on a single series, and know that they differ. I want that node to separate views on which the disagreement reveals something about content from views on which it reveals something about truth, and I suspect it cannot separate them cleanly, in which case say so.

The key should map answer patterns onto leaves, and it must also do two negative things. Every combination of answers the tree permits but that no position occupies should be listed and diagnosed: is the cell empty because the combination is incoherent, because it is merely unattractive, or because nobody has worked it out? And any pair of leaves the instrument cannot separate should be flagged as unseparated, with your verdict on whether the residual difference is substantive or merely verbal — I expect trouble at least between supervaluationism and epistemicism on the existence of sharp boundaries, and between supervaluationism and contextualism on disagreement cases.

Finally, I want the epistemic status of each step made explicit. Mark which branches and which mappings are settled, in the sense that they follow from what the position is, and which rest on a contested reading of a position or on a live disagreement among people who know the topic; where a mapping is contested, give both mappings rather than presenting one as the consensus. Tell me in particular which of my assumptions above — about where the load-bearing branches are, about subvaluationism and modus ponens, and about what the three cases are supposed to show — you think are wrong, contested, or merely stipulative on my part.
