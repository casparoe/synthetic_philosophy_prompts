hey — I need something sharper than the usual "is psychiatry medicalising normal life" essay, so bear with the setup.

I sit on a service-user involvement panel at a mental health trust (volunteer thing, couple hours a month), and three things came up this year that I can't work out. I want to know what the trust actually thinks a diagnosis *is*.

1. In our clinic a diagnosis is made from tick-box criteria: count the symptoms, hit the threshold, and for most categories there's a clause saying it only counts if it causes you distress or gets in the way of your life. Two clinicians have to agree before it goes on the record. As far as I can tell, when the manual was last revised the field tests of the new criteria produced pretty unimpressive agreement between clinicians for some very common diagnoses — and this got treated as a technical problem to be managed with better training, not as a reason to doubt the categories.

2. Everyone with an Asperger's diagnosis got a letter saying their diagnosis was now autism spectrum disorder. People were furious, and not just about the admin: lots of them said things like "that isn't what I am" or "I built my life around that word." The clinicians kept saying, very patiently, that it's the same condition, nothing about you has changed, only the label.

3. Someone on our panel proposed that diagnoses come with a review date, like a repeat prescription — three years, say — after which the diagnosis has to be made again or it lapses. She also floated putting a note on the diagnosis letter saying how many times the category has been revised and how much more common it's got since it was introduced. The clinicians hated both ideas, but I couldn't tell whether their objections were philosophical or just about workload.

So here's what I want. It looks to me like the two sides in episode 2 are running different theories of what a psychiatric kind is: the clinicians seem to assume the diagnosis picks out something that sits still while our words for it move around, and the patients seem to assume the label did some of the work of making them the kind of person they turned out to be. I've heard this called the looping effect and something like "making up people" — I want the sharper version, and I want to know what each of the three practices above commits the trust to.

Specifically:

- Does the distress-or-impairment clause, and especially the insistence that two clinicians agree before a diagnosis is real, presuppose that these categories are discovered rather than made? My guess is that requiring inter-rater agreement only makes sense if you think there's a stable kind out there that both clinicians are trying to track, and that disagreement is error. But if these are the interactive kind of kind — the ones that change when the people classified get classified — what are those agreement statistics even measuring, and does the training fix make it worse rather than better?
- Is "nothing about you has changed, only the label" even available to someone who takes looping seriously? If people really were made up under one description, doesn't it follow that reclassification is a real change in them, not a relabelling — and that the letter was, in some sense, wrong to be reassuring? Or can the clinician say: the underlying condition is stable, it's only the way people relate to the label that moved?
- What would the review-date proposal actually commit us to if it were adopted? It seems to presuppose that a diagnosis can go stale not because the person got better but because the kind moved under them. Is that coherent, or does it collapse into something silly like "your depression is true as of 2023"? And is the "how common it's got" note just the same thought in a more honest form, or is it doing something different — like admitting that prevalence is evidence about the kind rather than about the world?
- And where is this underdetermined? Could a straight realist — someone who thinks psychiatric kinds are real natural kinds with messy boundaries and imperfect criteria — run the exact same clinic, send the exact same letter, and only come apart from the looping picture on the review-date question? Because I need to know which of these I can argue from when I go back to them and which are genuinely open.

Basically: what does the trust have to believe to run its clinic the way it does, and what could it believe without changing a thing?
