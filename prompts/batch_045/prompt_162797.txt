I sit on the prioritisation committee of a regional health board, and we rank funding proposals without any formula, arguing case by case. I have been reading around social choice theory in the evenings and got pulled up short by Harsanyi's aggregation theorem: as I understand it, if each person's preferences and the social ordering both satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, and the social ordering respects unanimity over lotteries, then social welfare has to be expressible as a weighted sum of individuals' cardinal utilities — which sounds like a derivation of something utilitarian-ish, except that the weights are left entirely free, so it seems to tell me nothing about prioritising the worse off. A colleague says the theorem settles everything and that any residual pull towards priority is simply incoherent; another says we should drop the expected-utility assumption on the social ordering and stop worrying. Before I argue with either of them I want to know what I actually think, so here are my verdicts on a set of hypotheticals, all on a welfare scale where 0 is a life not worth living and 10 is excellent, with everyone stipulated to be risk-neutral wherever that matters and no one else affected. (1) Anna is at 3, Ben at 8, and one of them can get +2 for certain: I give it to Anna. (2) Anna, still at 3, asks for a treatment that gives her a fifty-fifty chance of +4 or −1: I let her have it and I count it as a gain of about +1.5, deferring to her own attitude to risk. (3) Same Anna, but now the committee chooses between a certain +2 for her and a fifty-fifty chance of +5 or −0.5, which she would take: I choose the certain +2, because I don't think we should gamble with the welfare of people who are already badly off — which is the same shape of gamble as in (2) with the opposite verdict, the only difference being who imposes it. (4) Option A inflicts a loss of 6 on one identified patient for certain; Option B spreads it so that sixty patients each face a one-in-sixty chance of the same loss, one person affected in expectation: I take B, and by more than a little, even though everyone involved would be indifferent between the two. (5) Programme P gives +0.1 to each of 10,000 people, a total of 1,000 units; Programme Q gives +5 to each of 100 people, a total of 500: I take Q, because a hundredth of a point on this scale isn't a benefit anyone would ever notice, and I don't think total sums should automatically win. (6) Preventing the existence of ten children whose lives would sit at level 1 strikes me as a larger good than lifting ten existing adults from 3 to 5; but bringing ten extra children into existence at level 8 strikes me as no benefit at all against the same alternative, even though the total is far larger.

My own tentative principle, such as it is: the weighted sum should govern risks people choose for themselves, something more risk-averse should govern risks we impose on them, and behind the veil of ignorance I should first put a probability over how many people there will be and only then an equal chance over which of them I am — which I think pushes me towards average rather than total welfare and might explain (6). Please take that apart and repair it rather than humouring it. I want a principle, or two or three candidates, stated precisely enough that someone else could apply it: tell me which clause each of my verdicts motivates, what it says about the case I keep going back and forth on (a treatment for 1,000 patients at level 2 that gives each a ninety per cent chance of +3 and a ten per cent chance of −4, with everyone consenting and expected value clearly positive), and what it predicts for some case I haven't listed where it comes apart from a plain weighted sum. Then be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which one I should give up, and say plainly whether what is left still deserves to be called a version of Harsanyi's conclusion or whether I have denied one of its premises — and if so which, and at what cost. Please write in American English, as I am drafting this up with an American co-author; and please argue it out rather than pointing me to a reading list.
