I'm in eleventh grade and I run my school's philosophy club — eight of us, Thursday lunchtimes — and at the end of term we each have to give a two-minute piece at a school ideas night, in front of parents and teachers who have not thought about any of this before. Our current unit is dream skepticism, and the text we began with is the old Chinese story in which a man named Zhuangzi wakes up and does not know whether he is Zhuangzi who has just dreamed of being a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming that it is Zhuangzi. I have deliberately not gone looking for the original wording, because I only care about the story as a philosophical set-up. My own two minutes can only cover one version of the worry, so I need help choosing between them, and I would much rather have a verdict than a survey: please commit to one ordering, with reasons, and don't fall back on saying that it depends what we mean by knowledge.

The choice is between eight variants of the set-up that our club came up with, and we invented two terms that we keep using, so let me define them first: by the anchor state we mean whichever condition you happen to be in at the moment you ask the question, and a set-up is swap-symmetric if, from the inside, nobody in any of its states can tell which state they are in. The variants are: (1) the story as it stands — one human state, one butterfly state, you wake from the second into the first, and both were equally vivid; (2) the nested version, in which the butterfly was itself only dreaming that it was a man, and you cannot tell how deep you are; (3) the scheduled version, in which the two states alternate every eight hours with memory carried across, so neither is privileged by being first, or recent, or longer; (4) the amnesiac version, in which the butterfly state remembers nothing of the human one and has no concepts like dream or remember at all, while the human state remembers both; (5) the lopsided version, in which the human life is dense and causally coherent and the butterfly life is thin, gappy, and barely a life; (6) the witness version, in which a friend you have excellent reason to think is not a butterfly assures you that you are a human and awake, except that you only ever encounter this friend in the human state; (7) the hidden-third version, in which there is some further condition you have no memory of at all and cannot rule out being in right now; and (8) the crowded version, in which a thousand creatures are each dreaming the others and there is no principled way to say whose dream is the one that is really going on.

What I want is a tier list of these eight, from the one that does the most damage to the ordinary claim that you know you are a human being and are awake right now down to the one that does the least, with a single sentence of justification for each and a note if you think any two of them are really the same variant in different words. Then, for whichever variant you put in the top tier, give me a number between 0 and 1 for how much of that ordinary confidence you think it actually destroys for someone who takes it seriously, and say what the remainder is resting on. Finally — the part I most want a definite answer on — tell me which single variant you think Zhuangzi's own point, as you read it, is best equipped to handle, and which one it is worst equipped to handle: our guess in the club is that the force of the story comes almost entirely from the return, the fact that he wakes up, and that variant 3 quietly removes it, so 3 would be the hardest case for him. Tell me plainly if that guess is wrong and why. Since I have to stand up and say this out loud, also mark where your ordering is a judgement call you would expect other people to contest, and where you think anyone who disagrees with you would simply be making a mistake.
