I am a journalist working on a feature about whether the weights of frontier AI systems should be published, and I keep running into one line that is doing far more argumentative work than anyone seems to notice. At a roundtable I attended last month, a well-known open-source advocate was asked how he answered the catastrophic-misuse objection — the worry that widely available weights put real capability in the hands of people who should not have it. He replied with the old maxim from Eric S. Raymond's essay on cathedral-building and bazaar-style development, "Given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow," and clearly took it to have settled the matter: on his view, releasing weights is not a risk but a safety measure, and restricting them is caution theater dressed up as responsibility. I want to be fair to him in print, but I cannot tell what the line is actually asserting. That is what I need from you.

Please treat the maxim as a piece of language to be disambiguated rather than a thesis to be explained, and lay out for me the distinct precise claims it could be making, from the near-trivial to the radical. I can see several axes along which it varies and I want your help making them explicit. It might be a claim about the *frequency and depth* of flaws: that defects in complex systems are numerous but shallow, rather than few but deep. It might be a claim about a *mechanism* — that exposure to many users produces, quickly, both a characterization of the problem and an obvious fix — and Raymond himself records Linus's correction that the person who finds a problem is usually not the person who understands it, with finding being the harder half. It might be a claim about *number*: that beyond some threshold of inspectors, remaining defects approach zero. It might be a claim about the *kind* of inspector required: a large base of beta-testers and co-developers, not merely a large audience. It might be a claim about *speed of remediation* rather than prevention at all. There may be readings I am missing. Give me the menu, and for each reading say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible empirical generalization, or something we have good reason to think false, along with what would have to be true of a system for that reading to hold.

Then the part I actually have to decide for the piece: which of those readings, if any, would carry the conclusion the advocate drew from it — that publishing frontier weights makes us safer, and that keeping them closed is mere caution theater? I suspect some readings support only the far weaker claim that openness helps for ordinary software, or for models below some capability threshold, and that the jump to frontier systems needs a premise the maxim does not supply. Say clearly which readings do the work he needs and which leave him short.

Let me also give you the doubts I have been unable to shake, since I would rather you engage them than produce a tidy answer that ignores them. First, the defects that made bazaar-style development work tended to announce themselves: a crash, a wrong result, a failing test, a user who knows something broke. A dangerous capability is not like that — there is no error signal, no failing test for "this system can now do real damage in the hands of a motivated non-expert," and no user who discovers it by using the product normally. If finding is the harder half of the work, what does finding even consist of here? Second, the maxim presumably assumes the eyeballs are, on balance, cooperative: people who find problems report them. Opening weights recruits the attention of adversaries as well, and an adversary who finds a dangerous capability has every reason not to file it. Does the reading survive if the eyeballs are not on your side? Third, source code is legible in a way trained weights are not — you can trace a failure to a function, read the intent, and patch it. There is, as far as I can tell, no comparable act of inspection for a large model, so a reading that depends on inspection by many readers may not even be applicable rather than merely false. Fourth, and relatedly, remediation is not symmetric: a patch is distributed and applied, whereas weights once released cannot be recalled, so the "fix is obvious to someone" half of the claim may be moot even when the "someone finds it" half succeeds. Fifth, and this is the one I find hardest to answer: some dangerous behavior is not a defect detachable from the product. The same competence that makes a system useful in chemistry or biology is what makes it risky, and there is no patch that removes the one while preserving the other — which suggests the very category of "bug" may not fit, and that the maxim is being asked to license a conclusion about trade-offs it was never about.

I am not after citations, a reading list, or links — I can get those myself, and I need the argumentative structure, not the literature. What I want is the menu of readings with verdicts on each, an account of what each would require of the artifact being inspected, a straight answer about which readings would actually support the advocate's conclusion, and a clear statement of where the analogy between publishing model weights and publishing source code breaks down. If it helps, imagine the output as material I could compress into a sidebar for readers who have heard the line a hundred times and have never once seen it taken apart.
