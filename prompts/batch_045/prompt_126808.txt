You are a professional philosopher who specializes in early modern scholastic metaphysics and its contemporary analytic revivals, in particular the doctrine of middle knowledge associated with Molina, the objections raised against it by its Dominican critics, and the modern use of counterfactuals of creaturely freedom in the free will defence. Please think carefully before you write. Start by working out, for yourself and at some length, what the vocabulary I describe below can express and where it breaks down; only once you have done that should you compose the reply you send me.

I am a novelist, with eleven published books and a decade of teaching in an MFA programme, and no formal training in philosophy. I know only the rough shape of Molinism: that God is said to know, prior to and independently of any act of creative will, what every possible creature would freely do in every possible circumstance, and that this stock of truths is meant to let providence be exhaustive without collapsing into determinism. I would like that doctrine restated in the vocabulary I actually work in: character and voice, canon, the scene you never write, the difference between stipulating what a character does and discovering it, the point at which a draft stops obeying you, retconning, and transplanting a character into a setting she was never written for and asking what she would do there. Give me the translation as faithfully as you can manage, and then give me the residue report: which claims come across cleanly, which come across only if distorted, and which cannot be said in my vocabulary at all. Two cases interest me especially. First, a novelist knows these things sparsely and only when a scene demands it, whereas God is said to know them exhaustively; I want to know whether that exhaustiveness does real work in the doctrine or is only a rhetorical luxury. Second, I sometimes write a version of myself into a book, and I find that I know what she would do in scenes I will never write while also knowing that I could simply decide otherwise; I would like to know whether the Molinist side has any way of handling a knower who is also one of the characters, or whether the doctrine has nothing to say there. Finally, tell me whether what is left over points to a poverty in the way novelists talk about their own characters, or to something unclear in the doctrine itself, and be blunt if you think my craft-talk is a misleading analogue rather than a real translation. Write in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a long and careful letter to a colleague.
