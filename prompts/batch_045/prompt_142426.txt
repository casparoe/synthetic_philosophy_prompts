I am a novelist, and the chapter I am currently trying to finish has turned into something I did not anticipate: an argument about decision theory that I have now had, in substantially the same shape, eight or nine times over the past four months with a friend who works in the field. Nothing moves. We agree on every feature of the cases, we agree about what the payoffs are, we agree about what the predictor does and how reliable it is — and then we disagree, flatly and without visible progress, about what the protagonist should do. I need a diagnosis, not a verdict, and I need it in a form I can use: I have to write the scene in which she decides, and I want the decision to be one a thoughtful reader could recognize as genuinely rational, rather than authorial fiat dressed up as reasoning.

Let me set out the cases, because two of them are my own invention and neither of us has seen them discussed anywhere.

The first is the familiar one. A predictor of established reliability flips a fair coin in advance. If it comes up heads, the predictor puts a large sum in a box if and only if it predicted that you would hand over a much smaller sum when asked in the tails case, and you are never asked. If it comes up tails, you are asked for the small sum and there is no box. So conditional on being asked, you are in the tails branch, and the money is either already there or already not there; nothing you do now changes that. My friend says pay, because what determines the money is what your decision procedure outputs and the counterfactual is just another application of it. I say the money's location is already fixed, and a theory that recommends paying here is recommending that you burn a thousand dollars in order to be the sort of thing that a million dollars would have been left for.

The second is my variation on it. The predictor's simulation is not perfectly faithful: with probability epsilon the channel between the simulation and the payout is corrupted, so that the payout goes through regardless of what the simulated agent decided; with probability one minus epsilon the standard setup holds. What I find striking, and what I want your view on, is that epsilon does no work in our argument. My friend's answer is flat: pay for any epsilon short of one. Mine is equally flat: never pay, for any epsilon at all. A disagreement that is completely insensitive to the parameter that makes the case realistic looks to me like evidence that the parameter is not what we are arguing about.

The third, which belongs to a subplot, is a blackmail case built on the same machinery: the threat is issued if and only if exactly one of two things holds — the blackmailer possesses the compromising material, or the recipient will pay. So receiving the letter is itself evidence about which branch you are in, and paying is evidence that you are in the branch where there is nothing to be revealed. Except that in my version the letter was composed years earlier by a dead man's switch, the simulation used to predict the response was destroyed before the letter was sent, and no one — no future blackmailer, no reputation, no observer — will ever learn what she did. I wrote it that way precisely to strip out every consideration except the structural one.

Here is my hypothesis, which I offer as a hypothesis. I suspect the argument is verbal, and that the word doing the work is you: what makes the counterfactual agent whose cooperation would have released the money the same agent as the one standing in the room, and what makes its decision your counterfactual decision? A weaker version of the same suspicion: we are not disagreeing about a fact or even about a principle, but about what a theory of rationality is for — whether it is a procedure for extracting money from situations or a characterization of the kind of agent one ought to be. A third possibility I take seriously: the split among my early readers mapped almost exactly onto whether they had encountered this family of problem before, which makes me doubt that intuitions here are data at all rather than artifacts of exposure.

Please tell me which of these, if any, is right, and support the diagnosis with what each side in the actual literature and in my argument is committed to. In particular: is the disagreement substantive or verbal; is it a clash about which cases count as evidence (is the claim that you cannot affect the past a datum, or a theoretical commitment smuggled in as one); is there a hidden formal question doing the work, about which decision procedure does better across a class of problems or about what a counterfactual on an agent's own output even is; are the two of us answering different questions under one heading; and is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed or merely that it is hard? My invented variants are a test of this: say whether they isolate anything real or merely add decoration. Then tell me, given your own diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a reformulation, an empirical fact about how agents and predictors actually behave, or an abandonment of the question — and be equally clear if your honest answer is that no progress should be expected and why.

Two further requests. First, I do not need citations, references, or reading suggestions; I want the reasoning, and I would rather have it from the structure of the cases than from authority. Second, I want you to flag your own exposure: state how confident you are in the diagnosis, identify the point in your reasoning most likely to be wrong, and say what argument or consideration — from my friend's side or from some third position I have not considered — would lead you to abandon or substantially revise it. Please write in American English.
