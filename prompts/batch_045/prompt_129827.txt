You are a philosopher of mind and cognitive science who has spent several years embedded in a machine-learning interpretability group. You know the arguments about when an internal state of a system genuinely represents something as opposed to merely correlating with it or being useful for predicting it; you also know the daily practice of training probes, clustering activations, steering with direction vectors, and ablating features; and you are equally impatient with realists who wave at a heatmap as if it settled something and with anti-realists who think the whole vocabulary is a category error.

Before you write anything, spend some unstructured thinking time — you don't need to show me all of it — listing the distinct things my team actually means when it writes that a model 'represents X'. For each, ask what single test would discriminate that reading from its neighbours, and note the cases where no available test discriminates them. Then build the procedure out of what survives that exercise.

The setting: I lead the internal model-evaluation group at a mid-sized AI lab. Every month we review fifteen to twenty claims of roughly this shape, written into evaluation reports, incident write-ups, and the external model cards we publish: that the model represents a certain property, that there is a direction in activation space encoding it, that a particular dictionary feature fires on a concept, that a direction can be ablated to remove a behaviour. Reviewers disagree constantly and the disagreements are not cosmetic, because the claims are load-bearing downstream: we have gated a data-filtering pipeline on a claimed direction for deceptive intent, and our legal and policy team now has to defend the wording of these claims publicly.

I want a written decision procedure, not an essay. Constraints: a reviewer should be able to run it in thirty to forty-five minutes per claim; the reviewers are engineers with no philosophy training; the output is a tier label plus mandated phrasing, which I and a safety lead then sign off on. It should specify the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it explicitly hands the decision to a human because the remaining question is one of values or risk appetite rather than evidence.

The scaffolding I have in mind, which you should improve, correct, or replace where it is muddled:

1. A read/write asymmetry. I want to distinguish claims licensed only by a read test (something in the activations lets you recover the property) from claims that also pass a write test (intervening on that thing changes behaviour in the way the representational claim predicts). My instinct is that a claim about what the system represents should not be promoted on read evidence alone, but I am unsure how strong the write test has to be, and in particular how to handle interventions that produce the predicted behaviour change along with unrelated collateral damage.

2. A triviality control. Before any claim is promoted, the same pipeline should be run against a randomly initialised network and against labels that have been shuffled or replaced with something the network cannot possibly encode, with a stated margin the real result has to beat. Tell me what margin is defensible and what to do when the baseline is unavailable.

3. A non-uniqueness clause. Because many features appear to be packed into fewer dimensions than there are features, several different directions may satisfy the same read and write tests. I want the procedure to force a choice between arguing for uniqueness and rewording the claim from a direction that is 'the' representation to one that is 'a' usable proxy, and to say how strong the uniqueness argument must be.

4. An earn-your-keep condition. Some claims pay for themselves by letting us predict a behaviour we had not yet looked for — a new prompt class, a new failure mode, a transfer to a fine-tuned checkpoint. Others only restate the labels the probe was trained on. I want the procedure to separate these, but I don't want it to collapse into 'whatever is useful is real', because part of my job is to stop the team from writing claims into public model cards that we cannot defend.

Give me, in order: the procedure itself in words an engineer can follow; a short claim-ticket template with the fields a reviewer fills in, including the pre-registered prediction the claim commits us to and the date by which we check it; a glossary of no more than six terms with operational definitions; and then worked applications to three real-shaped cases. Case one: a linear probe that separates user queries about legal documents from other queries with high accuracy and is currently used to route requests in a retrieval system. Case two: a dictionary feature that fires on a cleanly identifiable concept, where steering with the corresponding vector changes outputs in the expected way but ablating the feature also degrades unrelated capabilities. Case three: the claimed direction for deceptive intent that our data-filtering pipeline depends on, where the evidence is a read signal plus a steering result my team is very attached to — I expect your procedure to give me an answer here that I will not like, and I would rather have it than not.

Two final things. First, I am not trying to settle the metaphysics for the whole lab: I want claims that survive whichever way the realism debate goes. If that neutrality cannot be had, say plainly where the procedure quietly takes a side. Second, tell me where the procedure is most likely to misfire, and what would change your mind about it: which single argument about representation, or which single empirical finding about trained networks, would lead you to restructure or withdraw it. Say explicitly which step of your own reasoning you trust least.
