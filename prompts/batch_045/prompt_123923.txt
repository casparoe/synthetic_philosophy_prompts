I'm helping out with a philosophy-and-literature reading group at the local library — sixth-formers plus a few retirees, so a very mixed bunch — and in a few weeks we're doing Republic book II (Glaucon's story of the shepherd who finds the ring and turns the bezel to make himself invisible, and then Adeimantus chipping in that what people actually care about is the reputation for justice rather than justice itself) alongside Tolkien's The Lord of the Rings. I'm writing the handout and I've got stuck, because the obvious pairing suddenly struck me as a bad one and I want someone to tell me whether I'm just being pedantic.

Here's the worry. Glaucon needs his ring to be a pure impunity device: it changes the situation and leaves the agent alone, so that if the supposedly just person misbehaves the moment the witnesses disappear, we can conclude they were only ever after the wages and the good opinion. But the One Ring doesn't only remove consequences, it seems to add desires — it wants to be found, it works on the bearer's mind, it makes the wearer peculiarly visible to the Nazgûl rather than invisible full stop, and the whole business of gradual corruption means the longer you carry it the less you are the person who picked it up. So my draft note says that Tolkien isn't offering another instance of Glaucon's thought experiment, he's denying it can be run at all: you cannot hold what I want fixed while you vary what I can get away with, because impunity reshapes the agent. Is that fair to the novel, and is it a real reply to Glaucon or does it just change the subject? His claim is about the springs of action, and if the ring rewires those springs then it hasn't shown the springs were foul to begin with.

Three things I can't get straight either. First, I've been calling it the impunity asymmetry: in Plato the ring works the same on everyone, but in Tolkien it corrodes in proportion to what the bearer already has and already wants, so there is no single answer to whether anyone would stay just — the result of the test varies with the subject, which as far as I can tell means the experiment can't establish Glaucon's universal claim about human nature, only something about how differently different people break. Is that a genuine point or am I inventing a distinction to sound clever? Second, most of the case against using the Ring in the book looks consequentialist and external — you betray your position, you end up serving the enemy — which isn't Socrates' answer at all; so where, if anywhere, does Tolkien give the internal, soul-disordering answer? My candidates are Gollum, who is literally one person divided into two quarrelling halves, and the moment Sam briefly pictures himself as some sort of hero of the age and isn't tempted for five minutes because what he actually wants is a garden and Rosie. But maybe the honest thing to say is that the internal answer in Tolkien comes from pity and mercy and grace rather than anything a Platonist would recognise, since Frodo fails at the Crack of Doom and the quest succeeds anyway. Third, where does the book resist the reading outright? The Ring has a will of its own and is a bit of Sauron walking about outside him, which makes it a magical object rather than a moral-psychology probe, and some of the resistance to it seems to run on bloodline and ancestry rather than anything we'd count as virtue. I'd rather the group came away having read both the dialogue and the novel more carefully than with a tidy moral, so please be willing to say where the analogy simply breaks down.
