I teach an upper-level undergraduate metaphysics seminar and I am writing the final. I want one essay question on the Identity of Indiscernibles and Black's spheres that cannot be answered by reciting the two-sphere universe and then cataloguing three stock replies.

The question should be built around a variant I have been using in lecture, which I call the Coincident Twins. As in Black's case, the universe contains exactly two spheres, alike in every intrinsic respect, and the total state of the world — and of every possible world differing from it only by swapping the two — is invariant under the swap. But the spheres are coincident: same region, same time, zero distance, no interaction, so that every qualitative relation each bears to the other is one it also bears to itself.

I want the question to force candidates to do four things: state the principle over a suitably restricted domain, and say why the unrestricted version is either trivial or circular; explain why the weak-discernibility reply to Black was persuasive only because that case happens to contain a physical irreflexive relation, and why the twins leave nothing but numerical difference itself, which distinguishes every pair of distinct objects and therefore explains nothing; say what a defender of Black's spheres must now claim about the twins, and why that claim must not simply reapply the principle under test; and finally give some account of what the principle was supposed to be doing, for Leibniz and for us, so that the essay does not end with the sentence that Black refuted it.

Please write the question, a model answer of about 650 words, and a marking scheme organised around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth out of 20 and a one-line note on how candidates typically get each one wrong. Assume candidates have 45 minutes for this question.
