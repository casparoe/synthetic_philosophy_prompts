I work on a small team that maintains an internal assistant at a community health clinic — it's a large language model with read access to patient charts, and it drafts a first pass at visit notes that a clinician then edits and signs. Last week a nurse flagged a draft where the assistant wrote that the patient "reports chest pain" when what was actually in the chart was "tightness in the chest, worse on stairs." Everyone agrees the draft was wrong and got fixed before signing. What we can't agree on is how to log it, and I'm the one who has to write the taxonomy, so I have to pick actual words for a form field.

Our tech lead wants a category called "false belief." His argument is roughly: the assistant took the patient to have chest pain, that's something it took to be the case, it was wrong, and saying so is both honest and useful — it tells you what broke and sends the incident to the prompt team instead of the infra team. One of our engineers hates this. She says the thing doesn't take anything to be the case, it's a next-token predictor, and that if we start writing "the system believed X" in documents that auditors and eventually lawyers will read, we're smuggling in a claim about minds that nobody here can defend — and worse, we'll start debugging it the way you'd debug a careless human instead of checking whether the retrieval step even pulled the right chunk. Meanwhile the nurses themselves say things like "it thought she was having chest pain" all the time and it seems to work fine for them; they predict what it's going to do next and they're usually right.

Here's the part I can't get straight. Someone proposed a compromise that I liked for about a day: attribute a belief to the system only if the attribution survives a bit of pressure — it asserts the thing when asked plainly, still asserts it when the question is paraphrased or buried in a longer prompt, and doesn't retract when you show it the chart again. The trouble is that when I actually tried it, it bit on nothing. I ask "did you think the patient had chest pain?" and it says yes. I ask "or were you just pattern-matching on the words tightness and chest?" and it also says yes. I ask "so which is it?" and it produces a tidy little story about over-relying on surface features. It signs off on basically anything I propose about itself. So the pressure test never fails, which makes me suspect it isn't testing anything — but I can't tell whether that's an argument against the rule, an argument that I'm just bad at interrogating the thing, or an argument that the whole "false belief" framing was empty from the start. Add that the category label decides which team gets paged and what goes in a report to the state, so this isn't purely academic for me. What should I actually put in that form field, and who here is confused?
