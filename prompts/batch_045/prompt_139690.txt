I sit on the technical advisory panel of a private conservation funder whose charter was recently amended to add the welfare of wild animals as an objective alongside biodiversity and carbon. The amendment is easy to read and hard to use. It tells us to weigh wild animal welfare but not how, and every concrete decision in front of us turns on the same unresolved cluster of questions: whether draining a coastal wetland for rice paddy reduces suffering or merely relocates it, whether poisoning the rats on a seabird island is a welfare gain or a massacre, whether planting conifers on abandoned pasture creates welfare or destroys it, whether vaccinating a wild herd against a disease counts as a benefit when it works by preventing lives from existing.

Before we commission any modeling, I want the specification. Write the desiderata that any adequate account of the net welfare of an ecosystem must meet: what a theory has to deliver to be good enough for our purpose, which is to rank land-use interventions competing for a fixed five-year budget, and to return a reasoned not-yet-determinable in the cases where it cannot honestly rank them.

Fix the purpose as above, and assume the following background, which the panel treats as settled. Invertebrate sentience is unresolved. There is no cardinal interspecies scale of welfare, and no prospect of individual-level welfare data at landscape scale: what we can obtain are abundances, life histories, and remote sensing. Population size and age structure are endogenous to whatever we do, so the ledger cannot hold fixed the set of animals it is assessing. Effects propagate past the project boundary and past the budget window, and the systems are rarely at equilibrium.

Give me the requirements as a numbered list, each with the case or background consideration that motivates it, and be specific about the distinctions it forces. I expect at least these to be addressed: the difference between how well a life goes, how many lives there are, and how long they last; the difference between the welfare of the animals in a place and the value or integrity of the place itself; the fact that an index denominated in animals, or in biomass per hectare, is not yet an index of welfare; the choice about whether death is a loss to the animal who dies or merely the termination of a future, and how much of a verdict's sign depends on that choice; the fact that interventions change who exists later, so the counterfactual is not a fixed population with a different welfare level; displacement of harm outside the assessed boundary; and sensitivity of the verdict to the spatial boundary, the time horizon, and the cut-off for which organisms count at all.

For each requirement say whether it is non-negotiable, negotiable, or a preference of one camp dressed up as a requirement. Some candidate demands I want ruled in or out explicitly: that the account never recommend killing a sentient animal; that it treat intactness or naturalness as carrying independent weight; that it never aggregate across species; that it reproduce the judgment that a functioning ecosystem is a good thing; that it be expressible without any claim about which animals have lives worth living.

A consultant has proposed that we simply rank projects by expected net welfare-years per hectare-year, integrated over a century and discounted at three percent, with each animal weighted by the probability that it is sentient. Use that proposal as the stalking horse: state what such an index would have to presuppose, and where it meets or fails the list you have built.

Two further things I care about more than the rest. First, whether some subset of the desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied, so that the specification is itself a negative result: say which, and what would have to be given up. I suspect there is a deep tension between being fine-grained enough to discriminate between interventions and being computable from the coarse data we can actually get. Second, which single requirement does the most work in excluding otherwise attractive candidates.

Close with a compact table, no more than a few lines each, scoring four families of approach against the most discriminating requirements: suffering-focused consequentialism, totalist hedonic accounting over wild populations, biodiversity- or integrity-based views that treat welfare as downstream of ecological condition, and views that constrain what may be done to wild animals regardless of the arithmetic. Keep the weight on the criteria rather than the scoring. Argue from the cases; do not cite anything.
