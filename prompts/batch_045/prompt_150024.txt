I'm a journalist — I cover science and ideas — and I'm finishing a piece on waste as a credential: the way institutions make people burn something in order to be believed. Everywhere I turn in the reporting, somebody reaches for the same analogy. It's a peacock's tail. The cost *is* the message; make it cheap and you destroy the information. People keep telling me this all goes back to Zahavi's handicap principle and the formal versions people were writing in the 90s. I don't need citations, I just need to know whether the thing in my case studies is the same animal.

Three cases I'm actually writing about, so you can see the shape of it:

- A quant fund that makes final-round candidates fly themselves out for three unpaid visits before an offer. The partners described it to me as a filter for how badly people want it. It looked to me like a filter for having parents who can float you.
- A conservation fellowship that wants two years of unpaid field work before you're even allowed to apply. Same defence from the people who run it: the suffering is the point, it proves commitment.
- Proof-of-work and proof-of-burn in crypto: nobody can fake the electricity or the destroyed coins, so the signal is honest, and the corollary people draw is that switching to something efficient means switching to something gameable.

The analogy is being made to do real work. The conclusion people license with it is strong: any attempt to strip the waste out of credentialing — skills-based hiring instead of degrees, open publishing instead of prestige journals, proof-of-stake instead of proof-of-work — is naive, because it removes the very thing carrying the signal, and the institution will either grow a new costly hoop or slide into noise.

So: audit it. Give me the correspondence table first, explicitly — what plays the role of the male, the peahen, the tail, the genes, the predators, the environment that enforces the cost — and say what each piece is supposed to carry. Then where it breaks. The three things I can't get straight on my own:

1. Does the cost have to fall on the signaler, and what happens to the argument when somebody else can pay it — parents, a lender, a grant, an employer reimbursing the hoops retroactively?
2. Does it matter that the tail is paid automatically and, as far as I can tell, can't be forged, whereas every institutional version I can think of needs a rule and someone enforcing the rule, and therefore has an arbitrage?
3. Does it matter who benefits? A peahen choosing a well-tailed mate gets something out of it. A fellowship committee reading a CV full of unpaid labour arguably doesn't, and I have a hunch that difference changes whether the signal is stable rather than just whether it's nice.

Then the part I actually need for the piece: does the conclusion survive? Is waste-is-information-so-you-can't-remove-the-waste true, or true only under some condition that these institutions mostly fail? And is there a better analogy for what degrees and burn rates and unpaid internships really are — someone suggested a performance bond that you forfeit if you're caught lying, which feels closer to me but I can't say why.

One format thing: lead with the bottom line in your first sentence, then the reasoning. No preamble, no restating my question back at me.

And if I'm mangling this — if the handicap principle doesn't say what its fans keep telling me it says, or if my three cases aren't one phenomenon and I'm forcing them into a piece — say that plainly instead of politely answering the question I asked. I'd definately rather run a piece arguing that the analogy is carrying less weight than people think, with you telling me exactly where it does hold, than one that just launders a slogan.
