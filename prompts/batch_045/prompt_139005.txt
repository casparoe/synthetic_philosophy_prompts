I convene a final-year undergraduate seminar in political philosophy at a British university — eighteen students, a single ninety-minute session, a room with movable chairs, a projector and a pack of index cards, and no budget, so any stakes have to be points, tokens or something else symbolic rather than cash. The three-week block is on the ethics and political economy of military service, and we have already looked at three European reference points: a state with a long-standing militia tradition in which the citizen-soldier is the constitutional default, one that deactivated peacetime conscription in 2010 and reactivated it on a gender-neutral basis in 2017 after voluntary recruitment failed to fill the ranks, and one that never abandoned it and maintains a trained reserve of roughly 280,000 as a share of a small population. I ran this session last year as a structured debate and it collapsed inside twenty minutes into two slogans — conscription is forced labour, versus conscription is what you owe for citizenship — and nothing after that moved anyone. So this year I want something the students play rather than something they perform, and I would like you to design it.

The thing I want them to run into, without my naming it first, is that the choice between a conscript and a volunteer force is not one axis on which the two options can be ranked by efficiency. It is a bundle of considerations that can come apart, and a force can be better soldier for soldier and still be the wrong instrument for a small state whose strategy is territorial denial and whose neutrality means it expects to fight alone. At least four strands should be discoverable in the rules rather than in my introduction: fiscal cost to the treasury as against the real opportunity cost borne by whoever actually serves, which rises with civilian wages, so that volunteers become steadily more expensive as the country gets richer while conscripts on a nominal stipend look cheap mainly because part of their pay is being taken in kind; mobilisation lag, since a trained reserve has to exist before a crisis is visible while a volunteer force can only be expanded once the threat has already appeared; replacement depth, the capacity to absorb losses and keep fielding units in a long war rather than a short one; and signalling, because an aggressor's decision turns on expected resistance, and expected resistance depends on institutions that were put in place years earlier — you cannot build a mobilisation system, or a reputation for resisting, at the moment you discover you need it. The last of these is the one I most want them to feel: conscription can function as a pre-commitment and coordination device that binds a society to a course of action each individual member would defect from once the crisis actually arrives, and the free-riding temptation has to be something they personally face in the room, not something I describe.

Concretely, I would like an activity in which each student has a private stake that differs from the others' — an occupation with an earnings figure, perhaps a few cards that make service unusually costly or unusually cheap for particular people, maybe one or two conscientious objectors — and in which the group must repeatedly choose, under a budget, how much capability to buy and whom to compel. My instinct is that it should run through phases rather than a single decision: a long peace in which the temptation is to abolish compulsory service and buy a small professional force, a crisis in which the decision has to have been made already, and a sustained conflict in which losses have to be replaced. It matters a great deal to me that the adversary is not played by me: the students will read my face, and if I improvise the threat they will reason about what I am likely to do rather than about deterrence, so I need a scripted or mechanical rule for the aggressor's behaviour, ideally one that is only partly transparent to them. I would also like the rules to let the same budget buy genuinely different things — a mass force good at denial and a compact professional force that is good at expeditionary deployment abroad and at being useful to allies — so that the group can feel the pull between having good soldiers and having the right force for a neutral state. Please give me the full apparatus: set-up, materials and printable cards, the rules and payoff scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the play onto each of the four strands above. I also want the debrief honest about two things — what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, including the ways bright students will try to game the payoff structure or the adversary rule instead of engaging with the reasoning, and which feature of the problem the simulation simply cannot reproduce and therefore has to be supplied by me afterwards; my own guess is that it cannot reproduce the multi-decade horizon, genuine uncertainty about another state's intentions, or the moral remainder left over when an aggregate benefit is supposed to justify coercing some identifiable person. Do not lecture me on the philosophy in your answer, and please write it in British English.
