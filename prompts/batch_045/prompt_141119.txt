I work for a national museum service, and the same puzzle keeps surfacing when we plan remembrance and education work. In this country, which had no part in the events, the murder of Europe's Jews has a standing no other mass killing has: a national remembrance day, memorials in the capital, a fixed place in the school history curriculum, and, in several European states, a specific criminal offence of denying it. Yet the same international order operates with a general category — genocide, defined in the 1948 convention so as to cover any national, ethnical, racial or religious group — and comparative scholarship routinely places this event in a series with Armenia, Cambodia, Rwanda and Bosnia. The event is thus treated at once as the paradigm genocide, the case against which other candidates are measured, and as incomparable: in the German historians' dispute of the mid-1980s, proposals to treat Nazi mass killing as one instance of a twentieth-century pattern of ideological mass murder were denounced as an exculpatory manoeuvre rather than assessed as empirical theses.

What I want is the implicit theory of this practice. Please separate three things that seem to me to be run together: causal or explanatory comparability (could the same kind of account be given of another case?), moral comparability (can this event be ranked against, or is it incommensurable with, others in badness or wrongness?), and pedagogical or symbolic function (can it do for a pupil in this country what no other case could?). Which of these must the practice presuppose, which does it merely sit well with, and where is it philosophically underdetermined by several rival theories at once? In particular, does it presuppose that an event can be both a paradigm and incomparable, given that being a paradigm seems to require being comparable to something? And if Zygmunt Bauman is right that what modernity supplied was the means — bureaucracy, distance, the division of labour, the moral neutrality of procedure — while the ends were set elsewhere, then his account makes the event more, not less, comparable in the explanatory sense; does the practice quietly presuppose a rival, particularist causal story instead, or can the two be reconciled by distinguishing explanatory comparability from moral or symbolic uniqueness? Finally, what would our work have to become if we replaced the presupposed view with a straightforward comparativism — remembrance organised around a general category of bureaucratic mass killing, say — and what, if anything, would be lost?
