I hold the following view about the aggregation of tiny harms. There is no threshold of magnitude below which a harm stops counting morally: for any very mild bad effect — a momentary irritation no worse than a mote of dust in the eye — and any very severe one, say decades of unremitting agony, there is some number of distinct people each suffering the mild effect such that that outcome is worse than one person suffering the severe one, and worse by enough to settle what there is most reason to prevent. Two further commitments: the mild effects need never be noticed, remembered, or compared by anyone, since unnoticed harms still sum; and what is summed is the badness of the individual episodes themselves, not some further badness of the outcome's being widespread, unequal, or the product of a single decision.

Tell me what would count against this. I want the observations, cases, and arguments that should move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and sorted by whether a defender would concede them as counting against the view or dismiss them as importing a rival's assumptions. Two lines of attack are the ones I would personally find most persuasive, and I want them treated seriously rather than listed. First, the asymmetry between summation within a life and summation across lives: we routinely treat a long run of individually negligible discomforts — a bad mattress, chronic low-grade pain, years of small humiliations — as adding up to a serious burden for one person, and my view seems to need interpersonal summation to be continuous with that; so what would show that the two cases are relevantly alike, what would show that they differ, and which of those findings would actually defeat me rather than merely relocate the puzzle? Second, the decomposition case: a single severe ordeal can be described as an enormous number of sub-threshold increments spread across many people and moments, and if my opponents must say that no quantity of such increments ever approaches the badness of the ordeal, they seem to be making the moral arithmetic hostage to how finely harms are individuated; I want to know whether this is a genuine defeater for them, a standoff, or a problem I inherit myself.

I also want an honest assessment of how exposed the view is. Has it been insulated by qualifications — by retreating from claims about what anyone has reason to do to claims about which states of affairs are impersonally better, by retreating from determinate comparisons to merely existential ones, or by moving to expected-value formulations under uncertainty about where thresholds lie — and if so, is that insulation legitimate or is it a degenerating research programme? Finally, what would it take to show that the view collapses into a rival: that a threshold or lexical-priority view, or a view that restricts aggregation to harms within a single life, can recover everything my view was introduced to explain while avoiding the commitment to vast numbers of negligible harms outweighing atrocities?
