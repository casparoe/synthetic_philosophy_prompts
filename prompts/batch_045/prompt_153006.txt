I am refereeing a manuscript by a philosopher of language — I will call her Professor V — who has staked out a series of verdicts about vagueness, and I need to work out what rule she is running so that I can anticipate what she will say about the one case she does not discuss. Her verdicts so far are these:

1. The heap of sand, presented as a series in which grains are removed one at a time from a clear heap down to a single grain: she rules that the predicate is vague. She will not identify a number of grains at which the cutoff falls, and she treats the question whether a borderline pile is a heap as having no stable answer.

2. Baldness, presented as the parallel series of heads differing by a single hair: the same verdict, with the addition that the location of the cutoff is itself unsettled — the unsettled region has, so to speak, unsettled edges.

3. Red, presented as a series of color patches each imperceptibly different from its neighbor, running from a paradigm red to a paradigm orange: vague, with no statable boundary.

4. Tall, presented as a series of men whose heights differ by one millimeter: vague, with no statable boundary.

5. A statutory age term, where the statute fixes the age of majority at eighteen years: not vague. The boundary is sharp, and it is both stipulated and publicly statable.

6. Gold: not vague, even though ordinary speakers were for a long time unable to say where the boundary lay. The boundary is fixed by nature rather than by ordinary usage, and chemistry eventually made it statable.

7. Prime number: not vague at all, being a precise mathematical predicate.

8. A lexically ambiguous term, one that means both a financial institution and the edge of a river: she insists that this is a case of ambiguity rather than vagueness, and she declines to treat it as a sorites case at all.

9. The principle that removing a single grain cannot turn a heap into a non-heap: she holds that this principle is not to be asserted, even though each of its instances looks compelling when considered in isolation, and she holds that no one instance of it can be singled out as the one that fails.

As far as I can tell, these verdicts are jointly consistent with more than one rule, and I would like to know which one she is actually running. At least four fit them equally well. Call them: the ignorance rule, on which vagueness consists in our inability to locate a sharp boundary that is already there; the indeterminacy rule, on which vague predicates admit many equally admissible sharpenings and no fact of the matter selects one; the context-shift rule, on which the operative standard moves with the context of utterance, so that there is no single context-free boundary to be found; and the degree rule, on which borderline applications receive intermediate truth values rather than sharp or absent ones.

The case she does not discuss is this. Introduce a predicate I will call heap-star. Before any pile is presented, a sealed envelope is prepared containing an integer N, produced by a certified random draw from the range one to ten thousand; the envelope is sealed in a vault, and its contents will never be revealed to anyone. For any pile x, heap-star is stipulated to mean that x contains strictly more than N grains. The series is then run exactly as before, one grain removed at a time, and at each step she is asked both whether the pile is heap-star and whether heap-star is a vague predicate in the way that heap is. Note the combination: heap-star is stipulated, like the statutory term in the fifth verdict, but its boundary will never be statable by anyone, like the boundary in the first.

Please do the following. First, state what each of the four rules — and any further rule you think fits the track record as well as they do — predicts about her verdict on heap-star, saying in each case whether she will call it vague, as she called heap, or deny that it is vague on the ground that it has a determinate but permanently unknown boundary and is therefore a case of ignorance rather than vagueness. Second, give your best guess at what she will actually say, together with the reasons that make it the best guess: which rule does the track record as a whole, and the fifth, sixth, and ninth verdicts in particular, make most likely? Third, name the further case, or the shortest sequence of further cases, that would tell the surviving rules apart, and say what each of those rules predicts about it. I am less interested in which theory of vagueness is true than in the rule she is implicitly running, the other rules that fit her verdicts just as well, and the case that would separate them.
