I've somehow ended up having to give the five-minute opening remarks at a two-day workshop next month on whether fundamental physics has quietly changed what counts as evidence. About forty people in the room: a handful of string theorists who genuinely think arguments like the no-alternatives argument — the thought that if decades of very clever people have tried and failed to build any coherent rival framework, that failure is itself a reason to think this framework is on the right track — are a legitimate extension of scientific method rather than a surrender; two or three philosophers of science who think that's exactly the point at which physics stops being a science; a big pile of early-career people who mostly want to know whether they're allowed to spend their twenties on this; and, the bit I'm actually nervous about, the director of the foundation paying for the whole thing, who isn't a physicist and who'll decide in about six months whether there's a second one. My old doctoral supervisor, firmly in the second camp and never quite reconciled to my taking a postdoc with someone in the first, is flying in specially and will be in the front row.

The thing I keep going round in circles on is a worry I havent seen anyone put quite this way. The observation that no rival research programme has survived looks compatible with two opposite readings: either the framework is uniquely constrained, so the absence of rivals really is evidence about the world, or the field's hiring, funding, referee and seminar-invitation practices simply make rival programmes unliveable, in which case the absence of rivals is sociology and tells you nothing at all — and, annoyingly, the same sociology that produces the second reading also makes it hard to get a hearing for the second reading. And there's a second twist on top of it: if the framework's own solution space turns out to be a landscape of 10-to-the-500-ish vacua, then the very thing non-empirical arguments were supposed to establish — that there is basically one way fundamental physics could have been — looks false by the theory's own lights, and any prediction has to be extracted via a measure over the multiverse plus a typicality assumption nobody knows how to justify, so the non-empirical support and the multiverse seem to eat each other.

So can you write me the remarks? They have to be sayable by me out loud without either camp feeling sold out, and without collapsing into one of those dreary we-can-all-agree-there-are-interesting-questions-here non-speeches. The foundation director needs to come away feeling that funding this isn't a scandal; the early-career people need something honest rather than reassuring; my supervisor has to be able to laugh without feeling heckled. No jargon a smart non-physicist couldn't follow, one concrete image or analogy that carries the whole thing rather than decoration, roughly 600–700 words, and please no cheap post-empirical jibe to warm the room up — I definately don't want to start the two days with half the audience quietly furious.
