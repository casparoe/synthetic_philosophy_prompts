I teach an upper-level seminar in philosophy of mathematics — the students have had a first course in symbolic logic and nothing in set theory beyond the naïve — and I am considering assigning, as optional background reading, a short survey of the axiom of choice that a colleague in another department has drafted for a general-interest volume. It is well written and mostly careful, and my colleague in the mathematics department, when I showed it to him, shrugged and said that "that is just how people talk" and that I should not make trouble. Still, two or three sentences have been nagging at me since I read it, and I would rather not hand my students something that quietly misleads them. My own training is in philosophy of logic rather than set theory: I can follow a proof once it is put in front of me, but I do not have the literature on weak choice principles at my fingertips. The central passage of the draft runs as follows:

Suppose we are given a collection of objects together with an ordering — a relation of "comes before" that is transitive and never runs both ways between distinct objects. Call a subcollection a chain if any two of its members are comparable, and call an object maximal if nothing in the collection comes strictly after it. Zorn's Lemma says that if every chain in the collection has an object that comes after all of its members — an upper bound, which need not itself belong to the collection we began with, only to some larger ordering containing it — then the collection contains a maximal object. The lemma is equivalent, over the other axioms of set theory, to the Axiom of Choice, the principle that given any family of non-empty sets one may select a single element from each; so it is independent of those other axioms, and a mathematician is free to take it or to leave it. In practice nearly everyone takes it, because without it a great many ordinary results collapse: that every vector space has a basis, that every ring with a multiplicative identity has a maximal ideal, that any consistent set of sentences can be extended to a complete consistent theory, that every set can be well-ordered. What the lemma hands you in each case is a greatest object of the kind you wanted — a basis that includes every other candidate basis, an ideal inside which every other proper ideal sits — and the proof is really nothing more than an induction carried through infinitely many steps: you begin somewhere, and since you can always go higher so long as you are not yet maximal, you keep going, and the hypothesis about chains is what permits you to continue at the limit. This is why the lemma is so useful, and also why it is not entirely innocent. The objects it yields are never exhibited, only shown to exist, and anyone willing to accept existence without construction must also accept consequences such as the Banach–Tarski result, which cuts a solid ball into finitely many pieces and reassembles them into two balls each the same size as the original. A principle with consequences like that is not a mere convenience, and accordingly a growing number of mathematicians now treat the axiom as probably false and avoid it wherever they can.

Three things about this make me uneasy, and I would like to know whether my unease is well founded. First, I have a feeling that if the ordering is countable and an enumeration of it is available, a plain recursion along that enumeration already produces a maximal element, so that the lemma's real content concerns the uncountable case and the draft's "induction carried through infinitely many steps" is hiding exactly the hard part. Second, the word "equivalent" seems to me to be doing more work than it should: two principles can be equivalent over the remaining axioms while a particular application of one needs only a small fragment of the other, and I suspect the four results listed differ from one another in how much choice they actually require. Third, I doubt that the closing remark about mathematicians abandoning the axiom is fair, and I want to know whether it follows from anything true earlier in the passage.

Could you go through the passage and tell me, for each of its claims, whether a set theorist would accept it as stated, whether it is a simplification one could reasonably offer this audience, or whether it is simply wrong in a way that would mislead? Where a claim is wrong, I would like the corrected version and a sense of what goes wrong if a student takes the original at face value — in particular whether the author's final inference survives once the earlier claims are repaired. Please write your reply in British English: if the piece is salvageable I shall be adapting your comments into a handout co-authored with a colleague at a British university.
