I'm auditing a metaphysics reading group this term, and last week's session was on dispositions: how to get from *x is disposed to give response r to stimulus s* to some analysis in terms of conditionals, and then the two stock families of counterexample people use against such analyses — finks, where a mechanism would destroy the basis of the disposition just as the stimulus arrived (the standard case being a fragile glass wired to a device that would toughen it the instant anything struck it), and masks, where something leaves the basis intact but blocks the manifestation anyway (the same glass packed in foam). The example I brought came from my own family, which is why I got pulled into the argument: we run a small trout farm, and in winter we keep the ponds from icing over with aerators that run continuously. After the session two other members of the group argued about my case for most of the evening, so I promised to find out who was right and who was kidding themselves.

The case as I posed it was this. The air is at −8 °C, the pond has not frozen, and the question is whether the pond is disposed to freeze. I stipulated that the aerator does two things at once: it keeps the surface water moving, which prevents a coherent ice sheet from forming, and it keeps the water column mixed, which prevents a denser, more mineral-rich layer from settling to the top — a layer whose freezing point would be around −12 °C and which therefore would not freeze at −8 °C. So a single device blocks the manifestation and simultaneously prevents the one change that would remove the disposition. Their exchange, more or less verbatim: (1) Ines: the pond is plainly disposed to freeze; the aerator is a mask, and the whole point of separating masks from finks is that masks leave the disposition in place. (2) Tobias: that is too quick. The usual test for telling them apart is whether the interferer touches the *basis* of the disposition, and here it plainly could: switch the aerator off and the surface layer's composition changes, and with it the freezing point, which is finkish behaviour rather than mere masking. Run the standard reformed conditional analysis and you get *not disposed* anyway — among the nearest worlds in which the air is at −8 °C, the ones with the aerator off contain a brackish surface that would not freeze, and the ones with it on contain moving water that would not freeze either; no candidate nearest world yields ice, so the analysis says no disposition, and intuition should defer to it. (3) Ines: your survey of nearest worlds ignores timing. Ice nucleates within minutes of the water going still, while the stratification that would depress the freezing point takes about an hour, so a world in which the stimulus arrives and the basis is retained *does* give freezing — the reformed analysis delivers *disposed* after all. But that is exactly my point: the verdict now rides on a race between two processes rather than on what the water is like, so the conditional machinery is tracking the wrong thing. The disposition is grounded in the present microstructure of the surface water, and that microstructure is there. (4) Tobias: retreating to grounding is helping yourself to a theory rather than reporting a datum, and it buys you a disposition with no possible manifestation, which is the very worry about occult qualities the conditional analyses were meant to lay to rest. You have also quietly changed bearers mid-argument, from the pond to the surface layer at a time, and nobody has fixed which of those the disposition is supposed to belong to. (5) Ines: I will grant the bearer point and take the surface water at a time, and I will grant that the manifestation may never actually occur — but that is because one device here is at once a mask and an *anti-fink*, and the fink/mask vocabulary was built for a single interferer playing a single role, so what my case breaks is the vocabulary, not the disposition; and that, I think, is why we keep going in circles.

I would like a move-by-move adjudication rather than an overall verdict only: say what is right and what is wrong in each of the five moves, rate each somewhere between clearly mistaken and clearly right, and justify the rating briefly — distinguishing, where it matters, between a claim that is false and a claim that is true but does not do the work its speaker thinks it does. Then say who is more right overall, and what both of them are missing: is there a better description of the case than either of them offers, a further distinction the literature's two-category scheme cannot make, or a reason to think the question has no determinate answer until something else is settled? Finally, since I have to report back to people who will push back on whatever I bring, please mark the places where your own reasoning is most likely to fail and tell me what argument or further stipulation about the case would change your verdict.
