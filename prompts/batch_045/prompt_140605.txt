The thing we were fighting about: Hofstadter's Typogenetics section in Gödel, Escher, Bach — the formal system where strands of letters get treated as enzymes that copy, cut and splice other strands, so that a strand can end up directing the construction of a copy of itself. He uses it to press the analogy with DNA: nucleotides as symbols, the cell as a machine that reads them as instructions, and the thing that makes replication possible being the quine/fixed-point trick — the same self-referential structure that Gödel exploited when he built a formal sentence that manages to talk about itself. Call the strong reading this: any system that can replicate itself in the open-ended, code-based way — offspring can vary, and how complex the offspring are isn't capped by the parent, so it's evolution-capable rather than just crystal-growing — has to contain that self-referential structure, and not as a loose resemblance but as literally the same phenomenon that shows up in formal arithmetic.

I'm at about 0.6 that the strong reading is right, or at least right enough to be worth saying out loud. She's at about 0.2, on two moves: (1) von Neumann already gave the whole story — universal constructor plus a description that gets used twice, once as instruction and once as the thing to be copied — and a Gödelian fixed point is just one optional way to wire that up, so dragging in incompleteness is decoration; (2) if that's all it comes to, it's near-trivial, since anything with a code and a separate decoder trivially ends up with symbols whose effect is to bring about more of the machinery that reads them. I came back with a thought experiment: a replicator with no code and no distinct decoder at all, where the thing's own shape does the whole job — a crystal seed, a prion, pick your favourite. She said fine, that just shows the claim was only ever about code-based replication. I said then it isn't the deep claim Hofstadter is advertising, and we went in circles.
