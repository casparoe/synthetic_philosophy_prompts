I've been asked to referee an ethics-and-policy working paper, and I need to know how much of its treatment of Good's theorem on the value of information I can let stand. The paper states the result like this:

"Good's theorem says that information cannot have negative expected value. If you are an expected-utility maximizer who updates by conditionalization, then learning something before you act can only help you: for any result the observation might have, you remain free to perform exactly the act you would have performed had you not looked, so the expected utility of choosing after seeing the signal is at least the expected utility of choosing now. The argument is just Jensen's inequality applied to the maximum over acts, which makes it extremely robust. It does not depend on how probable any hypothesis is, and it carries over unchanged to an agent who maximizes worst-case expected utility over a set of priors, so even a strongly ambiguity-averse decisionmaker — one who plans by maximin — can never be made worse off in expectation by free information.

Two consequences are drawn. First, anyone who declines free information — the patient who refuses a predictive genetic test, the official who does not want an impact assessment run, the reader who avoids spoilers — must be either irrational or mistaken about the costs: her reluctance can only be about the nuisance of finding out or about the distress the answer will cause, and those are just costs to be netted out, so if the information is genuinely free, refusing it is incoherent. Second, the theorem yields a stopping rule for inquiry: keep gathering evidence while the expected value of the next test exceeds its cost, and stop at the first test whose cost exceeds its expected value of information."

The paper then applies all of this to the case I actually care about. A regulator must decide whether to commission a safety audit before deciding whether to license a system. Two features of the case: the audit's result is automatically published once produced, so courts, insurers, and the firm itself all change their behavior in response both to the result and to the mere fact that the regulator commissioned it; and the regulator's own staff cannot ignore the result, because agency rules make certain responses mandatory. The paper concludes that Good's theorem settles the question: commission the audit, since information cannot make you worse off.

I have a hunch that the stopping rule is the sound part and the application to the audit is where things go wrong, but I suspect the maximin remark is too quick and I can't say why, and I may be wrong about where the real problems are.

Please give me an accuracy audit I can turn into a referee report. Go through the summary claim by claim: what a decision theorist would accept as stated, what is an overstatement of a more modest thesis, what rests on a conflation the result itself keeps apart, and what is simply false. Say which assumptions the theorem actually needs — about updating, about the signal's relation to the acts and their outcomes, about where utility is defined — and which of them each step quietly uses. Then tell me whether either corollary survives correction, and whether the audit case falls under the theorem at all; if it doesn't, identify which feature puts it outside and what would have to change to bring it inside. Where your answer is "it depends," say what it depends on, so I can tell the authors what to add rather than just objecting. If the maximin claim is false, give me the smallest counterexample you can — two states, two acts, one free signal — in a form I could paste into the report.
