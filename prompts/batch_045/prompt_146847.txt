For the past several years I have worked as a workplace ombudsperson, and there is a pattern in the disputes I mediate that I keep having to describe and do not have a word for. Three instances, all lightly disguised:

A manager stops at an analyst's desk, looks at a draft and says, "It would be good if the quarterly summary didn't go out with these numbers in it." The analyst changes the numbers. When the change is later questioned, the manager says — and I believe him — "I was thinking out loud; I never instructed anyone to change anything." But from that day on, the analyst is described by her colleagues as having "been told to" and the manager as having "told her to," and no one can point to a sentence that is an instruction.

At a committee meeting the chair says, "I assume someone will take this on?" A junior member says, "I can do it." Months later, when the task turns out to be thankless, the very same pair of utterances is described in the opposite direction: the chair "never asked anyone," and the junior member "unaccountably took it on himself."

Two peers are talking. One says, "The word going round is that the client wants a full rewrite." The other treats this as a direction from the client and rebuilds the thing from scratch. The first lets it stand, and can honestly say afterwards, "I was only repeating what I heard." Note that neither of them was in a position to give the other that instruction; the utterance supplied the push anyway, and left no one holding it.

What seems to be going on is that how much an utterance *does* is not settled by what the speaker meant, and not settled by what the hearer understood at the time either, but settles later, depending on what follows and on how the episode gets retold — and that the retelling is not merely a report of what happened but the thing that fixes what happened. A friend who studied philosophy tells me there is a mid-century tradition about utterances that do things rather than describe things; he used the word "performative." But from what I can gather it deals with explicit formulae — "I promise," "I name this ship" — and none of my cases contains a formula at all.

I have been working with two provisional categories of my own, and I want to know whether they are real. In the first two cases the speaker was perfectly able to do the stronger thing openly and simply did not; the shift is a quiet reclassification of an act he could have owned. In the third case the speaker could not have performed the stronger act openly, or would not have dared to, and the utterance lets him supply its force while keeping the ability to deny having supplied it. If that distinction is a distinction, it matters enormously for how I write up a case. If I am carving at a joint that is not there, I would rather be told.

I am not after a label, and I will not be satisfied with one. What I want is the carving: what exactly this phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie. Specifically — does it require that the speaker had an opportunity to correct the shift and declined to take it? Does it matter whether the shift happens within seconds or over months? Does it occur in writing, where there is a record neither party can retreat from? Does it need an asymmetry of power or standing between the parties, or does it happen between equals? Is there a version in which both parties silently prefer the later version, so that the shift is convenient all round and no one is being manipulated? And is this a feature of the utterance at all, or merely a feature of how people subsequently narrate episodes — in which case, is it a different phenomenon from the ones the philosophical tradition concerns itself with, or the same one seen from the outside?

In arbitration we routinely ask what a reasonable person in the recipient's position would have understood. If the right answer is that this test already handles the whole thing and that nothing interesting is left over, say so plainly and tell me what, if anything, escapes it. If the nearest established concepts only partly fit, tell me which they are, how my cases differ from each, and which one fits best.

Please structure the answer as: (1) the nearest established concepts and how each falls short; (2) a definition of the phenomenon — or, if you think my two categories should be collapsed or split differently, the definition you would defend; (3) a table of the boundary cases I raise above and any you would add, each marked as inside, outside, or genuinely unclear, with a one-line reason.

One practical constraint: I have no library access, so please do not give me reading lists, bibliographies, or citations to works I could not check. I want the conceptual work itself. And for each substantive claim you make, say in a word or two how confident you are in it, then single out the one point you are least sure of and say why.
