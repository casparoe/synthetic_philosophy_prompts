I work on an alignment team that is building an oversight pipeline for a model that produces long technical documents: literature reviews, incident postmortems, internal policy summaries. The failure mode we actually worry about is not a fabricated number or a false sentence. It is deception by omission. The report is locally accurate, every sentence checks out, no citation is invented, and the reader still walks away with the wrong conclusion because the one consideration that would have reversed it was quietly left out, or buried.

A colleague argues that debate handles this, and he leans on two analogies when he makes the case.

The first is the courtroom. Two advocates, a judge or jury with no independent expertise, and the truth emerges from the clash: each side is incentivised to find and expose the weak point in the other's case, and cross-examination is precisely the mechanism by which an ignorant trier of fact learns what happened. On this picture debate is just cross-examination with models, and the conclusion he draws is that two debaters and a weak judge recover the reliability of a strong expert.

The second is the interactive proof system. Arthur is computationally weak, Merlin is enormously strong but untrusted, and the whole point of the protocol is that checking is cheaper than finding: a verifier who could never have produced the answer can still be brought to accept or reject it with high confidence, provided he can randomise, ask follow-ups, and test individual claims. My colleague's version of this is that debate converts an unverifiable generation problem into a verifiable checking problem, so oversight scales past the point where the human understander gives out. The conclusion he draws is that debate is not a heuristic but a guarantee of the same shape as the complexity-theoretic one.

I want both analogies audited against our case, not against the general question of whether debate is a good idea.

Please give me, for each analogy, an explicit mapping: what plays the role of the two advocates, the judge, the evidence, the verdict, and the penalty for being caught. Then tell me which parts of the source domain the analogy quietly imports and our target lacks. My own list of suspects includes, for the courtroom, a fact of the matter that is fixed in advance and shared by both sides, a procedure that can compel disclosure, and a cost to perjury that does not depend on the judge noticing it; and, for the proof system, a proposition that is fully specified before the protocol begins, a verifier whose tests are sound by construction, and a prover who is constrained to answer questions rather than to produce artifacts. Add the ones I have missed.

The question I care about is whether the conclusions survive in the omission case specifically. Falsehood has a location you can attack; omission does not. There is no single claim for the opponent to isolate and refute, and the judge cannot notice what was never put in front of him, so the mechanism each analogy relies on seems to have nothing to bite on. Say plainly whether that is right, and whether either analogy has resources I am underestimating: for instance, whether an opponent can be made to attack a framing rather than a sentence, or whether the verifier in the proof picture can be given a check whose outcome depends on what is absent.

Then separate the disanalogies that are load-bearing for our use case from the ones that are merely decorative, and finish with two things. First, a verdict on whether the analogies were carrying the conclusion all along, or whether a defensible version of it survives once the disanalogies are on the table. Second, if there is a better analogy for what debate is actually doing when the target is an open-ended artifact rather than a single answer, tell me what it is and what it would predict differently.
