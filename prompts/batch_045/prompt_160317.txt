I run a small fortnightly discussion group that works through one decision-theory / existential-risk paper a month, and on Tuesday we did the unilateralist's curse. Two of the regulars, Owen and Marisol, spent most of the session arguing past each other and I stupidly said I'd referee, and now that I look at my notes I genuinely can't tell who won.

The case on the whiteboard was one we invented: roughly 150 independent labs, each of which could entirely on its own release a system that each team privately judges to have maybe a 3% chance of being catastrophic and a large chance of being enormously beneficial — so every team's own expected-value calculation comes out positive, while the chance that at least one team goes ahead is near 1. The exchange, more or less: (1) Owen said this is why we should adopt the deference norm the paper argues for, the principle of conformity — when roughly equally informed others disagree with you about a unilateral high-stakes action, defer — because the individual EV computation is aimed at the wrong object; what you're actually choosing between is group-level outcomes, and 150 unilateralists make catastrophe close to certain. (2) Marisol said the norm is self-undermining by selection: the people most likely to act are the most confident and least risk-averse ones, and those are exactly the people who won't defer, so the norm binds the cautious and leaves the reckless untouched, and a norm only the wrong people follow is worse than no norm since it removes the moderates from the pool of actors. (3) Owen said that's an argument for institutionalising deference, not against it — licensing, pre-registration, a review board with teeth; if the hawks won't self-regulate, constrain them. (4) Marisol said that just relocates the curse one level up instead of dissolving it: the board is now itself a unilateralist relative to other jurisdictions, and centralising makes everyone's errors correlated, which is strictly worse than many independent errors — she drew two worlds, 150 labs whose mistakes are independent versus one committee of equal average competence whose single judgement is binding on all, and said the committee world is the scarier one. (5) Owen said the independence assumption is doing all the work in (4), that in the cases that matter the errors aren't independent at all (shared benchmarks, shared funding incentives, everyone reading the same evals), so error-diversification is a fantasy and what actually kills you is the sheer number of people holding a trigger — which is the curse restated, not a rival to it. (6) Marisol said then the honest conclusion is decorrelation rather than conformity (genuinely different methods, adversarial review, red teams picked for clashing priors), and added a symmetry claim Owen never really answered: there's a mirror case, which she called the unilateralist's blessing, where 40 parties to a treaty can each unilaterally veto a deployment everyone else thinks is net positive, so each vetoer's own EV looks fine and yet the good outcome almost never happens — and if you really believe in conformity, shouldn't it also tell you not to block when the others want to go ahead? Owen just said that's different and wouldn't say why.

Can you go through it move by move and tell me who's right about what at each step, flagging any move that's subtly confused or quietly changes the subject, and then close with one short paragraph on what both of them are missing? The thing I most want settled: is the curse fundamentally about how many agents there are, or is it really a symptom of the gap between one agent's evidence and the evidence available to the group as a whole — and is Marisol's blessing genuinely symmetric to it, or is there a real asymmetry?
