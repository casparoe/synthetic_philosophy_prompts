Hey — I could use a second brain on this. I teach a seminar on classical Indian epistemology and I'm in the middle of the Nyāya unit again, the pramāṇa material. Every year I come in with a pile of gut verdicts about which cognitions actually count as knowledge-producing and which don't, and every year, when I try to write the general principle on the board, it lets in something I don't want or kicks out something I do. I've read the literature already, so please don't give me a bibliography or a reading list — I want the principle, not pointers.

Here are the verdicts, going case by case.

Smoke on the hill, I infer fire. Obviously a case of knowledge. Mist on a hill at dusk that looks exactly like smoke; I conclude there's a fire, and there is one. Not knowledge, even though I end up true. A forester described a gavaya to me years ago; I see one in a sanctuary and recognise it. Knowledge, and it feels like it gets there in a different way from both perception and inference — I want comparison to earn its own slot on the list. I know Devadatta is alive, I know he's not in the house, and I thereby come to know he must be outside: knowledge, and it feels like postulation rather than inference, though I can't say why. I glance at my desk and thereby know my keys aren't on it. Knowledge. I glance at my desk and thereby know there's no intangible, odorless spirit sitting on it. Not knowledge at all — but I can't articulate what separates those two, since both are absences and both come from looking.

I glance at the wall thermometer, which works fine, and know the room is 21°C. Knowledge — though I go back and forth on whether that's perception or inference. Same thermometer with its sensor in direct sun: reads 21, room is 18. Not knowledge. But here's the pair I really can't reconcile: a thermometer I know to be broken, which happens to be accurate on this one occasion — not knowledge; versus a very competent diagnostician who reasons sloppily in my particular case and still lands on the correct diagnosis — knowledge, or at least I don't want to give it up. Those two look structurally identical to me: a source with a good track record that fails in the instance. I give them opposite verdicts and I don't know what licenses that.

Then the case that makes me suspicious of my own favourite clause. I smell smoke, infer fire, and then turn and see the flames. The second one clearly does something — I'm more confident, I'd act faster — but the novelty condition I like says the second cognition can't be from a pramāṇa at all, since it just repeats what I already had. Something has to give there. Relatedly: I remember where I parked. Knowledge, as far as I'm concerned, even though I know the tradition officially declines to count memory as a separate source. And I know I'm annoyed right now: knowledge.

Two modern ones. The airline app says the flight is delayed, and it got that from operations, which got it from somebody I'll never meet; no single link in that chain is a trustworthy speaker I could confront. Still knowledge, I think — but I've started joking in my notes that this needs a seventh pramāṇa for knowledge that arrives through a network of sources none of which is answerable to me, and I'm not sure whether that joke is a reductio of the whole framework. And a chatbot tells me some historical fact, which happens to be true: I lean toward not knowledge, but I lean less confidently than I'd like.

My rough principle, such as it is: a cognition is knowledge-producing just in case (a) its source is the kind of thing that generally gets things right in this sort of environment, (b) the source isn't defective in the instance in a way that bears on this particular deliverance, and (c) the cognition is new to me rather than a recycling of something I already had.

Please test that against the cases above and repair it where it breaks. I'd like a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause; I'd like to know which of my verdicts each one forces me to drop and which it saves only at the cost of getting baroque; and I'd like each one's predictions on cases I haven't listed — a brand-new instrument I've never calibrated but which is in fact reliable, a parent telling a small child something true but outside the parent's expertise, coming to know something from a photograph, and what should happen when two independent pramāṇas deliver conflicting results. If the honest answer is that my verdicts are just a mess, say that too, and tell me which one is the sacrificial lamb.
