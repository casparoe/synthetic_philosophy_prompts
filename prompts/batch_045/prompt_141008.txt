Okay, so I've been trying to work out what I actually think about Frege's puzzle about identity statements by just going case by case instead of starting from a theory, and my verdicts don't seem to line up with any single story. Here they are, roughly in the order I thought of them.

1. The classic one: "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is informative, it took observation to find out, and swapping one name for the other inside a belief report can flip whether the report is true. I'm confident about all three of those.
2. "Cicero is Tully" is barely informative at all. It feels like a philological footnote, not a discovery about the world. But as far as I can tell a Fregean sense difference is supposed to be there too, so either my verdict here is wrong or senses aren't doing the work people say they do.
3. The pianist-politician case: a guy admires the musician's recordings and separately has contempt for the politician, not realising it's one man, and the name he'd use is the same in both cases. I think this is a genuine puzzle of the same shape — and it survives there being only one name involved, so the puzzle can't really be about names.
4. The amnesiac: someone with full catalogue-level descriptive knowledge of everyone in the library, himself included, who still learns something from being told that the guy with the torn pants is him. Informative, and again no proper names in sight.
5. "This statue is this lump of bronze." Sort of informative, but it feels like a different kind of thing from case 1 — sortals and counting rather than who. I'm not even sure it belongs in the same pile, but I can't say why not.
6. My made-up case, which is the one I actually care about: a town dubs a river "Algon" and, in the same ceremony and the same breath, "Brenna", announces both names to everyone at once, and from day one everybody uses them interchangeably. Nobody ever has two bodies of evidence, two descriptions, or two ways of finding the thing. My verdict is that "Algon is Brenna" still sounds like a substantive identity claim about the world and not a reminder about words. If my verdict is right, whatever makes identity statements informative isn't a difference in associated information.
7. After the discovery: my astronomer, thirty years on, uses both names interchangeably and now finds "Hesperus is Phosphorus" about as thrilling as "Hesperus is Hesperus" — no jolt at all. But Lois still gets a jolt from "Clark Kent is Superman" every time, even though she knows it perfectly well. I keep going back and forth on whether that means informativeness can survive knowledge in a way the standard story doesn't allow.

The principle I keep falling back on is some kind of file thing: an identity statement a = b is informative for a subject just when the subject has, or could have, two distinct "files" on the same object open at once, where a file is the sort of thing that can be open at one time and closed at another, and where merely verbal differences don't count as distinct files.

So: is there one account — Millian, Fregean sense, files, hidden indexicals, whatever — that delivers all seven verdicts, and if not, which of my verdicts is the one that should go? Please also tell me if "informative" is even the right property to be tracking here, or whether I'm running together informativeness, cognitive significance, and being-something-you-could-learn. Don't bother telling me which papers any of this comes from, I just want the argument.

One more thing: I'd like to see what the account you land on says about three cases I haven't made up my mind about — (a) identity statements about a fictional character, e.g. "Superman is Clark Kent" versus "Superman is Kal-El", said in a discussion of the comics; (b) "the thing I'm touching is the thing I'm seeing", where it is in fact one cup, and the subject has no reason yet to connect the feel and the look; (c) "the caterpillar I fed in June is the butterfly on the screen", where the thing has changed a lot in between.
