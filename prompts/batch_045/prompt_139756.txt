I retired about six years ago from a lab that did mostly ion-channel electrophysiology — thirty-five years of patch clamps, nothing philosophical about it — and since then I've been sitting in on a consciousness reading group at the university, mostly as the person who can follow the maths. Over the last year or so I've more or less come round to integrated information theory. Not to the machinery of any particular version (I gather the details have been revised since the one I half-learned, and I've never made it all the way through), but to the core package: experience exists intrinsically, it's structured, specific, unified and definite; those get turned into requirements on a physical system; out falls a quantity — roughly, how much a system's cause–effect structure resists being decomposed, Φ — and then, instead of correlating that quantity with reports, you just read off which systems are conscious, how much, and of what. It's the only approach I've met where the physics falls out of the phenomenology instead of being bolted onto it afterwards.

Now I want the opposite of the sales pitch. What would count against this?

A few things to build into your answer. And please don't just send me off to read something — I've a pile of PDFs I'm already behind on, so argue it directly.

First, the objection I know I'm meant to worry about and mostly don't: the fading- and dancing-qualia style cases. Take something functionally identical to a person but built so that its causal work is done by a lookup table and a clock rather than by the thing itself; IIT says the lights go out, or dim, and my gut says that's absurd. The reply I've heard is that function isn't what matters, intrinsic causal power is, and I should get over the intuition. Is that a genuine cost, or a bullet the theory is supposed to bite? Say honestly how much of the theory's credibility rides on my being wrong about that intuition.

Second, the thing I made up myself and haven't seen addressed anywhere, which is what I most want your view on. Take a normal brain and imagine we can replace the long-range fibres connecting two regions with a link we can dial: sub-millisecond, then a few milliseconds, then tens, then whole seconds, everything else about the local dynamics unchanged. As I understand it, the whole formalism is computed over a transition matrix across some timestep, and whether this is one maximally irreducible complex or two separate ones depends on how much those cross-connections matter at that timestep. So: does IIT predict (a) a fairly sharp point where one subject becomes two, which seems mad to me when the dial is moving slowly and behaviour isn't changing qualitatively; (b) a smooth dimming as the link slows; or (c) nothing, because the answer flips depending on what timestep and what spatial grain you describe the system at? If it's (c), how bad is that — is the grain a free parameter the theory has no principled way to fix, and doesn't that sit badly with the results showing macro-level descriptions can beat micro-level ones, which IIT also seems to want? And if someone tells me the right timestep is just the system's own intrinsic timescale, isn't that circular, given that whether there is one system there is exactly what's in question?

Third, what's actually load-bearing empirically. IIT seems to stake something on the back of the cortex being special and on things like the cerebellum contributing little despite its cell count, and on measures of perturbational complexity tracking consciousness in patients. If those bets went the other way, or if a system with high Φ turned up with no signs of consciousness or a system with near-zero Φ turned up obviously conscious, would that be fatal or could the theory absorb it by retuning the measure or the grain? I want to know which predictions are the ones that could actually break it.

Fourth, what its own defenders would concede: the panpsychist-looking consequence that simple recurrent circuits have a trace of experience, and the consequence that a big purely feedforward network doing superhuman vision has none. Do they treat these as predictions, as costs, or as things they'd rather not talk about?

Fifth, and this is the bit I genuinely can't settle: has the whole thing been qualified into something I can't be wrong about? There's a letter going round from a crowd of consciousness researchers flatly calling it pseudoscience, and people in my group repeat the line that Φ can't be computed for any real brain, so nothing could ever refute it. I don't want to wave that away, but I don't want to be bullied by it either. So: is it unfalsifiable, and if so is that an engineering problem about computing Φ for large systems, or is it built into the theory that any outcome can be accommodated?

What would move me most is a concrete prediction coming out wrong in a way the theory couldn't absorb by adjusting the grain or the measure. Intuitions about zombies, or about whether a thermostat has a smidge of experience, move me much less — but tell me if that's the wrong thing to weight. Rank what you find from fatal down to merely awkward, and for each say whether it's something the theory's own people would concede or would shrug off.
