Analyze the March 2016 DeepMind Challenge Match in Seoul through the lens of the strange inversion of reasoning: the Darwin–Turing move, in Dennett's sense, of taking competence as primitive and comprehension as a late, optional, possibly unnecessary add-on, rather than treating understanding as what must come first and performance as its shadow. AlphaGo won the five-game match 4–1, having been trained first on human games and then by self-play; Lee Sedol's one win came in game four; game two contained a move that live commentary first took for an error and later treated as evidence of creativity, and game four contained Lee's improbable wedge, after which AlphaGo's play visibly fell apart.

I want three things. First, a disciplined mapping: what exactly counts as competence here, what is the comprehension that is supposedly absent, and in whom is it absent — the system, its designers, or the spectators? Note that the inversion was originally aimed at apparent design without a designer, so say whether transferring it to an artifact somebody deliberately built is licensed or a category shift, and what the transfer costs.

Second, I want you to disambiguate the claim, using a distinction I find useful and you should feel free to replace if it is wrong: call a system comprehension-equipped in the reason-tracking sense if its moves are backed by considerations it can in principle report; in the redistribution-robust sense if its competence survives shifts of the task distribution; and in the self-monitoring sense if it registers when it is out of its depth. Which of these does the match actually adjudicate, which does it leave untouched, and does that turn the episode into a confirmation of the inversion, a limit case, or evidence that the inversion never made a testable claim at all?

Third, run it backwards. Spectators credited AlphaGo with comprehension after game two and withdrew it after game four — so what are such attributions really tracking? If they track something like the intelligibility of a system's failures to us, then comprehension looks like an observer-relative honorific, and I want to know whether the theory can absorb that or whether it collapses the difference between having comprehension and being credited with it. And tell me where the theory is simply silent: in particular whether the case pushes toward reading comprehension as something required of the delegator rather than the agent, and what happens to the framework if the inversion is applied to the comprehenders themselves.
