**Question 4 (25 marks).** Benacerraf raises two related but distinct problems for a set-theoretic (platonist) account of the natural numbers.

(a) Set out the problem raised in "What Numbers Could Not Be" (*Philosophical Review*, 1965). Be explicit about what the competing identifications are and about exactly what they disagree over. [8]

(b) Say clearly how this problem differs from the one raised in "Mathematical Truth" (*Journal of Philosophy*, 1973). Is the 1965 problem merely a special case of the 1973 one, or does it have independent force? [6]

(c) *A "canonicality" reply.* It is sometimes said that the choice of identification is not arbitrary after all: the von Neumann identification is privileged, because ω is the unique transitive set whose membership relation is a linear ordering of order type ω; so if we require that the numbers form a transitive set and that their ordering relation be membership itself, the identification is forced. Grant the mathematics and assess the reply: does it answer the 1965 problem, and would it, if successful, do anything about the 1973 problem? [7]

(d) Does either problem, on its own, entitle us to the conclusion that numbers are not objects? Say briefly what Benacerraf himself takes the argument to show. [4]

And here is the answer, transcribed with the typos cleaned up.

**(a)** Benacerraf's 1965 paper asks which sets the natural numbers are, and argues that there is no answer. Set theory supplies several equally good candidates. Under Zermelo's identification, each number is the set of all of its predecessors: 0 = ∅, 1 = {∅}, 2 = {0, 1}, and so on, so that 2 has two members. Under von Neumann's, each number is the singleton of its predecessor: 1 = {0}, 2 = {1} = {{∅}}, so that 2 has one member. Both identifications are adequate: each allows us to define successor, addition and multiplication and to derive the Peano axioms, so each preserves everything the arithmetician actually cares about. But they disagree about questions that are not arithmetical questions at all: under von Neumann's identification 3 ∈ 17 is false (17 is just {16}, whose only member is 16), whereas under Zermelo's it is true, since 3 is one of the seventeen members of 17. Set theory cannot tell us which identification is the correct one, and since any adequate identification is as good as any other, there is no fact of the matter about which sets the numbers are. Worse, once an identification is made, questions like whether 3 is a member of 17 acquire determinate answers that they did not previously have, which means that even ordinary arithmetical claims such as 2 + 3 = 5 lose their determinate truth-value: they hold only relative to a reduction, and no reduction is the right one. That is the identification problem.

**(b)** The 1965 problem is really the same problem as the 1973 one, approached from a different direction. In "Mathematical Truth" Benacerraf argues that mathematical objects, being abstract, are causally inert, and that knowledge requires some causal connection between the knower and the thing known. We therefore cannot know which set the number 2 is, because sets are not the sort of thing one can bump into. The 1965 paper makes the same point at the level of identification: we have no way of singling out the right set. So the earlier paper is best read as a special case of the later, more general epistemological argument, and that argument refutes platonism: if numbers were sets we could not know any mathematical truths at all, since we have no causal contact with sets.

**(c)** The canonicality reply fails. Even granting the mathematical claim, other perfectly good identifications remain available — Zermelo's numerals, or the Frege–Russell identification of a number with the set of all sets having that many members — so nothing forces us to choose von Neumann's. The reply therefore leaves the arbitrariness untouched. It also does nothing about the 1973 problem, since we still lack causal contact with whatever sets we are asked to have knowledge of.

**(d)** Both problems point to the same conclusion, and it is the structuralist one. Benacerraf's own positive view is that numbers are places in the natural-number structure, a structure that exists independently of the sets that instantiate it. Since places are not sets, the question which set the number 2 is simply does not arise. This also disposes of the epistemological problem: we come to know the abstract structure by perceiving concrete systems of objects that instantiate it, so no causal contact with abstracta is required. Neither problem, then, shows that numbers are nothing at all; they show only that numbers are not sets.
