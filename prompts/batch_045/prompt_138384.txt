You are a philosopher of AI alignment who has also done formal work on agency — the sort of person who has actually tried to write down an impact penalty and watched it fail. Work slowly: brainstorm several candidate diagnoses, including ones you'll end up discarding, before committing to one.

I keep returning to an argument that seems to show low-impact AI is either empty or redundant, and I can't decide whether to believe it. Setup: we want an agent that pursues a goal G while not doing much else, and we want the penalty enforcing that to be (a) sensitive enough to catch real side effects, (b) loose enough not to paralyze the agent, and (c) value-neutral, in the sense that we can write it down without first having specified what we care about. The two standard strategies are: penalize divergence of the world from a baseline trajectory — what would have happened had the agent never acted, or had it shut down at each step; or penalize reduction in the agent's own future ability to achieve whatever goals it might later be given, aggregated impartially across a set of goals.

The argument:

(1) A penalty is value-neutral only if it does not need to know which changes matter; so it must treat all detectable changes alike — penalizing change as such.
(2) A usable penalty must exempt some changes: those constitutive of achieving G, and those causally downstream of it. So it cannot penalize change as such.
(3) Exempting the right changes requires sorting changes into the ones we don't mind and the ones we do.
(4) Sorting changes into the ones we mind and the ones we don't mind just is ranking states of the world by how much they matter to us: a partial theory of value.
(5) A partial theory of value is not enough. Whether a given change matters depends on what else the agent could have done instead, on how that change interacts with others, and on what the agent becomes able to do next; so getting the exemptions right in novel situations requires a ranking that extends to cases the designer never anticipated — a complete one.
(6) So a side-effect penalty is either paralyzing, or trivial in that it only catches changes it was hand-coded to catch, or it presupposes a complete specification of human values.
(7) But a complete specification of human values is exactly what low-impact methods were supposed to let us postpone; with it in hand we wouldn't need them.
(8) So the program is empty or redundant, and do not cause side effects is not an instruction we can cash out in advance of solving value.

Two replies I have been chewing on and cannot evaluate:

(A) A goal-achieved baseline. Instead of comparing the agent's trajectory to inaction, compare it to a counterfactual in which G somehow comes about and the agent does nothing else. Goal-constitutive changes are then exempted by construction rather than by a value judgment, so (3) is false. My worry is that this counterfactual is not unique, is only as good as our model of it, and seems to exempt everything causally downstream of G however bad.

(B) Offsetting. Rather than ranking states of the world, give the agent a scalar budget of total impact and let it spend that budget, including on restoring what it disturbed, the way a developer might fund habitat restoration to compensate for damage elsewhere. My worry is that a budget still needs a notion of when two changes cancel, and I cannot tell whether that notion is weaker or stronger than a value function.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you? I would rather keep (2), (7) and the spirit of (5) than the rest, but I would accept giving up (1) if you can say what value-neutral should have meant. Say also whether (A) and (B) genuinely escape the argument or merely relocate the value assumption, and where precisely each of them hides it.
