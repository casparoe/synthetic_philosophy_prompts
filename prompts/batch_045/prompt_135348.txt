I'm writing a term paper for a philosophy of science seminar on the epistemology of long-run prediction, using Malthus as my main case, and I'd like an audit of the package of views I've ended up with before I commit to the argument. I can feel that at least one commitment is doing work the others don't permit, but I can't locate which one, and a friend in economic history says the whole thing collapses as soon as I state it carefully. Here is where I think I've landed:

(1) Malthus's core mechanism — that population presses against a resource base, with positive checks like famine and disease and preventive checks like delayed marriage restoring the balance, so that long-run living standards hover near subsistence — was roughly correct of the human situation for the ten thousand years or so before 1800, and the fact that it stopped describing Europe after the Industrial Revolution shows it went dormant, not that it was wrong.
(2) The dormancy defense is only legitimate when the theory states in advance the conditions under which its mechanism is supposed to be active — call this the trigger condition. A theory that simply fails can always be declared dormant after the fact, and I think that is the main way bad theories get retrofitted into early ones, so I want the trigger condition to be checkable independently of whether the prediction succeeded.
(3) Malthus himself supplies no trigger condition, or at best one so loose that it can only be applied by looking at whether the prediction worked. So by my own (2), I seem committed to saying he was just wrong about the period after 1800, which sits badly with (1).
(4) But I also think the escape from the trap was endogenous in a way that reflects well on the theory: the pressure of population on resources is precisely what calls forth the technological and institutional changes that raise the ceiling, so the mechanism carries the source of its own dormancy inside it, and I don't want to count that as an auxiliary hypothesis bolted on after the fact.
(5) I hold, in a broadly Popperian spirit, that a theory reconcilable with any outcome is not empirical, and I take that to be the real objection to right-but-early reasoning in general.
(6) I also think Malthusian logic still binds at a long enough horizon: any population growing at a positive rate against a finite resource base eventually meets the constraint, so the prediction may yet come true within centuries or millennia, and if it does I want Malthus to get some retrospective credit — while denying that this gives anyone any reason to act now, since we can never tell in advance which failed predictions are merely early.
(7) And methodologically I'd rather evaluate theories as research programs over time, by whether they keep producing novel and independently confirmed results, than by single decisive refutations — while still thinking there is something abusive about keeping a program alive indefinitely by adding auxiliaries.

I also hold two case verdicts that I want the package to deliver: that the late-1960s population-bomb predictions of imminent mass famine in the following two decades were simply wrong, whereas the early-1970s system-dynamics world models that projected resource-driven overshoot and collapse somewhere around the middle of the twenty-first century were early rather than wrong, because they were explicitly about a finite planet with explicit overshoot dynamics and a window that has not closed yet. My friend says I cannot have both verdicts, since the two predictions look structurally identical; I think the difference in how specifically each one dated its own failure is enough to separate them, but I'm not sure that difference survives my trigger condition in (2).

So: go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly, and which only look tense — I already suspect (1) against (3), so I'm more interested in the ones I haven't spotted. In particular, does (4) survive (5) at all, or does endogenizing the escape make the theory unfalsifiable in exactly the sense (5) condemns? Is (6) self-undermining, in that a status we can never identify in advance is a eulogy rather than an epistemic achievement? Is (7) compatible with (2), or does the trigger condition presuppose the falsificationism that (7) is meant to replace? For each real tension say what would have to give and what that costs me downstream in the paper, rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most.
