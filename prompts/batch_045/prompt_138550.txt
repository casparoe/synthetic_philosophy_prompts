I'm a retired atmospheric chemist — most of my career was field campaigns where half the job was getting a dozen stubborn people to do the same thing at the same time — and since I stopped working I've been reading my way into philosophy on my own. I've ended up stuck on all this material about common knowledge and rationality in epistemic game theory. I think I follow it, and then I try to say it out loud to somebody and I clearly don't.

Here's why I want it turned into something usable. I chair the drought committee of our irrigation district — volunteer job, about thirty growers, we get twenty minutes in front of a whiteboard before the regular board meeting every week from April to September, and between meetings we run a phone tree. The problem is the same shape every time: the group has to act on the same day or nobody gains anything. Either we all shift to the emergency rotation, or the ditch goes dry anyway and whoever cut back has simply lost a week of water. A few times a summer something trips it — the gauge drops below the line, a gate stops holding, the ditch rider says the snowpack is finished — and we have to converge on one day. Sometimes we manage it. Sometimes half of us turn up. Last August nobody did, and afterwards every single grower told me separately that they had assumed the others weren't going to.

So: I want a procedure, not an explanation. Something I can actually run at that whiteboard in fifteen minutes, either to work out why a coordination attempt fell apart or to design the protocol before the season starts. Questions in what order. What counts as a good enough answer to each one — I can't hand out forms, these are farmers, and anything binding has to be signed off by the watermaster, and if a step needs arithmetic it had better be arithmetic I can do standing up. Where does the procedure stop and say no, don't do this at all. And where does it have to stop and hand the decision to a person because what we've got is a clash of values or of priors rather than an error anybody is making.

Things I'd like built in, as far as they really go: the gap between something being mutual knowledge to a few levels and being common knowledge; the way a chain like he knows that I know that he knows gets more brittle the longer it is; that electronic mail game result of Rubinstein's where no number of acknowledgement rounds gets you to common knowledge — I want to know what that says about our phone tree and its confirm-everyone-by-text variant, and whether we should stop doing that entirely; and the Aumann-style result that if people share priors and exchange information honestly then standing disagreement shouldn't be possible, which I take to mean that once all the data is on the table and my growers still disagree, more data isn't going to move them. Tell me whether I've got those about right, and what each one actually commits me to doing on Monday.

Three worked cases, please. One: our cascade rule for a frost-pump night, where the head of the tree calls two people, each of those calls two more, and everyone is meant to call back up the chain — is that protocol worth anything, and if not what replaces it. Two: last August, reconstructed — what actually went wrong and what should have been different. Three: a case where the procedure gives me an answer I don't want. My suspicion is it's going to tell me to drop our unanimous-consent rule for the shared pump and let the watermaster just announce the day, and I'd like to know whether the theory really says that or whether I'm leaning on it until it agrees with me.

Last thing, and this matters to me more than the checklist itself: I don't want to go around distributing something that rests on a result half the field treats as an artifact of an idealization. So say where the theory behind this is contested or fragile — where infinite-level reasoning is doing the real work, where actual people are known not to behave the way the model assumes — and say what would change your mind about the procedure you're handing me. Which argument would make you rewrite it, and where do you think it's most likely to misfire on my growers.
