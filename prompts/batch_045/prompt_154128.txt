I work in the methods unit of a national infrastructure authority, and I have been asked to write a short internal note on what our appraisal manual actually assumes about human welfare. I would like the philosophical version of that note, because the manual makes claims none of us can cash.

The practice, briefly. We rank projects by monetised net benefit, and benefits must be measured from observed behaviour rather than from what people say. A minute of travel time is priced by the fare premium people actually pay for the faster service. Risk of death is priced by the extra wages that riskier jobs command. Cleaner air is priced by what it adds to local house prices. Stated-preference surveys are inadmissible: the manual rejects them on the ground that someone filling in a questionnaire is not choosing, and so reveals nothing. Where no market exists, we transfer a value from a broadly similar market somewhere else, scaled by income. Losses are priced the same way. A household that will lose its home to a new line is credited with the market value of the house and a fixed statutory disturbance payment, not with what it says it would take to make it willing to move. Benefits and costs are then added across everyone affected at one unit of currency per unit, with no weighting by who ends up with them, and we do not require that anyone actually be paid. A short list of impacts — landscape, quiet, one particular wetland — is recorded in a separate table and never enters the arithmetic.

Two things sit badly with me.

First, the same authority runs a behavioural-insights team whose job is to change defaults so that people end up choosing differently — auto-enrolling commuters in the off-peak ticket, or in the cycle-to-work scheme — on the explicit ground that what those people would otherwise have chosen is not good for them. When that team's interventions are appraised, the resulting changes in behaviour are counted as benefits and priced with the very same revealed-preference numbers. So in one part of the building we treat choice as the only admissible evidence of value, and in the next we treat it as evidence we are entitled to override.

Second, we insist that only real choices count and then price most things using choices nobody made: the value transferred from another city, the house price paid by a buyer who never once considered air quality, the imputed willingness to pay of a child or of people not yet born.

What I want is the implicit theory. What view of utility, or of welfare, or of what a choice shows, does this package of rules only make sense under? Which parts of the package does that view strictly require, and which are merely habits that fit it comfortably? Is the combination coherent, or does the nudge work quietly presuppose something the valuation rules deny? My suspicion is that the word value is doing three different jobs in our arithmetic and we are adding the results as though it were one, but I cannot say what the jobs are or which one carries the weight.

And if we took seriously the austere version of revealed preference — the one on which choice data pins down nothing more than, for each person separately, an ordering of the options with no meaningful distances in it and no way of setting one person's ordering against another's — which of our rules would have to go, and what would replace the benefit-cost test as a way of choosing between projects?
