I moderate a residents' forum for a London borough — about four thousand members, most of them active on one side or the other of a proposed low-traffic neighbourhood scheme. I do it unpaid, in the evenings; by day I work in freight logistics, and I have no academic background in philosophy or social science. Everything I know about this subject I have picked up by reading on my own over the last three years, mostly because I got tired of watching a discussion I care about go bad.

Here is what worries me. Three years ago people on the forum disagreed about facts that are, in principle, checkable: whether traffic had been displaced onto the boundary roads, what the nitrogen dioxide monitors on the high street actually recorded, whether bus journey times got worse. Now the same arguments run, but the council's own published data is treated as fabricated whenever it tells against a member's side, the most trusted voices in the group are the ones who have never published anything anywhere else, and anyone arriving with inconvenient numbers is identified within a few posts as a plant, a consultant, or a cyclist lobbyist. I have read about a distinction that I think describes this: in the first kind of enclosure, contrary evidence simply never reaches you because of how your sources are arranged, so a single well-placed exposure can puncture it; in the second, the group has actively taught you to discount the outsiders who would carry that evidence, so exposure does not help, because the discrediting runs ahead of it. I find that distinction convincing, but I cannot tell which of the two I am actually looking at, and I do not know how to find out from the inside.

So I am considering a rule, and I want a probability on whether it would work. Call it the nominee rule: before a post making a factual claim about the scheme goes up, the poster must name and link one source they regard as the most credible source that disagrees with them — not a parody of the other side, but the strongest version of it that they themselves still reject. That link is pinned above the post, with no engagement counts shown and no replies permitted for the first hour. (A variant I am also considering: the moderators choose the opposing source rather than the poster. Give me a separate number for that variant, and tell me which term does the work in the gap between the two numbers.)

State the proposition precisely, as I intend to measure it: at baseline and again after six months, regular members answer a twelve-question quiz on checkable local facts — traffic counts, monitor readings, bus times, from published council and TfL data — and rate their confidence in each answer on a 0–100 scale. The proposition is that, in a forum like mine, six months of the poster-nominated rule leaves the average regular member both meaningfully less confident (mean confidence down by at least five points) and meaningfully more accurate (mean quiz score up by at least ten per cent), relative to what would have happened without the rule.

Give me your credence in that proposition, and give it to me decomposed: the sub-questions your number rides on, a rough probability for each, one line of justification per term, and then the combination. Here are the terms I would want to see addressed, though restructure them as you like: whether my forum is the first kind of enclosure or the second, with a hard core of the second inside a larger first, and how much the rule's success depends on which; whether credibility that has been actively destroyed can be repaired by any procedure internal to the forum, or only by something the forum cannot manufacture, such as members who left, changed their minds, and came back; whether members would comply in spirit or nominate deliberately weak opponents, and whether a weak nomination is worse than no nomination at all, on the grounds that having publicly disposed of a feeble opponent licenses you to stop listening; whether the mechanism, if it works at all, works on belief, on stated confidence, or only on hostility, and whether reduced confidence without reduced hostility should count as success; the base rate for any single moderation intervention producing a change large enough for my instruments to detect at six months, given how noisy self-reported belief is; and whether attrition swamps everything, since the members who remain after six months may simply be the ones the rule did not annoy.

My own number is 0.35. A moderator of a similar group in a neighbouring borough puts it at 0.1: he thinks being made to state the other side's case in front of your own side makes people defend harder, not softer, and that the rule will recruit the forum's split rather than soften it. Tell me which of the sub-questions most likely carries the disagreement between 0.35 and 0.1 — that is, what he would have to be right about, and I wrong about, for his number to be the better one. Then tell me which single term in your own decomposition you are least confident in, and what one piece of evidence, if I could somehow obtain it, would move your headline number furthest in either direction.

Please write the whole answer in British English. One constraint: I cannot look anything up and I am not asking you to either, so reason from what is in this message and from what you already know — do not cite papers, authors, or studies whose existence I could not verify, and do not tell me to go and read someone. Give me the number, the table of terms, the combination, the sensitivity, and the one thing that would move you most.
