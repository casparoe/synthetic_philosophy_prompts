**Question.** Avicenna asks us to imagine a person created all at once, fully mature, suspended in empty space, with no sensory contact with anything — not even with his own limbs. He claims that such a person would still affirm his own existence, while affirming none of his bodily parts.

(a) State as precisely as you can what the thought experiment is meant to establish. In particular, distinguish three claims that are easy to run together: (i) that a person's awareness of his own existence is not derived from or mediated by sense perception; (ii) that the self can exist without any body; (iii) that the self is not identical to the body or to any bodily faculty. Which of these does the argument support, and what would have to be added to get the others?

(b) In lecture we distinguished two ways of setting up the case. The *input-variant* removes everything for the senses to pick up: the subject keeps his eyes, ears, hands, and the internal senses seated in the brain, but there is nothing to perceive. The *faculty-variant* removes the faculties themselves: no external senses, and no imagination, memory, or estimation either — the subject is, as it were, a bare intellect attending to itself. Explain why the distinction matters, and say which variant does the real work in Avicenna's argument and which one risks assuming what is to be proved.

(c) Is the flying man's affirming of himself best read as immediate, non-propositional self-awareness, or as an inference or judgment along the lines of I exist? Does anything in the argument turn on which reading we choose, given that the subject is stipulated to have had no prior experiences and no concepts acquired through the senses?

(d) Briefly: how far is Avicenna's conclusion from Descartes's? If the argument works, does it hand us a *res cogitans*?

**Student's answer.**

(a) Avicenna's point is that the flying man, created all at once and already mature so that he cannot have learned anything, with his limbs spread apart and no contact with the air or with himself, would still be aware that he is; but he would not be aware of his hands, his feet, or his heart. So self-awareness is original to the soul and not acquired through the body. It is not derived from sense perception, and it needs no proof or demonstration, since anyone who entertains the case sees this at once. That is claim (i). Claim (iii) — that the self is not identical with the body or with any bodily faculty — is, I think, what the argument really establishes, because once we have subtracted everything bodily from what the flying man is aware of, what is left over cannot itself be a body. Claim (ii) would need extra premises and is not established on its own.

(b) The two variants come to the same thing. Taking away what the senses pick up and taking away the senses themselves are equivalent for Avicenna, since on his psychology the faculties just are the soul's ways of receiving input; with the input gone the faculty is idle, and an idle faculty might as well be absent. So the faculty-variant is the one doing the work: once the internal senses are out of the picture as well, nothing bodily is left for the self to be, and the argument shows that the self is not a bodily faculty.

(c) The awareness should be read as immediate rather than inferred. The flying man cannot reason from there being awareness to I exist, because he has no prior concepts and no earlier experiences to reason from, and any such inference would in any case be circular, since recognizing the awareness as his already presupposes the self. Avicenna is describing a kind of direct self-presence rather than an argument. And this is why he is entitled to set the internal senses aside: if self-awareness is not the presentation of anything, then no representing faculty, bodily or otherwise, is doing the work.

(d) Avicenna's conclusion is essentially the same as Descartes's. Both use a first-personal thought experiment to show that the mind's awareness of itself is independent of the body, and so both arrive at a thing whose essence is to be aware of itself; Descartes's version is just tidier, because it does without the faculty psychology. The distance between them is therefore mostly a difference of apparatus rather than of substance, and the argument does effectively give us a *res cogitans*.
