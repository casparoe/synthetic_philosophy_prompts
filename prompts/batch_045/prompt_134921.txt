I spent several hours last night arguing with a friend who works on early modern intellectual history, and I came away convinced that we agreed about every fact either of us could actually produce and still disagreed about the verdict. So I want a number instead of a dialectic, and I want the arithmetic that produces it.

The proposition I want a credence on: that there was a genuine episode in European natural philosophy running roughly from the mid-sixteenth to the late-seventeenth century, which was both causally integrated (the changes fed one another rather than merely coinciding) and a discontinuity rather than a mere acceleration, and which the label the Scientific Revolution picks out at least approximately — as opposed to that label being a device imposed retrospectively on a longer, smoother transformation that happens to have sped up. My credence is about 0.55. Hers is about 0.15. I want yours, and I want to know where the gap between us most likely lives.

I will say what I am counting as resolution, so the number has something to be about. Reconstruct, from what you know, the trajectory of European natural philosophy between 1300 and 1800 along a few axes: the mathematization of descriptions of nature; appeal to deliberately staged experiment; correspondence networks and print as machinery for assigning credit and priority; willingness to contradict ancient authorities openly; the social role and self-description of the person who studies nature. Now ask whether a boundary placed somewhere between 1540 and 1700 earns its keep — whether it predicts the distribution of those features better than an arbitrary cut in a series that is otherwise changing smoothly and at an increasing rate. If the cut point beats that smooth-trend null, the period is real; if the best-fitting story is continuous acceleration with no privileged cut, it is an invention. I think that test is the crux, but tell me if it is the wrong test, and why.

Give me a number for the proposition, decomposed. The terms I think belong, which you should feel free to add to, merge, or replace:

1. Clustering: how much of the relevant change is genuinely concentrated in that window rather than spread across the preceding two centuries?
2. Integration: conditional on clustering, how much of it is causal integration rather than co-occurrence of independently caused changes?
3. Discontinuity: conditional on integration, is the right description rupture or acceleration, given that a smooth series with rising slope will always generate the appearance of a turning point somewhere?
4. The actor's category: did participants experience and describe themselves as living through a break? And is their self-narration evidence about the reality of the break, or only about their polemical needs?
5. The meta-question: what does real require here — that the category carve at a joint, that it be indispensable to the best explanations, that it beat the predictive null, or something weaker? How much of my number is really a bet on this rather than on the history?
6. The genetic point: the label was coined long afterwards by people with a stake in a story of darkness, recovery, and progress. Does a concept's polemical origin defeat its reference, or can a term introduced for bad reasons still latch onto something?
7. The symmetry constraint: the three labels — the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, the Scientific Revolution — arrived as a package, each defined partly by contrast with the others, so that validating or debunking one is not independent of the others. Give me a separate number for each of the three, and if any two of them differ by more than about 0.2, say what principled asymmetry licenses the difference. I expect this is where I am least coherent.

For each term give a rough probability and one line of justification, then show how they combine into your headline number. Then tell me two things. First, which single term your final number is most sensitive to, and what piece of evidence or argument — not a bibliography, and not a survey of who has defended which position; give me the consideration itself — would move it most, in each direction. Second: is the disagreement between me at 0.55 and my friend at 0.15 most likely a disagreement about what happened in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, or a disagreement about what it takes for a period to be real? If it is the latter, I would like to know whether either of us could in principle be wrong about it, or whether it is only a difference in what we want the word to do.
