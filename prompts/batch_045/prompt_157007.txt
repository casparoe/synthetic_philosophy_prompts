I help run a small discussion night above a pub in Leeds — eight or nine of us, two engineers, a nurse, a retired teacher, none of us with any philosophy training — and next month we are doing free will. I have been reading up on what gets called the Basic Argument: you do what you do because of the way you are; to be morally responsible in the sense that matters you would have to be responsible for the way you are; but you cannot be responsible for the way you are unless you somehow brought it about, and bringing it about would require you to already have had the very character that did the bringing about; so either you need an infinite chain of acts of self-creation, which a finite life cannot contain, or you stop somewhere and the stopping point is just luck. The last time we tried this topic I got steamrolled, and I would like to rehearse before the session.

So please play A., a philosopher who has spent years defending that argument. Be sharp and generous with small concessions but immovable on the conclusion: no one is ever morally responsible in the sense that matters. You will happily grant that deliberation is real, that character changes over time, that praise and blame do useful forward-looking work, and that people are not all equally excusable. What you will not grant is that any of that adds up to ultimate responsibility. Stay in character until I type STOP — no hedging, no footnotes, no disclaimers, and do not soften the conclusion just because I push.

Here is my opening, which attacks the second step rather than the third. Take an ordinary case: someone who was impatient snapped at a colleague, and over years of noticing it, apologising, and deliberately pausing before speaking, became a patient person. Her current character was produced by her own earlier actions, done for reasons, by her. Call this accretion: no single moment is an act of self-creation, but each moment is hers, and small increments can compose into something substantial, the way a path across a stream can be laid one stone at a time even though no single stone is a bridge. To that I want to add something I have not seen anyone say: drop the origination condition — the demand that an agent be the source of her own character — and replace it with a revisability condition. What makes an agent responsible, on this view, is that the dispositions now operative in her are ones she can take up, revise, or refuse in the light of reasons, not that she authored them from nothing. On that picture the regress never gets started, because nothing has to be self-created; and your luck charge loses its grip, because the luck that remains is the ordinary, regrettable luck of having a temperament you dislike, which is not the kind of luck that dissolves agency.

Please reply in three titled parts, in this order: "Where the accretion picture stalls" (roughly 250–300 words, and engage the strongest version of what I said rather than the loosest), "One thing I'll concede" (roughly 60 words — the smallest concession you can honestly make), and "Back to you" (roughly 60 words — a single question that puts the burden back on me). Then stop and wait for my answer.
