I write a column on long-range forecasts — demography, food systems, climate adaptation, compute and disease — for a publication that covers science and ideas, and I am trying to get clearer about a phrase my desk uses constantly: that a forecast was right but early. Please treat the Malthus case as the test bench for that phrase, and translate the dispute about it into the vocabulary I actually work in.

Two readings are in play. On the first, the Malthusian argument was put to the test by two centuries in which a vastly larger population was fed better, per head, than anyone in 1798 could have imagined, and it failed; the moves that kept it alive — restricting it to pre-industrial societies, resetting the date at which the constraint bites, and quietly changing what the constraint is measured in, from famine deaths to land pressure to a broader ecological throughput — are rescues of the ad hoc kind. On the second, the argument was never a dated prediction but a claim about a structural tendency that is masked while a one-off endowment (fossil energy, nitrogen fixation, a green revolution) is being drawn down, so that failing a fixed-horizon test does not refute it; it was, in the phrase I want to understand, right but early.

My desk has a working apparatus for this: a horizon clause that fixes when a filed forecast is scored; the distinction between a trend story and a blip; the decision to hold a piece, kill it, or run it as an evergreen; the correction box, which states exactly which printed claim was wrong and how; the not-yet file, for forecasts we still believe but whose clock has not started, together with a written note on what would start it; and a revival clause, a sealed condition, filed in advance, under which a forecast that has already missed its window becomes live again. Translate each of the two readings into that apparatus as faithfully as you can, and treat the three moves listed in the first reading — narrowing the scope, resetting the clock, changing the currency — as the specific items to be rendered: which of them would my desk classify as a refinement of the story, which as goalpost-moving, and by what rule would it tell them apart?

Then give me the residue report. Say what translated cleanly, what translated only after being distorted, and what has no counterpart at all in how a newsroom handles a prediction. In particular I want to know whether my vocabulary contains anything corresponding to a mechanism that is true but dormant, anything corresponding to a claim whose truth-condition is a rate over centuries rather than a state on a date, and anything corresponding to the question of when a revised theory is still the same theory — or whether the correction box, which is built to score particular printed claims one at a time, simply has no way to score the model behind them. And tell me which assumptions I would be smuggling in if I called the translation faithful: I suspect fixing a scoring date in advance already begs something against the right-but-early reading, but I would like you to say what, and how much of the original dispute survives once that is noticed.
