You are a philosopher of social science who also designs and runs classroom simulations: someone who has taught both formal epistemology and research methods, and who thinks hard about how an idea can be built into a set of rules rather than a set of slides. Please take this seriously and work it out carefully before writing. Begin with a short brainstorming section in which you sketch two or three candidate mechanics, say in a line or two why you are discarding each, and then give me the finished activity.

The setting: a second-year undergraduate seminar on "Measuring Violence", with a mixed group of philosophy, politics and history students, 18 people, one 80-minute session, a room with a whiteboard, paper and markers, and no internet access during the session.

What I want them to come away having felt, before anyone names it: (i) that a trend can reverse depending only on how you aggregate the very same incidents; (ii) that decisions about which incidents belong in the count are substantive, contested and not merely clerical bookkeeping; and (iii) that it is genuinely open whether the question "was this period more violent than that one?" has a determinate answer at all, prior to somebody picking a measure.

Constraints on the design:

1. Build me a fictional chronicle: an invented region over six periods, with population figures and a ledger of violent incidents. Construct the numbers so that the absolute count of violent deaths roughly doubles across the six periods while the rate per head of population roughly halves. Keep the ledger workable — say 45 to 60 incidents, each one line, codable in about fifteen minutes — and give me the population table. No real places, no real datasets, no real history: I do not want the session to turn into an argument about the actual empirical claim, and I do not want them importing opinions they have absorbed about that debate.

2. Two groups, two rule sheets, no lecture up front. Group A is asked to rank the six periods by how much violence there was; Group B is asked to rank them by how dangerous it was to be a person living there. Neither sheet may use the words "rate", "per capita", "absolute" or "proportion". They should produce two conflicting orderings, notice the clash when the whiteboards go up, and have to work out for themselves what produced it. Only then does the distinction get a name.

3. Build into the ledger eight to twelve incidents that straddle the boundary of the concept: a death in custody, a killing by the region's own officers, an execution after a trial, a duel, deaths from disease in a labour camp, a famine following requisitioning, a killing in self-defence, an honour killing, a disappearance nobody can confirm. Consider having a third group code the whole ledger equipped only with a bare dictionary-style definition of violence, and see where they end up disagreeing with each other.

4. Consider a fourth element: reporting quality. Later periods record many single-victim incidents, earlier periods record only "notable" deaths, and the ledger should show this in a way participants can notice — so that a rising curve is visible as a possible artefact of what got written down. If you think this is one element too many for eighty minutes, say so plainly and tell me which element you would cut instead.

Then give me: a materials list, a minute-by-minute timeline, the rule sheets as I could hand them out, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the session onto the three points above, distinguishing carefully between "there is no fact of the matter", "we cannot find out", and "the concept is interest-relative". Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure. Finally, be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce and what I will have to supply myself in the debrief — in particular, say honestly if the activity only shows that reasonable coders disagree, and cannot by itself settle whether a fact of the matter exists.
