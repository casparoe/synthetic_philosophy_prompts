I spent forty years as an experimental geochemist, and since retiring I have been sitting — mostly by accident, through a friend who had to step down — as one of two lay members on a supported-decision-making panel at a hospital trust. Three or four times a month we review a care plan that proposes to override what an adult is currently saying they want: someone with treatment-resistant psychosis refusing medication during a relapse, someone with a frontal-lobe injury making impulsive gifts, someone with mid-stage dementia refusing to be washed. We have about ninety minutes per case, of which maybe forty can go to deliberation, we write a one-page rationale that either the clinical director or a judge has to sign off on, and the rest of the panel are two clinicians, a social worker and a lawyer, none of whom have read a philosophy paper. What I keep coming back to from my evening classes is the family of views that says the will you should honour is not the wish the person articulates now but the one they would arrive at under better conditions — better informed, unhurried, not frightened or coerced, with their beliefs made coherent and their options actually experienced rather than imagined. I have met the same shape of idea twice: once in the medical-ethics literature on autonomy and on preferences deformed by deprivation or illness, and once in a machine-intelligence setting under a name I will not get right, where it is applied to an agent that does not yet exist. Neither is written for a panel like mine.

What I want is a procedure I can hand to the other five members, in plain words, that turns that idea into something we can actually run. Give me the questions in the order we should ask them; for each one, say what would count as a sufficient answer and what would count as no answer, since in practice most of our evidence is a few conversations, a family's testimony and a clinical note. Mark the points where the procedure should simply stop and return a no — we do not override — and, separately, the points where it should hand the question back to a human being because the remaining disagreement is about values and not about facts about the person. Two distinctions I have been toying with and would like you to build in, improve, or tell me to drop: first, separating subtractive extrapolation, where you remove an identifiable defect such as a false belief, a coercive threat, pain, or an option never presented, from additive extrapolation, where you supply experiences or capacities the person has never had; my instinct is that the first needs only a traceable chain from the current wish to the corrected one, while the second requires that every goal in the corrected will be traceable to something this person has actually said, done, or been reliably observed to care about, and that anything untraceable stops the procedure. Second, a symmetry check on our own inputs: because we are the ones who specify the ideal conditions, I want the procedure to make us state which conditions we are counting as ideal, whose conception of a good life each one smuggles in, and whether reversing any single one of them would flip our conclusion, with the case ruled indeterminate and referred upward when it would. Be explicit, please, about which of your steps are genuine constraints and which are judgment calls wearing a procedure's clothing, and about which kinds of person or which kinds of evidence will make the whole thing misfire.

Then run it on three cases. A thirty-four-year-old man with psychotic relapses who now refuses clozapine on the ground that it is poison, but who, during a remission two years ago, signed a crisis statement authorising exactly this override and saying his future frightened self should not be believed; the current him says the earlier signature was obtained by doctors who lied to him. A seventy-eight-year-old retired district nurse with mid-stage Alzheimer's who resists being washed and has struck a carer, whose family insist she would be appalled, and whose notes show she only resists with one particular member of staff and when the water is hot. And a twenty-nine-year-old man with severe anorexia who is articulate, can recite his own mortality statistics, has said for years that he would rather die than gain weight, has a four-year-old daughter he says he loves, and whom his clinicians say is not legally incapable, so that any coercion would rest on our recommendation alone. I expect to dislike what the procedure says about the third case, and I would rather be told plainly that it licenses something I dislike than have it quietly tuned to agree with me — so give me the answer it gives, not the answer I would prefer. Finally, since I cannot tell from where I sit which parts of this are solid: mark for me, at each step, what is settled among people who actually know this literature and what is genuinely contested, and do not let a contested step inherit the authority of the sound ones around it. My guess, which you should correct, is that the existence of preferences deformed by ignorance, coercion or illness is not controversial, while the claim that there is any determinate fact of the matter about what someone would want under ideal conditions — and any way for us to know it — is very much not.
