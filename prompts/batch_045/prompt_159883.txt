There's a woman in my department — call her H. — who has spent something like thirty years on Wittgenstein and religious belief. She isn't a believer as far as anyone can tell, and she gets annoyed at being called a fideist; her line is that the point was never that religious people get a free pass, just that a lot of what philosophers do to religious utterances is like putting a sigh on a scale. She's always leaning on On Certainty, on hinges and groundless believing, on the difference between a picture that runs your life and a hypothesis you happen to hold.

I've been writing down her verdicts on cases, because I keep running into things that look like religion and aren't, or don't look like it and are. Roughly, in her words as near as I can recall them:

- Villagers who pray over a sick cow and never dream of tallying the hit rate: religious belief, and not superstition. She said they'd be puzzled if you asked for the success rate, the way you'd be puzzled if asked for the success rate of your own name.
- A man who holds that the resurrection is the best explanation of some historical data and can tell you what would disconfirm it: an empirical hypothesis in a religious costume.
- A man who says the creed every week and whose money, work, and love life look exactly like someone's for whom nothing is at stake: not a belief at all — the words have no use in his life.
- A creationist who wants Genesis taught next to biology: a rival science, and a bad one. (She added that the same stories can be religious when they aren't being used that way.)
- A Quaker who won't swear oaths or fight, who says there's that of God in everyone, who offers no grounds and wouldn't know what counting against it would even look like, and whose whole life is arranged around it: yes, obviously.
- A grad student who says God is the ground of Being, the condition of possibility of anything at all: metaphysics, and not very good metaphysics. Nothing religious is happening there.
- A man who says God told him to sell the house, sells it, gives away the money, and is otherwise unchanged — no church, nobody checks him, nothing else in his life moves: a tip, not a picture. Divination, not belief.
- A coven whose rites were written a couple of generations back by people you can name: yes, tentatively. They learned it from somebody, there's a technique to be taught, and she says she doesn't care that it isn't old.
- And the one that got me started: a human rights lawyer who says every person has equal worth, can't give a ground for it, doesn't treat it as up for evidence, gives away most of her income, and has organized her entire life around it. H. said something like: same grammar; I won't say it isn't religious, and she'd hate the description. Which I took to mean she wasn't going to answer.
- She also said once, about a kid raised evangelical who loses it at nineteen: what he lost was a picture, which is why it hurts more than changing your mind about a fact.

So here's the new one. The shop I work in: twenty-odd people, mostly engineers, doing safety work on AI systems. The stated belief is an ordinary forecast — within a couple of decades something much smarter than us gets built, and if the work goes badly then everything anyone would care about is over. They put numbers on it, ten percent, twenty, they revise the numbers, a couple of them have public betting records, and they argue about the numbers constantly.

But I definately don't think the numbers are what's running them. The people with the lowest numbers work just as hard as the people with the highest. There's a weekly meeting that ends in singing. There's a minute of silence before we ship anything big. People turn down offers that would set them up for life and describe it in a vocabulary of being called, or of a debt. They say things like "the future is watching" and "we're the generation that has to hold the line," and they talk about everything that could ever be as a thing that can be saved, lost, or betrayed. Nobody believes in God or an afterlife or anything spooky — the thing they're answerable to is people who don't exist yet and may never. Ask what would disconfirm the forecast and you get numbers; ask what would disconfirm that it matters and you get a look like you've asked why pain is bad. The whole thing is maybe eight years old, nobody's parents did this, there's a canonical pile of essays everybody has read, and newcomers get walked through it.

So what does H. say about us? And more usefully, what's the rule she's running? Give me two or three candidate rules that fit every verdict above and say what each one predicts here, tell me which you'd actually bet on and why, tell me which of the earlier cases is the closest analog, and name one further thing — a question I could ask my coworkers, or a case I could put to her over a drink — that would separate the rules still in play. If you think the disagreement between the rules is really just a fight about the word religious, say which part and why.

Last thing: I want to know how much to trust any of this. Put a number on your confidence if you can, tell me which single further fact about the shop would flip your prediction, and say where your reconstruction of her rule is most likely to break — including the possibility that she has no rule at all and is just going by feel.
