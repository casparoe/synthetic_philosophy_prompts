You are a philosopher of probability who works on anthropic reasoning — the self-sampling assumption, the self-indication assumption, observation-selection effects, and the reference-class problem. Before you write anything, list every step of the argument below that you might reject and what rejecting each would commit you to elsewhere; only then commit to one and defend it.

I use self-locating evidence in my own work — I have defended a modest doomsday-style shift from facts about my birth rank — and a colleague who thinks the whole enterprise is empty sent me the following. He says it shows that taking your own evidence seriously either annihilates anthropic reasoning or turns it into a doomsday argument of arbitrary strength. I think it proves too much, and I want to know exactly where to step off it.

Setup: two cosmological hypotheses, Sparse and Dense, with equal priors and no ordinary empirical evidence that distinguishes them. Sparse says the cosmos contains 10^11 observers in total, Dense says 10^24. Call the observers a hypothesis posits its candidates; let N(H) be the number of candidates under H, and n(E,H) the number of candidates whose total evidence is E.

(1) Self-locating evidence can bear on H only if, conditional on H, you treat yourself as uniformly randomly selected from the candidates. Both SSA and SIA accept this much; they differ only about the prior weighting, SSA giving a posterior proportional to prior(H) × n(E,H)/N(H) and SIA giving prior(H) × n(E,H).

(2) The E you feed in must be your total evidence, not a convenient summary of it. Coarsening E discards information, and discarded information produces error: a witness who conditions on the weaker proposition that the coin landed one way or the other, rather than on its having landed heads, gets the wrong answer. So E is everything you are in a position to know.

(3) Total evidence is individualizing. It fixes your birth rank, your distinctive memories, your place in the order of things, and everything else you can introspect or infer, so at most one candidate satisfies it — plausibly exactly one, on any hypothesis now in play. Hence n(E,H) = 1 for every live H.

(4) So on SSA the likelihood collapses to 1/N(H), and you get a doomsday argument whose strength is fixed by nothing but how large the largest hypothesis on the table is: near-certainty that there are no extraterrestrial observers, no far future, no big cosmos, inferred merely from the distinctiveness of your own memories. On SIA the likelihood collapses to 1, and no self-locating consideration ever moves a credence: no presumptuous philosopher, and no observational-selection reasoning anywhere, including in the cases where I think it plainly does work.

(5) Both consequences are unacceptable. The first is not a modest shift but an arbitrarily strong one; the second would make the number of observers a hypothesis posits evidentially inert.

Which step do you reject? Explain why the step looked fine — I want the reason it is tempting, not only the reason it is wrong — and then price the alternatives: what does an SSA theorist pay, what does an SIA theorist pay, and what does someone who rejects (1) outright pay? For what it is worth, I would rather keep (1) than keep the presumptuous philosopher, and I would rather keep a modest doomsday shift than none at all.

Two further things. First: is the granularity at which E is specified a genuine free parameter of anthropic reasoning, or is there a principled way to fix it — and if it is a free parameter, does that destroy the doomsday argument or only weaken it? Second: suppose we stipulate that the extra candidates Dense posits, over and above Sparse's, are exact and permanent subjective duplicates of you — the same memories, the same evidence, forever, never distinguishable from the inside. Does that change what (3) or (4) delivers? My colleague says it changes nothing; I suspect it does, but I cannot say why.
