So I got into an argument at a meetup last weekend about the cluelessness objection to consequentialism, and I've been turning it over since. You know the shape of it: for pretty much any action available to you, the downstream effects fan out into a chaotic system, the far-future terms are enormous and long-tailed, and by the time you add it all up you have no real idea whether the net sign is positive or negative — so expected-value reasoning either recommends paralysis, or returns roughly zero for everything by symmetry, and either way it isn't doing the work people want it to do.

What I've been chewing on — and what I actually want you to argue for — is that a big chunk of this is an artifact of aggregating in the wrong order. Here's the distinction I have in mind. Call it pooling: you flatten all your uncertainty into one big fine-grained distribution over outcomes, mixing together every hypothesis you're unsure about, and then take the expectation (and apply whatever risk- or ambiguity-aversion you like) exactly once, at the very end. Call the alternative conditioning-first: you notice that most of what's driving the fog isn't uncertainty about outcomes within a hypothesis, it's uncertainty about which coarse-grained regime you're in — which causal pathway actually dominates, whether the effect runs through institutions, through path-dependent tech trajectories, through demographic turnover, whether what you're doing permanently moves the level of some stock or just shifts the timing of a transition that was going to happen anyway. And conditional on any one such regime, the sign of what you're doing is often pretty legible and pretty stable. So you evaluate the action within each regime first, and only then do the second-order aggregation across regimes.

I want you to make the strongest case you can that conditioning-first is the right way round, and hence that the cluelessness worry is substantially overstated: decisions that look like coin flips under pooling come out with a determinate sign once you do it the other way. Two things I'd like you to actually engage with rather than hand-wave, since these are what my friend kept pushing on: (1) the deflationary reply that for a straight expected-utility maximizer the two orderings are mathematically equivalent, so this can't be doing any real work — I want you to explain why the ordering still definately matters for bounded agents like us, or for anyone whose decision rule isn't pure EU, or at the level of what you should believe rather than how you should choose; and (2) the reply that conditioning-first just relocates the problem one level up, because your uncertainty across regimes is itself clueless and the sign isn't stable across them either. Give me a few hundred words, no citations or reading recommendations (I know the literature, I want the argument), and feel free to use a concrete example if it helps — bonus points if you use the timing-versus-level distinction I sketched, since that's the case I think is cleanest.
