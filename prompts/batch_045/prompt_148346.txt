I run a monthly lunch thing at work where I try to explain one physics idea to the other engineers. Audience is about fifteen backend and distributed-systems people: they know what a bit is, they know what a memory leak is, most of them bailed on physics at sixteen. I've got forty minutes and a whiteboard next week and I want to do Maxwell's demon and the "information turns out to be physical" thing. I know the topic decently well at this point, so please don't explain it to me — I need a plan for how to explain it, to this room, in this order.

The complication: a staff engineer here gave a talk last spring and told everyone the demon fails because looking at the molecules costs more energy than the demon gains, and my manager repeated that version back to me last week. I'm pretty sure that's the wrong exorcism, and that the real one has to do with the demon having to clear its memory between runs rather than with the measurement itself. But I don't want the session to be forty minutes of "so, about Dave's talk..." Where in the sequence should that correction land, or should I just quietly absorb it without ever announcing that it is a correction?

Other things I'm stuck on:

- What has to be true in their heads before the demon even looks like a threat? My guess is they need to actually feel why the second law is statistical and why "hot side and cold side" is a claim about how many arrangements there are rather than about messiness — but I'm not sure how early I can get that without losing them, and I'm not sure I should do it with a gas at all.

- The example that has to carry the whole thing. I keep going back and forth between the one-molecule box with the partition, and a crowd version — a doorman at a bar who only opens the door for people moving fast. My worry about the one-molecule version is that it looks like a trick: one particle isn't a gas, so nobody will believe it generalizes. My worry about the doorman is that a real door has hinges and friction and a person in it, and irreversibility sneaks in the back way. Which one, and what does the example have to have and not have?

- Misconceptions. My list so far: erasing a bit obviously costs energy because my laptop gets hot (true, but at completely the wrong level, and it's the one they'll bring from their own jobs); entropy means messiness; the demon is basically a refrigerator so of course it needs power; information has to do with meaning. Which of these does the *order of my talk* create rather than the audience bring? And where does each one get formed, and where do I kill it — I don't want to pre-empt a mistake nobody has made yet.

- The part I care most about: I don't want them walking out thinking "the demon fails." The reason I picked this topic is that our team got into an actual argument about whether a bit is a real physical thing or just a pattern we impose on the world, and I want them to leave holding that information really does buy you work *and* that the second law comes out untouched. Is there a version of the arc where that's the punchline instead of "and then Landauer saves the day," and if so where does the reveal go? If I say "wait for it" they'll check out; if I say it in minute five the rest is anticlimactic.

- Check for understanding. I was going to hand them a variant at the end: the demon never erases, it just keeps allocating fresh memory forever and writing into it — can it go on extracting work indefinitely? Is that the right probe, what does an actual good answer sound like, and what do I do if half the room says yes, obviously it can.

- And what should I openly refuse to do at this level and say out loud that I'm not doing? I can't put anything harder than multiplication on the board — is the kT ln 2 business fatal without logarithms, or is there a way around it?
