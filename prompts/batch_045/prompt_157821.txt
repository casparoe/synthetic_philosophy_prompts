I've been reading the LessWrong-adjacent population-ethics and longtermism discourse for something like a decade, and I keep hitting the same wall. Every argument about climate policy, embryo selection, germline editing, or existential-risk mitigation eventually bottoms out in the non-identity problem as Parfit set it up in *Reasons and Persons*, and then it either collapses into the shrug that a person who would not otherwise have existed is not harmed, or into someone proposing that we taboo the word harm and start over. I would like to stop relitigating applications and instead write down the specification first.

Here is what I want: the requirements an account of the moral significance of identity-affecting choices has to meet, where the account is meant to do one particular job. The job is to serve as the module a decision procedure calls when it compares long-horizon policies whose outcomes differ not only in how well people fare but in which people, and how many, there will be. Not a theory of everything. A subroutine that returns (a) a ranking of the options available to the agent and (b) some verdict about what is owed, or what has gone wrong, when the chosen option is identity-affecting. Adequacy should be judged relative to that use, and I want you to say explicitly when a requirement follows from that purpose and when it is just something a particular camp has grown attached to.

These are the requirements I keep circling back to; treat them as raw material rather than as a list to endorse. Clean them up, delete the ones that are really cached conclusions wearing the costume of a desideratum, add the ones I have missed, and attach to each the case or consideration that motivates it:

- It must be able to say something about the depletion-style case — the policy that gives the present generation a small benefit and leaves later generations much worse off, where those later generations exist only because of that very policy. Condemning it is not compulsory; explaining the intuition away in a way I can steelman is acceptable. Silence is not.
- It must handle the contrast I cannot stop thinking about: selecting among candidates who already exist versus creating someone. Choosing which of several already-created embryos to implant, or editing the genome of an existing embryo, looks relevantly different from choosing to conceive now rather than later, or choosing which gametes to combine. Say whether that difference is moral or merely causal, and whether the timing of the intervention — before there is any candidate at all, versus once there are several — is supposed to matter.
- It must not produce cliff edges. Verdicts should not flip because a causal chain reaches a few hours further back into the circumstances of a conception, given how easily identity scrambles. Pair the obvious depletion case with a near-twin of it that leaves identity intact and ask whether the two verdicts can differ discontinuously.
- It must be usable by an agent who does not know and cannot know who will exist. No requirement to identify the counterfactual victim by name as a precondition for acting.
- It must respect some distinction among being worse for someone, being wronged, being impermissible, and being blameworthy, and it must state what residue is left — guilt, regret, duties of compensation or apology, a reason to change institutions — when an act is wrongful although it wrongs no one. Collapsing all four into one is a substantive move and should be flagged as such.
- It must take a position on the worth-living threshold and on whether creating a good life and creating a bad life are reasons of the same shape. Include the boundary case where a life is barely worth living, and the case where the alternative to creating someone is creating no one at all, so that the comparison class is empty.
- It must rank outcomes across variable populations and do so at scale, since any two long-run policies differ in the size and composition of the future population. State its stance on the repugnant-conclusion family of problems, and tell me whether avoiding them is a genuine requirement or a preference of one camp.
- It must not make the verdict depend on who is choosing — parent, IVF clinic, regulator, voter — unless it can say why the role of the agent carries moral weight here.
- It must not quietly inherit a contested metaphysics. If the verdict depends on how fragile personal identity is, or on whether a given person could have originated from different gametes, that dependence should be visible and the verdict should be stated conditionally on it.
- It must not double-count: benefit-of-existence, aggregate welfare, and any identity-independent value of a population have to be kept apart, and I want the argument for whichever of them is doing the work.
- It must handle mixed cases, where a policy both worsens the prospects of people who will exist either way and determines who exists later, including cases where the two groups partially overlap.

Three things I care about most. First, mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular tradition, and say which entries on my draft list are really conclusions smuggled in as criteria. Second, and this is the part I most want: identify which subsets of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, and say plainly whether the impossibility results in population ethics bear on the subsets you find — if the honest answer is that no module can do this job, I would rather have that than a list that looks satisfiable. Third, tell me which single requirement excludes the most candidates, and why.

Only after that: a short scorecard, a few lines each, for Parfit's no-difference style impersonal view, total and average utilitarianism, critical-level or threshold views, and person-affecting restricted views. I care much less about the scoring than about the criteria, so keep it brief and do not pad it. Assume the standard apparatus — the conservation and depletion case, the repugnant conclusion, the person-affecting intuition — as known; do not spend paragraphs restating it.
