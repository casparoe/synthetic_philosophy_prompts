On Saturday I am best man at the wedding of two moral philosophers. The groom has published a defence of transparent act-consequentialism: an act is right just in case it maximises well-being; a fully reflective agent should be moved by precisely that thought; and Williams's complaint that a man who stops to ask whether saving his wife really is for the best has one thought too many records, at most, a psychological or conversational awkwardness rather than a defect in his reasoning — a person's integrity, on the groom's view, matters only insofar as having it tends to produce good outcomes. Half the room will be their colleagues, several of whom think the integrity objection is decisive and who will notice if it is quietly traded for the quite different objection that consequentialism demands too much; the other half are family, who know the couple's work only as something about doing the most good and who expect an ordinary, warm wedding toast. Write me a three-minute toast in which the groom's commitment to the bride is his reason for marrying her and not merely an upshot of his reasons — she should not be able to hear herself as the best option currently available — while you neither refute him nor imply that he is a worse person for believing what he believes, and do not turn the toast into a lecture. Aim it at both halves of the room.
