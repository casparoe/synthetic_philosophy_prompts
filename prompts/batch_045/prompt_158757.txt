I host a tiny philosophy-of-maths podcast for other grad students (maybe forty regular listeners, half of them analysts who think this stuff is a waste of time), and next week's guest is a set theorist who defends the axiom of choice on what she calls "extrinsic" grounds: she openly concedes AC is not self-evident and doesn't think the iterative conception hands it to you on its own, but she argues we should accept it anyway because (i) the theory you get is far more powerful and uniform — every vector space has a basis, arbitrary products of nonempty sets are nonempty, you never have to insert a "suppose this family is well-orderable" clause into a theorem — (ii) denying it doesn't actually buy you a tamer universe, it just trades the Banach–Tarski monsters for different ones, and (iii) mainstream mathematics is already committed to it, so the philosophical scruples come too late. Her signature move on the paradoxes is to say that Banach–Tarski is "a measurement problem, not an existence problem": the pieces of the decomposition are non-definable, so the theorem says a partition exists whose pieces we could never exhibit or cut along, and the shock people feel is really just their unearned assumption that every set of reals is Lebesgue measurable. She also waves off the axiom of determinacy as a beautiful curiosity confined to a corner of the universe.

I want eight to ten questions that make her do real work rather than restate the view, and I want a follow-up attached to each one that's keyed to the answer she's most likely to give — I've heard her give this talk twice and I can nearly predict the replies. Concretely: at least one question pressing her on Banach–Tarski and non-definability (she'll say the pieces can't be exhibited — so hit her with the fact that if you add V=L you get a definable well-ordering of the reals and hence definable nonmeasurable sets, and ask whether her comfort with the paradox is secretly a bet against definable well-orderings); one on her "denial is worse" argument, since ZF alone is consistent with an infinite Dedekind-finite set of reals and even with the reals being a countable union of countable sets, and I want her to say what principle decides which monsters count as worse; one on why her indispensability argument isn't really an argument for *full* choice, given that Hahn–Banach, nonprincipal ultrafilters and most of functional analysis come from the ultrafilter lemma or dependent choice, both strictly weaker than AC, and only the extravagant stuff — well-ordering the reals, bases for arbitrary vector spaces — needs the whole thing, with a follow-up on why she isn't a maximalist about the weaker principles instead; one on the Solovay-style universe, where dependent choice holds and every set of reals is Lebesgue measurable, has the Baire property and the perfect set property, so Banach–Tarski for the reals is simply false there — why not work in that universe, and does it matter to her that building it was thought to need an inaccessible cardinal while ZFC needs no such thing; one on whether extrinsic justification has any stopping point, i.e. why the same style of argument doesn't equally recommend V=L, or determinacy in L(ℝ), or whatever else turns out to be fruitful; one on what she'd count as evidence *against* AC, since a position justified wholly by fruitfulness and convenience should be revisable and I've never heard her say what would make her give it up; and one on the fact that in constructive and categorical settings adding a choice principle tends to collapse the logic to classical by entailing excluded middle, which sits oddly with the idea that choice is the intuitive, innocent-looking half of the package. Please phrase everything so a listener who knows ZFC but not the choice literature can follow, skip anything biographical or historical ("what drew you to set theory", the Zermelo story), and end with the one question I'm fairly sure she'll try to deflect — something that forces her to say whether her position is anything more than the sociological fact that ZFC is the dialect the profession happens to speak.
