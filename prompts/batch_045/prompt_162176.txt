Hey — I run a tiny reading group, mostly people who came out of the old rationalist blog scene, and we've been grinding on the problem of induction for what feels like the fifth time, and I think we keep talking past each other because nobody has ever written down what a *succesful* answer would even look like. So before we go back to the recieved wisdom — universal priors, Occam-style simplicity, the grue riddle, and the no-free-lunch results saying that averaged over all possible worlds every learner does exactly as well as random guessing — I want the spec first.

Write me the desiderata for a theory of induction: what would an account have to deliver to count as solving, or at least properly dissolving, the problem, given that the no-free-lunch results seem to show that no inductive bias can be justified in a bias-free way. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and mark which ones you think are non-negotiable and which are just the aesthetic preferences of one camp (I suspect at least one thing we always demand is really just Bayesian tribalism). Some candidates I want you to either promote, demote, or replace:

- It has to explain why we are not in the worst case the no-free-lunch theorems describe, without secretly assuming the conclusion — "our prior works because the world happens to be compressible" sounds like restating the puzzle rather than answering it, but maybe it's actually the whole answer.
- It has to say what it would even mean for a prior to be correct or better justified, given that "this prior has worked on past data" is itself an inductive argument. I've started calling this the pulley problem, because every time you try to haul yourself up you just pull yourself back down, and I'd like to know if it's a real problem or a confusion.
- It has to say something about the case where the regularity itself changes. Concretely: you're an agent embedded in a world where the governing pattern drifts, on the order of once per N observations with N unknown, and you have to decide how much weight to put on recent versus ancient data. A theory of induction should either tell you what to do there, or explain clearly why nothing can be said and why that isn't a failure.
- It has to respect the distinction between "the world is such that induction works" and "my prior happens to fit the data so far," and separately the distinction between converging eventually and converging fast enough to matter on timescales that a human or a training run actually has. My group conflates both constantly.
- It has to cash out into something usable — advice for a forecaster deciding how much to update on a regime change, or for someone choosing how strongly to regularize — rather than a metaphysical shrug.

Then two things I care about most: tell me which pair of requirements on your final list you think can't be jointly satisfied (my guess is there's a real tension between wanting the justification for a prior to be a priori and wanting it to be tied to how the world actually is, but you tell me), and tell me which single requirement does the most work in ruling out the usual candidate answers — the simplicity-prior story, the "induction has worked so far so it'll probably keep working" story, and the view that the problem is badly posed and should be dropped. Skip a careful scorecard of those views against the list, we'll do that ourselves; the criteria and the cases behind them are the part I want.
