I work at a small climate-policy institute in Berlin, and I have been asked to draft a short briefing note on a question that keeps slipping out of the register my colleagues normally work in. Two neighbouring countries, inside the same coupled electricity market, bound by the same climate commitments and with comparable engineering capacity, have adopted opposite nuclear policies: Germany closed its last three reactors in April 2023 and is dismantling them, having replaced part of the lost output with gas-fired generation and with imports from its neighbours, while France still derives roughly two-thirds of its electricity from a fleet of about fifty-six reactors, is planning new builds, and has, as I understand the practice, permitted operating lives well beyond the original forty-year design assumption subject to periodic safety re-assessment. Everyone I ask for an explanation gives me either a political story — the strength of the German anti-nuclear movement since the 1970s, the origins of the French programme in the mid-1970s oil shock and the fact that it was launched without parliamentary debate — or a technical one about grids, geology and construction costs. I do not think those stories are wrong, but I want something different: treated as practices rather than as outcomes, what does each policy presuppose about risk, about the state, and about what is owed to people who do not yet exist?

Three features of the pair strike me as philosophically loaded, and I would like the implicit theory behind them reconstructed rather than the policies judged. First, the exit does not free Germany of a custodial obligation, it converts an operating fleet into a sealed one: both countries intend to isolate long-lived waste in a deep geological repository whose safety is argued over timescales of hundreds of thousands of years, which is longer than any state has so far lasted. So when the phase-out is defended as refusing to impose a burden on later generations, what exactly is being claimed — a constraint on adding to a stock, a claim about expected harm, or something expressive, about the kind of polity a nation is willing to be? Second, the same jurisdictions apply a near-absolute, demonstrate-that-it-is-safe standard to radioactivity while approaching climate risk through carbon budgets and a social cost of carbon, that is, through an explicit trade-off that licenses some non-zero quantity of harm. What has to be true about the difference between the two hazards for that to be coherent rather than convenient — their shape, their reversibility, the number and identifiability of the people who bear them, our confidence in the probabilities, or the fact that one harm is imposed by our act alone and the other only together with everyone else's? Third, the two technologies make different demands on whoever comes after us: a fleet requires a competent regulator and operator to persist for as long as it runs, whereas a sealed repository is, in principle, designed so that safety does not depend on anyone reading the archives, and a retrievable repository — which I gather is what French law asks for rather than permanent sealing — seems to presuppose something different again, that later generations should be left the option of revising our judgement and possibly wanting the material back.

So here is what I need. Reconstruct the theory that each practice requires, and be explicit about which parts are strictly presupposed and which merely fit comfortably: where is the practice underdetermined, so that several quite different theories would license the same policy, and where is it in tension with itself? Say whether a single underlying theory could generate both policies given different baselines — different grids, different publics, different inherited capital — or whether Berlin and Paris really are committed to incompatible views of risk. A colleague of mine suggests that the live axis is not how much risk a technology carries but what sort of afterlife it has, meaning what it demands of successor institutions: active custodianship for a century, passive safety with a warning that must stay legible for ten thousand years, or an open option that leaves the future our material to do as it likes with. I would like that suggestion tested rather than applauded — is it doing genuine work, or is it a redescription of the risk numbers? Finally, tell me what would have to change in each practice if the presupposition were swapped: if a country's energy mix is treated as partly constitutive of what it stands for rather than as a mere input to an electricity system, what follows for a Germany that buys nuclear power across the border in winter, and for a France that sells reactor technology to states with no comparable regulator; and if instead one adopted a flat expected-harm view with discounting, would the German closure simply become a moral error, and what else would that commit you to? Please do not give me a policy recommendation, and do not send me to a literature: I want the implicit theory and the places where it is thin.
