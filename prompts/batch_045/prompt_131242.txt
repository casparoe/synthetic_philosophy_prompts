You are a philosopher of language and philosophy of science with long experience translating between academic philosophy and mission-driven research funders. You are advising the scientific director of a small private institute that is about to commit its entire three-year budget – six researchers, one site, no renewal guaranteed – to a single program.

The institute's remit is what its director calls "interpretation without interview": decoding sign systems whose users cannot be queried. Its motivating cases are cetacean vocalizations, undeciphered historical scripts, the internal states of trained neural networks, and, speculatively, an artificial signal of non-human origin. The director has become convinced that Quine's indeterminacy of translation is not a curiosity of the philosophy seminar but the central theoretical obstacle to everything the institute wants to do, and wants the program built on that conviction. She has asked you to say what the program should actually work on.

Assume familiarity with the gavagai argument and with the picture of empirically equivalent, permuted translation manuals. Do not summarize, reconstruct, or explain them – I know them, and pages spent on them are pages wasted.

Before committing to anything, spend a few hundred words of unstructured brainstorming: what you think is really at stake in the institute's framing, which parts of it you find suspicious, and two or three candidate framings you considered and set aside. Then give me the defended agenda.

Five stipulations. These are my labels for distinctions I want you to use in exactly these senses; they are not standard terminology, so do not import other meanings.

1. Mute interpretation: the target system cannot be asked to assent to or dissent from observation sentences. The evidence base is only a stream of behavior or output to be predicted, perhaps under manipulations we choose, but never under query.
2. A patchy manual: a translation manual that agrees with a chosen baseline manual everywhere except on a restricted class of terms, contexts, or domains – as opposed to a global permutation that reinterprets the whole vocabulary at once.
3. The anchor question: what, if anything, breaks the regress of empirically equivalent manuals – an innate similarity metric, a simplicity ordering, causal-interventional access, developmental or acquisition history – and whether any candidate can be stated without already presupposing the semantic facts it was supposed to recover.
4. The identifiability parallel: in representation learning, the same mathematical predicament reappears as non-identifiability of learned representations, and various conditions are known to be provably insufficient or sufficient to pin a representation down. Treat this as a resource for the philosophical question, not as a replacement for it.
5. Home-language bleed: Quine's own extension of the worry to our own idioms, and what it implies for the institute if our own vocabulary is not exempt – in particular what "successful decoding" could then even mean.

Here is the candidate list the director is working from. You may discard any of it, merge items, or substitute questions of your own.

A. The shape of the equivocation space for mute targets: is the class of empirically equivalent manuals for a mute system larger, smaller, or merely differently structured than for a queried speaker, and does patchy indeterminacy behave differently from global indeterminacy in this setting?
B. The anchor question as stated above, including whether the answer differs between the mute and the queried case.
C. Home-language bleed: whether indeterminacy at home is a genuine threat to the institute's enterprise or a distraction that its cases can simply bracket.
D. Success criteria without semantics: which non-semantic benchmark – prediction, manipulation, counterfactual generalization, compression, transfer to a new task – would let the institute declare a decoding project successful, and which of these remain hostage to inscrutability even when achieved?
E. What the identifiability parallel actually licenses: which constraints are doing real work in the formal results, and which of them correspond to anything available to the institute's researchers?
F. The overrated-implications audit: which alleged upshots of indeterminacy do not follow at all – "anything goes", "translation is impossible", "meaning claims are never factual", "we could never know what a cetacean or a network means" – and which, by contrast, do genuine damage to the institute's aims.

My deliverable is an agenda, not a survey. Fund at most three lines. For each one: state the question precisely enough that a reader could tell an answer from a non-answer; say what it is upstream of and what depends on its resolution; assess tractability for six people in three years with philosophical training plus working knowledge of linguistics and machine learning; say what would count as a productive negative result rather than a waste; and name the evidence at roughly eighteen months that would move the line up or down your ranking. Then tell me explicitly which lines you are defunding and why.

I also want your criteria made explicit and their trade-offs argued, not just applied. Importance to whom – to the institute's mission, to philosophy, to the practical decoding projects – and how you weigh it against tractability and neglect. If importance and tractability conflict in your own ranking, say where you resolved the conflict and what it cost you.

Finally: if you think the framing itself is wrong – that the institute should not be funding this, or that some question I have not listed is upstream of everything I have – say so and argue for it rather than answering within my terms. And if you were forced to put the whole budget behind one line, name it in one sentence and give the reason you would defend under pressure.

Constraints: no citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; no references to specific papers, authors, or results that would require looking something up. Reason from the argument and from the stipulations above, and state any further assumption you need. Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words, prose with at most one short ranked list. Do not hedge symmetrically between options; commit to an ordering and defend it.
