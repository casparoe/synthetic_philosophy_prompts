I direct the outcomes-analysis unit of a state workforce agency. Four times a year my team ranks the twenty-odd contractors who run our publicly funded job-placement and training programs, and the ranking bites: the bottom tier loses renewal, the top tier earns a performance bonus, the middle tier goes on an improvement plan, and any contractor can appeal to a procurement board that has no statistical training. Right now the ranking rests on a single number — the share of enrolled clients placed into a job above a wage threshold within six months.

Contractors in the bottom tier always raise the same protest: our clients are harder. Sometimes the data bear this out in a way I do not know how to adjudicate, and three patterns keep recurring.

First: a contractor with the worst overall placement rate has the best rate within every stratum of the barrier index we compute at intake (prior incarceration, no high-school credential, limited English, unstable housing). The reversal is clean and the strata are large.

Second: another contractor's overall deficit disappears only once we sort clients by something recorded after enrollment — whether they finished the intake workshop, or how many weeks they stayed in the program before exiting. Sort by any pre-enrollment variable and this contractor still looks worst.

Third: a small rural contractor looks worst overall and best within strata only when we cut the data very finely, at which point several strata contain six to twelve clients and the ranking reshuffles if two clients are recoded.

There is also a general worry behind these. One of my analysts tells me that if you are free enough in how you define strata, you can manufacture a reversal in either direction for almost any comparison, and that the reversal you get depends on which population's stratum sizes you weight the subgroup rates back to. If that is even roughly right, then the procedure cannot decide whether to adjust by looking at whether a reversal shows up; it has to say which partitions are admissible and whose weights are the standard before anyone opens the spreadsheet. I also need it to be explicit that we make two genuinely different decisions from these numbers — whether a given contractor is doing a worse job with the clients it accepted, and whether the agency is buying worse outcomes for the mix of people we send out — because I suspect those two questions can be answered in opposite directions by the same data, and that no amount of adjustment collapses them into one.

My staff are competent with risk adjustment and logistic regression; the gap is not a technique. What is missing is a rule for which comparisons are decision-relevant, when adjusting is legitimate, and when to stop disaggregating.

Please write the procedure document I will circulate. It should be:

- An ordered set of tests, each stated so that an analyst can apply it to our own records using things we can actually check — the date a variable was measured, whether it was fixed before or after enrollment, who assigned its value, how large the strata are — rather than in the vocabulary of causal graphs. For each step, say what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the question to a named person because it is a question about values rather than facts.
- A stopping rule for disaggregation, including what to do when a reversal is directionally consistent but the strata are too small to support it.
- An explicit statement of what the choice of a weighting standard settles and who gets to make it.
- A short closing section on what the procedure cannot tell us, so the board does not read a ranking as a verdict.

Then apply the procedure to the three contractor cases above, showing the work at each step. I expect at least one of them to come out against what I am inclined to decide; tell me which one, and give me the reasoning I would have to read out to the appeals panel to defend it.

Constraints: eight analysts, one afternoon, a spreadsheet, no new data collection, and outcomes I can defend to contractors who will argue with them. Where the simplification needed to make this usable by my team costs something in correctness, say so and say how much it costs.
