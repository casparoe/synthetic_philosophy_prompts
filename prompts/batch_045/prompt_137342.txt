I'm in a small reading group on paradoxes of the infinite — we worked through Zeno a few weeks ago and this week we got onto Thomson's lamp — and one member, a mathematics graduate student who is far more at ease with limits than the rest of us, put an argument on the whiteboard that I have not been able to shake. Every step looks like something we were already inclined to say about the lamp, the reasoning looks valid, and yet it appears to entail that a point mass moving along a line never arrives anywhere. Please take the role of a philosopher of physics or a metaphysician who works on the structure of time and is equally comfortable with the topology of limits, and think it through carefully rather than giving me your first reaction: before you deliver a verdict, brainstorm two or three candidate replacements for whichever step you think fails, and price each of them.

Here is the argument as I have reconstructed it. (1) No brute facts about states: the state of a system at a time *t* is fixed by its states at times strictly before *t*, and there is no further fact — no bare *and then it is thus* — over and above the prior history. (2) A history can fix a state at *t* only by settling down: there must be some earlier time *t′* such that the system is in one and the same state throughout the interval from *t′* up to *t*. For if not — if the state keeps changing again and again however late you look before *t* — then every candidate verdict is overturned by something later, and nothing survives to *t*. (3) Thomson's lamp: off at *t* = 0, on at 1/2, off at 3/4, on at 7/8, and so on. Between any time you care to name and *t* = 1 there is another switching, so the lamp never settles down on any interval ending at 1. So there is no state of the lamp at *t* = 1: it is neither on nor off, the description determines no outcome, and the supertask cannot be completed. (4) But (2) is not special to supertasks. Take a point mass on a line with position *x*(*t*) = *t* over the interval [0, 1]. Its state — its position — is different at every instant, so there is no *t′* < 1 throughout which its position is the same; choose any *t′* at all and at the midpoint between *t′* and 1 it is somewhere else. By (1) and (2), its position at *t* = 1 is undetermined. (5) And the same goes for every continuous change: all motion, all growth, every clock hand. So the reasoning that was meant to dispose of the lamp disposes of motion as well, which is Zeno's conclusion, and I do not accept it: the mass is at *x* = 1 at *t* = 1, and elementary mechanics says so. (6) Therefore one of (1), (2), or the application in (3) has to go — yet (1) reads like a platitude about time, (2) reads like the very lesson of the lamp, and (3) reads like a mere application.

Three constraints on the answer. Assume classical continuous time throughout, and do not answer with the observations that real switches cannot move that fast or that time might be discrete; I know both, and I want the conceptual diagnosis rather than the engineering one. The two claims I would most like to keep are that the mass is determinately at *x* = 1 at *t* = 1 and that the Thomson lamp genuinely has no determinate final state — so tell me whether there is a principled way to have both or whether I must pay for one of them, and what the payment consists in. The obvious repair, which the graduate student offered, is that (2) is simply too strong: what matters is not settling down but convergence, the mass's positions converge on 1 while the lamp's states converge on nothing, and that is the whole difference between the cases. I see the appeal but I do not yet trust it, because it looks as though it makes the question whether a system has a determinate state at a time depend on which topology we impose on its state space, and I take it that a coarse enough topology would let an on/off oscilllation converge after all. Is that a substantive discovery about what determination requires, or a sign that we have quietly changed the subject — and if convergence is the right notion, what fixes the topology rather than leaving it up to us? Finally, one thing I cannot work out on my own: if I redescribe the whole setup in terms of the switch's own position rather than the lamp's being on or off, I seem to get a perfectly determinate answer, since the switch positions do converge on a limit, so why does that not simply settle the original question, and what does the gap between the two descriptions tell us about where the real difficulty lives? Please argue it out rather than pointing me to papers or reading lists — I am working offline from my notes and I want the reasoning, not the references.
