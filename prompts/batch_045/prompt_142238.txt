I work in real-time graphics: a dozen years in the industry, I shipped the rollback netcode on a fighting game and later led the late-stage reprojection work on a VR headset. The vocabulary I actually think in is framebuffers, double and triple buffering, vsync and frame pacing, presentation timestamps, input sampling and end-to-end latency, client-side prediction, rollback with re-simulation, server reconciliation, deterministic replay files, and asynchronous reprojection. I have no philosophy training beyond what I pick up arguing with people online.

Here is the thing I want translated. In the color phi display, a red dot is flashed at one position for about a hundred milliseconds, there is a short blank, and then a green dot is flashed a few degrees to the right. Observers report a single dot traveling from the first position to the second, and they report that it changes color partway along the path — at a time and place at which no green stimulus was ever presented. Daniel Dennett made this display central to his case against Cartesian materialism, and in his exchange with Marcel Kinsbourne the two accounts on the table got names. On the Orwellian account the subject first undergoes one experience — say, of a red dot moving — and the record of that experience is subsequently revised in light of the later input, so that what is remembered is the color change that never occurred. On the Stalinesque account the two events are reconciled before anything is presented to consciousness at all: the edited version is the first one the subject ever has. Both sides can agree in exhaustive detail about which neural events happen where and when; they disagree only about whether the point at which the erroneous content enters the causal pathway is to be counted as pre-experiential or post-experiential. Dennett pressed the line that there is no principled basis for choosing, and that the reason there is none is that both accounts presuppose a finish line where unconscious processing becomes conscious experience — a Cartesian theater — and there is no such place.

I want you to do two things. First, restate the dispute in my vocabulary, as faithfully and as literally as you can, in a way that keeps the two hypotheses genuinely distinct from each other rather than collapsing them into one pipeline. Say what the framebuffer, the presentation queue, the presentation timestamp, the replay file, the rollback window, and the reprojection step would each have to be doing on each account, and where in that machinery the two accounts locate the difference. Second, give me the residue report: which claims translate cleanly, which survive only after you quietly distort them, and which have no expression at all in the vocabulary of a rendering and networking pipeline. In particular I want to know whether the translation has a tendency to cheat — whether calling something the presented frame is doing the work that calling something conscious was doing, and whether the pipeline smuggles in an observer standing outside it with a high-speed camera pointed at the panel. For the residue that remains, tell me whether you think it indicts my vocabulary or whether it indicts the original distinction.

To make this concrete, here are three results I am going to stipulate, invented but I think in the spirit of the literature, and I want to know what each account predicts about them and whether the predictions really diverge.

The abort variant. On half the trials the second flash is simply omitted. Subjects press a key the moment they see any dot. Their latency on those trials runs about sixty milliseconds longer than in a control block in which they are told in advance that a second flash will never occur, and the same elevation appears on the trials where the second flash does occur. Does this release signature favor one account, and can the other one tell an equally good story about it?

The load variant. A demanding concurrent task is confined to the interval beginning at the first flash and ending shortly after the second. Under load the mid-path color change is reported on something like forty percent of trials instead of ninety; subjects instead describe a dot that stayed red and then was green at the destination, or two successive dots. Is this a revision process that failed and left the original showing through, or a reconciliation process that failed and left the unreconciled feed-forward content showing through, and is that a real difference?

The decoder variant. With high-density intracranial recording, a decoder trained on color finds a transient that looks like red at an intermediate position shortly after the first flash, and finds nothing looking like green at an intermediate position until after the second flash. Both accounts, I take it, will claim that transient. So here is the sharper question. In my world, the question of whether a frame was held in the buffer and never scanned out, or was scanned out and then patched in the replay file, has a determinate answer, and I know exactly what instrument would settle it. Tell me what the corresponding instrument would be for a brain, whether it exists, and whether its nonexistence is a measurement limitation on our part or a sign that the question was never well posed. If it is the latter, I want to know what, if anything, I should still care about in the original debate.
