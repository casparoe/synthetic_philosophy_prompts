i keep going in circles on this and I think what I need is someone to just do reflective equilibrium at me, because I can't get my own verdicts to line up.

Every couple of years the "Malthus was right, just early" discourse does the rounds in the LW-adjacent corners of the internet and it always ends in a stalemate, and I've decided the stalemate is because nobody ever says what would *distinguish* right-but-early from wrong-and-then-rescued. So: here are my gut verdicts on a pile of cases. In each one, stipulate that the theory made a quantitative long-run prediction with a rough date attached, and the date came and went. Don't tell me what Malthus "really" said — I'm not asking for exegesis and I couldn't check it anyway. Take the thesis as I set it up.

A. The possibly-apocryphal 1894 thing: London's streets will be buried nine feet deep in horse manure within fifty years, because horse-drawn traffic is growing and removal can't keep up. Didn't happen, because cars. My verdict: not early, just wrong, and not wrong in an interesting way. The mechanism was extrapolating a technology that turned out to be substitutable, and "we switched to a different input" doesn't strike me as the kind of thing that makes a theory early.

B. The guano/nitrogen scare, as I remember it, dates fuzzy: a late-1890s chemist warns a scientific association that the world's supply of fixed nitrogen (guano beds, nitrate deposits) will run out within a couple of decades, and that without industrially fixed nitrogen there will be mass starvation. The warning gets widely publicized, chemists go looking for a way to pull nitrogen out of the air, within about fifteen years there's an industrial process, and the starvation never happens. My verdict: this feels like the real deal. The constraint was genuinely binding and the warning is part of why it stopped being binding. I want to give the theory credit — though I'm not sure whether "credit" here means "was right" or something more like "was load-bearing."

C. A 1970 book predicts famines killing tens of millions in a named set of regions during the 1970s and 80s. They don't happen there. There are famines elsewhere, later, for reasons that look political rather than resource-driven. In the 1990s the same framework is re-run with fresh numbers and now puts the crunch around 2030. My verdict: disconfirmed. Not because one date was missed, but because the date kept getting regenerated by the same machinery and I can't point to a single case where the machinery called a famine correctly in advance. The "there were famines, just somewhere else" move strikes me as retro-fitting.

D. Circa 1950 a glaciologist predicts a particular ice sheet is losing mass at rate R and will be in trouble by 2050. The observing network of the time — a handful of coastal gauges, sparse soundings — shows no trend through 1990, and the theory is written off. From the satellite era onward the loss shows up clearly, and re-analysis shows it had been going on since before 1950; the old network just couldn't resolve it. My verdict: this wasn't early and it wasn't rescued. It was right the whole time and the evidence was bad. Full credit, zero disconfirmation, and it's a different animal from B.

E. Self-fulfilling: an economist's model says a currency peg will break in March, he says so prominently, capital flight starts, the peg breaks in March. My verdict: I withold credit. The world matching the prediction isn't evidence the model was onto anything, since the world would have matched a wrong model too once the prediction got out. But I'm less confident than I sound, because the counterfactual is a mess.

F. Right by accident: a theory predicts a country will overshoot its food supply around 1990 and that a mortality spike of a certain rough size will follow. No overshoot. In 1994 a war plus a sanctions regime produces a mortality spike of roughly the predicted size, for reasons having nothing to do with food. My verdict: no credit either — but I can't say why without it sounding like special pleading, since the theory said a spike of that size around then and a spike of that size around then happened. This is the case where I most want a principle to tell me I'm allowed to withhold.

G. The tautology: consumption growth on a finite planet can't continue forever, so there's a crunch at some point. Two hundred and fifty years on: no crunch, or a crunch that's always twenty years out. My verdict: neither credit nor blame, it's a theorem about finiteness rather than a prediction. Except: I notice I *would* give partial credit to a version that named a rate, even if the rate was off by 3x, and I don't know why the rate matters to me.

H. The scenario hedge: a 1972-style resource/limits model runs several scenarios and says explicitly it isn't predicting anything. Fifty years on, the business-as-usual trajectory tracks one scenario well on the consumption variables, badly and in the wrong direction on the price variables for about thirty years, and no collapse. My verdict: half vindicated on the trajectory, wrong on the crunch — but I genuinely can't tell if that's a verdict or whether I'm grading on a curve because the trajectory flatters something I already believed.

The two I can't reconcile are B and C. If B gets to say "the prediction caused the fix, so the miss doesn't count against it," then C's advocates get to say the same thing — fear of famine funded a lot of agricultural research — and yet my gut says B is legit and C is degenerating. And E and F pull against each other in a different way: in E the prediction helped bring about the outcome and I withhold credit, in F the prediction had nothing to do with the outcome and I also withhold credit, so "did the prediction cause the match" can't be doing all the work, and I can't see what else is.

My tentative rule, which I don't trust: a missed doom-date disconfirms a theory in proportion to how much of the theory's content *was* the date; it doesn't disconfirm if either (i) the mechanism is still running and the miss is explained by a factor outside the theory's scope, or (ii) the prediction itself caused the response that averted the outcome; and a match only confirms if the outcome was more likely given the mechanism than given the mechanism plus the prediction's own downstream effects. I think this over-generates on C, makes B unfalsifiable, and has nothing defintely wrong with it that I can name but something about F.

So: is there one principle behind my verdicts, or a small set of candidates? State it precisely, say which case motivates each clause, tell me which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and which one you'd overturn if you could only overturn one, and give its verdict on the cases I haven't made my mind up about — (1) a doom prediction dated 400 years out, so nobody involved can ever check it; (2) a theory that gets the mechanism right and the magnitude wrong by 10x, in the direction of over-predicting the doom; (3) advocates who've moved the date three times, each time after the old one expired, but each time with a mechanism-level argument for the new date rather than a vague "soon"; (4) the mirror of E, a predicted abundance that causes the investment that produces the abundance.

Last thing, I want to grep this later: end with a single line that is exactly \answer{...} containing your principle compressed into one sentence of forty words or fewer, and on the line immediately before it put "Overturned: " followed by the case letters.
