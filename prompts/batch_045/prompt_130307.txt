I am writing a short internal memo for a research-funding group that has to decide whether to carve out a standing budget line for work on invertebrate welfare. The draft memo makes two claims. The first is that our evidence for sentience in insects is thin enough that the probability of sentience for a typical insect species should be put low — I used low single-digit percentages as an illustrative placeholder, explicitly flagged as such. The second is that this does not settle the priority question, because the argument is multiplicative: a small probability times an enormous number of individuals is still an enormous expected quantity, and the numbers usually cited for insects killed in agriculture run to the trillions per year. So, I claimed, the priority survives the low probability, and the honest way to disagree with me is to attack the decision rule rather than the probability.

A colleague read the draft and replied, in effect: absence of evidence is not evidence of absence — you can't discount these animals just because nobody has turned up the evidence yet. That one sentence was doing a lot of work. It was meant to move me off the low probability, to shift the burden of proof onto anyone who would set the probability low, and to entomologically dignify the question — to make insect sentience a live moral possibility rather than a settled null. I suspect the line is being asked to do more than any precise version of it can, but I also suspect that at least one precise version is genuinely strong, and I can't cleanly separate the readings.

Please disambiguate it for me.

Give me a menu — at least six entries, ordered from the most defensible to the most ambitious — of the distinct precise theses the line could be asserting. Do not collapse them into the one reading you find most sympathetic; I want the alternatives side by side. Candidates I can see, which you should sharpen, correct, or replace with better ones:

- a purely dialectical reading about who bears the burden of argument, which may say nothing at all about probabilities;
- a claim about evidential sensitivity: where we have not run any test that would have been likely to show sentience had it been there, the silence carries little or no weight;
- the stronger claim that even screened silence — we applied the behavioural and neural markers we would expect to be positive if the animal were sentient, and got nothing — carries no evidential weight, which seems to me straightforwardly in tension with ordinary confirmation theory, since a low probability of silence given sentience is exactly what should drive the probability of sentience down;
- a precautionary or asymmetry-of-harms reading, on which the probability is irrelevant because the downside of treating a sentient animal as insentient is so much worse than the reverse;
- a claim about aggregation and fanaticism: that once the population is large enough, no sane decision rule lets the probability term do much discounting work;
- a claim about the second factor in the product rather than the first — not about whether insects are sentient but about how much their experiences matter if they are, and about whether a very small mind can have a welfare of large intensity.

I have found it useful, in thinking about this, to distinguish unscreened silence from screened silence, and also to distinguish silence about the probability from silence about the conditional magnitude. Tell me whether those distinctions do real work here, whether either of them has a standard name, and whether I have drawn them in the wrong place.

For each reading in the menu, give me four things: what the reading commits you to, in a sentence precise enough that I could put it in the memo; whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false; whether it would actually license the move my colleague was making with it — restoring the probability, shifting the burden, or neither; and what it would imply for the memo's structure, in particular whether it should change my number or only change what follows from my number. Then tell me which single reading is the strongest thing a defender of the line could reasonably claim, and whether even that reading gets my colleague his conclusion. If one of the readings is best understood as a claim about decision rules under uncertainty rather than about evidence or probability, say so explicitly, since that bears on where in the memo it belongs.

Finally, a note on confidence. I do not need citations and please do not give me a reading list; argue from the structure of the argument. But attach to each verdict a rough confidence marker — confident, fairly confident, or guessing — and close by singling out the one claim in your answer that you would most expect a specialist to reject, with a sentence on what they would say against it.
