At a reading group I sit in on, two of the regulars have spent three sessions arguing about what emotions are, and they have now asked me — as the only member who hasn't declared a side — to settle it. I would rather have your verdict than mine. I'll number the moves.

(1) Marta argues for a perceptual or attitudinal theory: emotions are experiences in which evaluative properties are presented to you, roughly as perception presents colours and shapes. Her opening point is recalcitrance: she is afraid of the spider on the wall while believing it is harmless. If fear just were the judgment that the spider is dangerous, she would be in flat self-contradiction, and yet nobody thinks her fear is irrational in that way; what she has is a residual experience that won't answer to her belief. Perception is recalcitrant in exactly this way — the stick still looks bent — so the perceptual model gets the phenomenon for free.

(2) Dev replies that recalcitrance cuts both ways. Emotions track reasons too: when he learns the insult was unintended, his anger typically dissolves, and something that evaporates on receiving new information looks more like a belief than a perception — knowing the stick is straight does not stop it looking bent. He adds a second complaint: perception presents ordinary mind-independent properties, whereas the fearsome and the insulting are response-dependent and motivating; calling the latter perceived is a metaphor doing the arguing.

(3) Marta answers that perception is penetrated by cognition and learning all the time — trained perceivers see structure novices don't, and what you know can change how something looks — so responsiveness to reasons is no disqualification. And the response-dependence of value properties isn't a disanalogy to be explained away; it is what you should expect if emotions are the mode of presentation of a certain kind of property.

(4) Dev then introduces a case he invented: a subject, call her Blanche, whose interoceptive and hedonic responses have been removed while appraisal, reasoning and action tendencies are untouched. Blanche recognises the threat, deliberates about it, avoids it, and will tell you the situation merits fear and that others' fear is fitting — but nothing feels like anything to her: no dread, no relief. Dev says Marta is committed to calling Blanche afraid, and that this is a reductio: strip the affect and what remains is a judgment with a shrug attached, so her theory has collapsed back into the judgment theory it was designed to replace.

(5) Marta bites the bullet: Blanche does have emotions, because things can still strike her as fearsome, insulting or lovely, and that striking is the presentation of value, not a bodily feeling. She turns the case around: Dev's own hybrid — feeling plus representation plus action tendencies — needs a binding condition, since a wave of dread occurring alongside a thought about a spider isn't fear unless the dread is about the spider, and that aboutness is exactly the intentionality feeling-based accounts struggle to supply; supply it and he has smuggled evaluation back in.

(6) Dev says the binding can be given causally and functionally: the state's causing and rationalising spider-directed avoidance is what makes it about the spider, no evaluation required. And he presses the point about Blanche: if all that survives in her is striking, and striking is phenomenologically weightless, then Marta's emotion is indistinguishable from a bare evaluative belief — which is what Marta began by denying.

One thing I can't tell, and it may matter more than any single move: is Blanche even a coherent case, or does stipulating away the affect quietly assume the answer? If the case is incoherent, moves (4) to (6) are idle, and I don't know which of them is then left standing.

Please adjudicate in the following sections:

- Bottom line — about 50 words: who is more right overall, and about what.
- Move by move — about 300 words: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with one or two sentences of justification each, flagging any move that is right for the wrong reason.
- What neither side sees — about 120 words: the distinction, consideration or unconsidered option that neither Marta nor Dev brings up, including your view on whether the Blanche case is coherent at all.
- What would change your mind — about 80 words: the argument or consideration that would flip your verdict, and which of your six ratings you hold least confidently.

State your confidence explicitly, but don't hedge everything into indifference — I want a verdict I could disagree with.
