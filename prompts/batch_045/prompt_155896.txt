I'm a science writer doing a long read on the hunt for dark matter — working title something like The Particle That Wouldn't Show Up — and I want the spine of the piece to be an actual analysis, not a string of physicist quotes strung together with wonder. I keep going back and forth with two sources: one says the WIMP search is the cleanest case you'll find of a reserach programme that has quietly gone degenerating, the other says that's rubbish because the same framework keeps landing novel predictions in cosmology and a direct-detection null is exclusion, not refutation. So I want you to run the dark matter programme through Lakatos properly. And please don't reach for Popper and Kuhn — I know the falsificationism and paradigms framing everyone defaults to, and it's too coarse for what I'm writing. Take the facts as given and don't try to bring me up to date on anything after roughly the last year or two: flat galactic rotation curves, gravitational lensing and the Bullet Cluster, CMB acoustic peaks and large-scale structure fit by a model with a cosmological constant plus cold dark matter, two decades of direct detection (xenon TPCs, cryogenic bolometers, that whole crowd) that keeps pushing limits down and never sees anything, axion searches running alongside, and modified-gravity types plus their relativistic descendants as the rival that keeps doing well on galaxy scales while doing badly on clusters and the microwave background.

What I need first is the mapping made explicit rather than assumed: write out what you take the hard core to be and what's in the protective belt, and then score the programme twice — once with the hard core stated as there exists a cold non-baryonic particle making up most of the mass, and once with it stated as general relativity plus a cosmological constant is the right theory of gravity on every scale — and tell me whether the verdict flips. If it does, I want to know what that says about how much of a Lakatosian appraisal is a decision about where to draw the core rather than a discovery. Then go through the sequence of modifications I can see in the literature: the mass and cross-section windows sliding down, the drift toward axions and other candidates, sterile neutrinos, self-interacting dark matter, fuzzy dark matter, baryonic feedback drafted in to fix the small-scale problems. For each, say whether it predicted something new that was then independently corroborated, whether it merely accommodated an anomaly after the fact, and whether any supposed prediction was really a retrodiction with better PR. And here's the bit I can't get straight and most want you to work on: nearly every result the direct-detection programme produces is a null result, and the programme still seems to be learning. Call it exclusionary progress — a search that shrinks the space of live possibilities is growing knowledge even when every test comes up empty. Does Lakatos's vocabulary have room for that, or is his apparatus just not built for programmes whose characteristic output is an upper limit? Push back on me there rather than agreeing; I'd rather find out it's a bad idea now.

Then run it the other way, because that's the part that will make the piece worth reading: what does this case do to Lakatos? If a programme can be progressive on one front and stalling on another, does that mean we've been appraising the wrong-sized unit the whole time? Does the fact that one generation of experiments now takes twenty-odd years blow up the how-long-do-we-wait problem he more or less punted on? And does the framework have anything to say about a programme that has become an infrastructure — thousand-person collaborations, detector technology that spins off into other fields, a whole cohort of PhDs whose careers are staked on it — or is that precisely the sociological mess his rational reconstruction is designed to leave out? Also I'm interested in whether the case is an anomaly for him in a stricter sense: Lakatos wanted appraisals to be comparative, so tell me honestly whether the modified-gravity rival is a competing programme in his sense or just a different hard core playing a different game, since one of my sources insists it's the latter and I think that's special pleading. Finally, be straight about where the lens goes quiet: if it turns out Lakatos can't tell me anything here that straight error-statistical or Bayesian reasoning couldn't tell me more precisely, say that and I'll restructure the piece around it instead. Flag any place where you'd need a fact I haven't handed you, and give me enough that I can mine it for about 3,000 words.
