I'm a third-year materials science major, and this term I'm taking a metaphysics course in which we've spent two weeks on dispositions: the thought that to be fragile is to be such that one would break if suitably struck, and then the three ways that thought comes apart — cases where the triggering condition itself cancels the disposition (my professor calls these finks), cases where something else blocks the manifestation from happening although the disposition is fully intact (she calls these masks), and cases where the manifestation shows up anyway, produced by something that has nothing to do with the disposition at all. She treats all of this as trouble for a philosophical analysis. I've been reading it as a description of my job, and I want to turn it into something I can actually use.

I work twenty hours a week as a student technician at a small independent product-safety lab. We certify consumer goods for a few retailers' own-brand lines: roughly a dozen requests a week, each of which I turn into a one-page recommendation that a staff engineer signs off on in about fifteen minutes, and whose wording then ends up in front of insurers, customs brokers and occasionally a lawyer. The constraints are tight. Most of our tests are destructive, so we can only ever test a sample, which means the certificate is really a claim about a production process rather than about the unit on the shelf. We cannot reproduce every condition a product will meet in the field. Some of our tests alter the very thing tested — a closure that has to be opened repeatedly in order to be tested at all, a backup battery that is partly drained by the self-test meant to prove it works. And the certificate has to be written in language a retailer will actually print, which means short, unhedged words like waterproof or child-resistant covering what are in fact thresholded, statistical and context-sensitive claims.

My own sketch, which I don't trust, is a four-question loop: name the manifestation; name the conditions; check whether those conditions could themselves remove the disposition; check whether anything else present at test time could be producing the manifestation instead. Please turn this — or replace it — into a decision procedure I can run in the time I have: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which I stop and write not certified, and the points where the procedure has to hand the question over to a person because what remains is a matter of how much risk we're willing to accept rather than a matter of fact. I especially want it to be explicit about what evidence is good enough when the honest answer is that we can't test for that, and about how to word a certificate when the disposition is real but holds only for some of the conditions the marketing copy implies.

Please also run it end to end on three files on my desk. (a) A child-resistant pill bottle that passes our standard protocol, except that the protocol requires more open-and-close cycles than the closure survives: by the end of the test the caps can be opened by a five-year-old, and the intended use is a bottle opened twice a day for a month. (b) A smoke alarm that sounds within thirty seconds in our chamber, but in the field is usually installed inside the decorative magnetic cover sold alongside it, where smoke arrives diluted and late. (c) A budget bike lock that survives ninety-six hours of salt spray with no corrosion, which on inspection turns out to arrive coated in a heavy shipping grease that any customer wipes off before first use — and, inconveniently, the premium lock from the same client passes the same test only because a removable cosmetic sleeve over the shackle absorbs the corrosion; that premium certification is one my lab has renewed annually for six years and it pays for a good part of my hours. If the procedure's verdict on (c) is that I should recommend against renewal, say so plainly and say what would have to change for it to be certifiable again. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot settle: where the vagueness of words like waterproof is doing real work, and where I should expect it to misfire on the kinds of product we see most often.
