I'm designing a 90-minute session for a week-long methods summer school for graduate students in philosophy and the social sciences — 18 to 22 people, roughly half with solid statistics training and half with almost none. The session sits in a sequence on causation and evidence; the slot before mine is on randomized experiments, so randomization is available as a foil. I can't use real money, but I can use chocolate, a small prize, or points that feed into a low-stakes end-of-week team competition. Participants will have laptops, though I'd rather the core of the session be paper: printed tables, index cards, envelopes, dice. I'd also rather they not hear the phrase for the phenomenon until the last ten minutes — several of them will half-remember it from an undergraduate methods class, and once the label is in the room it tends to do the thinking for them.

What I want them to leave with is not the moral that one should always disaggregate, which I suspect is what a straight walkthrough of the well-known admissions-by-department table actually produces. Three things instead. First, that the reversal is an arithmetic fact about weights: it can only happen when the cell sizes are lopsided relative to the within-cell margins, and I'd like them to feel that by trying and failing to build a reversal with balanced denominators. Second, that there is no real conflict between the cells all favouring one option and what any actual decision-maker should do; the aggregate numbers are a weighted average with weights fixed by how people were sorted into options, and nobody's choice is governed by those weights. The sorting should be something the room does, not something I announce. Third, that whether to condition is settled by where the partition variable sits in the causal order and not by anything visible in the table, and that there is a mirror-image case — partitioning on something the option itself causes — in which disaggregating is the error. One colleague also insists the reversal is impossible whenever allocation is randomized; I think that is only true in the population rather than in any finite sample we can generate in a room, and I'd like the session to survive that distinction rather than quietly rely on it.

The first two points I can half-see how to stage; the third I can't. My fragments are: a data-generation round in which half the room runs a triage or referral process that assigns cases to options and thereby produces the lopsided sorting on its own, and then a second run in which a coin flip replaces it and the reversal vanishes; a construction round in which pairs are handed target cell rates and told to make the aggregate flip, and discover they can only do it by inflating one cell enormously; and a role split between a quality-control officer writing the annual report on how the two options actually did last year, and a chief who must set next year's protocol for all comers regardless of which cell they fall into — the same table, two verdicts that I hope are both defensible, and the fight that follows. My worry about that last one is that it is too verbal and that students will just talk past each other. I also want the causal order to be something they can inspect in the machinery they built or were handed, and then bet against, rather than something I assert from the front of the room.

Please design the session: the rules, the materials, the roles, a minute-by-minute timeline for 90 minutes, and a payoff or scoring scheme that needs no money and that actually punishes the wrong rule. Give me the debrief that maps particular moments onto the three points, and say plainly which of the three the activity cannot produce on its own and which I will have to state anyway. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I am especially worried about the half of the room with no statistics, about someone spotting the mechanism in the first round and spoiling it for everyone, and about tables too small for the reversal to be visible — and how to handle each. Finally, tell me what would change your mind about the design as a whole: which argument would convince you that staging it this way teaches the wrong lesson, that the role split is a fake conflict, or that one of my three target takeaways is mistaken or less central than I am treating it.
