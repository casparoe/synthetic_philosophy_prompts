Write an Onion-style news article — headline, dateline, and roughly 800 words — datelined from the annual convention of a fictional trade association for corporate compliance and audit professionals, reporting on the runaway success of a consultancy called Meridian Incompleteness Partners, which sells undecidable findings as a product.

The premise: Meridian's core offering, marketed as Gödel Assurance, takes a client's existing formal system — a tax code, a model-risk framework, an antibiotic-stewardship protocol, anything stated as a computably axiomatizable theory strong enough to encode a modest amount of arithmetic — and returns that system's Gödel sentence, delivered in a leather binder as an "unprovable but true compliance finding," together with an invoice. The upsell, Consistency+, adopts the delivered sentence as a new axiom, yielding a strengthened system that has its own Gödel sentence, billed annually and in perpetuity.

The target is Gödel-mongering: the habit of invoking incompleteness as a free-floating license to announce that reason has limits, that minds outrun any machine, or that some favored claim is "provably unprovable." The jokes should come from getting the mathematics right, so that a mathematical logician laughs rather than winces, while a reader with no formal background can still follow the story.

Work the following material in without ever breaking the deadpan register or lecturing the reader:

- A sales engineer turning away a would-be client whose framework is too weak: its arithmetic has addition but no multiplication, so it is complete and decidable and there is no Gödel sentence to sell. The engineer's line should be about whether the client's policy set can encode its own syntax.
- A client insisting there must be some mistake, because first-order logic is complete, and a Meridian representative patiently explaining, at some length, that completeness of the underlying logic and negation-completeness of a theory are different things — billed as two separate line items.
- The fine print: the delivered sentence is unprovable only if the client's system is consistent. If the system turns out to be inconsistent it proves everything, including the sentence, which is then simply false, and Meridian's findings are retroactively unfounded. Note, without comment, that this clause has been invoked twice.
- The flagship offering Meridian cannot deliver: a system's proof of its own consistency, which clients keep demanding. Include the compliance note that a system which does manage to prove its own consistency statement is thereby a poor prospect, since an inconsistent system proves everything, including any audit report Meridian writes about it.
- A "human intuition" service tier in which Meridian partners certify the truth of a delivered sentence by simply seeing it. Let a customer testimonial reveal, without editorializing, that this certification quietly requires the partner to vouch for the consistency of a system the system itself cannot vouch for, and that Meridian's clients keep vouching for systems later found to be inconsistent.
- An offshore "interpretive services" division operating in a nonstandard model in which the delivered sentence comes out false, its refutation being a proof of nonstandard length.

Include at least two quotes from named fictional sources: one enthusiastic client and one skeptic — a rival consultant who sells "completeness certificates" and argues that most sentences Meridian delivers are undecidable in the client's system and utterly uninteresting.

Keep the register straight-faced trade journalism throughout: reporters treat the product as normal and the objections as minor industry friction. No winking, no footnotes, no explanatory asides, and no summarizing what the theorems "really mean." By the end, a reader should have absorbed why the "unprovable but true" label is conditional on consistency, why the deliverable regenerates itself forever, and why certifying it by intuition is not the free move it is advertised as.
