I'm on my university's student union allocations committee — we move about $40m a year out of a "student life fund" and every spring we have the same four-hour fight about splitting it between mental health services, subsidised housing, direct cash transfers to students, and just leaving it in the endowment. A friend of mine who works on DAO governance stuff keeps telling us to stop arguing and run it futarchy-style: we argue once about what "student flourishing" even means and fix it as a number, then let prediction markets tell us which allocation makes that number highest. I've got the general shape of the vote-on-values-bet-on-beliefs setup and I don't want another explainer of it. What I want is the spec: what would such a procedure actually have to get right before I'd let real money ride on it, given that our purpose is boringly practical — a decision procedure we could actually operate next spring, on real allocations, with no legal exposure, not an ideal yardstick for thinking about governance.

So: give me the desiderata list, each one with the concrete case behind it rather than just the label. Here's the material you should build the cases out of, since these are the things that keep me up. (1) Our only usable welfare metric is a "student flourishing index" built from a survey run five years later by the university's own institutional research office, which has its own incentives and which has already revised the index methodology twice. (2) The decision isn't binary — there's a knob for how much of the pot to spend at all, so conditional markets would have to be opened on a continuum of values and we can only discretise. (3) The likely traders are students, who are also the people whose survey answers constitute the metric, and at least one of them will be a landlord or a counselling-service vendor with a big side stake in the outcome; our market will be thin, maybe thirty serious participants, and possibly one genuinely informed one. (4) Whatever we adopt is the only branch that gets observed — the market conditional on the option we reject never resolves, so I don't see how the thing settles at all, and I want you to take that seriously rather than hand-wave it. (5) We probably can't run real-money markets under university rules, so it'd be play money or a small prize pool, and I genuinely don't know whether "the stakes have to be real" is a real requirement or a prejudice of the economics camp. (6) We can randomise: the housing pilot could be run in half the halls of residence and not the others, so for some of these questions we could get an actual counterfactual instead of a prediction about one.

For each requirement, tell me why it's on the list and whether it's non-negotiable for our purpose or a preference of one faction (the mechanism-design people, the democrats, the anti-Goodhart people). Then the part I most want: which subsets of the desiderata can't be satisfied together — I'd bet "settles to a definite result" and "tracks the genuine causal effect of the policy" are already in tension, and I suspect "metric we actually care about" and "metric traders can't influence" are too, but you tell me. And tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding designs. Keep the weight on the criteria and their cases; a short scorecard at the end — a straight student-body vote, an expert committee, a real-money conditional market, a play-money version, and just running the randomised pilot — is enough, five or six lines, I don't need prose.
