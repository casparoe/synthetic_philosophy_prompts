Assume for the sake of argument that the causal machinery works: I'm not asking whether karma is real, only whether a certain version of it really is a theory of moral desert.

Call it the ledger model. Every intentional act carries a fixed karmic weight; the weights aggregate into a single balance; the balance fixes the hedonic character of the agent's later experiences; and weights are fungible — an act of generosity can offset an act of cruelty, and in some versions merit can be dedicated to, or absorbed on behalf of, another person.

Two features make me doubt that this is desert at all. First, desert as I understand it is non-fungible and non-transferable: that I deserve to suffer for what I did does not entail that I may instead perform five good acts and pay the difference, nor that someone else may undergo the suffering in my place. Second, the ledger never identifies the charge. The agent who suffers is never shown which act the suffering answers, so it seems unable to do the communicative work that retributive theories of punishment assign to suffering — condemning the agent for that specific wrong.

Please explain, at the level of the concepts and without appealing to textual authority: (1) what is lost normatively if karma turns out to be a mechanism of compensation or of self-cultivation rather than of desert — in particular, does the claim that a sufferer is getting what she deserves survive, and does anything that functions as a reason to have acted otherwise survive? (2) whether a coherent middle position is available, one that preserves desert's proportionality and its tie to the agent's own acts while still permitting some form of offsetting, and what conditions such a position would have to impose; and (3) whether the silence about which act is being answered for is fatal to the desert reading or merely an inconvenience.
