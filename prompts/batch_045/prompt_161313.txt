I serve on the planning board of the town where I grew up, and with my sister I own a small two-family house on the older side of town. Last spring the board passed an energy and stormwater code that applies only to buildings constructed or substantially renovated after the effective date. Existing buildings keep whatever they had; the exemption travels with the building until it is sold, substantially renovated, or left vacant for a year, and it lapses for everyone after seven years. At the same meeting we voted, unanimously and with no compensation, to require owners of existing rental units to install hard-wired smoke and carbon-monoxide alarms and to replace old knob-and-tube wiring within three years. Our house is exempt from the first rule and subject to the second, so in a single hour I voted on rules that spared me money and rules that cost me money.

At a training session someone said that grandfathering is simply how you make a regulation a Pareto improvement: nobody is made worse off, so nobody has a complaint. One board member, who studied economics, was unsatisfied and kept asking whether the change was a safe improvement — his term, as he used it, for one that is still an improvement no matter how you settle three things: whose welfare counts at all, what counts as making someone better or worse off, and how the uncertainty about future costs and benefits resolves. On his way of putting it, a policy can leave everyone better off under one way of filling those in and still not be an improvement. I have been turning that over, because the exemption is not obviously a benefit even to the owners it covers: a landlord might have come out ahead under the new code through lower utility bills or higher rents, and neither she nor we know that in advance.

What does our exemption practice actually presuppose? Is it genuinely running a Pareto principle, and if so which one — that only identifiable, currently existing people can be made worse off; that the pre-reform baseline is the morally salient one and not merely the easiest to compute; that an option to comply that you never wanted is not something you can be deprived of? Or is it better explained by a compensation test, by reliance on expectations, by a desire not to provoke opposition, or by nothing normative at all? Where is the practice underdetermined, such that several quite different theories would license the same exemptions? And given that we imposed retrofit costs on the same owners in the same meeting, that our exemption expires in seven years, and that the tenants of unimproved units, the neighbors, and the people who will move here later are all affected, does grandfathering achieve safety in my colleague's sense, or only look like an improvement under one favored way of filling in the blanks? What would a board have to do differently if it took the safer notion seriously?
