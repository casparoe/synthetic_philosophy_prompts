I help put together a tiny satirical zine with some people from my pragmatics reading group — one issue a semester, last time we did a fake insurance policy covering "epistemic risk" — and for the next one I want a piece on Grice. Roughly 700-900 words, formatted as a numbered bureaucratic document: think a tax return or compliance filing rather than an op-ed.

Here's the setup I have in mind. It's "Form IC-4: Declaration of Conversational Implicature," issued by a fictional Bureau of Utterer's Meaning inside a Department of Cooperative Compliance. The Bureau has identified what it calls the Grice Gap — the assessable surplus of what you managed to convey over what you actually said — and the form exists to close it. I want the jokes to come from the doctrine being right rather than from name-dropping, and I want the whole thing to quietly work as a reductio: the form cannot succeed, and the reasons it can't are the reasons implicature is what it is.

The details I'd like baked in: a checkbox section distinguishing particularized implicatures (require a narrative explanation and a reconstructed "calculation" showing how a hearer was meant to get there) from generalized ones (pre-printed, flat rate), with conventional ones — the sort carried by "but" or "therefore" — stamped "NOT OUR JURISDICTION, REFER TO OFFICE OF LEXICAL CONTENT." A section on the four maxim categories where the filer must declare which one they exploited, and where the options for flouting, violating, infringing and opting out are all subtly traps. And then the mechanisms that make the form self-defeating: the Cancellation Rider (you may cancel an implicature, but a rider filed after the moment of utterance is void, so honest declarants always file late); the rule that writing the implicature down converts it into part of what you said, so the return destroys what it reports; the non-detachability provision (rephrasing in any form that preserves what was said preserves the liability); a "constructive cooperation" doctrine under which you may not opt out of the Cooperative Principle, since the filing of the opt-out is itself a contribution to the exchange; and the fact that implicature is indeterminate, so any two auditors assessing the same utterance come up with different figures. I'd also like at least one footnote or marginal note from an auditor wrestling with the signature block, since the thing they want signed is the speaker's reflexive intention — the intention that the hearer respond by recognizing that very intention — and there is no place on a form to put it.

Can you also include one quote from a sceptical outsider, a formal semanticist from some invented institute who insists that none of this belongs to semantic content and the Bureau has no jurisdiction at all, and maybe a line from a taxpayer who tried to comply by saying only what was literally true. Keep the tone deadpan and legible — someone who's never heard of Grice should still find it funny, and someone who has should wince in recognition. No citations or bibliography obviously, I just want the piece. American English please, and feel free to keep my typos in the spirit of things if you want.
