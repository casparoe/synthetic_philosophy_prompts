I'm in a small reading group that has spent the last two sessions on the problem of the identity of indiscernibles and Black's two-sphere universe, and I've come away convinced that the standard anti-haecceitist reply is doing less work than everyone in the room assumed — but I can't get clear on where exactly the shortfall is, so I want a careful explanation rather than a verdict on who wins. Here is where I am. The case: an otherwise empty universe containing exactly two spheres, alike in every intrinsic respect, five miles apart. Nobody thinks either has a monadic property the other lacks. The move that is supposed to save the day without primitive thisness is the relational one, in three grades: absolute discernibility (a monadic difference), relative discernibility (an antisymmetric relation that only one of the pair bears to the other), and weak discernibility (a symmetric but irreflexive relation — being five miles from — that each bears to the other but not to itself). The spheres are said to be merely weakly discernible, and that is supposed to ground their numerical distinctness while leaving no residue of haecceity.

My worry began with a variant I made up and that nobody in the group knew what to do with: keep everything as before, but put the two spheres in exactly the same place — fully superposed, same size, same shape, same everything — so that the only relations left between them (identity, colocation, exact similarity) are ones each also bears to itself, and there is no irreflexive relation anywhere in the world for the weak-discernibility move to exploit. My intuitions pull apart. It seems I can coherently describe a world containing two superposed spheres; and it seems equally that a world containing one sphere would be qualitatively indiscernible from it, so that 'two' here is a stipulation rather than a fact, which is what the haecceitist has been saying all along. And if the coincidence case does collapse in that way, then I no longer see how the separated case ever did the work people credit it with, since it only got moving because a contingent geometric feature of the example happened to hand it an irreflexive relation. A second thing bothers me about the statement of the reply itself: in order to say that A bears the distance relation to B but not to itself, I seem to need already to have latched onto A and B as separate relata, and that is precisely what an anti-haecceitist should say cannot be done in a world with no qualitative asymmetry to latch onto. I can't tell whether this is a real circularity or only the observation that we cannot refer to the spheres.

Please reply as exactly six numbered items, each one to three sentences, with no introduction and no summing-up at the end:

1. In the standard separated case, does the weak-discernibility reply establish the numerical distinctness of the spheres, or does it only restate that distinctness in relational vocabulary — and what would be observably different if it were the former?
2. Does the 'you must already have A and B separately in hand to state the premise' objection have any real force, given that irreflexivity plus the premise that A bears the relation to B is a formally valid route to the conclusion that A is not B?
3. Is the distinction that actually carries the weight here the one between grounding distinctness and making singular reference or cross-world tracking possible, and should the anti-haecceitist simply concede the latter while denying it was ever what the principle of the identity of indiscernibles was about?
4. In my coincidence variant, is there a genuine two-sphere possibility, must the anti-haecceitist identify it with the one-sphere world, and should they treat that identification as a cost or welcome it as an explanation of why co-located material objects are impossible?
5. Does the anti-haecceitist's qualitative grounding of number — that a one-sphere world contains no instance of being five miles from a sphere while a two-sphere world contains two — generalise, and what does it deliver in the coincidence case, where no such qualitative difference seems available?
6. Is the availability of an irreflexive relation doing the real work here, so that the reply is hostage to the geometry of the example rather than being a general account of numerical difference, and if so what would a general account have to supply instead — and is there something both the haecceitist and the anti-haecceitist are missing in this whole exchange?
