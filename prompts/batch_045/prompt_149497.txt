I'm on a small grantmaking committee — we hand out maybe a dozen grants a year, mostly health and poverty stuff, nothing fancy — and I keep noticing a move in our discussions that I don't have a word for.

It goes like this. We're comparing two proposals. One is a programme we've funded before, with audits and follow-up data. The other is something new, and somebody says: but we have no idea what the knock-on effects of *that* will be in ten years, so we can't really rank them against each other. And nobody ever says the same thing about the programme we've funded before, even though it's ten-year knock-on effects are just as unmodelable — we simply happen to have decent data on the first eighteen months.

Two more cases of the same shape. When somebody proposes funding research to work out which causes actually matter, this whole style of objection disappears: nobody says we can't predict the long-run effects of funding the research. And when somebody proposes *stopping* something we've been funding for years, the objection flips sides, and now it's deployed against stopping. That last one is what makes me think it isn't tracking any property of the options at all.

But I don't think it's just bias either, and that's the bit I want help with. There does seem to be a real asymmetry: we have reciepts and audits for the thing we've been running, and for the new thing we have a story plus some numbers somebody made up. And — this feels like the load-bearing part — mistakes in estimating push an option *up* our ranking but never down, so the proposals that look best on paper are systematically the ones most likely to have been over-estimated. That's a genuine reason to tilt toward the boring option. But the boring option's far-future effects are exactly as unknown as the exciting one's, and if the far-future effects are the ones doing the work, then the tilt should be tiny rather than the conversation-ender it usually is.

So: is there an established concept for this specific thing — the selective, one-sided deployment of "you can't predict the consequences" — as opposed to cluelessness in general? I know people talk about cluelessness for the general problem that long-run effects swamp short-run ones, but that's symmetric and mine isn't. "Status quo bias" isn't right either, since that's suppose to be a bare preference for how things already are, whereas this move is offered as an epistemic reason and at least sometimes is one.

If nothing fits, name it and define it carefully, and tell me where it stops: does it still apply when the incumbent is itself only a year old and has no track record? When both options have real data behind them? When the intervention is reversible and you can back out in two years? I'm after which part of the move is doing the real work and which part is decoration, not a label to stick on it.
