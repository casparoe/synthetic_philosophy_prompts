Doing a reading group on sentience and moral patienthood this term — I'm the one presenting next week, unfortunately — and we've gotten stuck on something I dont think has a settled name yet, so please bear with the setup.

Take some system — a brain, a model, whatever — that runs some computation C that we'd otherwise be inclined to say matters morally: a self-model, a distress signal, your favourite candidate. Now picture two variants. In A, C is implemented once. In B, the very same C is implemented about two hundred times over in parallel, the copies never interact, and you can't tell from the outside which one you've got, because overall behaviour and the input-output function at the personal level are identical. This isn't pure sci-fi: interpretability work keeps turning up the same abstract little circuit duplicated in different places, and systems tolerate having chunks knocked out, so there's a real question hiding here about how many times a given computation is "really" being done. And here's the wrinkle that actually got us stuck: you can fade continuously from A to B — start with two hundred copies and gradually make them less and less alike, or describe the system at a coarser grain where the two hundred collapse into one, or a finer grain where they explode into millions — so if the number of copies is doing moral work, there has to be a fact about where the counting stops, and nobody in the room could say what picks that out.

So: my credence is about 0.6 that B's claim on us is NOT roughly two hundred times A's; a friend of mine puts it at 0.15, on the grounds that if each copy is a subject then two hundred subjects undergoing the same distress is just two hundred times the badness, end of story. Give me your own number on that proposition — that B's moral claim is not roughly two hundred times A's, where we get to imagine settling it however you like, oracle, ideal reflection, whatever — but more importantly decompose it. I'd want roughly: how much rides on whether moral weight tracks causal structure at all versus only the abstract personal-level functional organisation; how much on whether N non-interacting copies make N subjects rather than one or none; how much on whether value aggregates roughly linearly across subjects having indistinguishable experiences; and how much on whether "the number of implementations of C" has a determinate value in the first place, given the grain stuff. A number plus one line of justification per term, then the combination, then tell me which term your final number is most sensitive to and which of the two of us is more likely wrong and about what. Also please say how confident you are in the overall verdict and flag the single point you're least sure about. And don't just tell me it depends on your theory of conciousness — I want the number, and then the hedging. If you think the setup is incoherent, say so and give the number anyway conditional on it making sense.
