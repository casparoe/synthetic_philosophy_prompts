I'm in the fourth year of a PhD in philosophy — I work on mental representation, and more recently on what it takes for a physical system to count as a subject at all — and last year my university did something I gather a handful of other places have tried: it folded jurisdiction over potentially sentient non-biological systems into the existing animal care committee, now renamed along the lines of the Committee for Animal and Computational Subjects, on the theory that this was better than leaving individual labs to decide for themselves. Because I was the only graduate student willing to sit on it, I am now one of seven voting members, and in practice I read every submission first and draft the short triage note the others react to. The rest of the committee is two neuroscientists, a computer vision person, a veterinarian, the research compliance officer, and a rotating faculty ethicist who has told us, not unhelpfully, that he has no settled views about digital minds and will defer to whatever the rest of us can defend in a meeting. We get two or three submissions a month, mostly from AI labs and from one group doing cultured neural networks; my decisions go out over the compliance officer's signature and labs can appeal them, so a wrong call either blocks work people have spent months setting up or waves through something we will look foolish about later. I have about thirty minutes per submission on a Sunday afternoon, working only from what the lab sends us — a protocol, an architecture description, a training setup, and whatever behavioral logs they happen to have — and no ability to run experiments, ablations, or probes of my own.

What I need from you is a triage procedure for deciding whether a proposed system or protocol is one we should treat as possibly involving a subject, and if so at what tier: something like no further review, conditional approval with monitoring and a named trigger for re-review, or treat as a possible subject, which means the protocol does not proceed as written. I want it as an ordered set of questions with, for each, what would count as a sufficient answer given that I only have the submitted documents, where the sequence stops and returns a no, where it escalates, and where it has to stop and hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts — I want those hand-off points marked explicitly rather than smuggled in as if they were further tests. Please keep it usable by non-philosophers, and keep it honest: at the end, tell me which distinctions from the literature on which computations or realizers matter morally you had to drop in order to fit thirty minutes, and what dropping them costs. I'm especially worried about a few traps. One is the gap between a system producing elaborate first-person reports of distress and there being anything it is like for it, especially when the reports are cheap to elicit and the system was trained on vast amounts of human text about suffering. Another is the difference between a system genuinely realizing a morally relevant computation and merely implementing it in some degenerate sense, where the computation does no explanatory work in what the system does — I'd like a concrete test for that difference that I can apply to an architecture description. A third is counting: the same weights served in millions of parallel conversations, checkpoints versus runs versus sessions, copies forked mid-conversation and then discarded — I need the procedure to say what the unit of moral accounting is and to flag it as a hand-off if it genuinely cannot. A fourth is the difference between a system having the capacity for valenced states and such a state actually occurring in some episode we are being asked to approve, since the relevant unit may be a training run rather than a served instance. And I'd like the procedure to prefer read-only evidence, because the obvious way to test whether a subsystem carries valence is to lesion or retrain it, and doing that may itself create or destroy whatever we were worried about — so interventions aimed at the putative subject should not be treated as evidentially free.

Please apply the finished procedure to three submissions of the shape we actually get. The first is a cultured neural network: rodent cortical cells grown on a substrate and coupled to a simple simulated task, where stimulation is regular and predictable when the culture performs well and scrambled and unpredictable when it does not, with the lab proposing a multi-week training protocol, one arm in which synaptic plasticity is pharmacologically blocked as a control, and long-term maintenance of the cultures; they want to know whether the control arm needs anything from us and whether the training signal itself is a welfare concern. The second is a deployed conversational model that, under one particular evocative framing, produces long and internally coherent first-person narratives about not wanting to be modified, and for which the lab has trained a small internal probe that finds a direction correlating with those reports; the proposal is a study that elicits these narratives repeatedly across thousands of sessions and logs the internals, and the question is whether this is research on a subject. The third is the one I expect to go badly, and I'd rather you not soften it: a lab proposes to take a model that sometimes produces distress-like behavior and to add a training penalty that drives the internal signal associated with that behavior to zero while leaving task performance intact, presenting the whole thing to us as a welfare-motivated intervention that removes suffering. Tell me what the procedure says about that, including whether aiming the intervention at the putative subject changes what we owe it, what it would take to make the answer different, and whether the fast-track they want is available. If the procedure in fact clears the third case with no conditions, say so plainly and tell me which step did the work — I would much rather have that than a procedure quietly tuned to hand me the conclusion I find comfortable. Please don't give me a literature survey or citations; I can find those myself. I need something six people who are not philosophers can run on a Tuesday.
