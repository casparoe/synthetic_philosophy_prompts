Hi — I'm the clinical lead for multidisciplinary review panels at a regional hospital system. Roughly 40–50 decisions a month, panels of 4 to 9 people (mix of consultants, registrars, senior nurses), simple majority, advisory to whoever is on call. Typical questions: is this imaging finding suspicious enough to recall the patient; is this patient deteriorating enough to escalate; is this discharge safe. I've been reading about the Condorcet jury theorem and I want to know whether it can be turned into something my team actually runs each week, rather than a thing I half-remember from a blog post.

What I want is a decision procedure we can follow in about fifteen minutes before a panel meets, no stats background required, that decides how to set the panel up for this class of case. In order, roughly:

1. Does the theorem even apply — is there a right answer that exists independently of what the panel says? I have a nagging feeling that for a good chunk of our calls the panel's say-so partly constitutes the answer, or it's really a values call about acceptable risk, and majority voting is just a way of laundering responsibility. If so, say so and hand it to a named person.
2. Is average competence on this class of case actually above half, or are we assuming it? Note that competence is an average over a type of case, not a fixed trait of a person — we get outcome data back on maybe half our calls, and for the escalation ones nobody ever observes what would have happened to a patient we didn't escalate.
3. Independence. Everyone reads the same chart, the same guideline, the same handover note from the same tired registrar, so the errors share a component. My hunch is there's an effective number of independent voters that saturates around four or five and a ninth person buys approximately nothing — but I dunno how to check which regime we're in, or how to weigh a smaller, genuinely defferent group against a bigger, correlated one.
4. Protocol: blind votes, then deliberate, then re-vote? Or just deliberate and vote? Show a running tally or not? I keep hearing both that discussion wrecks independence and that it's the entire point of meeting.
5. Weighting — do we weight by seniority? My guess is no unless someone can show the variable tracks accuracy on this case type.

Two more things. First, a hunch: for questions that are really forecasts about what will happen rather than facts already fixed, majority-of-yes/no is the wrong aggregation device and averaging probabilities would be better. Tell me when to switch, and be straight with me if the switch needs calibration data we don't have and won't get. Second, flag which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and mark where the procedure just stops and a human decides — and say for each stop whether it's because it's a values question or because we're data-poor.

Run it on three cases: (a) recall-or-not on a screening mammogram, five readers, same images, outcome known about a year later; (b) should this borderline patient go to a higher level of care tonight, ground truth never observed, on-call consultant has to sign; (c) a rare presentation where the two most senior people disagree and nobody in the room has seen more than a couple like it. I suspect at least one gives me an answer I don't want — say which, and don't soften it.

Don't bother with citations, I can chase those myself. Sorry for the rambling, I'm writing this between two meetings.
