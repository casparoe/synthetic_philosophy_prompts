I'm retired now — thirty-odd years in experimental astrophysics — and since I stopped working I've been sitting in on a reading group at the local philosophy department. I got interested in whether the simulation argument takes the wind out of longtermism, so I built the argument below to show that it does. Unfortunatly it seems to show a good deal more than that, and I can't find where I cheated.

(1) The case for weighing the far future heavily is just multiplication: the expected value of an intervention is roughly (probability it makes a difference) x (number of morally relevant minds downstream of it). Since the second factor can be ~10^40, even a one-in-a-million chance of steering things well beats anything you can do this decade.

(2) Moral status doesn't track substrate: a mind running on silicon is as considerable as one running on neurons.

(3) Once a civilization can run minds cheaply it can run a lot of them, so simulated minds should vastly outnumber biological ones; by the usual self-sampling move, most of my credence — call it 1 minus 10^-30, I dont care about the exact figure — belongs to "I'm in a simulation."

(4) Simulations are somebody's project, though. Whoever runs us wants a particular stretch of history, and when that stretch ends the run is shut down or archived. Conditional on (3), then, the minds downstream of what I do are bounded by the population that ever exists inside this run: call it 10^11, not 10^40.

(5) So the far-future term in (1) shrinks by about 29 orders of magnitude, and longtermism drops back to something commensurable with ordinary near-term altruism. Good. That's the conclusion I wanted.

(6) But everything that made sims cheap and numerous in (3) also makes re-running and copying them cheap. If our run gets archived and re-run, or if there are many near-identical copies of it, then every copy of me faces the decision I'm facing and — being a copy right up to that point — makes the same one. So the minds downstream of my actual act number (minds per run) x (number of reruns or copies), which by (3)'s own numbers is at least 10^41. Fanaticism isn't dampened, it's amplified — and amplified for trivial acts: in a cheap-copy world, whether I pick up a pen has 10^41 minds hanging on it.

(7) That's Pascal's mugging, and I don't believe it. My grandson says that's just what you get for multiplying big numbers, which is a label rather than a diagnosis.

I want to keep (1) and (2), and I'm fairly attached to (3) since without it nothing gets off the ground. My suspicion is that "downstream of what I do" is doing different work in (4) than in (6). But if I say that, I owe an account of why the local, causal reading is the right one in (4) but not in (6), and of what that does to (1). Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost me?
