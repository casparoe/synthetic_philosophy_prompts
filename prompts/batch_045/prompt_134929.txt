I'm a senior writing the final paper for a seminar on formal models in political philosophy, and I've gotten stuck on whether the idea I want to defend actually does any work. We spent the last two weeks on Sen's liberal paradox — the result that no social decision procedure can satisfy unrestricted domain, the weak Pareto principle, and even the barest condition of individual decisiveness, where each of two people gets their way over a single pair of alternatives in their own personal sphere. The readings present it as a formal impossibility, but in my notes I kept describing it to myself as a voting paradox of the Condorcet sort, and my TA wrote "there's no majority rule anywhere in this argument" in the margin, which I didn't understand at the time and still don't really.

What I want to propose is a way out. It seems to me that the cycle-generating profiles are pathological in one specific respect: they involve somebody caring, at some cost to themselves, about what somebody else does inside that other person's own sphere. So I've been sketching what I call a meddlesomeness filter. Call a person's preference over another person's personal pair meddlesome when they would be willing to pay a cost inside their own sphere in order to get their way in the other person's. The procedure discounts meddlesome preferences before aggregating, and then applies each person's say over their own sphere and Pareto to what remains. I tested it on the book example. Let x be that Lewd reads the book, y that Prude reads it, and z that neither reads it. Lewd ranks y above x above z; Prude ranks z above y above x. Lewd's say over the pair {x, z}, which differs only in whether Lewd reads, gives x socially preferred to z; Prude's say over {y, z}, which differs only in whether Prude reads, gives z socially preferred to y; and since both of them prefer y to x, Pareto gives y socially preferred to x. That is the cycle. But under my filter the comparison of y and x rests entirely on meddlesome considerations in both people's rankings, so it drops out, the cycle never gets generated, and I get an acyclic ordering. I've been reading this as a dissolution of the paradox rather than as a denial of one of its premises.

My roommate, who took the graduate version of this course last year, says I have only denied unrestricted domain in fancier words, and that the filter is self-defeating anyway, because a right that binds only when nobody wants to override it is no right at all. I half grasp her point and can't fully reconstruct it, and I genuinely cannot tell whether I have stumbled onto something interesting or walked straight into a mistake that is obvious to everyone else. So my question is this: is the filter a real dissolution, or just a disguised way of giving up one of the conditions? If it is the latter, I would like you to show me exactly where the result resurfaces — ideally by constructing for me a profile of entirely non-meddlesome preferences that still produces the cycle, or, if no such profile can exist, by explaining why it cannot and what that tells me about which condition I should actually be attacking. And what should I make of my roommate's objection and my TA's remark? I have to decide this week whether to build the paper around the filter or around giving up, or at least restricting, the Pareto principle instead, and right now I can't tell which of those is the more defensible target.
