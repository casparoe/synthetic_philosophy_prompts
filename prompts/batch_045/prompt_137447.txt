I am a novelist, and I need help with something that turns out, inconveniently, to be a problem in metaphysics rather than in plotting.

About two-thirds of my current manuscript takes place inside a persistent online world — call it Ashgate — and the decision my protagonist makes in the final chapter only carries the weight I want it to carry if some of the things that happen there are genuinely lost, damaged, or owed. I do not want the philosophy to be set dressing, and I do not want her to be right merely because the book sympathises with her. So what I need from you is an ordering, not a discussion.

Take the following as stipulated facts about Ashgate. It runs on servers owned by a company; the objects in it are patterns of a certain kind maintained by software and displayed to players through headsets. The company has the technical power to duplicate or restore anything, but it is bound by a publicly audited rule that forbids duplication, and it has announced that it will no longer roll the world back to earlier states. Scarcity is therefore enforced, not merely simulated. Some objects were generated procedurally by the company; others were shaped by players through an interface that records the movement of their hands, so that no two are alike. Some items can be traded for currency convertible, at a fixed rate and after tax, into the money people live on; for a minority of players this is their only income. Players form attachments, institutions, and grievances there, and their lives outside are affected by what happens inside. There is no doubt that the players themselves are real, embodied, and hurt-able.

Here are the cases from the book. Rank them.

1. A tower a player sculpted by hand over four years is destroyed by another player with a one-use item. No blueprint survives. The player who built it died two years ago.
2. A unique ring, given in-world as a pledge between two characters, is obtained by fraud and is now worn by the defrauder. It still exists.
3. A bedridden woman's avatar body is maimed. It can be rebuilt, at considerable cost in time and money, but never as it was. The avatar is the only body she has moved in nine years.
4. A guild's treasury is embezzled. The sum is roughly what two years of one member's wages would be.
5. A valley is renamed and its lighting altered after a sponsorship deal. Its geometry and coordinates are untouched.
6. A player's account is deleted along with every associated trace: the buildings, the correspondence, the record of a marriage performed in-world.
7. Nothing is destroyed at all, but a woman learns that the avatar she loved and lived with for three years was, for most of that time, operated by a paid substitute.
8. A tree is planted in a private garden, at real expense, by a character marking a miscarriage. The company later deletes the garden in a routine purge of unused plots.

For each case give me two scores out of ten. Score A: how real is the thing that is damaged, taken, or destroyed — where part of the task is that you tell me what you are scoring, and defend that choice of scale rather than assuming it. Score B: how much is genuinely lost, all things considered, by the person who loses it. Then combine the two into a single four-tier list, with one sentence per case explaining why it lands in that tier. If any case is really two cases, split it and say so.

Then tell me where the two orderings come apart, and what the gap is: I suspect my protagonist's climactic choice is a choice between losses that look comparable only because I have not thought hard enough about what makes them comparable, and I want to know which of the losses, on your view, is the smaller one and why.

I should also say that I have been working with a distinction of my own, and I want to know whether it is doing any real work or only sounding deep: the distinction between things that are unrepeatable because the rules forbid a second instance, and things that are unrepeatable because of how they came to exist — nothing forbids a duplicate, but this one was made by these hands over these years. Say whether that distinction underwrites anything in your ordering, or whether it collapses as soon as it is pressed.

Two constraints on how you answer. First, do not give me reading lists, citations, or bibliographies, and do not refer me to books: assume you have no access to anything outside this conversation, and argue from the case as I have set it out. Second, and this matters more: I can tell the difference between a settled result and a contested one only if you tell me. So mark, explicitly, which of your judgements most people who work on these questions would accept without argument; which are live disputes among competent people, and what the two or three genuine options in the dispute are; and which are your own position, held by you rather than by any consensus. Where you are uncertain, say so in a way that tells me how uncertain. I would rather have a ragged answer I can think with than a clean one that quietly pretends everyone agrees.
