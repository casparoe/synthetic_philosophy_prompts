I'm in a small reading group — two statisticians, two ML people — working through universal induction and minimum description length, and we have stalled on an analogy that one of my colleagues keeps leaning on. Her line is that Occam's razor just is the penalty term in a model-selection criterion: BIC charges you roughly log n per extra parameter, AIC charges a flat amount, and Solomonoff's prior is the very same idea with an uncomputable length measure bolted on where the parameter count used to be. She then uses this to draw a further conclusion: since heavily overparameterized networks interpolate the training set and still generalize, simplicity is finished as a guide to truth — what does the work is inductive bias, and parsimony was only ever a proxy for it that happened to look respectable inside parametric statistics. I am suspicious of both steps, but I can't locate exactly where the mapping fails, and I'd rather have the audit than a general essay on Occam's razor.

Two things we tried in the group made this sharper rather than clearer. First, we noticed that parameter count and description length can come apart in both directions: a five-parameter model whose parameters are each a million-digit constant is, intuitively, not simple, while a billion-parameter network whose weights are generated by a two-hundred-character program is, in one obvious sense, very simple indeed — so whatever Solomonoff's prior is penalizing, it is not the dimension of a parameter vector. Second, we tried scoring candidate hypotheses by the length of the shortest program written in a language that already includes the libraries we actually use — our numerical stack and our own helper functions — and the rankings came out inverted relative to the bare-machine version. The invariance theorem says the two should differ only by a fixed additive constant, but our constant is larger than our dataset, so at our sample sizes the choice of reference machine seems to be doing all the work. We then tried charging explicitly for the library in a two-part code, and then noticed that if the library is itself inferred from the same data, we can hide the data inside it, which suggests our score is not really normalized at all. I'd like you to say what is going wrong in that procedure, and whether the fix we attempted just collapses back into a mixture over reference machines.

So the audit I want, please. Give the correspondence explicitly — a table is fine: what plays the role of the model, the parameter, the penalty coefficient, the sample size, the noise, the hypothesis class, and the thing being selected. Then separate the disanalogies that actually matter for my colleague's conclusion from the ones that are merely curiosities, and say what features of model selection the analogy quietly imports into the Solomonoff setting that it does not have: computability, finite-dimensional families, identifiability, a clean split between model and data, simplicity as a property of a hypothesis on its own rather than relative to a reference machine, and any assumption that the target is a true generating hypothesis rather than cumulative predictive accuracy. Then the payoff: does her conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table? I can see a charitable half — the standard guarantee for Solomonoff prediction bounds total deviation from the truth by something like the description length of the true generator, which is a statement about prediction and not about recovering the truth or about the virtue of simple theories — and I can't tell how far that half goes, nor whether the overparameterized-network case is a counterexample to simplicity or merely a counterexample to counting parameters. If the penalty-term picture is the wrong analogy, propose a better one and say what work it does that the penalty picture cannot.
