I keep seeing the same exchange in comments around here: someone offers Nozick-style tracking, sensitivity plus adherence, as a way of keeping ordinary knowledge while denying the skeptic's conclusion, and the reply comes back that it only does so by breaking single-premise closure under known entailment: you keep the ordinary claim, you lose the entailed claim that the exotic error-possibility doesn't obtain, and the price is that deduction stops transmitting knowledge. The argument then lives entirely inside skeptical scenarios, so nothing in it can be checked against a case anyone would actually be in.

I want to see whether the price can be made to show up in ordinary life. Please build me a case satisfying all of the following.

1. An ordinary non-skeptical setting: an office, a kitchen, a clinic, a train platform. No brains in vats, demons, simulators or perfect deceivers, no lotteries, and no fake-barn-style environmental luck, since I don't want a failure in the first belief to be doing the work.
2. The subject knows p on the tracking account: p is true, they believe it, they would not believe it in the nearest worlds where p is false, and they would believe it in nearby worlds where p is true.
3. They know that p necessarily entails q, and they come to believe q only by that one valid step, performed correctly. Crucially, q must not be overdetermined for them: in the nearest worlds where q is false, their only route to believing q is still that same inference from p. This is the constraint that makes the case hard, since the standard way to break closure is to let the subject believe q anyway; I want the failure to fall out of the ordering of worlds by remoteness instead.
4. They nonetheless fail to know q, with the failure located in the tracking conditions rather than in truth or belief.

Then state p, q, and the method by which p is known; lay out the relevant nearby worlds; and show which condition passes and which fails for each of the two beliefs. Name the single feature of the scenario doing the work, and say what happens to the verdict if you remove it. Check the case against a safety analysis too, where knowledge requires that in nearby worlds where the subject believes q by this method, q is true: does your case generate a closure failure there as well, and if not, what exactly differs between the two accounts? Finally, given the case you built, which is the more attractive thing to give up, closure or the tracking conditions, and does building it change your sense of what closure failure actually costs us?

If the constraints cannot be met jointly, say which one has to be dropped and show why.
