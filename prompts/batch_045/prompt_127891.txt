I teach an upper-level seminar on rationality and choice — 16 students, one 80-minute session, and no TA available that week. The session is the one where we move from synchronic requirements on preferences to diachronic ones: the money pump argument for transitivity, and the question of what rationality demands of an agent over time (planning, commitment, and the contrast between sophisticated and resolute conceptions of diachronic choice). The students will already have met the standard money pump in the reading, and I do not want the class to be a recap of it. I want them to hit a difficulty the reading's version of the argument does not reach. Please design a single in-class activity that does this.

Constraints I want built in:

1. No lecture up front, and none of the terms money pump, transitivity, sophisticated choice, resolute choice, or backward induction until the debrief. The point has to arrive through what happens to them.

2. Stakes in chips or points, not cash. Chips should be worth something real but small (a ticket in a draw for a trivial prize, or a fraction of a participation credit) so that nobody treats them as worthless and nobody is distorted by the stakes.

3. The cyclic preferences have to be theirs, not stipulated. Give me a cover story with three options scored on three dimensions, together with the score table, such that a comparison rule a normal person would actually use — not one gerrymandered for the occasion — makes most students prefer the first to the second, the second to the third, and the third to the first. Show that the table really cycles under that rule.

4. Before any trading, each student privately records how many chips they would pay for each of the three possible swaps. This is what makes a later refusal cost them something they have already admitted they wanted.

5. The market rule should be uniform and easy to state: at any time you may exchange your holding for an option you prefer, for one chip. Everyone starts with the same option and the same stake.

6. Two market phases differing in exactly one respect. In phase A I announce in advance the exact number of times the floor will open and that it then closes. In phase B the floor stays open, the same swaps remain available, and the closing time is unannounced and not hinted at — there is no last round to reason back from.

What I want the debrief to be able to reach:

- In phase A, accepting every opportunity returns the trader to their original option minus three chips. But as I work it out, the student who looks ahead should not refuse the first swap either: refusing everything leaves them with the original option and no chips spent, while taking only the first swap leaves them with an option they prefer, one chip poorer — and they recorded a willingness to pay more than one chip for exactly that swap. So they are not being pumped at all; they are making one purchase. Verify this and calibrate the chip values so it comes out that way; tell me if I have it wrong.
- In phase B there is no terminal node, so the candidate policies are of the form take k swaps and then stop. As I work it out these are ranked cyclically: one swap beats none, two beats one, three beats two, and none beats three — so no policy is maximal, and the greedy policy (keep accepting) is the worst of them. Check this against the mechanism you design and adjust the mechanism if it does not come out.
- The moral I want them to feel is not that intransitive agents are shortsighted, and not simply that a clever trader can fleece them. It is that with cyclic preferences there is no policy their own preferences single out as best, so if rationality is diachronic it must demand something other than choosing the option you most prefer at each point you find yourself at.

In the second half, rotate half the class into the trader's role: give them another student's recorded swap values and have them try to extract as many chips as possible, so that they discover from the exploiting side which feature of a preference structure makes extraction possible and which makes it impossible.

Deliverables: the rules and materials; a minute-by-minute timeline for the 80 minutes; the exact handout text for the option descriptions and the record sheet; the debrief, with each move mapped to a specific moment in the activity; a frank list of what will go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each case — I expect some students to refuse to trade at all, some to treat chips as worthless, some to turn out to have transitive preferences, and some to conclude that the lesson is never to trade with the house; and an honest note on which part of the philosophical issue the activity cannot stage and I will have to supply in the following lecture. Do not attribute any of the positions to named authors or papers, and do not give me a reading list — I will supply the reading. Aim for about 1,000–1,300 words plus the handout text.
