I work as the quantitative lead at a small forecasting platform, and six months ago we started running model-based forecasters alongside our human panel, so the question of how to score a forecaster has stopped being academic for me. We currently report Brier and log scores side by side on a leaderboard, and they disagree about who is best: log lavishes reward on the forecaster who was confidently right and effectively destroys the one who was confidently wrong, while Brier is gentler and, according to my colleague, lets a cautious forecaster sit near the middle of the scale and look respectable. I have a decision meeting in a fortnight and have to recommend one number, which will then be used both to rank forecasters publicly and to pay out prizes, so it is simultaneously a measurement and an incentive. I started reading around and found the accuracy-first epistemology literature, which is clearly the right neighbourhood, but it seems mostly aimed at showing that incoherent credences are accuracy-dominated or that conditionalization maximizes expected accuracy, rather than at what I need. So before I choose, I want the specification. By an epistemic utility measure I mean: a function that takes a credence function over some partition of possibilities and a world, and returns how far off that credence function is at that world, lower being better, with the expectation of that quantity taken relative to the agent's own credences when we talk about expected inaccuracy. Write me the desiderata such a measure has to meet to be adequate for my purpose, with the case or consideration behind each.

Here are the cases and constraints I keep circling, to be organized, corrected, or thrown out rather than simply echoed back: our forecasters are scored on many questions at once and ranked by a single number, so whatever we use has to aggregate across questions and across forecasters who answered different subsets. A forecaster who says 0.99 and is wrong has to come out worse than one who says 0.6 and is wrong, but I am not sure how much worse, and I want to know what the right principle is for settling that. A forecaster who never leaves 0.5 on any binary question should not be able to win, and I want to know whether that is a requirement on the measure or a happy consequence of some other requirement. We do occasionally catch forecasters holding jointly incoherent sets of credences, and I would like the score to have something to say about that rather than scoring each question in isolation. We run conditional forecasts, where someone forecasts an outcome given an antecedent that then fails to occur, and we run long-range questions that never resolve, or that resolve in a way some forecasters call half true because the resolution criteria turned out ambiguous; I need to know what a measure owes me in those cases. We have had the same target event carved up at different levels of fineness in different tournaments, and I do not know whether the score ought to be indifferent to how finely the outcome space is partitioned or whether some sensitivity there is correct. And because the number is now also a training signal for the model forecasters, I care about whether a measure that gives honest reporting the best expected score is automatically a good objective to optimize, or whether that is a separate desideratum in its own right.

Two further things matter more to me than the scoring of particular rules. First, I want to know which of these demands are genuine requirements on a measure of epistemic value and which are prejudices of one camp: whether strict propriety, for instance, is really a constraint on epistemic goodness or only a cleverly disguised practical demand about eliciting honest reports; whether rewarding informativeness or resolution independently of accuracy is a defect or a feature; whether boundedness is a substantive epistemological commitment or an artifact of not wanting one catastrophic forecast to swamp a track record. Second, I suspect some subset of the desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied — that strict propriety pulls against, say, additivity across questions, or against insensitivity to how the outcome space is carved, or against some natural requirement about closeness to the truth for non-atomic propositions — and I want those incompatibilities flagged explicitly, with the reason each pair cannot both hold, since if the list is jointly unsatisfiable that is itself the answer to my question. Tell me which single requirement knocks out the most candidate measures, and mark which are non-negotiable given that the number must rank forecasters, pay them, and train them. A sentence or two each on the Brier, log, and spherical rules is plenty; the list is what I am paying for.
