I write about science and ideas for a national newspaper, and I have been asked to run a hands-on ninety-minute session for roughly eighteen people at a science-writing workshop next month. The participants will be mostly working reporters and editors, with a handful of researchers mixed in; they will share no technical background, they will not have done any reading in advance, and at least one of them will probably have read Hofstadter. I want them to leave having felt, rather than been told, his distinction between the frame, the outer, and the inner message from the chapter on the location of meaning in Gödel, Escher, Bach: the frame being the bare signal that something is a message and needs a decoder, the outer being whatever in the artifact tells you how to build that decoder, and the inner being the meaning that appears once the decoder actually runs.

The case I care about most is one I keep meeting in my own reporting, and it does not seem to me to be handled in the standard discussions of the location of meaning: the case in which a frame message propagates intact through a long chain of receivers while the inner message is lost, quietly replaced, or never arrives at all. A press office issues a release; the scientists-have-found frame survives every retelling while the finding itself is hollowed out or swapped for a different one; and every person in the chain displays all the outward signs of having understood. I want participants to perform this themselves, physically, in a chain, and then to notice that they transmitted a great deal and understood very little. Name the phenomenon whatever you think it should be called; the activity should earn it rather than announce it.

Design the session for me. There should be no lecture at the front and no vocabulary on the board: I do not want to use the words frame, outer, and inner until the debrief, and I need a plan for what to do if someone in the room supplies the terminology in the first ten minutes. Please give me:

- the materials, restricted to paper, envelopes, cards, and similar; nothing that requires screens, and assume no phones;
- the rules, and any scoring or small-stakes scheme you think the exercise needs, using no real money;
- a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, including how participants are divided and how the transmission chain is arranged;
- a sequence of rounds in which each round isolates one layer, so that participants run into the layers one at a time: one round in which the frame is unmistakably present but no decoder can be built; one in which a real decoder exists but is withheld from half the room; one in which the outer message acts on the receiver without being understood, doing something to them while its content stays out of reach; one in which the frame arrives intact but the content has been silently substituted upstream, so that participants confidently report a meaning nobody ever sent; and one in which two groups hold the identical artifact and extract different inner messages, with nothing available in the room to settle which is correct;
- the debrief, mapping specific moments I will be able to point back to onto the three layers, and showing where the fit between artifact and decoder holds and where it fails;
- an observable test of whether it worked, so that I can tell from what people do rather than from what they say afterwards;
- what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how I should handle it, including the reporter's instinct to turn a silent exercise into a conversation and simply talk instead of passing the artifacts along;
- and, frankly, which part of Hofstadter's claim the exercise cannot stage and which I will therefore have to state myself. My suspicion is that it cannot show that recovering an inner message requires something like general intelligence rather than the right key, and that it cannot settle whether the meaning was in the artifact all along. Tell me what else it leaves out.
