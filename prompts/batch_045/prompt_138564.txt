ok so context, which is that I'm one of five people who run a tiny mutual-aid fund out of a church basement and we hand out maybe six small grants a month from a pot that never covers demand. Last month we had two applications that were basically the same application — both rent, both people out on the 1st, both with kids, no tiebreaker we could honestly find — and we funded one. It's been bugging me ever since, but not in the way that would be fixed by having picked the other one. I think we did the right thing and I also think something bad happened that wasn't supposed to happen. My co-volunteer says that's just incoherent: if we did what we ought, there's nothing left over to feel. That sounds wrong to me, and it also sounds like it would make the whole category of hard choice evaporate, which can't be right either. So we've been arguing about it and I realized I don't actually know what I'm asking a theory of moral dilemmas to do.

So before I go read a pile of stuff I want the spec. What does a theory of moral dilemmas have to deliver to count as adequate for my purposes, where my purposes are concretely: (a) say whether it can ever be true that there is no option we ought to take, (b) tell me what language belongs in our policy about what volunteers are supposed to feel and do afterward — apology? remorse? public note that a claim went unmet? — and (c) not fall apart when the number of applicants is forty and the money covers six, i.e. handle the case where dozens of small unmet claims might add up to one big one. Write me the desiderata list: the cases it has to handle, the distinctions it can't collapse, the results from logic or decision theory it can't contradict, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and mark each one non-negotiable or just a preference of one camp. I'd rather have eight sharp requirements than twenty mushy ones.

Things from the argument I want you to actually engage rather than skip: my friend's principle that leftover guilt without an actual violation is irrational, versus my sense that there's residue either way; the agglomeration move (I ought to help A, I ought to help B, I can't do both, so apparently I ought to help both-and-not-both) and whether ought implies can is doing real work there or is just a slogan people gesture at; whether a dilemma I manufactured myself — we once double-committed money because nobody checked the bank balance — is the same animal as one that just lands on you, or whether the theory needs an separate story about culpable vs. blameless route into the conflict; and the mirror case, where the two claims are made of literally the same stuff with no tiebreaker at all, which I keep coming back to because if that isn't a genuine dilemma I don't know what would be, but if it is then coin-flipping looks like a cop-out rather than a solution.
