My father-in-law turns seventy next month and I've been asked to give the toast, and I've been staring at a blank page for three weeks, so I'm outsourcing it. Context, then the actual ask.

For about thirty-five years he was a mid-level official in the district water authority — the office that decides who gets a metered connection and when. He was also, unofficially, the person everyone in his town called first; his phone was basically a helpdesk. He'd make one call and a file that had been sitting for a year would move. And the deal, such as it was: he never asked for anything, and at harvest, or at a wedding, or when someone's kid got into engineering college, things would arrive at the house — a crate of fruit, a tank of diesel, sometimes an envelope. Everyone involved understood this perfectly and nobody found it strange.

The room: his side, extended family and neighbours, maybe sixty people for whom he is simply a good man who made the state work for people it normally ignores. Plus roughly ten others — my wife's colleagues from the governance/transparency nonprofit she works at, and a couple of her cousins who've lived abroad since they were kids — who will hear "clientelism" the moment I describe any of this and start at each other.

I can't find a sentence that's both true and sayable. I tried to do it the way I'd do it at work, by writing the predicate down: bribe = payment in exchange for an official act? He never named a price and the money came afterwards. Gift = something given with no return expected? Every one of those things created a debt he then honoured. Harm to third parties? Real, but the person actually harmed is whoever never learned the file could move at all, and half the room has only ever experienced that office as a door that doesn't open. I have no philosophy training — I write distributed systems — so assume I know none of the literature.

The half-formed idea I keep coming back to: that what he did was reorder a queue that isn't supposed to be reorderable, so the wrongness isn't in the envelope, it's in the fact that the queue was only legible to people who already knew him. My wife says that's a definitey nice line but that for most people in that room there never was a queue, only a door that opened or didn't.

So: write me a four-to-five-minute toast. Rules. No corrupt, corruption, patronage, clientelism or any synonym; also no "public servant", "honest", "integrity", "served the people" — both lists land as picking a side in front of the other side, and I don't want to lie in either direction. Don't smuggle in the assumption that the way my wife's colleagues think about this is the neutral default everyone else is failing to meet. Warm, specific, funny enough that the grandkids stay awake. True to what he actually did, arriving fruit included. He'll be sitting three feet away and his English is fine. And carry that queue idea if it can be said out loud without turning his birthday into a seminar — if the idea doesn't survive being said out loud, say so instead and tell me what's wrong with it.
