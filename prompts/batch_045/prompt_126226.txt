I am drafting a short position paper on epistemic dependence on AI systems, and I want its argumentative core actually worked out rather than surveyed. Please write the paper as a sustained argument for the position specified below, in your own voice, at roughly 900 to 1,100 words.

The position. Say that a system's failures are legible to a given user when the user can, without redoing the task themselves, tell that a particular output is likely to be wrong: they need not know what the right answer is, but something about the output's shape, provenance, or mismatch with what they expected flags it. Say that the legibility floor is the minimum degree of such legibility a user must retain over a task for their reliance on a system to leave their understanding of that task intact. Argue for the following three-part claim. First, what the erosion-of-understanding worry is really about is not dependence as such but illegible dependence: delegation that drops the user below the legibility floor. Second, understanding should consequently be analyzed not as the retained ability to produce a correct result unaided but as something closer to retained alarm sensitivity together with the ability to say, when the alarm fires, what kind of thing has gone wrong. Third, and this is the corollary I want defended rather than softened: where a choice of tools is available, it is epistemically better, other things being equal, to work with the weaker system whose failure modes you can predict than with the stronger system whose failures you cannot, and this holds even in educational settings where the stronger system would yield better final products.

Early in the paper, distinguish explicitly four competences that discussion of this topic tends to run together: producing a correct answer unaided; recognizing a correct answer when shown one; reconstructing a derivation step by step; and noticing that something has gone wrong. Say which of these your account identifies with understanding, and why the others are neither necessary nor sufficient. In doing so you must handle two cases that pull in opposite directions: the veteran whose unaided performance has decayed through years of delegation but who spots the flaw in a generated proof within seconds, and the student who can follow a generated derivation line by line while grasping nothing of the subject.

The argument should then make the following moves, in this order or a better one.

Steelman the de-skilling objection in its strongest form, not its crude form: any competence that goes unexercised atrophies, and the alarm sensitivity your account rests on is itself a competence that is parasitic on the very abilities delegation erodes, so there is no stable resting place above the legibility floor.

Resolve the self-reinforcement dilemma. As a system grows more reliable, the user's optimal policy converges on deferring to it even when their own alarm fires; but a user who routinely overrides their own alarm loses the calibration that made the alarm trustworthy, so increasing reliability tends to destroy the legibility floor from under the user. Take a side: either the dilemma is real and shows the floor to be unstable, or it is avoidable, and if it is avoidable say precisely which practice, training regime, or design feature prevents it.

Answer the simulacrum objection. Someone who has learned where a system breaks by watching it break may predict its failures accurately while having no grasp whatsoever of the domain: their legibility is a map of the machine, not of the subject matter. Explain whether your account counts this as understanding, and what turns on the answer.

Draw the institutional implication for a university department setting its policy on AI use in coursework, given the third part of the claim. Be concrete about what the department should permit, require, or forbid, and what it should be willing to give up.

Close by naming the single empirical finding that would most damage the argument if it came out the other way, and explain why it has that force.

Constraints. No citations, no named authors or papers, no bibliographic gestures of any kind; the paper must stand on its own reasoning. Define every technical term as you introduce it, and keep the whole thing comprehensible to a careful non-specialist. Argue rather than survey: include no passage whose only function is to report what is commonly said on the topic, and do not end by balancing the view against its rivals. Where you judge the position as I have stated it to be mistaken or in need of qualification, revise it into the strongest nearby version and say plainly what you changed.
