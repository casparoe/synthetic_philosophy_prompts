Last week we had a visiting speaker in the department on laws of nature — broadly Humean supervenience versus the necessitation crowd — and in Q&A I asked the thing I always end up asking: if the law facts are nothing over and above the mosaic of particular matters of fact, what makes it true that the law explains its instances rather than the instances merely adding up to the law? You know, the asymmetry. He killed it with one line: "laws don't govern the events, they just describe them." Then he moved on, and the room sort of nodded.

I don't think the nodding was agreement with anything in particular, and I have to teach this to my grad seminar on Thursday, so I want to put on the board the different precise claims that sentence could be making. Here's roughly where my head is, but it's mushy and I'd like it sharpened and corrected:

1. The platitude: "obey" and "govern" are metaphors — no legislator, no sanctions, nothing enforced. True, and nobody on the other side denies it.
2. The category claim: laws *are* descriptions — representational or linguistic items, not features of the world. This one I think has real trouble with laws nobody has ever formulated and with laws whose instances never occur.
3. The supervenience claim: whatever makes a law-statement true is just how the particular facts are distributed, nothing extra in the world.
4. The epistemic claim: we read laws off the data, so a law can't turn around and explain the data — inference and explanation run in opposite directions, so there's a circle. I think this is just confused, but I want to say exactly why.
5. The deflationary modal claim: laws carry no nomic necessity and no counterfactual import at all; "law" just marks a well-confirmed regularity. I take this to be false, or at least stronger than anything a Humean needs.
6. What I've started calling the filing-cabinet reading: a law explains not by *doing* anything to its instances but by compressing a lot of cases under one compact generalization — explanation as economical description rather than as production.

Then the part I actually care about: which of these, if any, would get him his conclusion? I.e., which reading licenses "so nothing needs pushing, your asymmetry worry is a pseudo-problem"? My suspicion is that only the platitude does any dismissive work, and the platitude is perfectly compatible with everything the anti-Humean believes; whereas the reading that would really do the work is 6, and 6 is a substantive thesis about explanation that needs an argument, not a sentence.

Here's the test case I keep using and want run through the menu: suppose a world just like ours except that one single micro-event goes the other way from what our best generalizations predict. Does the law still obtain there? On a "strict summary" reading of *describe*, no — one exception falsifies it. On a "best compression" reading, yes — the generalization still wins on the simplicity/strength tradeoff. On a governing reading, the deviant event wasn't merely unlikely, it was nomically excluded. So the slogan as stated doesn't settle it, and I want to know which reading puts us in which position and whether any of them is obviously the right one.

So: handout, please. Lay the readings out as a list with verdicts — trivial, substantive and defensible, substantive and needs an argument, or just false — say for each whether it supports swatting my question, and tell me what I've missed or where I'm conflating things. I worry in particular that I'm running together "describes" in the sense of *is a statement about* with "describes" in the sense of *merely summarizes what happened*, and those seem to pull in opposite directions on case 2. Casual register is fine, it's for a seminar not a journal.
