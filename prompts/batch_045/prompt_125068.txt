I maintain a small in-house tool for ranking policy options, and I have hit a snag I think is conceptual rather than computational. For each affected person *i* I have a von Neumann–Morgenstern utility function u_i over the relevant outcomes, estimated from a stated-preference lottery exercise. I need a social ranking of the feasible options. My reasoning runs as follows, and I would like to know where, exactly, it goes wrong.

Each u_i is unique only up to positive affine transformation, and the zero point and the unit can be chosen independently for each person. So I take it to be a consequence of the representation theorem that interpersonal comparisons of utility *differences* are not merely unsupported but mathematically ill-formed: the quantity "A loses more than B gains" has no invariant content, because I can rescale either function at will. On this basis I have so far restricted the tool to criteria that use only intrapersonal information — essentially the Pareto principle — and I take the familiar claims that interpersonal comparisons are impossible, both the older worry that no one can occupy another person's standpoint and the more recent impossibility theorems in social choice, to be the same point wearing different clothes.

That restriction is useless for my actual cases, which are almost always a large loss to a few set against a small loss to many. So I have adopted a convention I call attainable-range normalization: for each person *i*, set zero at the worst outcome *i* could end up with given the current feasible set and one at the best, measure everything as a fraction of that interval, and then sum across people, since cardinal utility is precisely what permits arithmetic. Fixing two anchor points uses up exactly the freedom the theorem leaves and no more, so I take the scheme to be forced by the mathematics once one grants VNM utility at all, and I take the residual choices to be bookkeeping.

One symptom I have noticed: when I drop a dominated option from the menu, the ranking of the remaining options sometimes changes, because someone's attainable range has shrunk. I have been treating this as an artefact of how I discretized the outcome space rather than as a property of the scheme.

My questions:

1. Is attainable-range normalization in fact entailed by, or at least the uniquely natural reading of, the VNM representation result, on the grounds that it does nothing beyond fixing a free zero and a free unit? If not, what is the extra ingredient, and is it mathematical, empirical, or ethical?
2. Is there any theorem, in the spirit of Arrow's result or of Sen's work on social welfare functionals, showing that interpersonal comparison cannot be made well-defined? If so, I want its informational assumptions stated. If not, I want to know what the results in that neighbourhood actually establish.
3. Once comparability is secured, does summation follow? I have been treating "comparable" and "additive" as a single requirement.

Please answer in the following sections, at roughly the lengths indicated:

- "Where the argument breaks" (about 150 words): identify the false step or false presupposition as precisely as you can, and sort my claims into those that are trivially true, those that are false, and those that are true only on a reading I have not earned.
- "What affine uniqueness does and does not entail" (about 200 words): the difference between a framework's being silent about interpersonal comparisons and their being meaningless or impossible.
- "Attainable-range normalization: what it buys and what it costs" (about 250 words): address the menu-dependence symptom directly, and say whether it is an implementation artefact or a structural feature, and in which direction the scheme redistributes weight.
- "Is interpersonal comparison impossible? What the impossibility results say" (about 200 words): distinguish the observability-style argument from representation-relative ones, and say what the actual theorems in the vicinity do and do not rule out — including whether adding interpersonal comparability is a way of escaping those theorems or one more thing they assume away.
- "Comparability versus aggregation" (about 150 words): whether summation follows from full comparability, or whether the aggregation rule is a separate commitment.
- "A defensible procedure for the tool" (about 150 words): what you would do instead, and which of the choices would have to be made explicitly by someone, since the mathematics will not make them.

Please do not rest on the observation that interpersonal comparisons are philosophically contested; I know that, and it does not tell me which step fails. If you think one of my questions is ill-posed, say so and reformulate it before answering.
