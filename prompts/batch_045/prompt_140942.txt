I am drafting a novel set almost entirely on an open boat in the days after a sinking, and one scene has outgrown me. Two characters, both of whom I want to be genuinely competent rather than one of them a convenience, are arguing about whether to pull a swimmer aboard. The mate, who has the only sextant, refuses: the boat carries forty days of rations for eleven, the passage takes twenty-five, and every added body eats the margin. The ship's surgeon, who has spent the morning sewing up a boy the mate would have left in the water, answers that the boat is not a fortress but a field hospital, that they are doing triage rather than defending property, and that triage allocates among all the patients, not only the ones already under the canvas. My first reader tells me the mate is running the lifeboat case that circulates in this debate — the well-provisioned boat, the sea full of swimmers, the safety-factor argument that hauling people aboard risks swamping everyone — and that the surgeon is answering it with the wrong analogy entirely. Since this exchange is the hinge of the book's second act, I need the argument to actually be right, not merely to sound like an argument.

I would like that settled, but I would also like it made harder than my reader makes it. Two things in my scene are not in the case as I have seen it stated, and I suspect they matter. First, my gunwale is graded rather than thresholded: the boat will not capsize at the twelfth body, but each additional body rides it lower, so every rescue buys a slightly larger probability of total loss in a swell, spread across everyone aboard — a small shared risk traded against one person's certain death. Second, one of the swimmers is the only other person alive who can read a sextant, and a shorter passage could leave everyone better fed than a longer one with the original eleven. I do not see how a case built around a hard, known capacity and passive burdens speaks to either of these, and I cannot tell whether that is a defect in my scene or in the case.

So please audit both analogies. Set out explicitly what maps onto what in each — the boat, the provisions, the length of the passage, the gunwale, the crew's authority, the duty to the people in the water — then where each mapping breaks, and which of those breaks are load-bearing for the refusal and which are decoration. I especially want what each analogy quietly imports that the other lacks: the lifeboat seems to me to smuggle in that the occupants may owe the swimmers nothing, that the total is fixed, that the emergency is one-shot and short-horizon, and that the numbers are known; the hospital seems to me to smuggle in a shared project and an impartial allocator. If the mate's conclusion survives only because the lifeboat was carrying it, say so plainly; if it survives the disanalogies, tell me what in it is doing the surviving. And if my two twists are the wrong twists, or if there is a better analogy for what I am actually asking these people to decide, I would rather have that than have my scene propped up. Lead with a one-sentence verdict, then the two correspondence tables, then the audit, and keep the whole thing under 700 words.
