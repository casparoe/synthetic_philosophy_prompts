I sit on the allocation committee of a foundation that must commit roughly $40 million a year across a mix of global health, animal welfare, and long-term-risk programs. For years our working framework has been the familiar three-factor one: score each candidate cause for importance, tractability, and neglectedness, multiply, rank. I have stopped trusting it. It now gives me answers that feel wrong in opposite directions — it dismisses things I am confident we should fund, and recommends things I am confident we should not — and I suspect the three factors are not three independent things. So I want a principle that captures the verdicts I actually hold, deduced from the cases rather than assumed in advance. The cases below are stylized deliberately: please do not read them as descriptions of any real organization, and no citations are needed.

A. A delivery program with strong evidence, in a country whose ministry has already budgeted to take it over next year; our money would be one year of bridge funding. Verdict: no.
B. The same program, same cost per unit of benefit, in a country where no takeover is planned and no other funder is present. Verdict: yes, obviously.
C. A crowded cause — dozens of funders, billions already in the field — where the intervention is buying and distributing a physical input, so that an extra dollar really does buy an extra unit, with no talent or regulatory bottleneck. Verdict: yes, and I do not think the crowding counts against it at all.
D. A different crowded cause where the binding constraint is a few hundred skilled people. Extra money there mostly raises salaries and moves those people between organizations inside the field. Verdict: no, even though on paper it is just as neglected as C.
E. A serious risk for which no known intervention works, but where cheap short studies could substantially narrow the uncertainty. Verdict: yes — fund the studies — and I would fund them even if I expected the answer to be that nothing works.
F. The same risk, the same cheap studies, except that the only actors who could act on the finding have publicly committed themselves against ever doing so. Verdict: no.
G. An advocacy campaign that, if it succeeds, changes a procurement rule so the desired behavior becomes the default across a large market and persists after we stop paying. Verdict: strong yes.
H. An advocacy campaign of equal cost and equal annual benefit while spending continues, which wins a budget line that must be re-won every year. Verdict: weak yes, and if I have to cut one, it is this one.
I. A field in which only three organizations are capable of training new practitioners. Funding to expand those three organizations' training capacity: yes. A scholarship for one named individual to be trained at one of them: no — even though per dollar the scholarship appears to create more trained people — because the trainee would largely have been trained anyway or would take a slot someone else would have filled, whereas expanding capacity changes the steady-state flow.
J. The case I keep going back and forth on: a single large gift to endow a permanent research institute in a neglected field, versus the same sum spread over ten years of renewable program grants. The institute has the look of a one-time purchase of durable capacity; the grants are flexible and let us exit if the field turns out to be a dead end. I lean toward the grants some months and toward the institute in others, and I cannot say what would settle it.

My own tentative reformulation, which I want tested and repaired rather than confirmed: drop neglectedness as a separate factor, treat it as a proxy for the funging fraction — the share of what my dollar nominally buys that would have happened anyway — and note that crowding and fungibility plainly come apart, as C and D show. Treat tractability not as a multiplier but as the shape of the production function from dollars to outcomes, including where it saturates. Measure importance at the margin, as the value of the change in the outcome rather than the size of the outcome. And add two further terms: a lock-in share, being the portion of an intervention's total benefit that survives the end of spending, and a decision-sensitivity term for the value of information, which is zero when no downstream actor will act on what is learned, as in F. Something in this package still feels wrong to me about I, where the same nominal output gets opposite verdicts at different levels of granularity, and I suspect the fix is a requirement that the unit of intervention match the level at which the constraint binds.

What I would like back: a principle, or at most three candidate principles, stated precisely enough that a colleague could apply it without me in the room; for each clause, the case above that earns it, and an explicit statement of which verdicts the principle cannot reproduce and which of them you think I should give up; the verdicts it yields on cases I have not listed, including at least one about an intervention whose whole effect is to make an outcome arrive sooner rather than to make it happen at all, and one about whether to spread a fixed sum across five causes or concentrate it in one when every production function is concave but uncertain; and finally, which clause is doing the most work in my own list of verdicts, and where my reformulation above is confused.
