I am applying for a nine-month, single-authored dissertation fellowship and need a two-page proposal for the project I have half-formed. I will paste it into an application read by a generalist philosophy panel, so it needs to be intelligible to someone who does not work in this area, but it should not soften the problem.

Here is the background as I see it. The wrong kind of reasons problem is standardly introduced as a problem for fitting-attitude accounts of value, and it is usually staged with a threat case: a powerful figure gives you a reason to admire them, or to desire your own future suffering, by threatening you if you do not. Those reasons are reasons for the attitude all right, but they are not the kind of reason that could constitute or reveal the value of the object, and so the fitting-attitude theorist needs a restriction to reasons of the right kind. The hard part, as everyone agrees, is saying what makes a reason right-kind without simply helping oneself to the notion of value one was trying to analyse.

My half-formed idea is that the debate has been run almost entirely attitude by attitude — desire, belief, admiration, blame, hope, fear, trust, each with its own threat case and its own patch — and that this has hidden a question about attitudes that are not independent of one another. Many attitudes look partly constituted by others: hope by a desire plus some uncertainty about whether the desired thing will obtain, regret by a belief about what one has done plus a desire that it were otherwise, blame by a belief about an agent's conduct plus some affective or conative response, trust by a belief about what someone will do plus something more. Call these composite attitudes, and call the question I want to fund the inheritance question: when a reason of the wrong kind bears on a constituent attitude, what kind of reason is it, if any, for the composite? Three answers seem available — that the wrong-kindness transmits upwards, that each attitude applies its own kind-filter at its own correctness condition so that nothing transmits, and that a wrong-kind reason for a constituent can become a right-kind reason for the composite — and, as far as I can tell, nobody has tried to work out which is correct or what follows.

The reason I think the existing positions leave this open is that the main families of solution were all designed and tested against single-attitude threat cases. Accounts that sort reasons by whether they are given by the object or by the state of having the attitude, accounts that require the right-kind reason to figure in the correct explanatory way in the attitude's fittingness, and accounts that give up on a unified treatment and treat right-kindness as attitude-relative: each yields a verdict for atomic cases, and none of them has been asked to give consistent verdicts across a composite and its constituents at once. A test of that shape would discriminate between them without needing any new first-order principle, and it would also inherit an independent constraint of adequacy, since whatever it says about leakage into a belief-constituent had better not beg questions against the well-worked-out debate about pragmatic reasons for belief.

What I want from you is the proposal document: a precise statement of the question, sharpened so that it has a determinate answer within nine months; why the three families of solution do not already settle it; the method, month by month, including how I would fix the taxonomy of attitudes and calibrate the case-set before drawing any conclusion; why this compositional stress test is a better use of nine months than the obvious alternatives, which I take to be writing further variants of the threat case and doing a broader conceptual analysis of fittingness; what the possible outcomes are and which considerations would decide between them; and an honest statement of what would count as the project failing. On that last point, my own worry is that the constitutive claims about hope, blame and trust that I am leaning on are contested enough that the test may have no stable input, and that the cases may become too baroque for verdicts to be trusted.
