Hi — I work in the budget office of a mid-sized city school district, about 40 schools and roughly 22,000 kids. Every spring we hand out a discretionary fund, around $4 million, on top of the state formula money. Two years ago the school board put a line in the budget resolution saying (I'm paraphrasing, I don't have it in front of me) that any inequality in how the discretionary money gets distributed is acceptable only if it works to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged students. Someone on the board said that's basically lifted from Rawls. I took one political philosophy class years ago, so I remember the difference principle and the veil of ignorance in outline and not much else.

What I actually need is a procedure, not a lecture. Five of us sit around a table for two or three two-hour meetings in April, we have a spreadsheet, and the superintendent signs off on whatever we hand her. Nobody on the committee has philosophy training, and two of them are principals who will absolutely advocate for their own buildings. So I want:

1. A short, ordered set of questions or tests to run on each proposal, in the order they should be asked, with what counts as a real answer to each one and what counts as hand-waving. One guy on the committee says "but it helps everybody" about literally everything, and I need the procedure to have an answer to that specific move built into it.
2. A way to settle, up front and using only the columns we actually have — test scores, free-and-reduced-lunch counts, English learner counts, a facilities condition score, teacher turnover, attendance — who the "least advantaged" group is for the purposes of the decision. I think there's a circularity here: you need the distribution to know who's at the bottom of it, and you need to know who's at the bottom to judge the distribution. Tell me how to break into that circle in a way five people can agree on in twenty minutes. Related: I vaguely remember something about representative people in positions rather than actual individual kids, and I can't tell whether that means we should be ranking schools, or ranking kids, or ranking some third thing that isn't either.
3. What the veil-of-ignorance part is supposed to do in a room where everyone in it really does know which schools and which kids they're attached to. My suspicion is that we're meant to use it once, at the level of the rule we're following, rather than re-running it line item by line item, but I'm not sure, and I also don't know how much of our ordinary knowledge we're supposed to pretend not to have — obviously we shouldn't pretend not to know how poverty affects kids, but there's some line and I can't find it.
4. Where the procedure just stops. The points where we should quit calculating and hand it to the superintendent or the board as a values call because the framework genuinely doesn't settle it.

Constraints that I think might break the whole thing: the state formula money has to be equalized per pupil, so this discretionary fund is our only real lever; the fund can't pay recurring salaries, only things that fit inside one budget year, which seems like it should matter a lot for a principle that's supposedly about people's prospects over a whole life rather than about one April; and we can't construct anything like a proper basket of primary goods, we have the spreadsheet and thats it. Also anything we produce needs to fit on one page that can go on the wall of the conference room.

Then please run it on three proposals that are floating around right now:

- A literacy coach in each of the six highest-poverty elementary schools, paid for this year by a one-year foundation grant with no plan for year two.
- A corporate donation that pays for new science labs at the two most affluent high schools, conditional on the same donor also funding a free summer STEM program for low-income middle schoolers for five years. We don't get either half unless we take both halves.
- Closing a tiny K–8 school in the poorest neighborhood and busing those kids to a bigger, better-resourced school twenty minutes away. On every measure in the spreadsheet the kids who move come out ahead, but the neighborhood is going to be furious and I think something real is being lost that none of our columns see.

I'd like at least one of those three to come out the way I don't want it to, and I want you to say plainly which one, and why I should accept the answer anyway. And tell me what the procedure is not able to do — the mistakes it will make every single year that I should just expect and stop fighting.

Plain American English, please, nothing fancy, and please don't cite anything or give me a reading list — there's no library here and I'm not going to chase down references.
