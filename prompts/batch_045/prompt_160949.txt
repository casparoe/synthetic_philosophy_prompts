I'm in a reading group on the foundations of probability and we've spent two sessions on how Everettian quantum mechanics — the no-collapse, "many worlds" version — is supposed to recover the probabilities we actually observe, and I remain unconvinced by the way one member of the group handles it. As I understand the difficulty: if the dynamics never selects an outcome, then after an unequal measurement every result is realised somewhere, so it is unclear why a rational agent should assign the squared-amplitude probabilities rather than treat the outcomes as equally likely, or balk at assigning probabilities at all. Her line is that this is a pseudo-problem, and her central analogy is a duplication case: you go to sleep tonight in your room; a device scans you and produces two successors, each of whom wakes in a qualitatively identical room remembering just what you remember; one is then told "heads", the other "tails". She says that before sleeping you should straightforwardly assign one half to "I will be told heads", and that your certainty that both successors will exist does nothing to undermine that — the probability here is just ordinary, first-personal uncertainty about which of several future people you are going to be. Transfer that to measurement: branching means every outcome occurs, but you can still be uncertain beforehand about which branch-successor you will turn out to be, and that is all the room an Everettian needs. When I pressed her on why the credences should be the squared amplitudes rather than uniform over branches, she added a second picture: think of branching as a river dividing into channels of unequal width. The water does not care how many channels there are; nearly all of it goes down the wide one. Likewise, on her view, more of you goes down the high-amplitude branch, and rational credence should track weight rather than number. She concluded that no separate derivation of the amplitude-squared rule is needed, that nothing distinctively quantum is going on in Everettian probability, and that anyone who disagrees is conflating a third-personal claim — every outcome is realised somewhere — with a first-personal one, which outcome this successor sees.

I want an audit of that analogy rather than a verdict on many-worlds as a whole. Start with the correspondence table: what plays the role of the sleeper, of the successors, of the coin, of the number of copies, of the channel widths, and of the moment of waking up? Then give me the disanalogies, separated into the ones that matter for the use she is making of the analogy and the ones that are merely cosmetic — and, if you think some of my worries below are misdescribed, say so and restate them correctly before answering. The ones I keep coming back to are these. First: in the duplication case the number of successors is a determinate integer and the equal-credence answer falls out of symmetry among them, whereas in branching there seems to be no canonical, non-arbitrary decomposition of the universal state into branches — branch count looks basis-relative, vague, and in the continuous case not even well defined — so the analogy may be borrowing from the source case exactly the structure (a definite set of candidate successors to spread credence over) that the target does not obviously have. Second: the duplication case on its own delivers equal credences, so the unequal ones have to come from the river picture; does that picture actually justify weighting credence by width, or does it only restate the conclusion in hydraulic language? What would make width normative for self-location — why should more of you going one way bear on what you should expect, given that every successor is equally one of you? Third: the analogy seems to presuppose a persisting self and a fact about which successor is me, which the bare unitary dynamics may not supply; is that a further commitment smuggled in by the story, and if so how much of her conclusion rests on it? Fourth: if all successors exist and matter equally, does it make any practical difference which one you turn out to be, and is the answer to that question doing quiet work in the argument?

Close by telling me whether her conclusion survives once those disanalogies are on the table. Specifically: does the duplication-plus-river package genuinely let her off the hook of deriving the amplitude-squared rule, or is the analogy carrying the load — supplying the weights by stipulation while appearing to derive them? If it fails, say what the remaining options actually are (arguments from the rationality of betting on branches, constraints on how credences must decompose when a system is one of several independent preparations, long-run-frequency arguments, or simply adding the rule as a postulate), what each of them would have to establish, and where each is thought to be weakest. Rank the disanalogies from fatal to cosmetic, and if there is a better analogy for Everettian probability than personal fission — or if the honest answer is that no analogy can substitute for a derivation — say so. For what it's worth, the thing that would move me most is an account of what fixes the weights without assuming them; I would not be much troubled if the analogy left questions about personal identity through branching murky, since I don't expect the physics to hand us a tidy theory of the self.
