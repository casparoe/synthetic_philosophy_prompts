hey — I need a hand with something and it's kind of a big ask. Please put your moral philosopher hat on first and your "person who can actually write a sentence" hat on second, and think carefully before drafting: I'd like you to brainstorm for a few paragraphs first about the concept of supererogation — what's supposed to be in the category, who denies that there is such a category and why, the demandingness objection, why praising someone as saintly can land as an insult or as a burden, and what it even means to praise someone for something they themselves took to be required. Then write the speech.

Here's the situation. My friend Mira died on Tuesday and I'm giving the eulogy on Friday, in a small chapel, maybe eighty people. She was a palliative-care nurse for twenty years; in 2019 she donated a kidney to a stranger; for about a decade she gave away roughly half her income; she spent the last eight months of her sabbatical caring for a friend who was dying. The room is going to be mixed in a way that makes this hard: a solid third of it is her effective-altruist and philosophy friends, who will hear every implication of what I say, and another third is family — her mother, her two brothers, her fifteen-year-old daughter Nora — plus neighbours and people from the ward who just loved her.

The problem is that the two things I need to do pull in opposite directions. On the one hand, a bunch of people in that room are going to spend Friday afternoon quietly concluding that they are bad people, because they still have both kidneys and they gave away nothing, and I need the speech to let them off that hook without lying. On the other hand, Mira herself flatly rejected the idea that there's such a thing as going beyond duty; she once told me she thought the whole category was invented to let comfortable people off the hook, and she got quietly furious when anyone called her generous. Her daughter Nora asked me for two things: don't make her mum sound like she was showing off, and don't make everyone else in the room sound like they failed. There are also two of Mira's friends there who are visibly burning themselves out in the same way she did, and I really don't want the speech to function as either a dare or a permission slip for them.

So I want the concept of supererogation to do real structural work in the speech — deciding what can and can't honestly be said, and how the praise can be framed — not to get name-dropped somewhere in the middle and then waved at.

Practical constraints: about a thousand words, seven minutes out loud. Plain enough for her mother and for Nora, precise enough that the philosophy contingent doesn't wince. No "saint", no "hero", no "selfless", no "inspiration" — she'd have hated all four and I don't want to put them in her mouth. She was an atheist, so nothing about watching over us or being at peace now, and nothing hedged and spiritual-ish either. It should comfort people who aren't philosophers and it should not say anything Mira herself would have thought false.

Two last things that might help or might just be noise: she used to say the only thing she was definately sure of was that nobody gets extra credit for doing what was obviously owed; and her brother Dave is speaking after me and will make a joke about her terrible handwriting, so I don't need to be funny.
