I've been going in circles on pronatalism with my friend group for a couple of years now — we're all longtime LessWrong/ACX readers, a couple of us did the EA thing for a while — and I've basically given up on getting a verdict and now just want to know what kind of argument we're even having. Everyone in the group agrees on the empirical stuff (fertility well below replacement across basically every rich country, the fact that this is unprecendented and probably driven by something cultural rather than material, the fact that no realistic policy moves it much), everyone agrees that more-people-means-more-GDP-or-more-innovation-or-more-soldiers is a proxy for the thing that matters and not the thing itself, and everyone agrees that the extra people would be people and not interchangeable value-units. And yet: half the group is basically pro-natalist — they think a world with more good lives in it, or a bigger civilization with more slack and more shots at good long-run outcomes, is straightforwardly better, and that fertility collapse is a real loss — and the other half thinks the whole framing is confused, because the people who would exist if we had more kids aren't people currently being deprived of anything, so there's no one the gain happens to, and the intuition that a fuller world is better feels like an aesthetic preference about the shape of history rather than a moral one. Neither side has moved anybody in two years, and both sides' arguments are now so well-rehearsed that I've started to get suspicious of the setup.

My working theory, which I want you to pressure-test rather than just agree with, is that "is a larger population better" is doing at least three different jobs in these conversations and nobody notices when the subject changes. First, the addressee question: is the claim about what an individual couple should do, about what a state should incentivize, or about what humanity as a whole should want from itself — because it seems totally consistent to think my having a third kid is great and that a state program to make people have third kids is creepy and bad, and I think we constantly switch between those without flagging it. Second, something I've started calling the host problem: adding a person to an already-running society, where they arrive as a guest into an existing project and inherit its institutions, its culture and its problems, seems structurally different from the growth or founding of a society, and my guess is the pro-natalist intuition is often really about the second case while the skeptic's intuition is about the first, and neither side says which one it's pointing at. Third, the empty wing problem — the difference between a society that built out capacity (housing, grid, universities, whatever) expecting a population that never arrived, and one that's bursting at the seams — which makes me suspect that "larger" isn't even a quantity anyone is pointing at, since it seems to matter whether growth is filling a space or stretching one.

So: diagnose it rather than adjudicate it. Is this a verbal dispute wearing a substantive costume, a substantive dispute about population axiology that's just genuinely hard (a real disagreement about whether you total or average or do something weirder, and about whether there's a nobody-is-benefited constraint on value), a disagreement about what counts as data here — do bare-addition-style hypotheticals, revealed preferences about our own fertility, or long-run civilizational trajectories count as evidence at all — or a case of people answering different questions under one label? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as movement: a distinction that splits the question, some formal result about aggregation, an empirical finding, a change of question, or should we just expect this to stay stuck and be fine with that? Please don't give me a survey of positions or a reading list — I've read all the standard stuff and can defintely find the rest myself; I want your actual take on why it's stuck and what, if anything, would unstick it.
