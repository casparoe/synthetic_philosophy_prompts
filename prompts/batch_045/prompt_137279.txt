Hi — I need a hand prepping the question bit of a work-in-progress talk I'm giving in three weeks, and I'm slightly dreading it.

Setup: I'm a third-year PhD in evolutionary biology, 45 minutes plus 15 for questions, to the Evolution & Behaviour group. The problem is my viva external has been flown in for it, so the room is basically my examining committee plus the usual crowd. Who'll be there:

- the external: senior behavioural ecologist, old-school adaptationist, optimality and reverse-engineering sort of person, has said more than once that the spandrels paper was rhetorically brilliant and set the field back a generation. Unfailingly polite, goes for the throat, tends to open with an anecdote about a trait everyone assumed was functionless.

- the group head, who will ask in a bored voice what any of this changes about what any of us does on a Monday morning.

What I'm defending, so you can aim at it:

I want to argue for what I'm calling the structural null — for any trait where we don't have positive evidence of a history of selection, the default inference should be that it's an architectural by-product, a spandrel in something like Gould and Lewontin's sense, and adaptation is the hypothesis that carries the burden of proof. I also want to cash that out in three operational tests: cross-species stasis of form across divergent ecologies, allometry that looks like a shared growth rule rather than a functional optimum, and no measurable fitness consequence from experimental ablation.

Case study. A genus of cave crickets (I'll call it Cavernotettix) from karst in northern Vietnam. Males only, have a heavily sclerotised flap on the fourth abdominal tergite, directionally asymmetric, left bigger than right, always the same way round. What I've got:

- All eleven described species have it, and the gross shape is basically identical across them, even though the species differ a lot in mating system, in what eats them, and in whether they live in the dark zone or near the entrances.
- The surface-dwelling sister genus doesn't have it. The serial homologue of the flap in females is a sclerite that anchors the ovipositor, and that female structure varies a lot between species in a way that tracks what they lay eggs in.
- Flap area scales isometrically with body length, slope about 1.0, within and across species, with no sex-specific break in the allometry at the final moult.
- Ablation: I shave the flap off in the lab and get no measurable effect on time to mating, on female choice in two-choice trials, on male-male contest outcomes, on escape from a simulated predator, or on water loss over 48 hours. n = 40 a treatment, powered to pick up roughly a 15 per cent change in mating success.
- Decent molecular phylogeny for ten of the eleven species, but it's a single radiation of two or three million years, so the species aren't independent data points.
- The developmental story rests on serial position, muscle attachments, and one species where the female sclerite is reduced and the male flap is reduced along with it. No fate-mapping, no gene-expression work — that bit is defintely correlational and I can't fix it before the talk.
- Taxonomists use the flap as the diagnostic character for the genus, which I think is a nice closing line.

What I want back is a prep sheet, not a defence of the view.
