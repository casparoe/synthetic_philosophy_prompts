I am putting together a dissertation prospectus in epistemology, and one chapter has to double as a stand-alone two-page proposal for a nine-month dissertation fellowship. I need that chapter proposal written, and I have only a half-formed idea for it.

The half-formed idea is this. The debate between conciliationist and steadfast responses to higher-order evidence seems to me to share an assumption: that when you get evidence that the reasoning behind your first-order credence is unreliable, there is exactly one thing that could be revised, namely the first-order credence itself, and the whole dispute is about how much and on what basis to revise it. That assumption is what generates the ugly options — either you revise and seem to throw away first-order evidence you still possess, or you hold steady and end up in an akratic profile in which you believe something while taking your own belief to be the product of an unreliable process.

I want to explore a structurally different response: the agent keeps two registers. The first-order credence continues to register her read of her first-order evidence, unrevised; the higher-order information is carried instead by a separate calibration index attached to the process that produced it, and that index governs action, assertion, inquiry, and further evidence-gathering rather than the credence itself. My suspicion is that this earns its keep only for one species of higher-order evidence, which I want to call provenance evidence as opposed to reliability-undermining evidence: evidence not that a process is unreliable but that one's total evidence was selected in a way that is correlated with the truth of the proposition at issue. My working example is a grader who forms confident verdicts on forty essays and then learns that the batch was drawn from a pool selected because it contained an unusual concentration of borderline papers. Here there seems to be no determinate amount by which to lower any individual verdict, since doing that properly would require a base rate she does not have.

The obvious danger, and the thing the proposal has to confront head-on, is that the two-register model is not a genuine third position at all. It may collapse into conciliationism if the index does all the work, or into a decision-theoretic version of steadfastness if the credence does, or into permissivism if the split merely relabels a permission to respond either way. So I need the proposal to do the following: state the question so that it has a determinate answer; say precisely why the existing conciliationist and steadfast positions leave that question open; specify a collapse test, stated in advance, that the two-register model fails if it is merely a redescription, together with the kind of case that would discriminate between the registers doing real work and the registers being notational; give a month-by-month plan for nine months of work; lay out the possible conclusions and which considerations would decide between them; say what would count as the chapter failing; and be honest about whether this is one chapter or a whole thesis. Please do not include citations or a bibliography — I will add those myself.
