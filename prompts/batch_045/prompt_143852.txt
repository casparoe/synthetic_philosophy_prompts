i'm reading the 1844 manuscripts with two friends (very casual reading group, no one is an academic) and I keep getting stuck on this bit:

"This fact expresses merely that the object which labor produces – labor’s product – confronts it as something alien, as a power independent of the producer. The product of labor is labor which has been embodied in an object, which has become material: it is the objectification of labor. Labor's realization is its objectification. Under these economic conditions this realization of labor appears as loss of realization for the workers; objectification as loss of the object and bondage to it; appropriation as estrangement, as alienation."

What I can't work out is how load-bearing the *object* is here. I've started calling a certain kind of work "trace labour" (my own term, dont google it): work that leaves no seperate object behind at all, only a change in a person or in a system. A night-shift carer calming down someone with dementia, a teacher getting a class through a lesson, someone moderating content all day. Nothing is produced that the worker could in principle hold, recognise as theirs, or that could be sold on its own.

So my question: Marx's chain here seems to go realization -> objectification -> the object stands over against you -> alienation. If there is no object, does the chain just not get started? Intuitively these workers look like textbook cases of being exploited (low pay, no control over what they do, someone else pockets the difference), and they also seem pretty miserable in the specific "this isn't mine, I'm just a function" way Marx describes. But if I follow the passage literally, the mechanism he gives shouldn't fire for them.

A couple of things I'd love help with:

- Am I right that the object is doing real work in that passage, or is it just his most vivid illustration of something that's really about the *act* of producing? If it's the latter, where in the passage do you see that?
- If alienation and exploitation can come apart for trace labour — exploited but not alienated, or alienated in a way his story can't explain — how big a problem is that for his overall picture? Does the "loss of the object" reading survive if the thing being lost was never an object to begin with, and the thing standing over the worker is more like a process or a schedule?

I'm less interested in what later Marxists said about this than in what you can get out of the passage itself, so please reason from the text rather than from any school of thought.
