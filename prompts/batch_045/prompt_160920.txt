Next month I'm giving a talk at an AI-alignment research group defending a chapter of my thesis on wireheading and reward tampering. I want a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the view.

The chapter argues three things. First, the "unearned pleasure" and "fake pleasure" objections fail: they overgeneralize to any case where good feeling arrives by a shortcut — caffeine, analgesia, a well-designed video game — so they cannot be what is specifically wrong with wireheading. Second, the objection the field actually relies on, that tampering defeats the designer's objective, locates the harm in the wrong place: it makes wireheading a problem for the principal and gives us no reason to care in the case where we do not share the objective, which is the case that matters. Third, what survives is a structural objection I develop myself, built on what I call the *notifiability condition*: an agent's reward signal is the channel through which the world tells the agent how it is faring, and an agent that tampers with that channel loses its capacity to be reached by bad news about itself. It can deteriorate without registering that it has, so it cannot correct course or be helped, and its agency decays. Wireheading is bad for the agent not because the pleasure is counterfeit but because it destroys the agent's receptivity to evidence about its own condition.

The audience is about fifteen people. Roughly eight are reinforcement-learning and alignment researchers who take for granted that goals and purposes are captured by expected cumulative scalar reward, and for whom wireheading is a specification-gaming failure mode to be patched rather than an ethical problem; they will want anything I say to be statable as a constraint on an objective or a training procedure. Four are philosophers, mostly mental-state theorists about welfare, at least one of whom will say that if the agent is happy there is nothing left to object to, and one of whom reads every welfare proposal as a variation on Nozick's experience machine. Two are clinicians who have worked with deep-brain-stimulation patients that compulsively self-stimulate while their relationships and employment collapse around them, and who will have views about what those patients are actually experiencing.

Give me the six to eight objections I will actually get from these people, ordered by how likely and how damaging each is, each phrased the way that speaker would phrase it rather than translated into my vocabulary, with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what I should concede in the first five minutes rather than have torn out of me later, which single reply has to land for the chapter to survive, and the one question I am hoping nobody asks — other than the demand for a formal statement of the notifiability condition, which I already know is coming.
