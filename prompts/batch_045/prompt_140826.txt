i build cost-effectiveness spreadsheets for a small farmed-animal advocacy org, and a few weeks ago I talked myself into an argument that I'm now fairly sure is broken, except I can't find the break. It's not even a work thing anymore, it just bugs me. Here it is as cleanly as I can put it.

(1) If I'm not sure whether a creature is sentient, the weight I should give its welfare is my credence that it's sentient times the weight I'd give it if I were certain.

(2) Expected moral value adds across individuals, so the expected disvalue of a state of affairs is the sum over every creature of credence times welfare.

(3) The number of insects alive at any moment is something like 10^18, which is around eight orders of magnitude more than the number of vertebrates we farm and could plausibly help. I know abundance estimates are soft, but I'd have to be wrong by five or six orders of magnitude for that gap to close, and I don't think I am.

(4) So even at a credence of 1% that your average beetle has any morally relevant experience at all, the expected disvalue in the insect column is roughly a million times the expected disvalue in the farmed-vertebrate column, which is the column my entire organisation exists to shrink.

(5) If a dollar does more expected good in column A than column B and I can give it to either, I give it to A. That's just expected value applied to giving.

(6) But nothing in (1) through (5) is specific to insects. Nematodes are maybe 10^21, bacteria maybe 10^30, I'm working from memory on those, and the multiplication goes through at any credence you like, however grudging, as long as it isn't literally zero.

(7) I am confident that we should not be spending the budget on bacteria.

(8) So at least one of (1), (2) and (5) is false, and I don't know which, or what I'd put in its place.

One colleague says the mistake is that the credences aren't independent: either insects are sentient or they aren't, it's one big coin flip and not 10^18 little ones, so multiplying per creature is illegitimate. I have a feeling that's wrong, because expectation adds up the same way whether the underlying events are correlated or not, but if it is wrong then the problem is somewhere else and I still can't see where.

The other thing I keep circling is a column in my own spreadsheet that I think might be doing more work than it deserves. I fill in welfare per animal-year as if the only open question is whether the creature is in or out, and then I use roughly the same per-animal number I'd use for a chicken. But if what I'm uncertain about is not just whether insects matter but how much, the thing I should be multiplying by is the expected welfare conditional on sentience, not the number for a creature I'm sure about, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's a real distinction or whether it quietly collapses back into the same figure. Relatedly, I don't know what the unit is supposed to be: if I count by individuals the insects win by a mile, and if I count by something like total neural machinery per second the cow probably outweighs a thousand of them, and nothing in my argument tells me which currency is the right one.

I'd rather keep (3), since numbers are just numbers, and I'd rather keep (1) or something very close to it, because if I drop it I have no idea how to think about moral uncertainty at all and every model I've ever built stops meaning anything. So if something has to go I'd like it to be something I can survive losing.

If the honest answer is that the trouble is in the move from a gigantic total to a funding decision rather than in the probability machinery, say that, but then tell me what happens to (1) and (2), because I still have to fill in the column.
