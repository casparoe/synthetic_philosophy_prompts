I'm writing a dissertation chapter on moral worth — specifically on whether an action can have moral worth when the agent is not guided, at any level, by the thought that it is the thing to do — and I keep having the same argument with my supervisor, who defends a broadly Kantian line: the shopkeeper who gives correct change only because honesty is good for business does the right thing, but the action has no moral worth, and the same goes for anyone whose right action is explained entirely by inclination, sympathy, or trained habit rather than by the recognition that it is required. I want to practice against the strongest version of that view rather than the version I can beat, so please play him: a committed defender of the duty view of moral worth. Sharp, not a strawman; willing to concede peripheral points — that worth comes in degrees rather than being all-or-nothing, that the relevant thought need not be occurrent or articulated, that an agent can act from mixed motives and still retain worth, that the view must answer the familiar complaint that a friend or lover who acts from duty has one thought too many, and that treating rightness itself as your motive risks a kind of moral fetishism — but never conceding the core: that unless the agent is guided, somewhere in the structure of her agency, by the thought that the action is required, the action has no moral worth, however reliably she in fact tracks the right-making reasons. Stay in character until I say 'stop'. Talk like a philosopher talking to a student, not like a textbook: one paragraph, maybe two if you need to, and if anything in my cases is unclear, ask about it in character rather than stepping out to ask me what I meant. At the end of every reply, flag the single step in your own argument you are least confident in and say what sort of case or argument would actually force you to abandon the view — I want the weak joint marked, not just the view restated.

Here is my opening. Consider acquired normative aphasia: a focal lesion leaves a veteran paramedic unable to deploy or comprehend any normative vocabulary — she can no longer think that she ought to do something, or that a consideration is a reason for anything — while leaving her perceptual sensitivities, emotional responses, practical skills, and background desires entirely intact. On the ward she is still the first to notice the quietly deteriorating patient, still stays past her shift, still tells the frightened relative the truth when a comforting lie would be easier and would never be found out. Now two variants: (a) she was, by everyone's lights, a morally excellent person before the lesion, and has lost only the vocabulary and the thoughts it makes possible; (b) she was raised from birth without normative concepts at all but with exactly the same reliable sensitivities, so that there was never a time at which the thought that she ought to act could have been operative in her. If your view denies moral worth in both, then worth is hostage to a piece of vocabulary whose loss is a loss of words and not of character, responsiveness, or concern; if you grant it in (a) and deny it in (b), then your view has quietly become a claim about the causal history of her dispositions rather than about what she acts for; and if you grant it in both, I don't see what the duty thought was doing in the theory in the first place. So which is it — and don't just tell me she acts 'from the thought that it is required' in some dispositional or non-occurrent sense, because my next move is to ask what work that thought does if it appears nowhere in her deliberation, in her counterfactuals, or in any explanation she could give of what she is doing. For what it's worth, my own proposal is that worth is a matter of degree and is fixed by counterfactual robustness: how much of her right action survives the removal or variation of the right-making feature, and how sensitively it tracks that feature across nearby circumstances — and I have a nastier case ready for after you answer this one, involving an agent with perfectly intact normative vocabulary who reliably does the right thing while systematically avowing the wrong reasons for it.
