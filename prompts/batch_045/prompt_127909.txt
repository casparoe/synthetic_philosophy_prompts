You're a professional philosopher who's spent a lot of time on animal ethics and the metaphysics of moral status — brainstorm a bit before you start drafting, and aim at the view's weakest joints rather than at easy point-scoring.

I host a small philosophy podcast and next week's guest is a philosopher who's built a bit of an online reputation as "the neuron counter". Her view, as I understand it: a creature's moral status is (roughly) a smoothly increasing function of how many neurons it has in its cortex or pallium — status comes in degrees, a typical human outranks a typical mouse, and she bites the bullet that a chimp or an elephant outranks a human with advanced dementia. Whenever a hard case comes up she says something like "that's just the argument from marginal cases, and every non-speciesist view has to swallow it", and then moves on.

Draft me eight to ten questions that make that move progressively harder to lean on, each with a follow-up keyed to what I think she'd actually say. I want at least one question on: why neuron count and not whatever it's supposed to be a proxy for (valenced experience? psychological richness?) — she'll probably say the proxy is the only thing we can measure, so push back on that; on the marginal-cases dodge itself, including the version where the human isn't impaired at all but is just small-brained; on a hypothetical creature with a tenth of the neurons but each of them faster, more richly connected and better myelinated, so total processing per second is the same or higher; on whether the currency is neurons-at-a-time or neurons integrated over a whole life — call them neuron-years — since that's what decides whether a long-lived insect beats a toddler who dies at two; on whether the scale is continuous all the way down or flattens out somewhere, and whether a nematode ends up with a whisper of status or none at all; and on a non-neural system that matches a crow's behavioural repertoire. Then finish with two more: one about what empirical finding would definately count against her view, and one that pins down the practical payoff — what exactly her view says about which animals we should be funding research into right now.

Keep the questions crisp enough to read out loud, and write the whole thing in British English.
