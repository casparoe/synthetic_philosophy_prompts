ok so my bio teacher says 'DNA is a code' like every day, and sometimes 'the instruction manual for building you'. Last week I asked if that means that if you had the full sequence of some organism you could basically read off what it was going to be like, and she said yeah, that's the whole idea, the enviroment only decides details like scars and accents. I didn't buy it, for two reasons I half remember from arguing with my friend:

1) the stuff that 'reads' the sequence (ribosomes, tRNA, whatever) is itself made of proteins that the sequence codes for, so the reader is part of the thing being read, wich never happens with a book or a computer program
2) a code is supposed to be arbitrary — the word 'dog' doesn't smell like a dog — but the codon-to-amino-acid pairing is done by actual chemical matching, so maybe it's more like a lock and key than like a word. I might be getting the chemistry wrong, but: IF the pairing turned out to be pretty much forced by chemistry, would that stop it being a code?

So can you give me the menu of things 'DNA is a code' could actually be claiming, from the boring one (there's just a regular mapping, 64 codons onto 20ish amino acids) all the way up to the wild one (the molecule literally has meaning, or contains a description of the animal, the way a manual describes the thing you're assembling)? For each one say if it's trivial, if it's a real claim that could turn out false, and if it's just circular or empty. And the part I actually care about: which of those readings would let her get to her conclusion, that the sequence settles what the organism is like and everything else is detail, and which ones don't get her there at all? Also, if there's a better word than code — people are always saying recipe or blueprint instead — say what each of those quietly smuggles in.
