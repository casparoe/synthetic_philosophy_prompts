Hi — I've got to give a short talk in my epistemology seminar next week on the norms of assertion, and before I stand up in front of everyone I wanted to check that my own position actually holds together, because I've got a feeling it might not. Roughly where I've landed:

1. The norm of assertion is knowledge: you're permitted to assert p only if you know p, and violating it is a defect of the assertion itself — not just of your manners, or of how helpful you're being.
2. Knowledge isn't stakes-sensitive. Whether someone knows something doesn't shift about with how much is riding on it, for them or for anybody else.
3. How strong your evidence has to be before you may flat-out assert definitely does depend on what's at stake. Same 95%-reliable test, same everything else: telling a colleague which batch the berries came from is fine, telling a patient her scan's clear is not, and afterwards "you shouldn't have said that" is a fair criticism. Relatedly, you're allowed to say things you'd have no business acting on.
4. The Moore's paradox stuff — "it's raining but I don't believe it" being absurd — shows that assertion constitutively expresses belief, and that this is really the more basic norm, with the knowledge norm just a beefed-up version of it.
5. Assertions you don't believe can be in perfectly good order. A supply teacher who privately believes none of the evolution material but knows it and flat-out tells the class that humans evolved from earlier primates seems to me to be asserting exactly as she should; and a bald-faced lie in a show trial is still an assertion, and a defective one, even though nobody's meant to believe it.
6. I think the reason the knowledge norm has the grip it does is that the point of asserting is to give your hearer a reason to believe what you say.
