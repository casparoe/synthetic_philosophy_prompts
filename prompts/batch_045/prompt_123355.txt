I work as a sensory scientist for a food manufacturer during the day and write tasting notes for a wine publication in the evenings, so my working vocabulary is this one: trained descriptive panels, lexicons of descriptors fixed to physical reference standards, anchoring sessions and drift correction, blind and randomized serving, replicate scoring, panelist reproducibility and inter-rater agreement, the consensus score as the panel's output, and above all the idea that the panel is a measurement instrument whose readings mean anything only relative to the conditions under which it was calibrated. I have exactly one survey course in philosophy behind me.

I have been reading about a puzzle in aesthetics that I suspect my vocabulary handles badly, and I would like to see how badly. The puzzle starts from the observation that testimony transmits knowledge all over the place: I know when the train leaves because you told me, and nothing about my ignorance of the timetable itself seems to defect my knowledge. But there is a strong pull toward saying that this fails for aesthetic judgments — that I cannot come to know that a piece of music is beautiful, or that a wine is balanced and complex, merely because a reliable person told me so. Call this the acquaintance requirement: first-hand experience of the object is required for the knowledge. There is a second, apparently separable thought I want you to keep distinct from it: that even where deference would be perfectly reliable, there is something wrong with deferring at all, because making up your own mind about such matters is part of what the judgment is for. Call that the independence requirement. My sense is that people run these together and that they may not stand or fall together.

Please do three things.

First, restate both requirements and the strongest available case for each, one at a time, in my vocabulary: panels, instruments, calibration, lexicons, reference standards, drift, blind service, consensus, reproducibility. I want the translation to be honest rather than convenient. Where the only way to express a philosophical claim is to import an assumption my practice does not make — for instance, that the point of an assessment is the accuracy of its verdict rather than the quality of the experience it consists in — say explicitly that you are doing that instead of smuggling it in as translation.

Second, give me the residue report in three columns: what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion, and what has no expression in my vocabulary at all. I am particularly interested in whether the acquaintance requirement and the independence requirement survive as two theses here. My panel work has no patience whatsoever with independence: a trained panelist whose scores diverge from the panel is treated as instrument error, to be retrained against the standards or dropped, not as someone exercising a virtue. If the distinction collapses in my vocabulary, I want your view on why. Is it because my vocabulary lacks the category of appreciation — it can talk all day about whether a panel reliably detects greenness, and not at all about whether anyone enjoyed the wine — or is it because the two requirements were never really independent, and the appearance of two theses is an artifact of how the philosophers set the cases up?

Third, run the translation past three cases I care about, and tell me which philosophical distinctions do real work on them and which go idle.

The tuned critic. Suppose someone whose judgments have matched yours on every one of several hundred works you have both assessed, a track record you have actually measured rather than assumed. Her verdict on a work you cannot access comes to you with that record attached. Does the acquaintance requirement still bite? Does the independence requirement? In my vocabulary this is a second instrument, well correlated with mine but run on a sample I cannot taste; I am unsure which of my reactions to that is epistemic and which is professional jealousness about instruments.

Your own earlier self. You lost your sense of smell to a viral illness last year. You find a note you wrote fifteen years ago on a wine now open in front of you: specific descriptors, a score, and reasons. Your palate drifted measurably over those years, in known directions. Taking your past note as a guide is either a case of testimony, or a case of memory, or a case of consulting a differently calibrated instrument that happens to be you. Which one does the philosophical apparatus say it is, and does the answer change what you know?

Testimony that retunes perception. This is the one I care about most. In my world, being told to look for a methoxypyrazine character reliably makes people find it, and being told the tannin is slightly stemmy makes people locate an astringency they would otherwise have scored as structure. Testimony here does not merely add a belief; it reweights attention and changes what the instrument reads. If what testimony does is restructure the experience rather than substitute for it, I cannot tell whether that supports the acquaintance requirement's opponents, because the hearer now really does perceive what they were told, or supports its friends, because the whole point of the requirement may turn out to be about the activity of sensing rather than about where accurate belief comes from. Please say which, and why.

One last thing. If the acquaintance requirement is true, would anything about how I run panels or write notes have to change? If the answer is that nothing would, tell me whether that is a reductio of the requirement or evidence that my practice has already taken a side it never argued for. And please argue for your claims rather than attributing them; I cannot check references, and a name would tell me nothing.
