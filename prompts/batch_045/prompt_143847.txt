Bit of an odd ask, but here goes. I'm a philosophy professor — mostly normative theory, some decision theory — and I've had these notes for a short paper sittin around for about two years. They came out of a graduate seminar that got completely derailed from the syllabus and spent three weeks on whether the simulation argument should change what we think about the far future. I keep not finishing it because I genuinely can't tell whether the central move is a real argument or a cute observation with a lot of scaffolding built around it. So I'd like you to referee it hard before I decide whether to bin it. Also: please don't tell me what to read or who to cite — I'm somewhere without library access for a few days and I'd much rather have your own verdict than a guessed-at bibliography.

Here are the notes, more or less as they stand (compressed, sorry, I write them for myself):

Setup. Longtermist fanaticism: since the far future plausibly contains astronomically many beings, even a minuscule chance of nudging its trajectory swamps anything we could do in the near term. Call the two ingredients SIZE (how many beings) and SPAN (how long the future runs). Almost all critical attention has gone to SIZE — population ethics, whether value aggregates additively, what happens if digital minds replace biological ones. I want to go after SPAN.

The move. Don't argue that we're probably in a simulation. Assume only this: conditional on being in a simulation, the remaining runtime of the run is fixed by the simulators' purposes, not by the internal physics of the world. Then:

(1) Truncation isn't a constant. The usual move is to treat the simulation hypothesis as a discount factor on longtermist value — multiply by one minus something, note that the numbers are so huge it doesn't matter. But if the stopping rule is interest-sensitive rather than budget-fixed, duration isn't independent of what happens inside the run. Remaining runtime is a function of whether the world stays worth watching.

(2) The boring-success problem. The futures longtermism aims at — stable, well-governed, low-drama, no civilizational collapse — are the least interesting ones viewed from outside. The futures in which we blow it, or in which the peril drags on for centuries, are the ones worth running to the end. So the intervention that maximizes expected far-future value is negatively correlated with there being any far future at all. Your action moves the size of the payoff and the probability of its being realized in opposite directions, so the neat multiplicative independence fails and you actually have to do the joint calculation. A wrinkle: conditional on an interest-sensitive stopping rule, our finding ourselves in a high-stakes century is itself evidence for that rule, which is evidence the run ends once the stakes resolve.

(3) A reductio, I think. Suppose future civilizations run many simulations. Then most of the value in the far future is simulated value, and the fate of base-level reality is a rounding error. Longtermism would then have to be roughly indifferent between protecting base-level reality and raising the number or quality of simulations — i.e. it should be fanatical about the simulation rate, a quantity about which we have no evidence and over which we have no causal purchase. I take this as a reductio of the framework rather than a conclusion, but I'm not sure it doesn't quietly assume that simulated minds count the same way, which is the very thing in dispute.

(4) What survives. Under every hypothesis in play — base-level, sim with a budget-fixed end, sim with an interest horizon — the near future is the part that is real and gets realized. So the simulation consideration doesn't scale far-future value down by a constant; it front-loads value, tilting the ordering of interventions toward the near term even if far-future work still wins on raw expectation. Corollary I only half believe: what we most need is information about which stopping rule we're under, and near-term work is the cheapest source of it.

(5) The generalization worry. None of this strictly needs simulation. What's doing the work is an observer-dependent horizon: our future gets cut short by something outside it that is sensitive to what we do inside it. Simulation is one member of a class. That's either the making of the paper — it no longer depends on a wild hypothesis being probable — or its undoing, because the class is so broad and unconstrained that it yields no verdict at all.

One more piece of context: a colleague who works on anthropics says (2) is just the familiar anthropic-shadow sort of point in a Halloween costume, and I can't decide whether she's right. If she is, my job is to say what's different, and I don't currently know what that is.

So: give me exactly five numbered comments, each one or two sentences, no introduction and no summing-up at the end. In order: (1) the objection you'd lead with if you were refereeing this; (2) the weakest load-bearing premise and why it's weakest; (3) whether (2) above survives the anthropic-selection objection or collapses into it; (4) whether (3) is a genuine reductio or begs the question about simulated minds; (5) whether the generalization in (5) saves the paper or dissolves it — and whether there's a paper left if I strip it down to the minimal claim that survives.
