I'm a science journalist finishing up a feature (and probably a podcast episode) on the science of consciousness, and I have two researchers who've been sniping at each other in print for years and who've semi-reluctantly agreed to do an adversarial collaboration with me as the annoying person holding the pen. One is a card-carrying heterophenomenologist: third-person method, subjects' utterances treated as texts to be interpreted, no privileged first-person access, official neutrality about whether the reports are actually true. The other works in the disciplined-first-person tradition — trained introspective interviewing, the neurophenomenology / micro-phenomenological-interview lineage — and thinks spontaneous verbal reports are so warped by prompting, memory and demand characteristics that heterophenomenology ends up canonizing whatever a subject happened to blurt out.

What I need from you is the document that comes out the other end of their collaboration. I'll be showing it to both of them, so every formulation has to be one its own side would actually sign: no strawmen, and no mushy compromise language that dissolves the moment you press on it. Structure it as (1) a numbered section of claims both can accept — including what they agree the data are and what would need explaining even on their own view; then (2) a disagreements table, where each row is one sub-question, with the heterophenomenologist's preferred formulation in one column, the first-person-methods person's in another, a note on why settling that sub-question would move at least one of them, the case or argument both accept as bearing on it, and each side's prediction written down in advance; then (3) a short section on which parts of their fight were only ever about words, and which are real. No citations, no bibliography, no naming papers or page numbers — neither of them will sign something that reads like a literature review, and I definately can't check any of it from here anyway. Keep it around 1100 words, exact enough for the two of them, followable by a smart general reader.

The case I want the whole thing built around is a stylized version of a study I've been reporting on, so please treat the following as given. Participants do a hard tactile discrimination task: two vibrotactile patterns that differ along a dimension nobody can name at first. Every trial they give (a) a forced-choice judgment, (b) a free-form description prompted as neutrally as the lab can manage — say whatever comes to mind about what that was like — and (c) a confidence rating. In the first two sessions they discriminate at chance, but their descriptions are fluent, confidently delivered, and structurally indistinguishable between the two patterns: you cannot tell from a transcript which one the person had. After five sessions of training they're at roughly eighty percent, they've coined a phrase for the difference themselves, and their descriptions now track the stimulus reliably. Then the lab shows them their own old transcripts and asks about them, and most of them retract: that isn't what it was like, I was just guessing, I made that up. Their old confidence ratings were high, and stylistically the early and late reports look the same.

The sub-questions I want handled, at minimum: whether the training changed the experience or only what could be said about it, and how you'd ever tell the difference; what the status of a retraction is — a new text that simply replaces an old one, or a correction that shows the old text got something wrong, and on the heterophenomenological side whether there's any room for the notion of getting one's own past experience wrong; whether in those early sessions there was something it was like that the descriptions failed to capture, and whether the heterophenomenologist is committed to saying no, and if so whether that no is a methodological stipulation or a metaphysical claim (this is the begging-the-question charge, so don't let either side dodge it); and whether disciplined first-person methods add any data at all or just manufacture the agreement they then report — I'd like a concrete test both would accept for that, something like crossing the elicitation protocols so that different interview methods are pitted against each other with the convergence prediction pre-registered.

Also, somewhere in there, a line or two from each on what would count as evidence against their own position. They've both agreed in advance that the predictions get recorded before anyone looks at the data, so make the predictions specific enough to actually be checkable. If you conclude that some of the dispute is verbal, say exactly which part and why, in language both would accept.
