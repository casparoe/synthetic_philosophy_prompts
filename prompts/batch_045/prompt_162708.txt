I'm on the editorial board of my school paper, and next month we're putting out our annual satire issue. Last spring our administration rolled out a genuinely well-intentioned framework for allocating basically everything the school has to ration — honors placements, varsity spots, laboratory equipment, the two practice rooms with pianos that are actually in tune — and the rollout has been a fiasco in a way I want to write about but can't quite put my finger on. The governing principle, which our assistant principal repeats in every assembly, is that no student should end up worse off than another through no fault of their own: advantages and disadvantages traceable to circumstances are to be compensated or neutralized, while those traceable to gambles a student knowingly chose to take are the student's own to bear. My worry is that if I just write it as "ha ha, the paperwork is endless," I'll be making fun of the forms rather than of the idea, and I'd rather the piece be unfair to nothing — I want someone who has actually thought hard about this kind of view to read it and wince in recognition, not at a straw version of something they believe.

What I'd like you to write is the letter to families from the Office of Circumstance Equity that announces this year's round of reforms: a memo of roughly 650–750 words, in the deadpan, warm-but-procedural voice our administration actually uses, with a headline and two or three short section headings. It should be entirely straight — no winking at the reader, no "critics might object that…" paragraph, no editorializing outside the voice — and it should be legible to a parent with no background in philosophy. The jokes have to come from the machinery working exactly as designed: a student who knew the odds and lost getting nothing while a student who never chose her situation gets compensated; the varsity crew and the honors orchestra being dissolved rather than left unequal, because the office cannot raise everyone and refuses to leave anyone behind; the requirement that a student seeking compensation appear before a panel and document, in detail, the respect in which she is worse off through no fault of her own, which the office presents as a dignity-preserving affirmation of her equal standing. Please also have the office handle, with total bureaucratic confidence, two wrinkles I've been turning over: first, "inherited option luck," where a student's hardship traces to a deliberate gamble taken by a parent before the student was born (a parent who quit a tenured job to open a bakery), which the office rules to be the student's own option luck, transmitted at birth; and second, "cultivated circumstance," where a student who was dealt an unchosen deficit practiced obsessively and overcame it, and the office rules that the compulsion to practice was itself a product of early circumstance, so the resulting advantage is partly unchosen and must be partially clawed back. Somewhere in the piece I'd like the office to deal with a student who, through no choice of hers, now needs very costly resources to reach the same level of flourishing as everyone else, and to deal with it badly but not dishonestly. Close with a short note on a problem the office has noticed: students are beginning to under-practice and avoid opportunities on purpose, so that their deficits stay legible as unchosen, and the office's remedy — a signed "ambition ledger" subject to random audit. Please don't name any philosopher or use any academic label for the view; keep the vocabulary the office would use, and let the reader feel the objections without anyone stating them.
