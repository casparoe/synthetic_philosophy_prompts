I'm the grad-student rep on my institute's ethics committee, which mostly means I take the minutes, but this time I also have to draft the actual recommendation, and I'm stuck. The committee assigns every kind of animal or preparation a "protection tier" from 0 to 4, which determines what procedures need full review and how much justification has to be written up. Here's our track record, as far as I can reconstruct it from the minutes: typical adult human, 4; human infant, 4; an adult human with severe microcephaly (roughly a third of typical brain mass, profound cognitive impairment), 4, and the discussion was short; an anencephalic infant, 2 — the only time we've ever placed a human below 3, and the vote was 4–3, with the dissenters saying the tier system should just stop at the species line. Chimpanzee, 3; gorilla, 3, and someone explicitly wrote in the minutes that "we are not going to split hairs over grams," even though a gorilla brain is substantially bigger than a chimp's. Sperm whale, 2 — biggest brain on earth, no bump at all. African elephant, 2. Raven, 2, and African grey parrot, 2, both on behavioural grounds (tool use, flexible problem solving). Pig, 1. Mouse, 1. Zebrafish, 0. And a human-derived cerebral organoid with a few million neurons and no behaviour whatsoever, 0, with a line saying the species the cells came from is irrelevant. Oh, and dog, 2 versus pig, 1, which everyone privately admits is basically cultural, but nobody on the committee will reopen it.

The new proposal is from a postdoc who wants to do aversive conditioning on common octopuses — mild shock they can learn to avoid by moving to a different compartment — plus some terminal surgery under anaesthesia afterwards to look at gene expression. The briefing she attached says an octopus brain is about half a gram, that she has around half a billion neurons but most of them are out in the arms with maybe 40–50 million in the central brain, that the body can run to several kilos, that they're solitary, semelparous and live a year or two, and that the evidence on flexible problem solving is good while anything like self-recognition is contested at best. (One caveat: the briefing also claims the sperm whale's cortex is thin and comparatively neuron-poor, and I've since been told that's disputed for at least some toothed whales, so please don't lean hard on that particular fact.)

What I can't figure out is what rule we've actually been running, and I'd rather you tell me than have me invent one after the fact. As far as I can see the precedents are compatible with something like "absolute forebrain neuron count, with a floor for humans," with something like "behavioural capacities, with anatomy only as evidence and the human cases handled as a separate legal fact," and maybe with some brain-to-body-ratio thing that would explain why the whale and the elephant didn't move and the raven did — but those rules come apart on the octopus, and possibly come apart badly. So: what are the two or three rules that fit our verdicts, what does each predict for the octopus, what's your best guess for what this committee will actually do once I put it to a vote, and which of our old cases is the closest analog? I also want the honest version of what we'd be committing to either way — if we give the octopus a 2 on behaviour despite a half-gram brain, we've admitted size is only ever a proxy; if we give it a 1 because of the central neuron count, we've picked a specific anatomical currency and I'd like to know why that one. Last thing: my own candidate for the case that would settle it is one nobody's proposed — suppose we could swap an animal's neurons for functionally identical but physically bigger units, so the brain triples in volume, the neuron count doesn't change, and nothing about the animal's behaviour changes. If our rule says the tier goes up, we've been doing size-per-se the whole time. Tell me whether you buy that as the decisive test, or whether you've got a better one, and whether we should just stop using brain metrics altogether and switch to something behavioural (and what we'd lose if we did).
