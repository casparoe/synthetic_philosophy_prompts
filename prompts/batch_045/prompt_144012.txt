At the reading group I go to on Sunday evenings — mostly compsci PhDs, a couple of philosophers, and me (I'm a data engineer, so properly out of my depth) — one of the compsci guys keeps using the line that Solomonoff induction is just Occam's razor with the hand-waving taken out. He's not using it loosely. He's using it to carry a big claim: that we now basically have a proof that simplicity is truth-conducive, so an ideal agent that starts from a simplicity prior over computable hypotheses and just conditions will zero in on the truth whatever the world turns out to be like, and therefore all the domain-specific machinery in actual science — and in actual ML, the architectures and inductive biases and hand-built priors — is only an efficiency hack that a big enough Solomonoff inductor wouldn't need.

I said the slogan felt like it was doing a lot of work, and he had an answer for everything I raised, so I'd rather have an audit than a verdict on vibes. Can you give me the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the entities, the theory, the data, "fitting the data", "simplicity", on each side — and then take the mapping apart? The things I can't get straight:

1. The razor as I was taught it is a tie-breaker: among hypotheses that fit the data, prefer the simpler. Solomonoff's 2^-length weighting is a prior, applied before the data and with no seperate notion of fit. Those look like different jobs to me, and I think he slides between them, but he says the tie-breaker is just what the prior looks like from the inside once you've conditioned.

2. The medieval razor is about not multiplying entities; Kolmogorov complexity is about short programs. A 200-bit program can specify a universe with 10^80 particles in it, and a unified field equation with thirty measured constants looks simple one way and not the other. Is "simplicity" even the same property across the mapping? His reply is that this just shows the medieval version was muddled and that KC captures what was always defensible in it — which he counts as a point for the analogy, not against it.

3. Which programs are short depends on the machine and on how you encode the observation stream, and the invariance theorem only says the differences are bounded by a constant — but the constant isn't bounded across choices of machine, and it feeds straight into the convergence guarantee. His reply: a constant is precisely the sort of thing that washes out, and if I'm going to object to that I'm objecting to Bayesianism generally, since his prior is no more arbitrary than mine. I couldnt answer that one.

4. The convergence result is over computable sources only, and what it bounds is cumulative error, not how long you wait — wether that's a hundred steps or 10^30 is left open. And the prior itself is uncomputable. His reply: uncomputability is an engineering objection, not an epistemic one; the ideal can be uncomputable and still be the ideal, the same way expected utility maximisation is.

There's one more thing I can't place at all. Solomonoff's prior doesn't select the simplest hypothesis — it mixes over all of them with weight tilted toward the short ones, and it doesn't care whether the short program says anything interpretable or unifies anything. There's a grue-style worry in there somewhere about which predicates count as natural being fixed by the encoding rather than by the world. Where does that leave "Occam's razor"?

So: which of these disanalogies actually bite on his conclusion and which are noise? Does the conclusion survive any of them — is there a weaker claim nearby that the analogy does support? And is there a better analogy for what Solomonoff induction is, or is this one of those cases where the thing is just itself and the analogy is decoration?
