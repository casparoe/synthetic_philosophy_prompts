I had a long argument on Saturday with a friend who works in structural biology, and I want to know whether the philosophical literature can settle it well enough to change what my team actually does on Monday. Her position was that a function annotation should simply record what the gene product currently contributes to the organism that carries it — that selection history is a separate, optional piece of information, and that mixing the two into one field is what makes our annotations incoherent. My position was that this throws away the difference between what a trait is *for* and what it happens to do, and that it leaves us unable to say that anything has gone wrong: if function is just current contribution, then a protein that no longer performs the effect it was maintained for isn't malfunctioning, it just isn't contributing. We went back and forth for about two hours and got nowhere, and I suspect we were each defending a different thing the word "function" might be picking out.

I am the curation lead for a genome-function database maintained by a small consortium. We process roughly forty annotation tickets a week with a staff of twelve curators who have master's-level training in molecular biology and no philosophy. Each curator has about fifteen minutes per ticket. The output has to be one of a small closed set of labels that can be entered directly into the database, because our annotations are consumed downstream by a machine-learning group that predicts phenotype from genotype and, indirectly, by clinical partners who use them to decide whether a loss-of-function variant is worth investigating in a rare-disease case. Routine annotations are signed off by the curator; anything that changes an already-published annotation has to go to me and, if it affects more than a handful of entries, to the consortium steering group.

My team has floated a compromise I want you to evaluate rather than assume: split the existing "function" field into three subfields — a selected-effect field (what, if anything, the trait appears to have been maintained by selection for), a current-contribution field (what it now systematically does that bears on some capacity of the system), and a provenance field recording what kind of evidence supports each — and then let downstream users choose which one they care about. I want to know whether that split dissolves the disagreement or merely relabels it, in particular for the cases where a user needs a single answer and cannot be given two.

Please write the procedure we would actually run on a ticket: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and returns no annotation, and where it hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is not one the evidence can settle. It has to be usable by someone who has never heard the etiological/causal-role distinction, so say explicitly which of the philosophical distinctions must survive that simplification and which can be dropped without loss. Most of our tickets do not come with a phylogeny or a selection scan and we cannot afford to produce one, so the procedure has to say what to do when the historical evidence simply isn't there. Flag the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run it on three stylized tickets of the kind we get.

1. A light-gated ion channel in a soil bacterium, acquired from an unrelated lineage by horizontal transfer within the last few decades, spreading in the population, and shown in assay to modulate the cell's response to a wavelength present in its current environment. Both the historical and the present-day stories appear to point the same way; I want to see whether the procedure wastes a curator's time confirming this or clears it in one step.

2. An opsin-like light-sensitive protein in a vertebrate lineage that has lived in permanent darkness for a very long time. The protein is still expressed in tissue beneath the skin, still binds its chromophore, and still changes conformation when illuminated in the lab, but nothing in the animal's life is affected by light, and the gene shows signs of relaxed selection. A colleague has pointed out that the protein also binds and sequesters a metal ion, and that this sequestration measurably improves survival in the chemistry of the cave water.

3. A gene whose product was maintained by selection for a metabolic step that no longer occurs in the organism's current environment, because the organism now obtains the metabolite from a host. The same protein, as a matter of present causal structure, binds a host protein and suppresses a host immune response, and that suppression is the only thing about it anyone currently cares about. I want this ticket annotated as immune suppression, and I want you to run your procedure honestly even if it refuses to say that: if it does refuse, tell me what it says instead and why I should live with it.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: which tickets it will misclassify systematically, which of our existing annotations it would force us to revisit if we applied it retroactively, and what I should say to the steering group if they ask whether adopting it commits us to one side of the philosophical dispute. Please don't give me citations or a reading list; I'll handle the literature myself.
