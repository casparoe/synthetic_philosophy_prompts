Hey — I run a tiny reading group (five of us, mostly grad students plus one retired engineer who keeps asking annoying questions) and we're working through Scanlon's contractualism, specifically the aggregation problem. I want to build a decision-tree handout that starts from "I accept that morality is about what no one could reasonably reject, and that we compare complaints person by person" and ends at a named position on whether and how numbers count. Every branch node should be a question we can answer about a concrete case without already knowing which leaf it leads to, and leaves should be real positions (call them whatever you think the standard label is, or "unnamed" if you think nobody actually holds it).

Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first the splitting questions and the combinations they allow, then which combinations are actually occupied, and for the empty ones a one-line note on whether nobody lives there because the view is incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unexplored.

The angle we care about: we want to use the tree for an actual decision, whether our department should spend a small travel fund on one person who'd otherwise miss a career-critical conference, or on a pile of small things that add up to more total benefit. So the cases should mostly look like that, not only the classic rescue cases. Distinctions I want to matter:

- one-shot vs iterated: does anything change if this same kind of choice comes up twenty times and you can alternate who gets helped?
- de minimis: complaints so tiny that no single person could reasonably raise one on their own — is the filter applied per person, or only to the pooled total?
- shared vs separate complaints: the many each loose a bit of the same thing they jointly use (a shared instrument, a commons) versus each losing their own separate thing
- whether the people in the "many" are the same people across the options or a completely different set

Leaves I want to appear: strict anti-aggregation, numbers-only-as-tie-breaker, the "total up the fraction of each person's claim that gets satisfied" style view, weighted-lottery / equal-chances ex ante, some threshold-or-sufficiency variant, and plain aggregation. Seven or eight branch questions at most, and flag any pair of leaves my cases can't pry apart — I'd rather know than have it glossed over. No need for citations or a reading list, we've got those.
