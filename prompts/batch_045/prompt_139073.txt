I'm in a seminar on the philosophy of cognitive science and we've spent the last two weeks on nativism about language; I drafted an argument for my final paper that I now think proves far too much, and I can't find the break. Setting it out with numbered steps: (1) The poverty of the stimulus: the child's input is a finite, fragmentary, error-ridden sample of speech, together with essentially no information about which strings are ungrammatical and no systematic correction of grammaticality; many grammars incompatible with the one the child ends up with are consistent with that sample, yet children converge on one anyway, quickly and uniformly, and they retreat from overgeneralizations nobody ever corrected. (2) If the data underdetermine the acquired system in that way, the learner must contribute something beyond the data, some constraint that rules out the incompatible alternatives. (3) Hence a substantial part of the child's eventual grammar is not learned but innate: an innately specified constraint on the space of possible grammars, rich enough to make the actual data sufficient. Call this Content-Nativism, as against mere Format-Nativism, the weaker claim that what is innate is only the representational format and learning architecture, and no specifically linguistic principle. (4) But the relation between input and acquired system in (1) is not peculiar to language: any learner acquiring any competence from finite, noisy, unlabelled samples faces the same gap, since no finite set of observations entails a generalization about unobserved cases. If the child's data underdetermine the grammar, then the physicist's data underdetermine the theory, the mechanic's data underdetermine the diagnosis, and the infant's data underdetermine the difference between faces and other objects, kin and strangers, reliable and unreliable informants. (5) So by (2) every acquired competence rests on innate content specific to it: language is not special but one item on a long list that includes face recognition, navigation, arithmetic, and chess mastery. (6) And the list doesn't stop at species-typical capacities or at childhood: whatever does the work in (2) does it for competences nobody could have anticipated, such as reading or computer programming, and for expertise acquired in adulthood, such as a radiologist's. (7) But (6) is unacceptable twice over. It collapses the distinction the argument was meant to exploit, since if every acquisition is underwritten by innate content then calling grammar innate explains nothing and the victory is verbal. And it makes the thesis unfalsifiable: for any putative case of learning one can always find some unobserved case the data failed to entail, hence some innate constraint, so no evidence could ever show that a competence was learned. (8) Therefore (1)–(3) is either trivial or it proves far too much: the nativist argument, run in full generality, destroys the contrast between learned and innate that gave it its force.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict. Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked sound, and what rejecting it costs. My suspicion is an equivocation somewhere around (2)–(3), between the near-truism that learning requires some bias over hypotheses and the substantive claim that the bias has specifically linguistic content in it; formal work on learning from positive evidence alone seems to me to support only the truism, and I can't see how to get from the truism to Universal Grammar without help. But I also can't see how the nativist blocks (4) without a premise that just re-describes the conclusion: what would such a premise even have to say — something about the absence of negative evidence, about the target being a system of rules over unobservable structure, about the input being degenerate rather than merely finite — and does it do real work or only relabel the problem? I'd rather keep (1) than give it up, and I'd like to keep something recognizable as (3), but I'll drop either if that's where the pressure is. Please also price the alternatives: what does the empiricist or usage-based theorist pay for rejecting (1), given that they owe an account of convergence and of the uncorrected retreat from overgeneralization; what does the nativist pay for biting the bullet on (5) and accepting massive innate content while denying (6); and what, if anything, is left of the debate if the third option is right and we should accept a domain-general learner plus rich input plus statistical learning, so that the disagreement was never about innateness at all?

Two further things I'd like handled, because they're where I expect to be pushed in the seminar. First, a case: suppose a cohort of children is exposed only to a pidgin-like system with no consistent word order and no bound morphology, and that cohort stabilizes it into a system with consistent order and agreement morphology that none of the adults ever produced. Does that case decide anything between the positions, or does it just relocate the argument — and if it relocates it, where exactly, and in whose favour? Second, a worry about granularity: suppose we try to make (1) quantitative, by asking what fraction of the learner's final confidence comes from the data as opposed to the prior, or how many candidate grammars the actual sample eliminates relative to the whole hypothesis space. If innateness then becomes a matter of degree, the argument seems to lose its power to establish a sharp conclusion, and language's alleged specialness looks like a claim about where on a continuum it sits rather than about which side of a line. Is that the right way to think about it, or does treating innateness as a scalar quantity misdescribe what the nativist actually claims? I'd like to come away knowing whether this disagreement is empirical — how rich the input is, how much work the prior does — or conceptual, a fight about what would count as innate content in the first place, since my paper currently can't tell the difference.
