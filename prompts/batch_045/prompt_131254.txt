I have argued myself into a conclusion I do not want, and I would like help finding the exit rather than reassurance that my instincts were right.

The setting is concrete. There is a planning application to build a distribution warehouse on roughly forty hectares of lowland wet grassland near where I live. The ecological survey is unremarkable: no protected species of note, a decent invertebrate assemblage, breeding waders, and resident populations of small mammals and amphibians. A friend who works on animal ethics read the report and remarked that it is at least arguable that the development reduces net suffering, and that conservationists who object have the sign wrong. I have tried to reconstruct her reasoning as a valid argument, and this is what I get:

(1) What matters morally about a population of sentient animals is the total net welfare it contains, summed across individuals and across time; a life is net-negative when the unpleasant experiences in it outweigh the pleasant ones, and net welfare is additive and comparable across individuals.

(2) The lives of small, short-lived, highly fecund wild vertebrates are typically net-negative: most of the individuals conceived die before or shortly after nutritional independence, through starvation, exposure, parasitism or predation, and the minority that survive have short lives dominated by the effort of staying alive. This is the step usually put in terms of r-selection and juvenile mortality.

(3) A fixed area of habitat sustains a population whose size is set largely by its carrying capacity, so, holding habitat type fixed, the number of animal-years lived per year on that area is roughly proportional to its area. Define the welfare throughput of a habitat as the average net welfare per animal-year multiplied by the number of animal-years lived per unit area per year. Given (2), the throughput of wet grassland is negative.

(4) Converting forty hectares of wet grassland to buildings and hard standing permanently removes the throughput of those hectares: not merely for the decades the warehouse stands but indefinitely, since the conversion is not reversible on any timescale over which anyone plans.

(5) The loss is not offset by gains elsewhere: the site is not a fragment whose occupants will simply be absorbed by an adjoining block of the same habitat, and the built footprint supports only the small commensal populations that follow people about, which are in any case tiny per hectare by comparison with the grassland they replace.

(6) Hence the development reduces the total quantity of net-negative experience in the world, and it does so by a large absolute margin, because the per-year saving is multiplied by an open-ended future.

(7) If an act reduces total suffering, and such suffering as it causes directly is small by comparison — here, the animals killed or displaced in clearing the site — then, other things equal, it is good that the act be done.

(8) Therefore, other things equal, it is good that the warehouse be built, and objections made to it on welfare grounds have the sign wrong.

What troubles me is not step (8) but what happens when the argument is pressed:

(9) Nothing in (1) to (7) turns on the wetland's being ordinary. Run it on the last remaining fragment of a habitat, or on a nature reserve, and it says that the best fate for that land is to be paved: the more valuable it is as habitat, the more negative its throughput, and so the larger the gain from removing it.

(10) Nothing in it turns on the mechanism either. If it is the reduction in the number of net-negative lives that does the work, then any means of securing that reduction is eligible: fertility control, culling, a deliberately introduced pathogen, degrading the habitat so that breeding success collapses while the adults linger on. And if we may worsen the habitat in order to cut the birth rate, it is unclear what rules out worsening the lives of the animals already present in order to cut it further.

(11) Nor does anything turn on what I do. If I decline to support the application, someone else will build the warehouse and my objection will change nothing; so the argument supplies me with a reason to support this conversion, and by identical arithmetic a reason to support every comparable one I can identify, which looks less like a permission than a duty.

I do not want to accept (9) to (11). But I am also reluctant to give up (1): I think the suffering of wild animals matters, and I do not see why it should matter less than the suffering of farmed ones. And I would rather keep (2) than lose it, since denying it appears to require a sanguine view of juvenile mortality that I have no evidence for.

So: which step would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, I would be grateful if you could say whether the fault lies in the moral architecture — (1) or (7) — or in the empirical bridge between area and welfare — (3) to (5) — and, if the latter, whether it is a conceptual mistake about what throughput measures or merely an empirical bet that could go either way. One move I have considered and cannot evaluate: adding a side-constraint to the effect that no intervention may worsen the condition of any animal already alive, which would block (10) but appears to block the warehouse as well, since the animals on the site are killed or displaced, and would in any case sit badly with (1). If you think the argument is sound and that (9) to (11) are conclusions I should learn to live with, say so, and tell me which of them is least repugnant and why.

Finally, please state how confident you are in the diagnosis you offer, and single out the point in your answer of which you are least sure — especially where you are relying on claims about population dynamics or about the sentience and affective lives of small vertebrates that you are not able to check.
