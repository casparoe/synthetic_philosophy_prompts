hey — I'm in a practical reason seminar this term and we've now spent about four weeks on incommensurable values and hard choices, and nothing has moved at all, and I dont think it's because anyone is being dense. The thing is, everyone agrees on the cases (career as a lawyer vs as a philosopher, selling something you love for money you really need, a friendship against a principle you'd have to betray to keep it), everyone agrees these choices feel agonising in a way ordinary tradeoffs dont, everyone agrees there's no procedure that cranks out an answer, and everyone agrees that after you choose there's some residue left over — regret, or the sense that you can't fully explain yourself to someone who chose the other way. And yet: one camp says the values are genuinely incomparable, there's a gap in value itself, no common scale; another says of course they're comparable and "incommensurable" at most means our grip on the magnitudes is vague or imprecise, so a hard choice is a measurement problem wearing a metaphysical costume; a third says comparability is the wrong axis, the options can be *on a par* — genuinely comparable but neither better nor worse nor equally good, a fourth value relation rather than a failure to find one. Four weeks, same three speeches, zero movement.

Here's a gadget I made up to try to break the tie, and I want you to tell me if it's actually a good test or a dud. Call it a parity-meter: a black box that takes a pair of options plus a particular person at a particular time and prints exactly one of four lines — A is better, B is better, equally good, on a par. Stipulate that it is never wrong about which relation holds and that it never refuses. But it gives no reasons, no weights, no scale, no explanation, and it never tells you what to do. Now ask each camp (a) what they predict the box prints for the standard cases and (b) whether a printout would end the argument. My sense is that the answers come apart in a revealing way: the imprecision camp should predict mostly "better"/"equally good" and treat "on a par" as the box being unspecific; the gap camp should say the printout changes nothing, because even a perfect readout isn't a reason and the fight was never about which relation holds; the parity camp should say a single honest "on a par" line vindicates them. But I'm not sure — maybe all three can absorb every possible printout, which would itself be the diagnosis.

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the real disagreement is about rational agency, not about value. Something like — must there be a fact of the matter about which option is better for the choice to be fully rational, or can an agent's will confer value exactly where value runs out, so that choosing well isn't discovering a ranking but making one? And the metaphysics of value (gaps in value, fourth relations, vagueness) is just where that disagreement goes to hide, because it lets everyone talk about scales instead of about what they think agency requires.

So: don't survey the literature or give me anything to read, I've got all that. Diagnose the stalemate. Is it verbal (three senses of "incommensurable" / two senses of "hard" — hard to tell vs. unsettled even with all the facts), substantive metaphysics, an epistemic dispute in disguise, a hidden normative disagreement about rationality or about what counts as a reason, three groups answering three different questions under one heading, or a badly posed question? I want reasons grounded in what each side actually argues, not a scorecard. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a conceptual distinction, some sort of empirical result about deliberation or regret, a formal result about the logic of value relations (transitivity, small-improvement arguments, that kind of thing), a change of question — or an honest argument that no progress should be expected and why.
