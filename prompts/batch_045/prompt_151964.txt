I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science, finishing a dissertation chapter on fine-tuning arguments, and I want to run my own position past someone before I present it at our work-in-progress seminar. I've been holding these views for a couple of years, each of them picked up for what felt like a good local reason, and a committee member recently told me the package "can't have all of these doing work at the same time" without saying which one she thought was the problem. I genuinely can't see the fault line, so I'd like an audit rather than a tutorial. Here is where I've landed, roughly in the order I acquired the commitments. (1) I take the fine-tuning datum to be robust, and I treat two readings of it as interchangeable: the fragility reading, on which life-permittingness is destroyed by very small perturbations of the constants, and the rarity reading, on which the life-permitting region occupies a small fraction of the parameter space under any reasonable measure. I help myself to whichever reading makes the argument go in a given dialectical context. (2) I'm a likelihoodist about confirmation: evidence E supports H against its rivals just in case P(E|H) exceeds P(E|rivals), and I don't think H needs independent antecedent plausibility, independent testability, or a track record in order to be confirmed by E — which is why I'm unmoved by the standard complaint that multiverse hypotheses are untestable. (3) I nevertheless think the fine-tuning data give some real, if modest, support to a design or theistic hypothesis. (4) I also think the anthropic point does genuine deflationary work: conditional on our own existence we should have expected to find ourselves in a life-permitting universe, and that takes much of the sting out of the datum. (5) I reject typicality reasoning when it delivers Boltzmann-brain-style conclusions about what we should expect to observe; I read those results as showing that something has gone wrong in applying a self-sampling assumption to observers, not as discoveries about the world. (6) I think anthropic reasoning is in the end just ordinary Bayesian conditioning on the fact that we exist, with no special "anthropic" logic and no freestanding principle of typicality to be justified. (7) I'm fairly tolerant of brute facts: I don't accept the inference from "this would be surprising if unexplained" to "there must be an explanation," and I don't treat the demand for explanation as a methodological primitive.

Two case verdicts I'm fairly confident about, and which I'd be reluctant to give up. (8a) If we were to discover that the constants are fixed uniquely by a deeper theory with no free parameters, I'd say the design argument collapses outright and that nothing evidentially interesting survives: the datum would simply carry no support for design. (8b) If we were to discover that the life-permitting values form a scattered "archipelago" of many disconnected patches in parameter space with no unifying physical characterization — a Goodman-style gerrymandered predicate over the space, except that the patches are genuinely tiny, so the total measure of the life-permitting region is exactly the same as before — I'd say that noticeably weakens the design inference even though the rarity number is unchanged, while leaving the multiverse inference untouched or slightly strengthened. I should also say that I take the inverse-gambler's-fallacy style objection to leave the multiverse inference intact, but I think any multiverse explanation owes an account of why we observe this universe rather than merely that some universe is life-permitting.

What I want is a consistency audit of this set, ordered rather than flat. Which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension, and how severe is each — I'd rather have three real problems ranked than eight items all flagged as "in tension with each other." For each real tension, tell me precisely what would have to give and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set. Equally important: tell me which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection, and why, since I suspect at least one item on my list only looks like it conflicts with another. Then name the single revision that would clean up the most, and say what I'd lose by making it. Please don't include a reading list, bibliography, or citations — I have that literature covered and will fill references in myself — and please write the answer in American English.
