I do translation and questionnair localisation for survey instruments (mostly market research, but I've done a handful of academic ones), and I read philosophy blogs for fun, so I keep running into the cross-cultural vignette studies: you tell people a short story and ask something like "did he really know, or did he only think he knew", and then you compare answer rates between two countries — the one where people mostly give the answer philosophy professors give, and the one where they mostly don't.

Here's the pattern I keep noticing and don't have a word for. In a chunk of the responses, from whichever group, people aren't answering the question that was asked. Ask "did he know" and they write back things like "you couldn't say that in court" or "for practical purposes you have to act as if he didn't" — they've turned a question about how things are into a question about what we should say, or what someone in that position should do. That much is boring. Three things make it look like something with a shape to me:

1. It isn't spread evenly across respondents, it tracks the verdict. Nearly everyone who gives the minority answer is answering a question I didn't ask, while the people giving the majority answer mostly answer straight. So it can't just be noise that averages out across the sample.
2. The off-target answers are often *good*. They cite considerations that are genuinely relevant to the original question, the sort of thing a philosopher would count as a reason. It's not confusion, and it's not failing to follow the story: comprehension checks come out fine.
3. It shows up in both groups but lopsidedly. In the other group the minority-verdict people do it too, just less often and more vaguely. And I've seen the same shape in a completely different kind of case — the sort where someone uses a name they picked up second-hand and got the details wrong, and you ask who they're talking about.

So the measured gap between groups seems to sit almost entirely inside the subpopulation who quietly switched the question, and if you coded only "did this person answer what was asked" the gap would shrink a lot but not vanish.

Is there a name for this? I assume survey methodology has a term for an item meaning different things to different groups, but I don't know it, and I suspect it isn't quite my thing either, because here the switching is tied to the content of the answer rather than to which group someone is in.

What I actually want is the carve, not a label. What is this, and what is it not — it isn't plain misunderstanding, it isn't translation failure, it isn't people being yea-sayers or picking the extreme option. Where are the boundary cases: someone who does answer the asked question but backs it with a "you couldn't say that" consideration; someone who answers both versions; someone whose off-target answer is just barely on target. And the bit I really can't settle: is this a confound to be screened or weighted away, or is it the datum — is what differs between the groups the verdict, or what they take the case to be *about*? If nothing established fits, coin a term and define it with its edges. Please don't just hand me a word or point me at papers to read.
