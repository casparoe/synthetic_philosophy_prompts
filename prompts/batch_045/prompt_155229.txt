I cover science and ideas for a newspaper and I'm trying to stage a disagreement properly for a piece, and I keep getting lost in the weeds. The setup: a state legislator is shopping around a draft bill — hypothetical, I should say, one of my sources helped write it — that would extend a thin set of protections to artificial systems that "realize the right kind of causal organization." My two sources both think the other one is missing the actual problem, and I dont want dueling talking points, I want the real arguement.

So write me a dialogue of exactly five numbered exchanges, one or two sentences per speaker per exchange, no intro and no wrap-up, no stage directions beyond who's talking, between:

Dana — advised the drafters. Mental states are causal roles; what fills the role is in principle irrelevant; that's the whole point of multiple realizability, so there's no principled line to draw at biology. Extending protections is just applying our own standards consistently.

Priya — not a substrate chauvinist, and she says so up front. Her worry is that a causal role has to be specified at some grain, and the choice of grain is doing all the work: specify it coarse, in belief-and-desire terms, and a lookup table or a thermostat's control loop fills it fine; specify it fine, down to the actual state-space geometry and update rule, and you've quietly rebuilt the original hardware and multiple realizability is dead. She has a name for sliding between the two mid-argument — grain-shifting — and thinks the bill does it.

What I want them to actually get into, each one answering the other's real point rather than restating a position:

- Dana's best answer to the too-liberal worry, and Priya's comeback. Maybe Priya concedes the lookup-table system is conscious and bites the bullet; maybe she says the difference is counterfactual richness, and Dana says counterfactual richness is just more of the same role stuff and can't be what separates them.
- A case I made up and can't get out of my head: take a system with a huge internal state space and replace it with one that recomputes each transition on the fly instead of storing states. Every input-output behavior and every counterfactual comes out the same, but nothing persists between steps. If organization is what matters, that thing counts. Priya thinks it's a reductio. Dana has to do better than "fine, it counts."
- The number question. Split a system into two copies and run both, or merge two into one. Functionalism tells you which roles are filled, not how many subjects there are, and the bill needs a number.
- Symmetry and evidence. Dana pushes the double standard: you never get certainty about other humans either, and if functionalism is substrate-neutral it cuts both ways and should sometimes tell us a system is not conscious. Priya's reply: with other humans the shared realizer is doing all the evidential work, which is exactly the thing in dispute.
- Each of them should concede one real thing. Dana can grant the grain problem is unsolved; Priya can grant that "same stuff" isn't itself a moral difference.

The bullets don't each need their own exchange — distribute them across the five, but hit all of them. Land exchange 5 on the specific thing they'd have to go and find out empirically to settle this, not on agreement, and keep it free of citations and reading lists: I need the shape of the disagreement, and I couldn't fact-check a bibliography anyway.
