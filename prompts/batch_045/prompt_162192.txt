I'm a backend engineer by trade — no formal training in philosophy or political science — and on evenings and weekends I volunteer as one of the maintainers of a small open-source project: a hand-curated public dataset that logs discrete institutional changes in roughly forty countries and tags each one as democratic backsliding, ordinary reform, democratic deepening, or unclear. We have about twelve volunteer annotators (mostly software people, one retired lawyer, two grad students), we make several hundred of these calls a year, and because the dataset is public and gets cited by journalists and occasionally by NGO reports, a sloppy tag does real damage — not abstract harm, but "someone's country gets called a backsliding democracy in print because a volunteer was having a bad Tuesday." Our current codebook is essentially a page of adjectives, and it shows: inter-annotator agreement sits around 0.5 on Cohen's kappa, and the same measure gets coded differently depending on whether the annotator happens to approve of the government that passed it. A friend in grad school told me, only half-jokingly, that our tag is drifting into being a partisan insult with a database schema, and I don't have a principled reply to that.

So I want a coding protocol I can actually hand to an annotator: an ordered sequence of tests to run on a single proposed entry, each with an explicit statement of what counts as passing it, an explicit early exit for "this is not backsliding, stop," and an explicit escalation to a human editor at the point where the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. Assume the annotator has twenty minutes, a search engine, no country expertise, and no ability to do deep archival research; the protocol has to say what to do when the evidence simply isn't there (and I'd rather it record "insufficient evidence" honestly than infer from vibes). Assume also that the output of the protocol is a structured record: a tag, a one-line justification, a confidence level, and a note saying which test decided the call — because I want to diff those notes when we version the codebook.

A few things I specifically need the protocol to handle, and on which I want you to take a position rather than offer me a menu. First, the unit-of-analysis problem: most of the changes we see are trivial in isolation and only mean something as part of a sequence, and I don't have a rule for when several small measures should be coded as one event — I've been toying with something like a linkage rule (same governing coalition passed them, and each one lowers the cost or raises the payoff of the next), so refine or replace that. Second, and this is the part that worries me most: I need the protocol to make an annotator actually run a symmetry check rather than assert that they are being even-handed — my current idea is a swap test where the measure is paraphrased with country and party names stripped, coded blind, and only then unblinded, with a defined consequence for a mismatch, but I'm not sure it survives contact with real annotators. Third, the consent problem: what do we do with a change that genuinely had majority support or passed a referendum that observers consider broadly fair — is popular mandate a defence, and if not, why not? Fourth, the baseline problem: at what point is a country already below the line such that further changes are not backsliding but something else, and what tag should we use then? Fifth, I want a distinction we don't currently have between a power that has merely been granted on paper and one that has actually been exercised against an opponent, with a rule for how to code the latent case.

Please apply the finished protocol to four cases: the Hungarian constitutional and electoral changes of the early 2010s, the Polish judicial reforms after 2015, the Turkish constitutional referendum of 2017, and a mid-decade US state-level change of the sort we get dozens of submissions about — say a state legislature moving authority over election administration away from an independent body to itself, or replacing a redistricting commission's output with its own map. One of these should come out as an answer I won't like; I'm American, I expect to dislike at least one verdict, and I want you to name which one you think that is and refuse to soften it. Then tell me, concretely, what the protocol cannot do — the two or three kinds of case where it will misfire or where no amount of procedure removes the need for judgment — and where you yourself are least confident.

Be committed throughout: make the contested calls, give your reasons, and where a reasonable annotator or a strand of the comparative-politics literature would disagree with you, say plainly that you think they are wrong and why, rather than presenting the disagreement as an open option. Please structure the response exactly as follows, with roughly these lengths: "The protocol" (about 700 words, the ordered tests with their thresholds and exits); "Thresholds, exits, and incomplete evidence" (about 200 words); "Four worked cases" (about 900 words, one subsection each, each ending with the structured record the annotator would file); "The verdict you won't like" (about 150 words); "Known failure modes" (about 300 words); "Where I'm guessing" (about 150 words).
