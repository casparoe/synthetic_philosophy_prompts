hey — I've somehow let myself be talked into running next month's session of our local philosophy reading group, and I want a plan for it rather than an explaination of it. The group is about a dozen people, mostly in their 30s to 60s, mixed backgrounds: most did a bit of utilitarianism at school and stopped there, two of them are quite into effective altruism and will definately arrive with their minds already made up, and there's a retired GP who'll want everything to be concrete and clinical. Ninety minutes, a whiteboard, no handouts.

The topic I've been asked for is lexical threshold negative utilitarianism, and I want to teach a slightly more specific version than the slogan version, because the slogan version is where everyone gets stuck:

- Suffering above some threshold intensity lexically dominates pleasure. No finite quantity of pleasures, however numerous and however mild, can outweigh a single episode of suffering above the threshold.
- Below the threshold, everything trades off in the ordinary aggregative way — pleasures against mild pains, and milder sufferings against worse sufferings, all on one scale.
- And the bit I care most about: the threshold applies per episode, not per lifetime and not to totals. A thousand miseries that each sit just under the line don't sum their way over it, any more than a thousand short people make one tall person.

I have a feeling that last clause is either the thing that makes the view interesting or the thing that breaks it, and I'd like the session to be built so that the group actually arrives at that fork rather than being told about it.

So, what I want from you:

1. The order the ideas have to go in, and why each one depends on what came before. My worry is that "lexical" and "threshold" both sound like they mean "weigh pain more", and if people leave thinking that, I've failed. What has to be in place first — some grip on the difference between an ordering and a weighting? on comparability versus measurability? on what "on a single scale" even commits you to? — and in what sequence?
2. The one example or case that should carry the whole thing, what it has to have and what it has to lack. I've been toying with something like one hour of cluster-headache-level pain versus a very large number of small pleasant experiences, but tell me what the example needs structurally: should the sufferers be the same person or different people, should the numbers be finite or huge, should there be anything else at stake that I should deliberately strip out (rights, desert, risk, death, whether anyone exists at all)? And I'd guess I should avoid the torture-versus-dust-specks style case even though it's the obvious one — tell me why, or tell me I'm wrong and it's fine.
3. The misconceptions, in the order they'll actually surface, and the exact point in the sequence where I should head each one off. The ones I'm anticipating, more or less: that lexical dominance is just another way of saying suffering matters infinitely much; that the view is plain negative utilitarianism with the dial turned up; that if the threshold location is even slightly vague the whole view collapses; that mild pleasures must therefore be worthless (rather than merely unable to compete across the line); that this is the same thing as the asymmetry about creating happy versus miserable people; that since pain intensities can't really be measured across people the view is unactionable anyway; and the one that will derail the room the moment someone says "negative utilitarianism", namely that the view seems to recommend ending everything. Say which of these I should pre-empt before I state the view, which I should let someone raise and then use, and which I should park and return to at the end.
4. One check at the end that would show me whether the structure has landed rather than just the mood. Ideally a question whose right answer differs from what both plain negative utilitarianism and plain classical utilitarianism would say — so that someone who has only absorbed "pain matters more" gets it wrong and someone who has the ordering in their head gets it right. A variant case they haven't seen, and what a wrong-but-plausible answer would tell me about where they went off.
5. What I should openly refuse to cover in ninety minutes, and say out loud that I'm refusing — the formal machinery of lexicographic orderings, measurement theory, neuroscience of pain scaling, whatever — and why leaving it out won't leave them with a distorted picture.

Write it in British English please (organise, colour, and so on); a couple of American spellings and this particular group will spend ten minutes on those instead of on the argument.
