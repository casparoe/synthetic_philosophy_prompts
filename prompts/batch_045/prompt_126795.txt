You are a professional philosopher who specializes in the metaphysics of material objects, retained as a consultant to the restitution and acquisitions committee of a large museum. Please think carefully, and work in two labelled stages. First, a candid brainstorm: restate in your own words what the genuinely hard question in this case is (I suspect most people misidentify which feature is doing the load-bearing work), list the candidate loci of disagreement, and mark where your own answer is going to turn on a stipulation rather than on the metaphysics. Second, your considered advice. Argue from the characterisations I give you below; do not reach for quotations, citations or readings, and do not tell me what the literature says. I want your reasoning, not a map of it.

Take the following three positions as given; do not argue for or against them, only draw out what follows from them.

(1) The coincidence view. Whenever a clay figure is intact, there are at least two numerically distinct objects exactly occupying the same region and sharing all of their parts at that time: the statue and the lump of clay. They are not identical, because they differ in their modal and temporal properties: the lump can survive being rolled into a ball, the statue cannot; the statue can survive the replacement of a finger, the lump cannot.

(2) The one-thing view. There is only one object there, and 'statue' and 'lump of clay' are two sortals under which it falls. On the strongest version, identity claims are sortal-relative: something can be the same statue as a thing while not being the same lump of clay as it, without contradiction, because 'same' is always 'same F'.

(3) The pluralist view. Where common sense finds one statue, there are in fact very many spatiotemporally coinciding objects, differing minutely in what they could survive and in how long they last (the thing that could survive losing a finger; the thing that could not; the thing that survives squashing; and so on), with no principled way to single out one of them as the statue.

Here is the case. In 1897 a donor bequeathed to the museum a small fired-clay figure, The Kneeling Scribe, on the condition that the bequeathed object itself remain on public display in perpetuity; if the condition fails, the whole collection reverts to the donor's heirs, who are now litigating. Four things are true.

(a) Between 1961 and 1998, during desalination and structural consolidation, conservators gradually replaced most of the original clay with a modern resin-and-glass-microsphere fill. About 12% of the mass of the displayed object is original matter.

(b) Every removed fragment was labelled and boxed into the study collection. If the contents of those boxes were tipped out and reassembled, the result would be a fragile, discoloured thing containing all of the original matter and none of the modern fill.

(c) In 1974 the figure was crated and shipped to an exhibition abroad in eleven pieces, and reassembled on arrival. For roughly six weeks in 1974 there was no figure-shaped object anywhere.

(d) The committee is seriously considering adopting a continuous-matter clause: an object counts as the same object as the one accessioned only if at least half, by mass, of the matter present at accession is still present in it, and matter that has been removed and stored counts as present only if it is physically continuous with the displayed object.

What are the most important implications of each of the three views for: (i) whether the museum is in breach of the bequest condition; (ii) what kind of thing the display condition is best read as attaching to — an artifact kind such as statue, a lump of matter, an artwork, or an accession item tracked by the museum's own records and insurance schedule; and (iii) whether the continuous-matter clause is doing any real metaphysical work, or is merely a bright-line proxy for something else the committee should have written down instead?

I am most interested in implications that are surprising or that cut against what a clever non-specialist would say: places where the coincidence view and the pluralist view, standardly treated as rivals, deliver the same practical verdict for opposite reasons; places where the one-thing view's verdict quietly depends on a choice of sortal that the committee has not noticed it is making; and anything about stipulation (c) that matters more, or less, than it appears to. Close by naming the single implication you would put in front of the committee as the most important, and by stating explicitly which of your claims are stipulative rather than forced by the metaphysics. Around 900 words.
