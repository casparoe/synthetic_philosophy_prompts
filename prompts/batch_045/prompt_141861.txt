You have designed active-learning exercises for philosophy and history students for years, and you are thoroughly at home with the empirical work on hindsight distortion and on the narrative shaping of historical explanation. Think the problem through carefully before you answer: consider at least two or three candidate mechanics, discard them for stated reasons, and design the one that survives. I want something runnable, not a sketch.

The setting: an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of history, sixteen students, one ninety-minute session, a room with movable chairs, index cards, markers, a whiteboard and a projector, no laptops and no phones. The students should encounter the hindsight effect and the narrative pull in their own judgments rather than hear about them first; neither label should appear until the debrief. Pick the historical episode yourself — my candidate is the July Crisis of 1914, since they know it ended in a general European war but cannot recount its steps — or replace it and tell me in a clause why yours is better. It must be an episode whose outcome they already know and whose intermediate sequence they do not.

The distinction I am really after is threefold, and undergraduates reliably collapse it into one: how probable the outcome would have looked to a well-placed contemporary observer beforehand; whether, now that we know it happened, the sequence reads as having had to end that way; and how well the outcome can be explained after the fact. A competent historian holds these apart — she can treat an outcome as thoroughly explicable while denying both that it was foreseeable and that it was inevitable — and I want the activity to make that separation available to the students as something they find, not something I assert at the board. Design the rules so that the three come apart in what the students do.

Two constraints I want you to design around, and improve on if you can. First, no memory test: the obvious version — write a prediction, reveal the outcome, ask what you predicted — measures forgetting as much as anything else, and students who kept their own slip of paper are merely embarrassed. My own idea is that each student writes an anonymous forecast on a card, the cards are swapped before the reveal, and afterwards each student estimates the foresight state of an identified peer rather than recalling their own; I cannot tell whether this isolates reconstruction or just relocates the confound. Second, the narrative half: my draft is to give each pair the same dozen event-cards, ask them to lay out the shortest causal chain that makes the actual outcome look inevitable, then tell them the outcome was in fact the opposite and have them build a second chain from the same cards, on the theory that the ease of the second construction is what does the work. My worry is that this teaches only that stories are cheap, which is not my point.

Also give me, inside the same answer: a minute-by-minute timeline including exactly when and how the outcome is revealed; the debrief, mapping particular moments in the session onto the threefold distinction; what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how I should handle it in the room; and, honestly, which feature of these phenomena the activity cannot reproduce and which I will therefore have to supply in the last five minutes.

Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, in this order: the mechanism with its rules and materials; the timeline; the debrief; the failure modes and their fixes; the limit of the exercise. Each item is two or three sentences. No preamble, no headings other than the numbers, no summary at the end, and mention any mechanic you rejected in half a sentence where it belongs.
