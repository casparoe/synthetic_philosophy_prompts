weird request but I think you might be good at this. I help run a discussion section for an early modern philosophy class and I like to open each week with a short peice of historical fiction that dramatizes the position instead of summarizing it. Next up is Descartes on animals, and I really dont want the usual thing where the dog looks sad and therefore Descartes is refuted.

Here's the shape I have in mind, take it wherever you like. Set it in the 1640s, in a university town in the Dutch Republic. The POV character is a convinced Cartesian in his late twenties — a physician, does comparative anatomy, cuts up live animals without any fuss. Not a villain: for him the beast-machine doctrine is genuinely beautiful, and the animal body is better engineering than anything a clockmaker has built. He also has a dog he's fond of, and that should read as consistent rather than as hypocrisy. As I understand it the claim isn't that animals are junk, it's that there is nothing it is like to be one, while the machine still does everything it looks like it does.

Then one specific observation that won't sit still in the frame. In part five of the Discourse he gives two ways of telling a real human from a machine shaped and acting like one: a machine could never use words or signs to declare its thoughts to us the way even the dullest or craziest person does (I think he adds that people born deaf and mute invent signs of their own), and separately, a machine might match us at some tasks but would always botch others, because reason is one universal instrument that works in whatever circumstances come up, while an organ needs its own particular disposition fitted in advance for each particular job, so no machine could ever contain enough dispositions to get through life the way reason does. Animals fail both, he says.

What I want is an animal that fails the first test clean and passes the second, or looks like it does. My idea: a showman's ape — a Barbary monkey if that's right for the period, I don't care about the zoology — and the trick isn't tool use, it's deception. It steals something, hides it, and when someone who didn't see where it went starts searching, it goes and looks in a different place, or gestures the wrong way, and only fetches the thing once it's alone. I like it because what's being tracked there isn't a stimulus but what the other one does and doesn't know, which changes minute to minute, so disposition-of-organs-fitted-in-advance has nothing to bite on. But theres no language in it. So the two criteria come apart.

I want the story to stay inside that: he has to say something to the showman, or write to his old teacher, or decide what to do with the animal, and whatever he does costs him one of the two tests. No resolution, no deathbed conversion, no narrator telling us what to think, and the ape's behavior has to be real — no reveal that it was trained or faked.

Under 1200 words. Dialogue in period terms — souls, thoughts, organs, dispositions, engines, clocks — nobody says consciousness or qualia. You can give the ape one gratuitous sign, something not called for by anything present, if that sharpens the language test; your call. If I've got Descartes wrong anywhere, say so in a couple of lines after the story and write the story to the corrected version.
