Write me a dialogue, 800–1000 words, between two philosophers of math arguing about Benacerraf's dilemma, but with both of them anchored to the same made-up case instead of floating around in the usual abstract back-and-forth.

The case: two isolated mathematical communities, no contact at all, completely unrelated notations. When their work finally gets translated into ours, the results match — not just the true theorems, but the order they found them in, the flashy false lemma both communities adopt around year 40 and abandon around year 55, and the conjecture both groups feel certain about decades before anybody proves it. Nobody, including them, can say what either community's symbols refer to.

Call the two characters the tracker and the internalist. The tracker says the convergence is too reliable to be a coincidence, so the causal constraint on knowledge is the horn of the dilemma that has to go: both communities are locked onto the same abstract structure. The internalist says no, it's the semantic horn that goes — what makes a claim correct here is something inside the practice (proof, coherence, not letting you derive any new plain physical facts), and the convergence just shows there's basically one route through that terrain.

Make them both actually good at this. Each has to answer the other's real objection rather than restating their view: the tracker has to say what "locked onto" amounts to when you can't causally touch or point at the structure, and why a tracking story predicts fifteen years of shared error instead of immediate success; the internalist has to explain why the *mistakes* line up too, since a story about what follows from the axioms seems to predict nothing at all about which wrong turns will look tempting. No knockout blows. End with the disagreement narrowed to something specific the two of them would need to find out.
