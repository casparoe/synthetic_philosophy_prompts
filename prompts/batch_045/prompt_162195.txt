**Two pragmatisms, and the belief that makes itself true**

Peirce and James both tie truth to something practical, but they locate the practical differently. For Peirce, truth is what inquiry would settle on at its ideal limit: the opinion that would survive indefinitely prolonged investigation by the community of those who investigate, so that truth is fixed by an ideal endpoint and is independent of what anyone actually finds useful now. For James, truth accrues to ideas in the course of their working: an idea is true insofar as believing it lets us assimilate it, act on it successfully, and find it corroborated, so that usefulness is at least the mark of truth and arguably what truth consists in.

The familiar verdict is that James vulgarises Peirce: on James's account a comforting falsehood apparently becomes true, which is intolerable, whereas Peirce's idealisation lets him keep truth independent of our current preferences. I do not want to re-run that dispute, because I think it mislocates the real discontinuity. For ordinary empirical propositions — the cat is on the mat, the sample contains lead — the two accounts barely diverge, since both can treat an idea's working as evidence for its truth rather than as its constitution. The interesting divergence shows up only for what I will call *reflexive* propositions: propositions whose truth depends partly on what people believe about them. The bank will fail next week; the ceasefire will hold; the queue will move quickly; the patient will improve once she is told the treatment is effective; the currency will hold its peg. These are not the same as useful falsehoods. A reflexive belief can be both useful and, precisely through its uptake, truth-making.

That case produces trouble for James. If usefulness is merely evidential for ordinary propositions, it cannot be merely evidential here: the working of the belief is part of the causal history that makes the proposition true. So on a Jamesian account, reflexive truths are in part products of belief. The trouble is that reflexivity is indexical. Whether the bank fails depends on what these depositors believe now, and there is no guarantee that the reflexive beliefs of different communities are jointly consistent. Two isolated communities can each hold a belief about their own bank, each self-verifying, and hence each true in the Jamesian sense, while the corresponding global claim — every bank of this type will fail next Tuesday — is false. Jamesian truth, applied to the reflexive class, therefore seems to fragment into something community-relative, unless it is supplemented by a consistency constraint across all believers. But a requirement that everyone's reflexive beliefs be jointly satisfiable is, in effect, an idealisation over a single community of inquirers: it is Peirce's limit smuggled back in. So James's account is either relativist for the reflexive class or it survives only by covertly borrowing the apparatus it was supposed to replace.

The same test can be run against Peirce, and here I am least confident. Peirce's construction needs inquiry to continue indefinitely, but for reflexive propositions inquiry is itself an intervention: publishing the analysis of whether the bank will fail can make it fail, and publishing the forecast that it will not may prevent its failing. The ideal limit of inquiry is then not well defined, because the endpoint depends on which investigations are actually performed along the way. Peirce's idealisation, it seems, cannot even be stated for the reflexive class without specifying the path of inquiry, and any such specification is itself a choice with truth-making consequences. If that is right, neither pragmatism is at home with reflexive propositions, and what is needed instead is something like a fixed-point treatment: for such propositions, truth is a property of a pair consisting of a belief and an outcome, not of a proposition alone.

The upshot I want to defend is diagnostic rather than destructive. The Peirce–James contrast is best understood as a contrast about which idealisations over communities and over inquiries one is permitted to make, and reflexive propositions are the stress test that exposes the contrast, since for them both the community and the path of inquiry figure in the truth-condition. If that is right, it also matters for practices that traffic almost entirely in reflexive claims — central-bank communication, financial regulation, public-health messaging, election forecasting — where the question of whether to say what you believe is not merely prudential but bears on what is true.
