I'm a PhD student in philosophy finishing a dissertation chapter on the normative force of coherence arguments in decision theory, and before I present it at a work-in-progress session I want an audit of the package of views I have ended up defending. Assume familiarity with the standard Dutch-book and money-pump arguments and with the expected-utility representation theorems; please don't reconstruct them, and please don't cite or refer me to any literature — I want you to work only with the commitments as I state them, since I can already locate the bibliography myself.

Here is where I have landed:

(1) Rational credence is probabilistically coherent, and the argument that actually persuades me is the synchronic sure-loss argument: an agent whose credences violate the probability axioms will, at her own betting prices, accept a package of bets that loses whatever happens.

(2) Asymmetry: no coherence argument of this shape establishes anything about choice. In particular, none of them establishes expected-utility maximization — every attempt I know to make a sure-loss argument bite on preferences has to import an empirical premise about what the agent will later be offered or will later do.

(3) Independence is not a requirement of rationality for a single, one-off choice; the Allais pattern is rationally permissible.

(4) Diachronic money-pump arguments do have force, but conditionally: they criticize an agent only if she foresees the sequence and will in fact be offered it. Call this the contingency premise. In what I call the blind sequence, where the agent is led through the stages without foreseeing the later offers, I want to say there is nothing to criticize.

(5) Completeness is not a requirement of rationality either: under incommensurability it can be permissible to have no preference between two options, and to decline to choose when the choice is unforced.

(6) Internalism: a rationality criticism has to be available to the agent from her own point of view, on the basis of what she can reasonably be expected to know. A criticism whose force depends on how the world happens to treat her is not the right kind of criticism.

(7) Costless delegation is rationally optional. In what I call the escrow world, where an agent may at no cost hand her future choices to a faultless expected-utility maximizer, declining the arrangement is not irrational, and neither is accepting it.

(8) In the one-bookmaker world, where she is visited once by a bookmaker, knows she never will be again, and takes the book, she is criticizable — so the force of the argument in (1) does not come from repetition or from long-run pragmatic success.

I cannot locate the instability myself. Different readers have pointed at different items on the list, which makes me suspect they are reacting to the package rather than to any single claim, and that is what I need checked.

What I want from you: go through these pairwise and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which only look as if they are, ordered by how severe the tension is rather than in the order I listed them. For each genuine tension, say which commitment has to give, what the cheapest revision would be, and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the chapter. For each merely apparent tension, say briefly why it dissolves. If you think there is a further commitment I am relying on that does not appear on the list, name it and say where it is doing work. If, contrary to my readers, you find the set consistent as stated, say so plainly and tell me what I should expect to be accused of anyway. Please write in American English, in prose with as little notation as possible, around 800 to 1,000 words.
