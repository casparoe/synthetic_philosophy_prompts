The question I set was this. Moral worth is often said to require acting for the right reasons, but it is not obvious what that requirement comes to. First, set the Kantian condition — that an action has moral worth only if it is done from duty — against the Arpaly-style alternative that what matters is the agent's being moved by the right-making features of her situation, whether or not she represents them to herself as right-making, and explain which of the two better handles a case in which someone does the right thing from a motive she herself would repudiate. Second, apply your answer to a case I'll call the borrowed-reason case. Ada knows that her own judgment about what matters is unreliable: she is biased, distractible, and bad at seeing what is at stake. So she has arranged her practical life so that on such questions she defers to a friend whose judgment she trusts and who, as it happens, is reliable. He tells her, correctly, that a colleague's project will collapse unless someone gives up the weekend to help, and that this is what matters here. Ada gives up the weekend because he told her to; she neither sees nor cares about the colleague's predicament. Had he told her that nothing was at stake, she would have stayed home. Does Ada's action have moral worth? Answer consistently with what you said in the first part.

Here is one student's answer, transcribed verbatim. Kant's condition is that an action has moral worth only if it is done from duty: the agent must act as she does because the moral law requires it, and not because acting that way happens to suit her. This is the distinction between acting in accordance with duty and acting from duty, and only the latter has worth; an action that conforms to duty because the agent feels like performing it may be useful and may even be reliably produced, but on Kant's view it has none. Indeed, Kant's position appears to be that the more the agent enjoys doing what is right, the less moral worth her action has, which is why he uses the unsympathetic benefactor as the pure instance of the duty motive. That is the point at which the Kantian view looks least plausible, and Bernard Stocker's well-known objection about one thought too many captures it: if an agent helps a friend only after asking herself what morality demands, a thought has entered her deliberation that is foreign to the relationship, so the requirement that she act from duty alienates her from the very features that make her action right. The Arpaly-style alternative avoids this result, because on it what matters is not whether the agent represents her action to herself as morally required but whether she is moved by the right-making features. That is why an agent such as Huck Finn, who helps Jim while believing that he is doing something wrong, can nevertheless act with moral worth: he is responding to Jim's predicament, and that is what moral credit consists in. To state the view more exactly, moral worth consists in the agent's motivation being sensitive to the right-making features: if she would have acted otherwise had those features been absent, she responds to them, and her action has worth. This settles the borrowed-reason case. Ada's action issues from a mechanism that tracks what matters, her friend's judgment is reliable, and she would have stayed home had he said nothing was at stake; so her action is sensitive to the right-making feature in the way required, and it therefore has moral worth. It also helps that she has deliberately handed her deliberation over to someone who sees better than she does: she thereby acts for the reasons he acts for, and worth is transmitted along the chain of deference. The opposite verdict would force us to say that an agent loses moral credit simply by compensating for her own defective judgment, which is absurd.
