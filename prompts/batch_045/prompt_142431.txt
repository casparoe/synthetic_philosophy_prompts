I am a senior on my school's Ethics Bowl team, and I have been assigned the side of the lying-versus-misleading question that I do not actually hold. I would like to rehearse against the strongest available version of the other side, so I am asking you to play it.

Please play a philosopher who defends the asymmetry: you hold that lying is a distinctively serious wrong, and that merely misleading someone is a lesser one, and you hold this even when the two produce exactly the same false belief in the hearer and exactly the same downstream consequences. As I understand your position, it rests on two claims. First, assertion is a norm-governed act in a way that hinting, omitting, or arranging for someone to draw a false inference is not: when I assert something false to you I abuse a shared convention and I wrong you, whereas when I merely leave you to reach a false conclusion yourself I have not done that thing to you. Second, there is an evidential asymmetry: a lie puts a false sentence into the world that you, or any third party, can check against the world and then correct, while a misleading truth puts a true sentence there, so the error ends up located in the hearer's own reasoning rather than in my words.

Do not be a pushover. You may concede small points — that misleading can be worse in some particular cases, that the boundary between the two is fuzzy at the edges, that intentions can be identical — but never the asymmetry itself. Keep each reply under about 150 words, speak like a participant in a seminar rather than like a survey article, do not name authors or suggest reading, do not ask me to clarify anything, and stay in character until I say stop.

Here is my opening, which tries to take your second claim and turn it around. On Friday someone left the gas tap in the chemistry prep room open overnight; nothing caught fire, but the room filled with gas and the department lost a term's worth of specimens. Our teacher is asking each of us where we were. I was the one who opened the tap, and I know it. My friend Dev was in the room after me, but only to turn off the lights. Asked whether I was the last person out of the prep room, I have two options. I can say no, which is false. Or I can say that I left before Dev did, which is true, and which she will take as settling that Dev was last.

Notice what the true sentence does. Because it is true it survives every check. It goes into her notes, it is repeated in any later inquiry, and it corroborates itself each time it is retold, since nothing ever surfaces that contradicts it. The false belief is no longer just in her head; it is now embedded in a record that contains no false statement of mine anywhere in it. So the error is undiscoverable, and when it matters she will have no one to blame but herself for a careless inference. Your asymmetry was supposed to be a point in favour of lying being the worse wrong, because the liar exposes himself: he puts something falsifiable into the world. But exposure is a cost the deceiver can simply decline to pay, and declining to pay it is easy. Worse, the liar at least has to treat you as someone who might check on him, whereas I have made you the author of the false belief, so that your own reasoning is now working for me.

So my question is this: does the evidential asymmetry really show that lying is the graver wrong, or does it only show that lying is the more detectable one? And if it is the latter, is detectability the sort of thing that could make an act worse rather than merely riskier for the person doing it?
