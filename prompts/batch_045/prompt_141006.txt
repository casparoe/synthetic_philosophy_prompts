I write novels, and the book I have been working on for three years contains a device I introduced for reasons of craft alone and have since come to suspect is a version of an argument in metaphysics. That suspicion is what brings me to you.

At the centre of the book is a chapter that is, by design, undetectable. In an earlier draft the whole scene — the room, the two people in it, the light, the ash on the sleeve — sat three feet to the east of where it sits now. Every distance between the things in the scene is unchanged, and every distance from those things to the narrator's vantage is unchanged too, because the vantage moves with them; no character could notice, and not one sentence of the book needs altering. My editor says the two drafts are the same novel written twice. I have never been able to agree: I know which draft I wrote, and I resist the claim that there is nothing to choose between them, since an unused stage direction has changed.

I gather this is close to the old quarrel about whether space is a thing or only a system of relations: Newton's bucket, with its water that climbs the pail whether or not anything else is around to be climbed relative to; Leibniz's reply, that a universe displaced three feet east with everything in it displaced equally is not a second possibility but the same possibility described twice. I went looking for how this is settled and found that it is not. In general relativity the argument seems to have sharpened rather than closed: take a model of the universe, choose some matter-free region, and smoothly redistribute the metric and the matter fields inside it while leaving everything outside as it was. The result is a second mathematically distinct model of the same theory, agreeing with the first everywhere outside the region and in everything observable. If spacetime points are individuals with their own identity, then, runs the argument, this is a genuine alternative history that the theory cannot rule out — the theory goes indeterministic on the cheap. The standard reply is that such models are not alternatives at all but two descriptions of one physical situation, the surplus being gauge. And the reply to that is that a substantivalist who grants it has become a relationist who has kept the vocabulary — while a substantivalist who refuses to grant it seems to be committed to facts no observation could ever reach. Nobody appears to have converted anybody in either direction for decades.

My hypothesis is that the quarrel was never really about space at all. It looks to me like a quarrel about whether a difference that no possible observation could reveal is a difference, and therefore about what it takes for something to be real — with the physics supplying the occasion rather than the substance. Possibly it is also two questions under one name: what our best theories are committed to, and what there is.

Am I right? Please tell me: what kind of impasse this is — verbal, substantive, two questions under one heading, or a buried technical question about how surplus structure is identified; whether my three-feet-east chapter is a genuine analogue or a false one; and what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, an observation, or nothing at all. Lead with a single sentence saying whether my hypothesis is right, roughly right, or wrong, then give the diagnosis; the whole answer in under 600 words.
