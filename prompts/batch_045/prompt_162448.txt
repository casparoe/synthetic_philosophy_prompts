I have been defending a behavioral criterion for attributing minds, and a friend ran an argument past me whose conclusion I cannot accept but whose steps I cannot break.

(1) Parity. The only evidence I have ever had for attributing mental states to anyone other than myself is behavioral: what they do, say, and would say under questioning. So if two systems differ in no respect that could show up in behavior, I have no ground for attributing mentality to one and denying it to the other.

(2) Robustness. The behavior that does the evidential work cannot be the actual stream of outputs, which is a vanishingly small sample, but the whole counterfactual profile: how the system would respond to any probe — any question, any novel situation, any intervention on its internals.

(3) Finitude. Any system I could encounter or build has a finite lifetime, finitely many distinguishable inputs at finite resolution, and finitely many distinguishable outputs. So the set of probe-sequences anyone could ever actually run on it is finite.

(4) Simulation. For any finite set of probe-sequences and any target system, there is a device that matches the target exactly on every sequence in that set and has, by construction, no mental states: a lookup table or precomputed response tree keyed to the inputs received so far, which emits the right output without representing, registering, or understanding anything.

(5) By (3) and (4), for every candidate system there is a mindless duplicate that matches it under every probe that could possibly be run. By (1) and (2) I have no ground for telling them apart. So I have no ground for attributing mentality to anything — including other human beings.

(6) But I do have ground for that: I have my own case, and other humans are not relevantly different from me. So (1) and (2) cannot both stand.

I cannot find where it breaks. Steps (3) and (4) look like a quantifier trick — a table can be built for any finite bound on probe length, not for all probes at once — but once I allow probes of unbounded length, an infinite tree matches just as well and the argument returns. And if I retreat to (2) and insist that only actually executed behavior counts, I get the opposite absurdity: a device that produces a person's exact behavioral profile for forty years and then, no matter what anyone does next, falls apart, counts as conscious for precisely as long as nobody asks it anything outside its table.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would rather keep (1) than (2): the parity thought is the whole reason a test like Turing's has ever been taken as evidence of mind, and I would rather keep the claim that other people have minds than any particular theory of what makes it true. So tell me what I am paying if I give up the counterfactual reading of behavior, and whether there is some fourth option that keeps both.
