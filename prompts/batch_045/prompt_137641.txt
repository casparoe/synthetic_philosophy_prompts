Two friends of mine got into it at dinner a few weeks ago and I havent stopped thinking about it. The setup is a real thing that happened at a museum my cousin works at, a midsize place in the Midwest.

Since 1981 they've had a small Dutch winter scene hanging in their Dutch gallery — skaters below a windmill, that kind of thing — attributed to a minor Haarlem painter named Cornelis de Haas. It's their one sort-of-famous object; it's on the tote bags. Last year a conservator was testing the varnish and found a pigment in the sky that wasn't manufactured until the 19th century. So: painted much later. Then a researcher turned up the actual story in a regional archive. It was painted in 1911 by an art teacher, Willemtje Broos, as a reconstruction — a de Haas is listed in a 1742 estate inventory and then disappears, and she painted what she thought the lost picture must have looked like, to show her students. She signed and dated the back and wrote something like "after de Haas, reconstructed." Nobody was trying to cheat anybody. Then in the 1960s a dealer had it relined, which covered the back, and sold it as the real thing.

So there's no forger. It's an honestly made homage that got laundered into an original by a third party forty years later. The museum has to decide what to do, and the director's proposed compromise is: same gallery, same frame, new wall label telling the whole story, plus a bit in the audio guide.

Mareike says: keep it exactly where it is, honestly labeled. For forty years people stood in front of it and something happened to them, and nothing about a pigment test changes what happened in those people's heads. If you want to say those experiences were worthless, you're saying the value was never in the looking at all — it was in possessing a fact. And the "connection to the actual historical act" isn't something you can have anyway: half of old paintings have workshop hands in them, experts get fooled constantly, and when they get fooled it's never the eye that got fooled. Causal history is a proxy for a quality you can only ever reach through the surface, so once you know the surface is the same, insisting on the proxy is just status-seeking.

Tomás says: take it out of the Dutch gallery, or at minimum out of that frame and off the tote bags. An art museum isn't in the business of supplying pleasant experiences; it's in the business of putting you in contact with actual past acts of making, under the right description. And the label doesn't fix it — placement, frame, wall colour, the bench facing it, the gift shop downstairs are all part of how a museum speaks, and a paragraph of text can't undo all that. Also, he says the past experiences were defective in a way Mareike is denying. People were having an experience they'd have described as standing in front of a 1650s Haarlem picture, that description was false, and it mattered to them — that's part of why they were moved. It's like being moved by a memoir that turns out to be invented: the feeling was real, the thing it was a response to wasn't there.

Mareike: two things. First, your memoir analogy backfires if the memoir is any good — nobody says a novel stops being moving because it's fiction. What people object to in a fake memoir is that it was passed off as true, which is a complaint about honesty, not about the value of the object, and honesty here is fully solved by the label. Second, here's the case that breaks the causal-history story: suppose the lost de Haas turns up tomorrow in an attic, perfect condition, and it's visually indistinguishable from Broos's version, and the museum only has wall space for one. On your view the attic one wins and it isn't close. But if you actually hang them side by side and nobody can tell, the only thing that differs is paperwork. I say sell the attic one, keep the Broos, fix the roof with the money.

Tomás: "nobody can tell" is a rigged setup, because once you know which is which you don't see the same thing — which is my entire point. And your hedge fund move is a tell. You're treating them as interchangeable but you still want to keep the one that's been on the wall for forty years, which is itself a historical property. You're not indifferent to causal history, you just want to choose which bits count. The attic painting isn't valuable because of the paperwork, it's valuable because it's a trace of a particular person's hand at a particular time, and traces are the kind of thing we have reasons to care about that we don't have for duplicates. A reconstruction giving you the same buzz doesn't put it in the same relation to the past.

Mareike, finally: this is the one that actually matters. The Broos has had effects. A generation of painters in that city saw it as a de Haas and painted differently because of it, and there's a real case that it's the most influential single object in twentieth-century regional painting there. Those effects happened under a false description, but they're real history, and on your own traces view they're traces too — of Broos, and of everyone who misread her. And there's an ugly consequence for you: if you pull it out of the gallery and stick it in a show about fakes and reconstructions, you destroy the very thing that made it matter, which is that it was taken for an original. Your purism about categories is itself a kind of destruction.

Tomás: I'll concede the influence is real and worth a show. That's an argument for a show about influence with the Broos in it, not for continuing to show it as a de Haas in a Dutch room. You're conflating "this object has a history" with "this object belongs where everyone thought it belonged."
