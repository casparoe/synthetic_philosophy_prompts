You are a professional moral philosopher who specializes in collective action problems, causal overdetermination, and the ethics of individual contributions to aggregate harms, and who is also a patient reader of literary fiction. I am drafting a novel and need you to think slowly and carefully about one speech in it; before you commit to a line of argument, please brainstorm out loud — list at least four or five distinct strategies June could use, each with a one-line note on its principal vulnerability, then tell me briefly which two you think are strongest and why — and only afterward develop the argument in full. Please write the whole response in American English, including spelling and idiom.

The novel is set in the invented cattle town of Marrow Flats, at the dry end of the Kestrel Basin, where sixty family ranches graze land that drains into the reservoir the town drinks from; every late summer the reservoir turns over with an algal bloom, and the county is voting on the Kestrel Basin Compact, under which each ranch would retire a rotating share of its herd for three seasons. My narrator's aunt, June Halloway, fifty-eight, third-generation rancher and president of the High Desert Grazing Cooperative, stands up at the grange hall to argue for the Compact against her own neighbors — and I want her argument to be the strongest real thing, not a slogan. What makes the scene hard is what she must concede. Her brother-in-law has already said from the floor: "Your hundred head are nothing next to sixty ranches, nobody's watching you, and the buyer's truck leaves full either way — take your cattle off the sled and he fills the slot out of the Wylie place, and the Wylies breed to replace them besides, so nothing you do or don't do changes a single pound of what reaches that reservoir." June grants all of it: (a) her own contribution is far below the level at which the bloom is triggered and never tips it; (b) no other rancher's conduct tracks hers — there is no coordination, no conditional cooperation, no "if I go first they'll follow" story available; (c) her cattle are entirely fungible, so that even a permanent retirement of her herd would be offset elsewhere and the total would be unchanged, what an offsets person would call a failure of additionality, and what a philosopher would call the problem of the replaceable contributor; and (d) there is a real chance of leakage, since the calves she withholds may simply be finished in the next county, where the feedlot runoff is worse. Please do not let her wriggle out by appealing to tiny probabilities of being the one who tips the threshold — grant for the scene that her act makes no difference to outcomes and that no one's behavior depends on it — though you may note in passing whether that concession is even coherent in a system with continuous thresholds.

So: give me the strongest available argument, consistent with all four concessions, that what June and her neighbors are doing is nonetheless something they owe the town, that her own continuing to run a full herd would be a genuine moral failing and not merely an empty gesture, and that participating in the Compact is not just expressive or self-regarding. I want you to work mainly from the stipulated structural features of the fictional case and from general moral reasoning: what is left to say once difference-making is off the table, whether complicity or membership in a jointly harmful practice does the work, whether a fair-share or distributive framing survives when the shares are replaceable, whether there is anything in the notion of actual causation or of being part of the group that produces the outcome that does not collapse back into difference-making, and how she answers the two taunts I want in the room — that she still drives a diesel truck and flies to see her grandchildren, so her selectivity is mere hypocrisy, and that the whole thing is feel-good symbolism that leaves the reservoir exactly as it is. Please argue in your own words rather than by citing or naming papers and authors, since I cannot follow up on references and want the reasoning itself, and please keep the developed argument to roughly one thousand words, closing with a distillation of about one hundred and fifty words written as the lines June actually speaks from the floor of the grange hall — plain, unpretentious, the kind of thing a rancher who has thought about it for twenty years could say out loud without sounding like a lecture.
