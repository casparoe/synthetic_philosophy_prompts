So in my modal metaphysics seminar we've been going in circles on counterpart theory vs. transworld identity — Kripke-style worries about whether some other-worldly thing being similar to me is really *me* possibly being otherwise, Lewis-style replies that identity across worlds was never what the quantifiers were doing, and after a term nobody has moved an inch. My working hypothesis is that the label covers two independent axes nobody keeps apart: one about what relation makes de re modal claims true (numerical identity vs. some similarity/representation relation), and a separate one about what worlds are (concrete pluralism vs. actualist or ersatz constructions). On that reading a lot of the famous arguments only hit a package deal nobody has to accept — you can be an actualist counterpart theorist, or hold transworld identity over abstract worlds — and what looks like a standoff is partly people attacking different conjunctions. I also suspect some of the friction isn't metaphysical at all: in counterpart theory whether "I could have been a poached egg" comes out true depends on how respects of similarity get weighed and how much the context lets importance override similarity, so part of the disagreement might really be about context-sensitivity in modal discourse.

Give me a proper diagnosis rather than a survey or a verdict: is this substantive, verbal (dissolved by some translation scheme between the two frameworks), two questions under one name, or clashing bedrock intuitions about what de re modality requires? Argue for it using what each side actually commits itself to. Three things I care most about: (a) whether the intertranslatability point — that any counterpart structure can be recoded with a derived transworld "identity" relation and vice versa — actually defuses the dispute or just relocates it into a fight about which recoding is metaphysically apt; (b) whether the hard cases do asymmetric work — things with no counterpart anywhere, "I might never have existed at all", the actual world containing something no other world resembles even slightly; and (c) whether claims about how many things could have existed, and haecceitistic differences between worlds that are qualitatively identical, even get the same truth conditions on the two views.
