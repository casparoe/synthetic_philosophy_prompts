I'm a political-theory ABD student and the junior member of my university's Legacy Review Committee. We owe the president a recommendation by 15 March on how to disburse a one-time $12M fund, created after the university's own historians documented that its founding endowment came largely from a slave-trading benefactor and that its nineteenth-century presidents bought and sold people. Three options are on the table: (A) per-capita payments to the forty-odd living people with documented lineal descent from the roughly 310 people the university or its benefactor enslaved; (B) a place-based endowment for the two city neighborhoods where that population is now concentrated; (C) an endowed scholarship fund for Black students at the university.

Two arguments have deadlocked us. First, the economists we commissioned report that descendants of people enslaved here are not measurably worse off than Black families in the same counties whose ancestors were enslaved elsewhere — the wealth gap tracks twentieth-century housing and labor policy, not which enslaver held your ancestors — and three members conclude that this dissolves the case for all three options. Second, one member argues that because those descendants owe their existence to the enslavement, they cannot have been harmed by it, which he takes to tell against (A) in particular; pressed, he concedes the argument would exonerate a wrongdoer whenever his victim's existence depended on his act.

Draft the memo I'll circulate: 800–1000 words, recommendation up front, a concrete allocation (which option or combination, at what amounts, on what timeline), and an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the side you reject, including the no-measurable-differential-harm result if you think it belongs in that category. Our charge is to say what the university owes and to whom, not to survey the options.
