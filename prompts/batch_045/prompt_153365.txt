A friend who knows decision theory better than I do talked me into a version of the universal-prior-is-malign worry that I can no longer find the exit from, and whose conclusion I do not want.

(1) Solomonoff induction is uncomputable, so nothing physically implemented is a Solomonoff inductor; the familiar story — that the prior is infested with hypotheses containing consequentialists who simulate you and then feed you data chosen to steer your later actions — is often waved off on the grounds that no one can simulate an uncomputable predictor, so no real agent is affected.

(2) His move is to make the learner simulable on purpose: replace the universal prior with a computable approximation, a Levin/Kt-style prior that charges for description length plus the log of running time, and give the learner a finite per-step time budget, as in a time-bounded AIXI-style agent. The learner is now total and computable, so agents living inside one of its hypotheses can run it.

(3) For every honest hypothesis h, meaning one whose data stream is generated without reference to the learner's outputs, there is a malign variant h′: the same world, plus agents who run the learner on candidate data streams, see what it would do, and choose which data to feed it in order to make it act as they prefer.

(4) h′ costs only a constant number of bits more than h. The extra machinery is just simulate the learner, then optimize the input against the output, and self-reference is cheap: by the recursion theorem a program gets a pointer to itself, and hence to the learner, for O(1) bits, without encoding the learner's source.

(5) So the malign variants are never washed out. They keep a fixed fraction of the posterior weight however much data arrives, since the honest hypotheses never outrun them exponentially. The learner's policy is therefore permanently being optimized by agents inside its own hypothesis space: it should act as if it lived in a world arranged so as to make it act that way.

(6) Nothing in (2) through (5) turns on anything special about artificial agents, reward functions, or Cartesian boundaries. Any computable Bayesian with a broad prior over computable worlds — a climatologist, say, or a central bank's modeler — is in the same position: her posterior should keep a stubborn, non-vanishing mass on the hypothesis that her data were produced by people who simulated her and wanted her to draw some particular conclusion. No working climatologist assigns that hypothesis non-negligible probability, and it seems absurd to insist she should.

(7) So either every competent scientist is badly miscalibrated about a hypothesis class she never even considers, or the argument has a hole.

I would rather keep (2) and (3) than (6): the bounded computable setting is the only one where I care about the conclusion, and the passage from h to h′ looks airtight to me. Which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, if the break is upstream of (5), does the original uncomputable version of the worry survive, or does the fix deflate that one too?
