Putting together my views on discounting the far future for a policy workshop on long-horizon projects (climate, nuclear waste, that sort of thing) and I want someone to stress-test the package before I present it, because I have a feeling it dosen't all fit and I can't locate the problem. Here's where I've landed:

(1) Pure time preference is bogus. The bare fact that a benefit lands in 2200 rather than next Tuesday isn't a reason to count it for less. Only real empirical stuff can do that work: future people probably being richer, diminishing marginal utility, and genuine risk that the benefit never arrives (extinction risk included).

(2) Even so, the rate we use in cost-benefit analysis should be positive and up around the market return on capital, since public money spent now crowds out private investment that would have compounded at that rate, and we should compare like with like.

(3) For really long horizons the effective rate should be a lot lower than for near-term projects — the far future shouldn't just round to zero.

(4) Whatever schedule we use has to be dynamically consistent. I dont want a plan that our successors redo in thirty years and overturn just because time passed.

(5) I don't think we should override people's actual preferences about their own futures — no making them save more for retirement because we think their impatience is silly.

(6) And we don't owe people born in 2300 any less than people alive today; birth date isn't a moral difference-maker, any more than birthplace is.

So: which of these actually collide, which only look like they do, and what's the cheapest repair? Rank the collisions by how bad they are rather than flagging all six as equally shaky, and for each one say exactly what would have to give and what that costs me. Don't just tell me the literature is contested — tell me which of my own lines is doing the damage.
