I am a novelist rather than a philosopher, and I am trying to get one chapter right. My narrator is a field linguist in her late fifties who has spent two decades documenting a language with fewer than forty surviving speakers, and the chapter turns on an ordinary afternoon of elicitation in which she realizes she cannot tell what one of their most common nouns applies to — and that nothing available to her in the field will settle it. I have read a little around the familiar "gavagai" set-up and the proxy-function maneuver that goes with it, and I know well enough not to let my narrator deliver a lecture; but I would like her reasoning to be the real article rather than a strawman. So I have written down my own verdicts on the cases she might face, and I would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

First, the plain field case: speakers utter "gavagai" in the presence of rabbits and withhold it otherwise, and there is no way to ask about parts or stages. I think there is no fact of the matter between "rabbit," "undetached rabbit part," and "rabbit-stage," and that the competing manuals are genuinely on a par. Second, the same word in a community that has never encountered taxidermy but is shown a mounted rabbit for the first time and accepts the word for it, accepts it for a cleaned skeleton, refuses it for a severed foot and for a rabbit-fur hat, and refuses it for a rabbit-shaped cloud; here I think the symmetry is broken and "the bounded rabbit-body, dead or alive" is simply the right translation. Third, counting: if they count as we do — one rabbit, one gavagai — I feel that settles nothing, but if, asked how many gavagai are in front of a single rabbit, they had said "two," I am convinced that would have been decisive evidence for a part- or stage-reading; I know these two verdicts sit badly together and I cannot say why the divergence should carry weight when the agreement carries none. Fourth, a different word of theirs, call it "glug," which turns up in a generalization they rely on to predict what will appear where; the predictions succeed, and I think "glug" means whatever makes their generalization come out true, with no inscrutability left. Fifth, a community that also applies the word to the heat-shimmer above the burrows; here I do not feel indeterminacy at all but a positive finding — the extension really is gerrymandered, and the honest translation is a coinage or a disjunction, not any word of ours. Sixth, a fluent bilingual speaker of the language who, asked in English whether the word covers the whole animal or also undetached parts, answers without hesitation that it is the whole animal; I find this settles the matter, though I cannot say why her testimony should succeed where my narrator's elicitations fail, since her answer had to be interpreted too. Seventh, an utterance the community makes only at burials: I think here we cannot even determine the force — assertion, prayer, or something performative — so that translation is indeterminate at the level of the whole sentence, whereas for an ordinary observation cry I think the sentence is perfectly well translated even when the reference of the noun inside it is not.

My own tentative proposal is that a term's application is as determinate as the community's dispositions to project it to novel cases and the generalizations it figures in jointly make it, and that where these run out there is nothing to be right about. Please test that against my seven cases and repair it where it fails; tell me which of my verdicts a defensible principle has to overturn, whether the price of saving all of them is a principle too baroque to be worth having, and what the resulting account predicts for three cases I have not listed — our own word "rabbit" as reconstructed from our behavior by a linguist from outside, their word for "same" and the reidentification practices it enters into, and a machine trained on an enormous corpus of their speech that produces fluent and predictively successful English renderings of it. My friend, who does documentary linguistics for a living, says the entire puzzle is an armchair fantasy with no bearing on how translation is actually done, and I would like to know whether that is a serious position or a misunderstanding of what is being claimed. Finally: tell me how confident you are in the principle you settle on, single out the one claim in your answer you are least sure of, and mark clearly the places where the honest answer is that the question is genuinely unsettled as opposed to the places where there is a broad consensus I have simply got wrong.
