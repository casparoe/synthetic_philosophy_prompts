I write fiction, and I am revising a novel for which I need a piece of in-world ephemera. I would be grateful if you could draft it for me.

The novel is set some decades from now, in a country that has made an unusual administrative decision. A thesis that currently lives in graduate seminars has become official practice: the state, its schools, its health service and its courts now hold that the propositional attitudes — beliefs, desires, and everything built on them — are posits of a failed theory, and that ordinary talk of them is to be handled the way we now handle talk of humours and elemental balances, as a deep and infectious explanatory mistake that survives mainly because it is woven into the grammar of everyday speech. In its place the state recommends a redescriptive vocabulary borrowed from neuroscience: people are not said to believe or want things, but to occupy this or that region of a high-dimensional activation space, to be moving along a trajectory through it, to be more or less well calibrated to their environment. There is a licensed profession of people who do the redescribing, and a compliance apparatus for stamping attitudinal language out of official documents.

My protagonist is a novelist — I am afraid it is that kind of novel — whose manuscript has failed a descriptive-adequacy review at her publisher's: too many sentences of the form "she believed that…", "he wanted to…". She is sent a house-style manual and told to revise. I want the reader to meet that manual. So what I need from you is an excerpt from it: a run of consecutive entries from a document I am calling *The Plain Neural Style Guide*, issued by an Office of Descriptive Standards.

Format: seven or eight entries, roughly 900 to 1,100 words in total, each with a headword, a short statement of the banned usage, the approved replacement, and a pair of example sentences (noncompliant / compliant), followed by a short prefatory note from the Office explaining why the edition exists. The voice is the voice of a style guide: flat, patient, faintly disappointed in you, and certain that any difficulty you are having is a difficulty with your old habits rather than with the new vocabulary. Deadpan throughout — the reader should be able to tell what is being sent up without anyone winking.

The comedy has to come from the substance of the view, so I would like the entries to walk into the following trouble spots. (1) An entry whose approved vocabulary has to mint a single unanalysable term for an entire configuration of a thought, and then another, and another, for every recombination of its parts, with the guide treating the resulting lexical explosion as a matter of merely expanding the dictionary. (2) An entry that fails to remove the word it is removing — the preference stated in terms of what the writer "means", "intends" or "believes" the sentence to convey — and fails without noticing. (3) An entry on "because" and "therefore", which are to be replaced by something merely sequential; I want to see what this does to a sentence about someone who acted as she did because she took something to be right, although it was not — the gap between acting for a bad reason and being causally pushed is precisely what the new idiom cannot mark, and the guide should sail past it. (4) Somewhere the guide should concede, in a boastful tone, that the outlawed vocabulary is still the only thing anyone can actually use to anticipate what a person will do on Tuesday, and reclassify that as a convenience — a rule of thumb that gets the weather right without understanding it. (5) An entry on the first person, on "it seems to me", on pain and other felt qualities, where the advice becomes noticeably vaguer and the confidence sounds borrowed. (6) If there is room, an entry on promising or apologising, where the reformulation quietly stops being a promise or an apology. (7) If there is room for a final entry, one on "as if": the suggestion that people might be treated as if they had beliefs for predictive purposes without anything's being true of them; let the guide dispose of this in a single line that does not quite touch it.

Two constraints of fairness, since I want this to be funny to a philosopher of mind and not only to a general reader. First, do not make the guide deny that people are in real inner states, or imply that the science is bogus: the view being administered is that the states are perfectly real and the folk theory about them is false, and the piece should keep that straight. Second, invent nothing that is not already a feature of the position — exaggerate a real feature rather than supplying one. No quoted material from real texts; everything inside the document should be your invention.

Finally, a short note after the excerpt, no more than 200 words, addressed to me. Take a definite position in it: of the objections your style guide smuggles in, commit to the one you think actually bites, say in one or two sentences why it bites and what it shows about the view, and tell me where — if anywhere — the piece leans on a contested reading of eliminativism or takes a liberty for the sake of a joke. I can always soften a committed claim later; what I do not want is a survey of candidate objections with the choice left to me.
