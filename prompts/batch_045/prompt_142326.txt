I am writing a chapter on whether a hereditary head of state is compatible with democratic equality, and I keep encountering one line used as a conversation-stopper: the sovereign reigns but does not govern.

It surfaced most recently in a seminar as the hinge of a dilemma. My interlocutor's argument was roughly this: either the monarch's office carries no real power, in which case it is ceremony and cannot be a democratic defect worth arguing about; or it carries real power, in which case the country is not really a constitutional monarchy at all and the interesting question is a different one, about elected presidencies. So there is supposedly no middle case in which the office matters politically while remaining constitutionally proper — and, correspondingly, no distinctive question about abolishing it. On the first horn, abolition is like deleting a logo; on the second, it is ordinary constitutional reform. Nothing is at stake that the debate about republicanism does not already cover.

I am convinced the slogan is doing the work here, and that it is being asked to carry more weight than it can bear, but I cannot yet say precisely where it buckles. I would like a disambiguation I can work from.

In my own notes I have been separating three things that the phrase "does not govern" might be denying, and I want your menu to refine and correct the separation rather than accept it:

1. Not exercising a governing power (the sovereign never actually decides).
2. Not possessing one (there is nothing there to exercise).
3. Not exerting what I have been calling the gravitational effect of a power that is possessed but left unexercised: ministers who never put a certain proposal to the sovereign, or never advise a certain appointment, because they know it would be refused — so the refusal never happens and leaves no trace in any record. The 1975 Australian case, where a representative of the Crown dismissed a sitting government, is the sort of thing that makes me think the distinction between (2) and (3) is real and not merely academic.

I would also like you to consider distinctions of a different kind, which may cut across the first three:

- A descriptive reading (the sovereign in fact abstains) versus a normative one (the sovereign ought to abstain, and would act unconstitutionally by not doing so). The slogan's grammar does not mark the difference, and it seems to me that a claim about what an office-holder must not do is doing very different argumentative work from a claim about what they do.
- A legal-institutional reading (no power is vested in the office) versus a conventional one (power is vested there but is exercisable only on advice, by settled practice). My understanding is that these come apart most sharply in the United Kingdom, where a great deal of executive authority is formally the Crown's while being exercised by ministers, and come apart differently in Japan, where the Emperor is constitutionally designated a symbol of the state and of the unity of the people with no powers of government, and differently again in the Scandinavian kingdoms, where the monarch still formally assents to legislation and presides over the Council of State. I would be grateful if you did not take my sketch of these arrangements as authoritative, and instead said what the readings would amount to if the details came out one way rather than another.
- A reading on which "governing" is being contrasted not with "symbolizing" but with something like "making government possible": supplying the continuity across changes of government, the locus of allegiance, the authority to commission someone to form a government after an indecisive election, the public validation that incumbents can be seen to borrow. Call this the legitimating role if you like a label. The question I care about is whether exercising it is a way of governing.
- The variant phrasing "reigns but does not rule," and whether substituting "rule" for "govern" shifts which reading is most natural.

What I want from you is a numbered menu of the distinct precise claims the line could be making, ordered roughly from the thinnest to the most demanding, and for each one:

(a) the claim stated precisely enough to be argued with;
(b) whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, a substantive but doubtful one, or straightforwardly false;
(c) which of the three families of case above it is most likely true of, and what its truth or falsity there would depend on;
(d) whether it is the reading someone who repeats the line in a seminar is most likely to have in mind;
(e) most importantly, whether it would actually support my interlocutor's dilemma — and if so, only its first horn, only its second, or the claim that there is no middle case.

Then, in a short closing section: given the most charitable reading available, does the dilemma survive, and if it does not, what is the middle case it is blind to? I would also value one sentence I could actually say aloud in a seminar in reply, and a note on what sort of evidence — not which sources, just what kind of fact — would settle whether the gravitational effect in (3) is operating in a given country, given that its success consists in leaving no documentary trace.
