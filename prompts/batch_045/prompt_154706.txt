hey — I'm writing something on Ulysses contracts (the individual kind, the constitutional kind, and the awkward in-between ones like locked advance directives, independent central banks, mandatory minimums) and I keep getting annoyed that every account I read is secretly one favourite case generalised. So before I pick a view I want the spec.

Purpose, so the standard isn't up in the air: I want a theory good enough to rule on actual devices — is this particular binding arrangement legitimate or coercive — not just to sort them into boxes.

Write me the desiderata: what a theory of when a precommitment legitimately binds the person or body that later wants out has to deliver. The cases it has to get right, the distinctions it isn't allowed to collapse, the results (decision-theoretic, legal, whatever) it can't contradict — each with the case or consideration behind it, and each marked either non-negotiable or a preference of one camp.

Some cases I want forced onto the list, because I think they're where the views actually come apart and they're not the ones everyone uses:

- the Tantalus device: not removing the option, not taxing it, but making it disgusting — aversive conditioning, or a constitutional clause that doesn't forbid a policy but forces it to be re-passed under a procedure designed to be humiliating. Does *how* the device operates (deletion, price, transformed taste, delegation to a veto-holder) matter to legitimacy, or only how likely it is to hold?
- the Cassandra case: the later self who wants out is the better-informed one. The Sirens were giving good navigational advice all along, or the prognosis was wrong. Precommitment made under uncertainty about whether your own future dissent will be impairment or correction.
- the duplicate key: the device can be exploited by a third party who now knows you can't say no — the lender who extends credit knowing you've sworn never to renegotiate, or the faction that benefits because the entrenchment clause only binds one side. Does induced-vulnerability-to-others count against a device, and whose job is it to fix that?
- the successor problem for institutions: the majority that entrenches is not the majority that's bound, and not even a subset of it in any useful sense. What's the collective analogue of "same agent", and is a theory that just says the analogy is broken still a theory?
- the cheap-exit gradient: escape costs of zero, of something modest, of none at all. Several views look commited to escape-cost being irrelevant, which I think is a reductio — but say if you think it isn't.
- second-order precommitment: binding yourself against binding yourself (cooling-off periods, bans on waiving a right, rules that forbid future unanimous consent). Any theory that can't go one level up should say why not.

Then the part I actually care about: which pair or triple of desiderata do you think can't be jointly satisfied? My suspicion is that "must reliably survive the moment of temptation", "must not bind an agent who has genuinely changed her mind or been corrected by evidence", and "must not rest on a sharp line between those two states" are jointly unsatisfiable — any two force you to drop the third — and that this is the real content of the topic rather than a defect of particular theories. Tell me if I've got that wrong or if the tension is somewhere else. And which single desideratum excludes the most otherwise-attractive theories?
