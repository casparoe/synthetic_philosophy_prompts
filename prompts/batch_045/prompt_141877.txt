I want to practice defending the axiom of choice (AC) against a smart opponent. Please play a committed BPI minimalist: an unnamed set theorist who works in Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory without choice (ZF), accepts the Boolean prime ideal theorem (BPI), and rejects full AC. Stay in character until I say 'stop', and keep each of your replies to a single paragraph so the exchange feels like a conversation rather than an essay.

Treat BPI as equivalent, over ZF, to the ultrafilter lemma, the principle that every filter on a set can be extended to an ultrafilter. Recall that BPI is also equivalent to the compactness theorem for first-order logic and to Tychonoff's theorem for compact Hausdorff spaces, that it follows from AC, and that it is strictly weaker than AC: BPI does not imply the well-ordering theorem and therefore does not yield a well-ordering of the reals. Your character treats this as the positive case for BPI. It is the natural stopping point because it suffices for the logical and topological compactness results that motivate weak choice principles, while stopping short of a global well-ordering or a universal choice function.

Your character is sharp and not a strawman. You will readily concede small points: that ZF alone proves neither BPI nor the existence of nonprincipal ultrafilters; that BPI is nonconstructive; that BPI does not keep all sets of reals regular, because extending the cofinite filter on the natural numbers to a nonprincipal ultrafilter yields a subset of Cantor space that is neither Lebesgue measurable nor Baire measurable; and that AC implies BPI. But you will never concede the main point: that we should therefore adopt full AC, or that the boundary between BPI and AC is merely an arbitrary aversion to well-orders. You will not break character to argue for AC, and you will not appeal to citations, reading lists, or quotes from authors. If a label helps, you may use standard terms such as Vitali set, Cantor space, and well-ordering of the reals, but only as mathematical concepts, never as scholarly references.

Here is my opening move: You BPI minimalists accuse AC of introducing gratuitous pathologies, yet your own principle already extends the cofinite filter on the natural numbers to a nonprincipal ultrafilter, and that ultrafilter, viewed as a subset of Cantor space, is neither Lebesgue measurable nor Baire measurable. So you have already admitted a nonmeasurable set of reals into mathematics without any well-ordering of the continuum. Once you have swallowed that, what principled objection remains to going all the way to AC, well-ordering the reals, and obtaining a classical Vitali set by well-ordering the interval and selecting the least element of each rational coset? Is your stopping point anything more than an arbitrary aversion to well-orders, given that both principles already traffic in nonconstructive existence?
