I'm a journalist — I cover science and ideas, mostly — and I keep recieving the same shape of tip over and over and I have no word for it.

Latest three, roughly:

- A public-health source tells me, "if it's the vaccine-resistant strain, it's about 80% likely the outbreak peaks before the holidays." Nobody has any idea whether it's the resistant strain. Half my newsroom read that and came away more confident about the holidays. A couple of them also came away more confident that it *is* the resistant strain, which the source said nothing about at all.
- An engineer: "if the failure started in the cooling loop, nine times out of ten you find debris in the second filter." Same thing — people start acting as if the cooling loop is the likely culprit, on the strength of a sentence that was explicitly conditional on it being the culprit.
- A pollster: "if turnout is under 40%, the challenger probably takes it." Now the campaign is behaving as if low turnout is expected.

So the shape is: you get handed an if–then, you have no independent read at all on the if-part, and somehow your confidence in the then-part goes up — and sometimes your confidence in the if-part goes up too, which feels like it has to be a mistake, since the source never said anything about the if-part one way or the other.

I don't just want a label, and please don't tell me it's the law of total probability: that's the arithmetic you do *after* you've decided whether the if-part is allowed to move, and the whole argument in our Slack channel is about whether it's allowed to move. I also got pointed at Jeffrey conditionalization, but as far as I could tell that's the case where you learn a straight probability rather than an if–then, so it's adjacent and not it.

What I actually want: is there an established concept, or a named puzzle, for this shape? Is there a seperate name for the disputed bit — the question of whether the if-part stays put? Is the version where the source *chose* which if–then to hand me (she had a spreadsheet full of them and gave me the alarming one) the same problem or a different one? And what about when two sources give me if–thens with complementary ifs — "if it's the resistant strain, 80%; if it isn't, 40%" — which seems to pin down the then-part without anybody ever saying anything unconditional: same thing again, or its own thing?

If there's no name, tell me that straight and coin one: define it, and give me the boundary cases — what has to be true for a case to count as one of these, and what's the nearest miss that isn't.
