I spent most of last Sunday arguing with a friend who works on Quaternary stratigraphy about when the Anthropocene begins, and we got exactly nowhere — partly, I now think, because we were answering different questions. Her view: the only legitimate question is whether there is a globally synchronous, physically datable horizon in the rock record, and since the relevant stratigraphic bodies voted down the formal epoch proposal a couple of years ago while conceding the term would keep circulating as a loose descriptor of human impact, the dating question is for her closed by that procedure. My view: the interesting question is normative and historical rather than stratigraphic, and I have been pushing a criterion I call the ratchet — the boundary should be the earliest moment after which the Earth system could no longer return to its previous trajectory without deliberate human intervention, so that the date marks a change in what later generations inherit rather than a spike in some measured quantity. She replied with a counterfactual I could not shake: if a large volcanic episode had produced the same physical signature, with no one to blame and no intention anywhere, would my date move? I said no, and that this is precisely why blame is not doing the work for me — irreversibility is. She said my ratchet cannot be located in a sediment core at all, so it is not a boundary but an editorial opinion. That is where we left it, and it is the stalemate I want an instrument to sort.

The concrete job: I am on a small team producing a permanent gallery label and an accompanying touchscreen element on the Anthropocene for a regional natural history museum, and I want the interactive piece to be a decision tree rather than a quiz with a score. It should take a visitor from a starting commitment to a named position through six to eight branch questions, with between ten and fourteen leaves. Every node has to be phrased as a question that a reasonably informed fifteen-year-old visitor could answer without already knowing which position it leads to — no node may ask whether we should listen to science or to politics, and none may ask whether human impact is real, since everyone in the room grants that. At least two nodes should be concrete hypotheticals rather than abstract principles: the volcanic-counterfactual case my friend used on me is one candidate, and another might be a case in which the same marker appears in high-latitude deposits three centuries before it appears in the tropics, or in which a marker is unmistakable in sediment but invisible in ice. The leaves need to include, at minimum: a formal mid-century stratigraphic marker tied to radionuclide fallout; an early-seventeenth-century onset keyed to a dip in atmospheric carbon dioxide and the reforestation that followed the collapse of Indigenous populations in the Americas; an industrial-revolution onset keyed to steam and coal; a deep-onset view that puts the beginning with agriculture or rice cultivation several thousand years back; a diachronous or regionally staggered view with no single date; a view on which the Anthropocene is an event or process rather than a time unit and so has no business being dated as an epoch; views that rename the subject rather than move the date, whether the culprit is a mode of production, a plantation-and-slavery complex, fire, or plastic; a view on which nothing epochal has happened and this is just the Holocene with people in it; my ratchet view; and a view on which the authority to settle this belongs to affected publics, legislatures, or historians rather than to stratigraphers at all.

What I want back is the tree and the reasoning behind it, not prose about the debate. Please give me the branch questions in the order you would ask them, and tell me which one earns the first slot by doing the most sorting work and why. Each leaf should carry its name, one sentence of statement, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment — the thing a holder of that view would rather not say out loud on a gallery wall. Where the logical space of answer-combinations contains cells nobody occupies, mark them and say briefly why the vacancy is there: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unoccupied for want of anyone having bothered. Where two named leaves are genuinely indistinguishable by your questions, flag the collision rather than inventing a distinction to hide it, and say what extra question would separate them if the museum were willing to spend a screen on it. Finally, tell me which of my own commitments the tree exposes as less settled than I think: I would rather find out now than in front of the advisory board, which includes my friend, a curator of human history who will object that any date we print implicitly assigns blame, and two people who just want to know what to put on the wall. Please keep it free of citations, author names, and reading suggestions — I have the literature, and what I lack is a structure I can hand to a designer.
