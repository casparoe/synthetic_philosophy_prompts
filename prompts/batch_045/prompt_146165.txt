I am in the final year of a philosophy and economics degree, taking a seminar on rational choice, and I have been asked to design and lead one fifty-minute session of the first-year course that the seminar is attached to. There will be about twenty-four students, mostly philosophy and PPE. They have had a single lecture on expected utility: they can compute an expected value and have seen what a utility function is for, but they have met neither the independence axiom nor Allais. The session is mine to design, subject to a departmental rule that I may not offer cash, though I have a twenty-pound budget for materials.

What I want the room to feel, before anyone names anything, is that one's own preference can shift between two rounds of a choice while nothing about the prizes or the probabilities has changed — only the way the chance event is described. My plan is therefore to run the Allais pairs twice. In the first round, the two pairs are presented in the ordinary way — a guaranteed million against a gamble that pays five million with ten per cent, one million with eighty-nine per cent, and nothing with one per cent; and then the corresponding pair in which that eighty-nine per cent branch is replaced by nothing at all — with choices recorded anonymously, and no mini-lecture beforehand. In the second round, the very same pairs are re-presented as a table over a finer partition of the chance event: the branch carrying the common consequence is written out as two separately labelled rows, say 0.44 and 0.45 marked by differently coloured cards, adding to the same figure as before. Someone in my seminar mentioned having seen data suggesting that choices move toward the pattern expected utility requires when the branch is split in this way. I have no idea whether that is right, and I would rather build a session that is instructive either way than one that collapses if the effect fails to show up in a room of twenty-four.

So this is what I am asking for. Give me a runnable design: the rules, the materials, a payoff scheme that makes the choices bite without money or with something close to it, the wording I should use for each round, and a minute-by-minute timeline for the fifty minutes. Then give me a debrief that maps specific moments in the room onto specific parts of the idea — what independence requires, why the two pairs stand or fall together, and what the axiom is doing inside the representation result, which I would prefer to make visible rather than assert.

The debrief also has to separate three diagnoses, and I want to know what in the activity, if anything, discriminates between them: that the students are genuinely rejecting independence as a normative requirement; that the outcome space was described too coarsely, because what they care about includes things such as the anticipation or the disappointment of what they would otherwise have won, so that expected utility may survive once the outcomes are described more richly; and that the entire pattern is an artefact of how the event was carved up and corresponds to no stable preference at all. For each, tell me whether the second round is evidence, and in which direction.

Finally, be candid about what will predictably go wrong when I run this with twenty-four people who have read nothing, and about which part of the idea no activity of this kind can deliver, so that I know what I will have to say in my own voice.
