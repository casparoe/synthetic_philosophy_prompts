A podcast on behavioural design that I listen to a lot (the host is an economist, and usually careful) devoted an episode to weakness of will, and gave this as the state of the art:

> Rational choice says you should discount the future exponentially: a fixed percentage off for every unit of delay, which is just compounding at a constant rate. Real people don't. We discount hyperbolically — the rate we apply to the next chunk of delay is much steeper than the rate we apply to later chunks — and that makes our preferences dynamically inconsistent, so they flip as time passes. You book the larger later reward, then when the smaller sooner one is finally on offer you switch. That is literally what akrasia is: the ancient problem of weakness of will finally has a formal model. It explains the gym membership taken out in January and abandoned by March, the one-episode promise that becomes five, the tax return deferred until the deadline. And because a preference reversal just is an inconsistency, the hyperbolic discounter is straightforwardly irrational: there is no coherent point of view from which both of her preferences are correct. So the only remedy is to bind your future self — delete the app in advance, automate the transfer — because your future self's preferences are simply not to be trusted. The model behavioural economists actually use, the beta-delta one, with a fixed impatience penalty on anything that isn't immediate plus ordinary exponential discounting, captures all of this and predicts every anomaly we know about in intertemporal choice: why we're more impatient about £20 than about £2,000, why we discount gains faster than losses, and why we'll wait longer to speed up a delivery we were already owed. Which is the real moral: willpower is a myth, and what we call self-control is just self-binding successfully arranged in advance.

I trust this host on economics, but the claim that the whole philosophical problem has been "solved" by a discount function set off my alarm bells, and I can't locate the problem myself.

Two things I've heard pull me in opposite directions. A friend who read some philosophy says the reversal stuff isn't akrasia at all: akrasia is acting against your own better judgement *at the moment you act*, whereas a shifting discount rate just means that the later self genuinely, all things considered, prefers the smaller sooner — there's no conflict left over to explain, only a change of mind. If that's right, the podcast's central identification looks like a conflation. But then I can't see why its remedy would still be good advice, and it obviously is: people really do solve these problems by removing the option in advance.

And there's a version of the case I can't get past, which nobody seems to address. If steep short-run discounting is what makes my future self's preferences untrustworthy, why does my *present* self get to be the one whose preferences bind? The same argument seems to make my preferences a minute from now the ones I should defer to, in which case I should put off arranging the precommitment too — and then again, and again. There must be an asymmetry somewhere, but I don't know whether it comes from the model or has to be imported into it.
