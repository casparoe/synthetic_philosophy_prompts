You are a research strategist with deep expertise in alignment theory, game theory, and formal epistemology, and I want you to think carefully before answering — spend a few paragraphs up front laying out candidate framings of the problem space and explicitly rejecting the ones you think are wrong, then commit to an ordering and defend it.

Here is the situation. I sit on the program committee of a small philanthropic fund that has to decide, this winter, whether debate-style scalable oversight deserves to be one of three pillars of its next portfolio. (Quick gloss, so we agree on the object: two agents are assigned fixed opposing answers to a question, they take turns making short statements, a weaker judge — a human, or a model we are trying to improve — picks the winner, and training rewards only winning, not honesty.) The budget we control is roughly six to eight researcher-years over two years, and the constraint that matters is that our grantees will have no frontier-scale training access: they can run experiments with open-weight models and do theory, but they cannot settle questions empirically at the scale where the interesting failure modes are thought to appear. So the question I want answered is not "is debate a good idea" but: given that budget and that constraint, which four or five open questions should this money actually buy progress on, and in what order, and why?

To make this concrete, here are the candidate directions currently on our whiteboard, some of which I suspect are overrated. (1) A taxonomy of obfuscation: separating *logical* obfuscation (an invalid step buried in a long derivation) from *empirical* obfuscation (a false premise buried in a chain of individually plausible, individually checkable claims) from *pragmatic* obfuscation (argument shaped to exploit judge heuristics rather than judge reasoning) — and then asking which type is actually the binding constraint, and whether the standard remedies (cross-examination, bounded proof length, a "verification tax" requiring each claim to carry a pointer to a machine-checkable artifact that the judge samples at random) are aimed at the right one. (2) The precedent-library question: real deployment isn't one-shot, it's a long stream, and adjudicated sub-questions could be cached and cited in later debates; does an accumulating body of precedent make deception cheaper (a falsehood spread across a chain of citations, each audited long ago by a weaker judge) or more expensive (the liar must now stay consistent with a growing public record)? (3) The steelman-swap variant: rather than having each debater defend its own assigned answer, require each to construct the strongest possible case for the *opponent's* answer, with roles assigned at random after the arguments are submitted, so that the winner is whoever produced the better case for a position they didn't choose — does this remove the structural asymmetry in which the dishonest side may pick the single most convincing available false argument while the honest side must locate the specific flaw? (4) The bootstrapping question: when the judge is itself the system whose reliability we are trying to improve, what do the equilibria look like — convergence, oscillation, collapse — and is there a formal version of the worry that you cannot bootstrap oversight of a reasoner from that same reasoner? (5) The task-structure question: what fraction of alignment-relevant oversight tasks have a "hard to produce, easy to check" profile (does this patch contain a backdoor? is this claimed result reproducible?) versus being irreducibly contestable (is this policy argument sound?), and can we characterize the split in terms of the existence of short certificates rather than by intuition? (6) The consolidation question: is debate genuinely a distinct mechanism from other scalable-oversight schemes — recursive reward modeling, forecasting or prediction tournaments, setups where a model both proposes and critiques — or is it a special case of one of them, and does the answer change what we fund?

Please give me a dependency-aware prioritization of roughly four questions (yours, not necessarily mine — feel free to merge, replace, or cut any of the six, and to add ones I haven't listed). For each: what makes it upstream of the others, how tractable it is for people doing theory plus small-scale experiments rather than frontier empirical work, how neglected it is relative to its importance, and what concrete result — a theorem, a clean counterexample in a toy formal model, an experiment on small models — would in eighteen months move it up or down the list. I also want the trade-offs made explicit: name at least one question you are recommending *against* funding despite its genuine importance, and say why it fails on one of your criteria. Finally, if you had to put sixty percent of the fund behind a single question on an inside view, which would it be, and what would have to be true for that bet to pay off? Do not give me a literature review or a survey of who has argued what — I want a defended agenda with your own reasoning exposed, and flag honestly wherever your ordering depends on empirical facts we currently have no way to obtain.
