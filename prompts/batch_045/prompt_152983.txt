Here are three features of existing practice as I understand them — correct me if I have any of them wrong:

(a) In Anglo-American law, falsity is an element and truth is a defense. A statement that turns out true cannot be defamatory, fraudulent, or perjurious, however reckless the speaker was about whether it was true. A witness who swears to something that is in fact true, while believing it false and intending to deceive the court, commits no perjury; a defendant who guesses wildly and happens to guess right has defrauded no one.

(b) Yet culpability tracks the speaker's epistemic position. Defamation liability and damages turn on negligence or on knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard; fraud requires knowledge or reckless disregard; perjury requires asserting what one does not believe true. The scrupulous speaker of a falsehood and the careless speaker of a falsehood are treated very differently, and the careless speaker of a truth is treated as having done nothing wrong at all.

(c) Research-integrity proceedings run the other way. Fabrication and falsification are sanctionable whether or not the claim they were used to support is true — even where independent work later confirms it — while honest error is not misconduct, however false and damaging the published claim turns out to be.

The law appears to locate the wrong in what was said; the integrity code appears to locate it in how the speaker arrived at it. What norm of assertion does each practice presuppose: truth, belief, knowledge, reasonable belief? Is the legal pattern a single coherent norm or a hybrid that merely looks coherent? Does the knowledge norm predict that the law is wrong in both directions at once — the reckless truth-teller goes free while the scrupulous falsity-teller is exposed to liability — and if so, is that a reductio of the knowledge norm or of the practice?

An objection I cannot shake: perhaps none of these institutions regulates assertion at all. Tort and criminal law compensate and punish reliance and harm; they track falsity because false beliefs cause harm, not because assertion has a constitutive norm. If that is right, the reconstruction is a category mistake and a knowledge norm would have nothing to say to legal design. Take that possibility seriously rather than setting it aside.

Which brings me to a live case. A professional body I advise is drafting a conduct code for expert witnesses under which the sanctionable act is asserting without knowledge: a witness who asserts what she merely suspects, and who turns out to be right, is sanctionable; a witness who asserts what she knows, and who turns out wrong, is not. A board member says this is incoherent — no one was harmed by the lucky guess, hearers got the truth, and the whole point of regulating assertion is that people act on what they hear.

So: (i) is that objection any good, and what does it presuppose about what assertion is for? (ii) What do the three practices strictly presuppose, as opposed to merely fit well with? (iii) Where are they underdetermined — could a truth norm plus a separate culpability doctrine reproduce all three features? (iv) What would concretely have to change if we replaced the implicit norm with a knowledge norm: would we start sanctioning lucky guesses under oath, would defamation lose the truth defense?
