For an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of language I need one take-home exam question on Grice (90 minutes, one of three questions, 20 marks) that cannot be answered by rehearsing the Cooperative Principle and the maxims, or by walking the reader through the usual "some, and indeed not all" scalar example. The crux I want to isolate is whether a conversational implicature must be a species of speaker meaning — whether an inference has to be attributable to the speaker's reflexive communicative intentions in order to count as implicated at all — or whether the standard Gricean diagnostics (calculability, non-detachability, cancellability) can be jointly satisfied by an inference whose actual source is not the speaker's intentions.

Build the question around the following case, or a tidied version of it. A truth-filtering intermediary — call it the Verifier — sits between a speaker and a hearer. It has a fixed inventory of sentences and reliable knowledge of which of them are true. When the speaker's utterance is true, the Verifier retransmits it unchanged; when it is false, the Verifier suppresses it and transmits instead the strongest true sentence in the inventory that is logically weaker than what the speaker said. The hearer knows this protocol but is never told whether a substitution has occurred. A speaker, addressing a hearer through the Verifier, says "All of the guests have left." In fact only some have, so what the hearer receives is "Some of the guests have left." The hearer thereupon infers that not all of the guests have left. That inference is calculable from the design of the channel; it is insensitive to which weaker formulation had been used; and it is wholly unmoved by anything the speaker says by way of cancellation, since the speaker meant, and would cheerfully insist on, the stronger claim.

Students should be pushed to do three things in sequence: first, work the three diagnostics one by one on this inference and say which way each cuts, then give a verdict on whether it is an implicature; second, consider a variant in which the speaker, before transmission, adds "whatever you receive, infer nothing beyond its literal content," and say what the fate of that attempted cancellation shows about cancellability as a test; third, say what follows for Grice's programme of analysing speaker meaning in terms of intention — in particular whether the case forces a third category alongside "said" and "implicated," whether it is better read as evidence that generalised conversational implicatures are not speaker meaning, or whether it should simply be ruled out as an implicature because the inference tracks the mechanism's design rather than any speaker's communicative intention.

Assume students have read only Grice's "Logic and Conversation" plus lectures covering what is said versus what is implicated, the particularised/generalised distinction, and the three diagnostics. The question must be answerable without any secondary literature, and it should be framed so that there is no safe answer: whichever verdict a student gives should earn top marks only if they also state what their verdict forces them to concede to the other side. A paper that stops at "the speaker didn't mean it, so there is no implicature here" should be capped at a B.

What I need from you: the polished question exactly as it would appear on the paper, including any brief stage-setting the students will need and the mark allocation; a model answer of roughly 700 words that commits to one verdict while naming, in a sentence, the strongest objection the opposing verdict can raise; and a marking scheme out of 20 organised around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on how strong candidates typically go wrong on that point. Add a short closing note distinguishing an A answer from a B answer in terms of the specific moves each must contain, and flag one optional further move — something involving the role of the Cooperative Principle as a presumption about speakers rather than about utterances, or the circularity worry about analysing meaning in terms of intentions — that would justify full marks even if one of the five points is missing.
