I'm on our hospital's drugs formulary committee — got roped onto it last spring, and my dad is on one of the drugs we're arguing about, so I've been thinking about this more than is healthy. Next month I've got twenty minutes to argue that we should fund Drug A (expensive, helps maybe 40 patients a year, large effect each) over Drug B (cheap, helps around 600, small effect each), and I want to make the case with a veil-of-ignorance argument instead of a cost-per-QALY argument.

Sketch of what I'm going to say: run the original position locally rather than for society as a whole. I've been calling it the catchment veil. The parties know they'll be a random resident of our catchment area for the rest of their life; they know the area's deprivation profile and disease incidence; they know which broad health trajectory they're on (roughly: "I'll need hospital treatment for a chronic condition in my sixties") but not which condition, not which postcode, and not whether they'll end up the patient, the carer, or the taxpayer footing the bill. Behind that veil they choose a funding rule, and I claim they'd choose something like: maximize the health floor of the worst-off group, subject to a constraint that nobody else's prospects get pushed down by more than some threshold — leximin-lite, if you want a name for it. That rule picks A, because A's recipients are the people whose floor is lowest, whereas a QALY calculation with a standard equity weight picks B.

The room: two oncologists, a geriatrician, a palliative care physician, two health economists who work on distributional cost-effectiveness, our finance director, and a lay patient representative. Maybe one of the economists and possibly the patient rep have read some Rawls; nobody there is a political philosopher. The economists care about opportunity cost, thresholds, and equity weights, and think of justice talk as a roundabout way of picking a weighting. The clinicians care about the duty to the patient in front of them and about professional guidelines. The finance director cares that the budget is fixed. The patient rep will want to know who gets to call her "the worst off".

Can you write me the Q&A prep sheet? What I want is the objections I'll actually get from that exact room, in roughly the order they'll come and roughly ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that person would phrase it — the economist's version of a worry and the clinician's version are different objections and I need both. For each: a reply I can deliver in about sixty seconds, and one line on where that reply is softest. Tell me what I should concede in the first two slides, before anyone has a chance to ask it. And include the question I'm hoping nobody asks; I have a feeling it's something like "the worst-off person in this catchment isn't any of the patients in front of you at all — housing, lead paint, debt — so why is a drugs budget where you do this?", and I'd much rather have an answer ready than improvise.

Keep the whole thing under 800 words. No citations or reading suggestions — I just need what to say out loud.
