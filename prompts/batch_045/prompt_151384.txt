Not sure this is the right place for this, but I've been chewing on it for weeks and I'd rather ask than keep going in circles.

I do a lot of night sailing, more than is sensible, and I read a bit of formal epistemology on the side for fun, which is where this comes from.

Say I'm trying to work out whether a light in the fog is a channel buoy or a trawler. The fog means no cue ever makes me certain of anything; every cue just nudges my confidence one way or the other. Now suppose two nudges arrive at what feels like the same moment: the light looks a bit too steady to be a boat, and there's a sound that might be a diesel or might be the buoy's bell. I really can't say which one I took in first. Here's the thing — when I do the calculation the way the books tell you to for this kind of nudgy evidence, I get a different answer depending on which nudge I feed in first, and the gap isn't rounding error; it's big enough to change whether I alter course. If both cues had been crisp, the order obviously wouldn't matter: I'd just take them together. So the order-sensitivity seems to appear precisely because the evidence is fuzzy.

Poking at toy versions, I think I've found the shape of it: when the two cues are independent of each other given what I already believed, the order stops mattering and both routes land in the same place. The more correlated they are — and in my case they're correlated because the same fog degrades both — the wider the gap between the two orderings. So there looks like there's a clean boundary somewhere near independence, and I'd like to know whether that's really the exact boundary or just a sufficient condition that happens to cover my examples.

There's a mirror-image case too, which might be the same thing under a different description or might be separate. I glance at the same light twice through the same fog. Feeding the calculation once and feeding it twice with the same input gives me literally the same number — two looks buy me nothing. Intuitively two glances should beat one. But I can see the reply coming: maybe I'm supposed to feed in a *different* number the second time, and the method just doesn't say where that number comes from, which is its own sort of complaint.

I did stumble on the phrase conditioning on uncertain evidence while looking around, but everything I found presents it as the *solution* to fuzzy evidence, not as having this problem, so I don't think that's the name of what I'm after.

So: does the order-sensitivity have an established name, and does the repetition-doing-nothing thing, and are they one phenomenon or two? I don't want a bare label — I want the carving. Specifically: how this differs from ordinary conditioning on two crisp pieces of evidence, where order plainly can't matter; how it differs from the psychological order effects people talk about in judgment and decision-making, which I take to be about human bias rather than about the arithmetic; whether independence really is the boundary or whether there are other special cases where the order drops out; whether to think of it as a defect in the update rule or as a genuine feature of fuzzy evidence that we were wrong to expect to be order-free; and, if there's no established term for either half, what you'd call it, how you'd define it, and which cases fall just outside it.
