I'm writing a piece on why institutional metrics decay, and in interview after interview I hear the same move: the analogy to quantum measurement. The claim is that a performance metric is like an observation on a physical system — Heisenberg's lesson being that observing a system disturbs it, and that precision about one quantity is paid for in ignorance of another — so a school district graded on test scores, a hospital graded on waiting times, a lab graded on a benchmark, will end up corrupting exactly what the number was meant to track. Speakers lean on it to license something strong: that this is a fact about measurement as such rather than a remediable design flaw, so the rational posture is to measure less, to discount any quantitative target, and to treat every metric as eventually self-defeating.

Please audit it. I want the correspondence table — what plays the system, the observable, the conjugate quantity, the disturbance — and then the disanalogies, separated into the ones that bite on that conclusion and the ones that are cosmetic. Two I would like you to press hard on. First: quantum disturbance is fixed by physics and is not something the measured system does because it anticipates being measured, whereas specification gaming is done by agents who model the metric and optimize against it; the size of the distortion should then be a function of incentives, information, and optimization pressure rather than a constant, and it should respond to how the metric is designed. Second: the failure modes are not all the same shape. A proxy can come apart from its target because we started selecting on the extreme tail of the range in which the old correlation held, or because acting on the proxy changed the causal structure that generated the correlation, or because the graded party reshaped its behavior to hit the number. Only some of those are about the target reacting to measurement, and presumably only some are cured by measuring less.

Where does that leave the conclusion the analogy is being used to support? I have also heard control theory offered as a better analogy — a governor or a thermostat, where reading and system are coupled on purpose and what goes wrong is gain and lag rather than the reading itself — and if that is better, does it change the practical advice? Please answer in American English.
