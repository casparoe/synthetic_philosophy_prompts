I'm writing a short discussion paper for a graduate seminar on formal epistemology, and I've got as far as noticing that my own reactions to the two standard paradoxes of aggregated belief don't add up to anything I can state. The setups are the familiar ones: a fair lottery with a thousand tickets and exactly one winner, where I am perfectly happy to believe of each ticket that it will lose and perfectly unwilling to believe that they all will; and a historian who has written two hundred assertions into a book, believes each of them, and adds in the preface that there are surely errors somewhere. I take the second to be not merely permissible but exemplary — the author who omits the preface strikes me as the worse epistemic citizen — while in the lottery I take no attitude toward the conjunction at all and feel no pressure to have one. The trouble is that nearly everything I want to say in one case has a counterpart in the other that I want to answer differently. Below are my verdicts, as honestly as I can report them; what I want from you is a principle.

1. Lottery. I believe of ticket 47 that it loses; likewise for all thousand. I don't believe they all lose, and I don't think I'm criticizable for failing to. I'll bet against ticket 47 at slightly better than the fair odds, and against each of the others too; I won't sell you all thousand bets bundled, since that's a certain loss. But the bundling refusal doesn't, I think, show that I don't really believe each — it shows something about prudence, or so I want to say. 2. Preface. I believe each of the two hundred claims, I believe the hedge, I believe publishing the hedge is a virtue, and I'll act on any one of the claims — cite it, build an argument on it — without ceremony. 3. Small numbers. Same book, but with three claims plus the hedge: fine, though I'm noticeably less comfortable. Two claims, where the second one *is* the hedge ("at least one claim in this book is false"): not fine — believing both is inconsistent, and I withdraw my license to hedge. So the number of claims matters, and I can't say why or where the cutoff falls. 4. Located versus unlocated doubt. A referee believes each of twelve claims in a paper, believes that at least one is probably wrong, and recommends acceptance: fine. A referee who comes to believe that claim 7 specifically is wrong must say so and must not recommend acceptance unqualified. Asked "which one?", "I don't know" is an acceptable answer. But a referee who has localized the error only to the effect that it lies somewhere in chapter 3, which contains four claims, must flag chapter 3 — even though she still can't say which claim. 5. Fragmentation. A company knows that roughly two hundred of fifty thousand records were accessed in a breach. Sending fifty thousand separate letters each saying "your record was not accessed": not fine. Publishing one page that lists all fifty thousand names with "not accessed" beside each and the aggregate estimate visible at the top: fine, or much closer to fine. Sending the letters with the base rate stated in each: fine. Same content, different presentation, different verdict — which makes me suspect my verdict here isn't about belief at all. 6. Statistical versus evidence-specific support. In the lottery nothing distinguishes ticket 47 from ticket 812; my belief rests entirely on the base rate. In the preface my belief in claim 12 rests on evidence specific to claim 12 — I checked it, I remember the source. And I treat them differently: I'll build on claim 12, I won't build anything on "ticket 47 will lose," and I'd be embarrassed to cite the latter as a reason for anything. Is that principled, or a prejudice? If it is principled, then the lottery and the preface aren't the same animal, and the habit of treating them as one family is a mistake. 7. Conjunction and thresholds. Two independent claims, each about ninety percent credible: I believe the conjunction. Five: I think I still believe it. Ten, at about thirty-five percent: no. So there's a cutoff somewhere, but I feel no sharp one and my confidence doesn't feel numerical. Relatedly: I believe p, I believe that if p then q, and q might be only fifty percent likely. If I've actually drawn the inference I want to say I believe q; if it hasn't occurred to me, I want to say I'm under no requirement. Is "closure only for inferences actually drawn" a coherent position, and does it reach the conjunction cases or only the one-step ones?

The best I've managed on my own is this: call a doubt *unlocated* when my evidence gives me no more reason to suspect one member of a set than any other. Then: (i) I may believe each member of a set while also believing that some member is false, exactly when my doubt is unlocated; (ii) unlocated doubt licenses acting on any single member but never on the conjunction; (iii) it licenses asserting members only to audiences who can also see the aggregate; (iv) where support is purely statistical I may believe each member but may not use any one of them as a premise, whereas where support is evidence-specific I may do both. This is built to fit 1, 2, 4 and 5, so I don't trust it. It says nothing about 3 or 7, and clause (iii) turns one of my verdicts into a matter of audience etiquette rather than of rationality, which I take as a sign that I've really got two principles stapled together.

So: give me a principle, or a short list of candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case you haven't been shown. For each clause, tell me which of my cases is doing the motivating work. Then tell me what it says about cases I haven't listed — a radiologist who reads two hundred scans a day and knows her own error rate, a judge who signs forty warrants a week while knowing that some small fraction of them are not properly supported, a labeller of ten thousand images who is ninety-nine percent accurate and ships the dataset with a documented error rate — and tell me plainly which of my verdicts above your principle cannot accommodate and which one you'd overturn. If my tentative principle is salvageable, repair it; if it's confused, say where. Please write in American English, and please don't give me a bibliography or a reading list — I have plenty of reading, and what I want is your own analysis.
