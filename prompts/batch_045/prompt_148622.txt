Hi — I run a six-week film-and-history discussion group in the back room of our library in town (about twelve regulars, terrible coffee, one retired naval officer who derails everything), and the theme this term is 'lost causes and how countries decide to remember them'. We've done two sessions already: the Confederate 'Lost Cause' after 1865, and the Kosovo field in Serbian national memory, where a battle lost in 1389 gets treated as a moral possession inherited by people born six centuries later. Next Tuesday it's Dunkirk, and I'm showing Nolan's 2017 film rather than the 1958 one, because everyone's seen the old one and I want them off balance. What I need from you is a reading of the film through this whole lost-cause lens — not a review, not a history lesson, not a list of things to google afterwards (we've got ninety minutes, so no pointers to reading please), and in British English.

Here's the frame we've been building over the first two weeks, which I'd like you to test rather than just agree with. We ended up with three types: inheritance myths, where a defeated cause is handed down as a duty to the living (the Confederacy, Kosovo); vindication myths, where the defeat is redeemed by what came later, so the loss turns out to have been the price of something; and — my own coinage — deliverance myths, where the loss is quietly recast as a rescue, so nothing was actually lost and nothing therefore needs vindicating. I think Dunkirk is the pure case of the third type, and that's what makes it odd on our list: it's the only item where the thing commemorated isn't a cause anyone wanted to win (nobody now thinks Britain was fighting for something that lost), and isn't even really a defeat — over three hundred thousand men got off, the operation worked, and the only people who'd have called it a catastrophe were the ones left on the beach. My hypothesis is that the myth has to keep the defeat in view precisely in order to keep the achievement legible: if you say it plainly, it was a successful evacuation after a lost campaign, and there's nothing sacred there at all. Someone in the group also brought up Renan's old point in his 1882 lecture that shared grief is worth more to a nation than shared victories, because grief imposes duties — and I can't work out whether that explains the Dunkirk case or is in tension with it, since Dunkirk is a grief that everyone agrees was worth having.

The other thing I want you to take seriously is the film's form, since that's what we'll actually be watching. Nolan gives us three intercut time-scales — a week on the beach, a day on the little boats, an hour in the air — almost no dialogue, no backstories, no scenes with generals or politicians, and an enemy that's basically never shown: you hear the planes, you see bullet holes in a trawler, and apart from a few distant figures at the very end the other side is an impersonal force, more like weather. (Churchill telling the Commons that wars aren't won by evacuations gets read out of a newspaper at the end, and that's about as close to politics as the film gets; I don't think anyone on screen ever says 'spirit'.) My suspicion is that this abstraction is itself the myth-making mechanism, and I've been calling it myth by subtraction: if you strip an event of causes, of blame and of who was actually shooting at whom, what's left is endurance and small decencies, which is exactly the raw material a national myth needs. But I can also see the opposite reading, and I want you to push on it, because the film keeps showing us the machinery: Gibson is a Frenchman who has stolen a dead British soldier's identity to get a place on a boat and then drowns entangled in a chain; George, the teenage hand, dies of an accident in a scuffle below decks and Peter immediately goes to the local paper and gets him turned into a hero on the front page; Tommy and Alex get on a train braced to be spat at and recieve a hero's welcome instead; Commander Bolton stays behind for the French. So: is the film quietly exposing how a defeat gets laundered into a moral achievement, or is it doing the laundering and congratulating itself for being too cool to say so? Which scenes would you have the group stop on — I'm thinking the trawler, George's newspaper, Farrier burning his plane and being taken prisoner — where does the reading stop being fair to the film, and does the film end up adding anything to the argument about national myth that the philosophy on its own doesn't get?
