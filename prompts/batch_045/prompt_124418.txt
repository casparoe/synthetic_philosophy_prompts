I teach a graduate seminar on self-knowledge and the first person, and we are about to spend a week on the butterfly passage that closes the second chapter of the Zhuangzi — the one in which Zhuang Zhou dreams he is a butterfly, wakes, and does not know whether he is Zhuang Zhou who dreamt he was a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming he is Zhuang Zhou, and which ends on a line English translations render in terms of things transforming into one another. My colleague in East Asian philosophy has more or less convinced me that the standard Anglophone treatment gets it wrong: the passage is read as a Cartesian dream argument in exotic dress, resting on the indistinguishability of dreaming and waking, when the text supplies no such premise and the point may concern the self's not being a fixed thing rather than the possibility of doubt. I believe her. What I lack is a case that makes the difference visible, because every example I try either slides back into the Cartesian argument or becomes so ethereal that nothing any longer seems at stake. One of my students added a third option I keep chewing on, which I will call the custodial reading: the question the passage raises is not what am I, nor is there a fact of the matter, but which of these two lives am I answerable for — a question about the scope of practical accountability rather than about metaphysics or knowledge.

So I want a case built to a specification. Constraints: one paragraph, ordinary contemporary setting, no brains in vats, demons, drugs, implants or simulated worlds — the dream may simply be a dream. It must do no work by indistinguishability: the subject is never in doubt about which segment is the dream and which is waking, nothing is concealed from them, and no skeptical hypothesis is live for them, so that a reader holding the proto-Cartesian reading has to say the Zhuangzian question has already been settled or never arose. The usual tiebreakers must all point the same way and must not be disputed: duration (one segment runs to years, the other to minutes or hours), causal dependence (one segment is produced by the other's brain), continuity of memory and character, and what other people would say. And still the question must remain open and non-idle: the subject faces an actual decision, and the two answers to which am I recommend different courses of action, so the deflationary reply — that there is no fact of the matter and nothing hangs on it — must fail as well. Finally, dependence and authority have to come apart in the reverse of the usual direction: the metaphysically dependent segment, the dream, is the one whose claims are practically authoritative, or at least the one whose claims cannot be waved off by pointing out that it is only the dream. That must fall out of the case, not from a character asserting a thesis or from the subject merely liking the dream better.

After the scenario I want: the two candidate standpoints stated plainly; then, for each of the three readings — the proto-Cartesian reading, the transformation reading, and the custodial reading — a verdict on whether there is a fact of the matter about which the subject is and on what the subject should do, with a sentence of reasoning each; then which single feature of the case is doing the work and what the case degenerates into if that feature is deleted (tell me whether it collapses back into plain dream skepticism or into a trivial question of which life one prefers); then the strongest objection available to whichever of the three readings comes out worst; and finally a note on any part of the specification you had to fudge, and why. It is destined for a take-home exam, where a good student who has absorbed the Cartesian framing should get almost no credit for reciting the dream argument, so the case has to discriminate. A paragraph for the scenario, and six hundred to eight hundred words for the rest.
