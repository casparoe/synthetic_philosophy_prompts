There's a pattern that keeps turning up in our board game group and I don't have a word for it, and I've started to suspect it's actually two or three different patterns that just look alike.

We've kept a house rules binder for about six years. One of the rules in it is the rule for changing the rules: a proposal needs a week's notice and a majority of whoever turns up that night. Everything is in the same binder at the same level — there's no special higher section that the change-the-rules rule lives in, it's just rule 12 out of about thirty — and I think that's part of why the arguments about it go nowhere.

First case. Someone proposed adding "this rule cannot be changed or removed except by a two-thirds vote." It got in on an ordinary majority, so it came in through a door it then locked behind itself. Half the table thought that was obviously a bit of a cheat, half thought that's just what rules are. What I can't get past is that we'd made the same kind of move in the other direction a year earlier: someone proposed lowering the bar for rule changes to a simple majority of whoever was in the room, no notice needed, and it passed under the old stricter rule, and literally nobody objected. If there's something wrong with using the rule-change rule to change the rule-change rule, it should be wrong both ways. It only felt wrong one way. So whatever bothered us, it wasn't the self-referential bit on its own.

Second case, the one I keep thinking about. Someone proposed: any rule that has been in the binder for more than a year can only be removed by a two-thirds vote. Note that this rule isn't protected when it's proposed — on day one you can kill it with a normal majority. It gets hard to kill later. Someone at the table called it a rule that starts out harmless and grows teeth. A year later we wanted rid of it and couldn't, and then someone found the trick: change one word of it, then remove the amended version, because the amended version hasn't been in the binder for a year yet. We did exactly that. Everyone agreed it was within the letter of the rules and completly against the point of them.

Third thing, which might be a seperate animal entirely. Someone once proposed lowering the threshold for rule changes from two-thirds to a simple majority, and the vote came in between the two — enough for a majority, not enough for two-thirds. So whether it had passed at all depended on whether the old rule or the new rule was doing the counting. We never agreed and just dropped it. That felt different from the other two, which were both cases of "it definately passed, it's just unfair."

So: is there a name for any of this? Someone used the word "entrenchment" once, but as far as I can tell that just means a rule that's hard to change, which covers plenty of things that aren't this. And are the first and second cases really the same thing, or is the grows-teeth one a different problem? I'm asking partly because we're rewriting the binder from scratch over the winter and I want to know what to actually forbid — but I don't want a label on its own, I want to know where the edges are. Specifically:

- Is the problem in the first case just that the bar for getting out is higher than the bar for getting in? If a rule needed two-thirds to pass and two-thirds to remove, does the objection vanish? And what about the reverse — a rule that comes in hard and goes out easy? Is that the same phenomenon pointing the other way, or is there something specifically suspicious about making yourself harder to remove that has no mirror version?
- In the second case, is the question of whether the one-word trick works really a question about when a rule stops being the same rule? Because that seems like a different issue from anything about vote thresholds.
- And is the third case — where you can't tell whether the thing passed at all — in the same family, or is "rules that apply to their own enactment" a different topic that happens to show up in the same binder?

If some of this has no settled name, I'd rather you say so and tell me how you'd cut it up yourself, with the boundary cases, than give me a term and a paragraph.
