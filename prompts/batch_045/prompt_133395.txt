I am a PhD student in political theory, writing a dissertation chapter on epistemic defences of democratic legitimacy, and I need help auditing a secondary source before I cite it. A handbook chapter I read (by a respected figure in American pragmatism scholarship — I'd rather not name them here) summarizes Dewey's experimentalism about democracy and inquiry roughly as follows:

"Dewey's central idea is that democracy is just the scientific method applied to politics. In the laboratory you form a hypothesis, test it against the evidence, and revise it in light of the results; in a democracy you do the same thing with policy, except that the experiments are run by elected officials advised by experts, and the 'results' are the consequences for the people affected. Because Dewey was a thoroughgoing fallibilist, he denied that beliefs can be true: he replaced truth with 'warranted assertibility,' which means only that acting on a claim produces consequences that are satisfactory to the person asserting it — so his epistemology is really a form of relativism dressed up in the language of science. Correspondingly, his political theory rests on a simple definition: a public is the set of people affected by the indirect consequences of some transaction, and the state is that public organized; the whole task of politics, on this view, is to map who is affected and give them a say. But because inquiry never ends and every conclusion is revisable, Dewey can never say that any particular democratic outcome is correct, only that it is the current output of the process. His position therefore collapses into one of two unattractive options: either deference to experts who know how to run the experiment, or sheer majoritarianism. The 'experimental' vocabulary adds nothing that could not be said in terms of trial and error."

I am suspicious of this, but not in a way I can articulate well. My instinct is that the laboratory analogy is being asked to bear more weight than Dewey could have intended, and that the last two sentences attribute a refutation to what may be only a familiar objection — but I cannot say exactly where the distortion sets in, and I do not want to misrepresent the view in print.

Please go through the passage claim by claim and separate three things: what a sympathetic Dewey scholar would accept as fair; what is a defensible simplification or a contested interpretive choice that the chapter is entitled to make; and what is a misrepresentation — in particular, whether the chapter has conflated claims Dewey keeps apart, overstated a modest thesis, or used a technical term (warranted assertibility, public, inquiry, situation) in an everyday sense. I would be especially grateful for attention to three points: (i) the characterization of warranted assertibility and of Dewey's relationship to the concept of truth; (ii) the inference from the social character of inquiry to either expert deference or majority rule, and whether there are intermediate positions Dewey actually occupies — for instance about the conditions inquiry requires, or about what experts owe the publics they advise; and (iii) the treatment of a public as simply the set of people affected, given that much of *The Public and Its Problems* is about why publics fail to form or to recognize themselves.

Finally, one further question that the chapter does not address and that bears on my own argument. Suppose we grant that inquiry is initiated by an indeterminate situation and terminates in a resolved one. In social inquiry, unlike in the natural sciences, the people whose situation is to be resolved are also the people who must carry out the inquiry, and the inquiry itself — publicizing consequences, re-describing interests, reorganizing associations — changes the situation it was meant to resolve. Call this the problem of constitutive instability: there may be no determinate situation that stays still long enough to be resolved, and hence no stable target at which inquiry aims. Is this a genuine difficulty for Dewey's account, or does his conception of inquiry — the role of existential consequences, the reconstruction of ends in the course of action, the claim that inquiry transforms the situation rather than representing a fixed one — already anticipate and accommodate it? Does it tell against the laboratory analogy specifically, or against something more central? I would welcome a discussion that draws on the structure of Dewey's account rather than on any particular scholarly reading of it.
