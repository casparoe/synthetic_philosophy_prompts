Bit of a strange request, so bear with me — assume the role of a population ethicist, or just a professional philosopher who has spent an unhealthy amount of time on the creating-people literature. What I want is an audit of my own position, not a survey of the field. Please think it through properly and do a bit of brainstorming before you settle on anything: I'd rather have something careful and slightly messy than something tidy and wrong.

Its not purely academic for me: I sit on the clinical ethics committee at my hospital trust and we keep getting referrals about preimplantation genetic testing for late-onset conditions — the sort where the child would have maybe forty decent years and then a decade of fairly severe decline, a life that is clearly worth living overall but not one you would wish on anyone. Trying to get my own view straight for those discussions, here is where I think I have landed. (1) The procreation asymmetry: there is no duty to bring a happy person into existence, but there is a duty not to bring a miserable one. I take this as my fixed point. (2) I think the reason for the first limb is person-affecting: nobody is deprived by not being created, and in the non-identity cases the child is not made worse off by being created, so there is no one the act is bad for. (3) But I still want to say it can be wrong to create someone who is not made worse off — a life of unremitting suffering obviously, and I would also condemn creating a child I knew would have that bad decade at the end, even granting the life is worth living. (4) I have what I privately call the clear-of-the-cliff rule: creating a child is permissible only if you can reasonably expect their life to land comfortably above the worth-living line, not merely a whisker on the right side of it. (5) I am not an anti-natalist — having children is permissible and often wonderful, my own two have lives that are good for them, and I am happy to say they have benefited from existing even though the alternative was never existing. (6) On risk: a smallish risk of a wretched outcome is tolerable, since otherwise nobody could have children at all and I am not saying that; but I also think the asymmetry carries into uncertainty, so a ten per cent shot at a miserable life is not cancelled out by a ninety per cent shot at a good one the way someone totting up total well-being would claim, because the good case is not a gain to anyone who would otherwise miss out. (7) And in the embryo case I come out like this: it is wrong to implant the affected embryo when an unaffected one is available, it is not wrong to implant none at all, and there is no duty to pick whichever one is expected to go best.

So: which of these actually bite each other, and which only look as though they do? I have a feeling (2) and (3) are in trouble together, and someone at the meeting muttered that (4) and (6) quietly make ordinary parenthood impermissible, but I can't tell whether that is right or just a debating point. Please rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing six equal problems; for each one say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me somewhere else in the set; flag explicitly which pairs dissolve on inspection and why they dissolve; then tell me the single cheapest revision that leaves me with a package that hangs together, on the assumption that I most want to keep (1) and (5). If one of my items is doing no real work, or is just another item in different words, say that too.
