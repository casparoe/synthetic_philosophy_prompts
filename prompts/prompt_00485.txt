I'm working on a longform piece about moral luck for a science/culture outlet, and I keep getting stuck on one move that philosophers make to try to dissolve the puzzle. Nagel's original taxonomy has resultant luck as its own category — the drunk driver who hits a kid vs. the one who makes it home safe, same recklessness, different outcome, different blame. But a bunch of people (I think Zimmerman is the clearest version, though Judith Jarvis Thomson gestures at something similar) argue this is basically an illusion: we don't actually think the *outcome itself* changes the moral facts, we just get better epistemic access to the agent's negligence/bad will when things go wrong, and we're sloppily conflating "more evidence of blameworthiness" with "more blameworthy." On this view resultant luck collapses into an artifact of epistemology, not a real ethical phenomenon, and only circumstantial/constitutive luck (or maybe not even those) survive as genuine.

I'd like you to push back hard on this and argue that resultant luck is a real, irreducible category — that even holding fixed our total evidence about the agent's mental states and negligence, the actual outcome makes a legitimate difference to how much blame or praise is deserved, not just to how much we can infer. Feel free to lean on Williams's stuff about agent-regret if it's useful, but I want an actual constructive case for the position, not just a survey of the debate.
