I've been teaching a seminar on the metaphysics of quantum mechanics, and this week's session on the Everett interpretation prompted a debate among my students that I haven't been able to fully resolve myself. Several of them, having encountered popular treatments of "quantum immortality," argued that if the Many-Worlds Interpretation is correct, then each of us is effectively guaranteed indefinite subjective survival: since every outcome consistent with the wavefunction is realized in some branch, there must always be a branch in which a given observer survives an otherwise fatal event, and so—reasoning purely from the first-person perspective—we should expect, with something like certainty rather than mere low probability, to find ourselves alive after any brush with death, no matter how improbable the survival. One student went further and suggested that this makes "quantum suicide" experiments a rational method for empirically confirming the Everett interpretation, since the experimenter should anticipate surviving every trial indefinitely from their own point of view.

I'd like your help thinking through whether this line of reasoning is actually sound, and if not, exactly where it breaks down. In particular, I'm interested in what it would even mean to assign a coherent subjective probability to future experience across branches, and whether the inference from "some branch contains a surviving observer" to "I should expect to be that observer" smuggles in assumptions about personal identity or anticipation that the Everett interpretation itself doesn't license.
