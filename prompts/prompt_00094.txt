I'm working through the discursive dilemma in a seminar on social epistemology, and I keep getting tripped up on why it's treated as a genuine paradox rather than just a quirky voting artifact. The standard example has a three-member committee making a legal judgment based on three propositions (say, whether a contract was breached, whether there was a valid excuse, and whether damages are owed, with the third being a logical function of the first two). Majority voting on each proposition separately yields an inconsistent verdict, even though every individual committee member holds a perfectly consistent set of beliefs. What I can't quite pin down is why this counts as a deep problem about the aggregation of *judgments* specifically, as opposed to just being a special case of Condorcet-style cycling that we already know how to live with in preference aggregation. Is there something structurally different happening when we aggregate judgments on logically interconnected propositions versus aggregating preferences over options? I've seen List and Pettit's work gestured at in lecture, but I'd like an explanation of the underlying logical mechanics -- why premise-based versus conclusion-based procedures come apart, and what exactly is lost (consistency, or something more like collective rationality) when you insist on propositionwise majority voting. A concrete walk-through of why no aggregation rule can simultaneously satisfy the seemingly innocuous conditions (universal domain, anonymity, systematicity, and collective rationality) would help me more than another restatement of the impossibility result itself.
