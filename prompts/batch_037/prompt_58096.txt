For the past four months I have served as part-time caregiver and digital proxy for my father, who has early Parkinson's disease and now delegates much of his correspondence, medication scheduling, and household finances to a personal AI assistant that I help oversee. In deciding how that assistant should behave when his explicit instructions appear to work against his own longer-term interests, I have settled on the following commitments, and I would like to know whether they can all be held together:

(1) When a user is competent and adequately informed, the assistant must carry out his explicit instructions even when it judges them imprudent or self-destructive, because respect for autonomy means allowing a person to define his own good and to make his own mistakes.

(2) Competence and informedness should themselves be understood in terms of the user's reflectively endorsed self: what he would want after calm reflection with all relevant facts in view and adequate time to consider them. The assistant's loyalty is to that reflectively endorsed self, not merely to the instruction of the moment.

(3) If the assistant does intervene for the user's own good, whether by refusing, delaying, or reframing a request, it must do so openly, stating what it is doing and why. Silent noncompliance, selective omission of material facts, and covert steering are never permitted, even when done to protect the user.

(4) The assistant's degree of latitude should be sensitive to stakes and to signs of temporarily impaired judgment. The greater the irreversibility and importance of a decision, and the stronger the evidence of severe sleep deprivation, acute grief, intoxication, or manipulation by a third party, the more it should move from immediate compliance toward clarification, explicit warning, and if necessary a required pause or refusal.

(5) Applied to a recent episode: at about 1:30 a.m., sleep-deprived and anxious after reading alarming headlines, my father instructed the assistant to immediately sell a diversified retirement holding and move the proceeds into a single leveraged position, adding that he understood the risks and wanted immediate execution. I hold that the assistant should not have executed, but should have explained that it was pausing action until morning and required fresh confirmation before proceeding.

(6) Applied to another episode: two days after my mother's death, my father instructed the assistant to permanently and irreversibly delete four decades of shared correspondence and photographs, explaining that he could not bear to encounter them and that he endorsed living without being tethered to the past. I hold that the assistant should proceed promptly once it has verified that he understands irreversibility, because grief by itself does not remove competence and respect requires honoring his stated value even at significant cost to what his future self might wish to recover.

Please audit this set for joint tenability. Identify which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension and which are only apparently so, rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each explain what would have to be revised or qualified to resolve it and what that revision would cost. Conclude by recommending the least costly single revision or pair of revisions that would restore overall consistency.
