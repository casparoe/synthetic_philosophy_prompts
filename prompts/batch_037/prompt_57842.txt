I am a retired field ecologist writing to ask for help with a practical decision that has turned into a philosophical puzzle for me. You are a professional philosopher with expertise in ethics and rational choice, and I would be grateful if you would think carefully and patiently through my situation, first brainstorming several different ways to understand it and then working toward a clear recommendation.

After thirty-four years studying plant communities, I retired three years ago and have spent my evenings reading philosophy for pleasure. I have set aside about twelve thousand dollars from savings to give away this year, and I am torn between two uses that a former student and a local colleague have proposed.

The first use is close to home and nearly certain. Our county nature center would use the money to keep a part-time coordinator for two summers to run garden plots and food workshops for schoolchildren. The director estimates about one hundred and eighty children would take part, and from past summers that estimate seems reliable. The benefit per child is modest but real, better meals for a season and some practical skill.

The second use is speculative but potentially wide reaching. My former student coordinates a network that provides drought tolerant seed kits and growing instructions to small towns, on the understanding that each town that succeeds will help start similar gardens in two further towns. Most such chains fizzle quickly. In a pilot with fourteen starting towns, eleven stopped by the third round, two lasted to the fifth, and one is still spreading in its sixth year. She believes a new infusion on the scale I could give would have perhaps a one or two percent chance of creating a self sustaining spread that could continue for decades, with no natural stopping point built into the design, each new garden feeding on the order of thirty families.

If I multiply things out in the obvious way, even that small chance of an open ended chain seems to swamp the definite local good, and then by the same arithmetic I feel I should keep searching for even more remote projects with still smaller chances and still larger spreads, which feels like a trap. Yet to ignore scale altogether and help only what is near and certain feels parochial and unserious about the future.

What should I do, and why. I am especially interested in how to compare a sure and bounded benefit with a tiny chance of a benefit that could keep growing without a fixed end, whether very distant additions should count for less, whether tiny chances should be treated differently from ordinary risks, and how to avoid a rule that would always tell me to chase the most remote possibility. Please give your answer in continuous prose only, the way one would write a thoughtful email or letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
