I am a doctoral student in philosophy of science preparing a prospectus on how scientific thought experiments can produce knowledge about the physical world, and I am seeking guidance on where to direct the project.

The familiar episodes remain both compelling and puzzling: imagined falling bodies that appear to overturn a theory of motion without collecting new data, imagined elevators or trains that appear to establish consequences of a principle before the relevant measurements exist, and imagined demons or enclosed systems that appear to clarify the limits of a law. Competing accounts treat such episodes as disguised arguments, as exercises of a quasi-observational capacity, or as continuous with idealized models and simulations, and I am unsure which contrasts among these accounts carry weight for scientific practice.

To make the difficulty concrete, I keep returning to two variations of my own that unsettle the standard stories: a falling-bodies scenario relocated to a viscous fluid, where intuitive verdicts wobble depending on which background idealizations are held fixed, and a comparison between a purely imagined elevator scenario and a later high-resolution simulation of the same setup, where the simulation team claims the imagined version already contained the result.

Given roughly two years of full-time philosophical work, with a background in general analytic philosophy and undergraduate physics and with no scope for archival research or advanced formal methods, which open questions or lines of work on the epistemology of scientific thought experiments deserve priority, and in what order. I need a defended agenda for that specific use rather than a survey, one that weighs importance for understanding how such episodes generate warrant, upstreamness with respect to debates about idealization and modeling, and tractability for someone with my preparation and timeframe, with tradeoffs made explicit.

Please present your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each consisting of one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Each item should identify one ranked question or line of work and state in the same item why it belongs at that rank in terms of dependency, tractability, and payoff, including what finding or development would move it up or down the list.
