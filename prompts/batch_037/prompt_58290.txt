Last night I got into a long argument with a close friend about whether death can be bad for the person who dies. He takes the old Epicurean line that since we feel no dread or regret toward the eternity before our birth, consistency requires the same indifference toward the eternity after our death, and he treats that as settling that fearing death or regarding it as a misfortune is irrational. I was defending the more commonsense view that death deprives us of future goods we would otherwise have had, and we went back and forth for hours without either of us budging.

To press his point he offered a paired thought experiment he had devised: in one version you discover hospital records were mixed up and you were actually born five years later than you believed, so you lacked five years of possible earlier life, while in the other you learn from a reliable prognosis that a condition will cut five years off the end of your life, and he insisted a fully rational person with no future bias would regard these as symmetrical losses and be equally untroubled by both, so any extra sting in the second case merely reflects an irrational bias toward the future and toward already-formed plans. I replied that the two cases differ because only the later loss interrupts projects, relationships, and anticipations that give the present its meaning, and that valuing the future more is not obviously a bias at all, but he accused me of smuggling in exactly what needed to be proved.

Could you audit this prenatal-nonexistence to posthumous-nonexistence analogy for me? Please set out explicitly how the two sides are supposed to correspond, then where the correspondence fails, distinguishing failures that actually matter for his indifference conclusion from incidental differences, and explaining which features of the prenatal side the analogy quietly relies on that the death side lacks. Tell me whether his conclusion that indifference is rational survives once those disanalogies are in view, and if the analogy is defective suggest whether a more apt comparison is available. In doing so, please separate what is broadly settled among philosophers who work on this from what remains genuinely contested, rather than presenting one contested position as the consensus.
