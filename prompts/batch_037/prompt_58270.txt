I am preparing an essay for a seminar in philosophical aesthetics on whether moral condemnation of an artist bears on the standing of the artist's work, and I find myself repeatedly confronted with the maxim 'separate the art from the artist'.

It was invoked in our discussion to do two quite different pieces of work: first, to reject a biographical reading of a hypothetical novel whose narrator endorses cruelty — on the ground that the author's own convictions are irrelevant to what the novel means — and second, to reject a proposal to remove from the syllabus the work of a hypothetical painter guilty of serious but thematically unrelated wrongdoing — on the ground that continued exhibition and study are unobjectionable.

Could you disentangle the distinct precise theses this single sentence might be expressing? I have in mind, among others, metaphysical claims about the identity of the work, claims about whether moral defects can constitute aesthetic defects, claims about interpretation, and claims about the ethics of engagement, exhibition, and purchase. Please present them as a numbered menu from the most trivial to the most ambitious, indicating for each whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible position, or false, and explain which readings, if any, would actually license the two uses described above.

In particular, I would like to understand how the analysis changes across three variants: where the wrong is external to the work, where the work itself articulates the repugnant outlook, and where continued engagement is paired with contextualizing wall text and diversion of proceeds.
