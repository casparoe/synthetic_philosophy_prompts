I am a novelist drafting a late chapter of a historical novel set in a tidal shipyard town, and I need a philosophically serious exchange between two characters to anchor the chapter.

The dispute concerns the town ferry Maren, maintained in continuous service for thirty years while every timber was gradually replaced, plank by plank, for soundness. The discarded timbers were warehoused, and years later a rival yard reassembled them on a new keel into a second vessel, now displayed ashore under the same name. One sister, a working shipwright who oversaw the gradual repairs, maintains that the working ferry in service is still Maren because uninterrupted use, form, and communal recognition preserve identity through part replacement. Her sister, the town archivist who reconstructed the vessel from discarded timbers, maintains that Maren is the reassembled vessel ashore because identity follows the original matter, and that a thing cannot survive complete replacement of its material.

Please compose a sustained dialogue between the two sisters, each played as an intelligent and sympathetic reasoner, for me to adapt. Let the shipwright press considerations of function, continuity, and gradual versus sudden change, and let the archivist press considerations of material origin, counting, and what would be said if both vessels claimed the name at once. They should answer each other point for point, raising at least one telling objection to the opposing view and attempting a reply, rather than delivering alternating monologues. Keep the exchange focused on which vessel, if either, is Maren and what that suggests about whether sameness over time is a matter of history or of stuff, and leave the disagreement intelligently unresolved rather than giving one sister an easy victory.
