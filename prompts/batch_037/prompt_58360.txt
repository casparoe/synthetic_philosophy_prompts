I am preparing a dissertation chapter on skilled action and have become puzzled by the persistence of the dispute over the relation between knowing how and knowing that.

For purposes of the chapter, I understand the two central positions roughly as follows. Intellectualists hold that knowing how to perform an action is a species of propositional knowledge, namely knowledge of a way of acting, entertained under a practical or action-guiding manner of thinking about that way. Ability theorists hold that knowing how is fundamentally distinct, consisting in a reliable ability, disposition, or embodied competence that can come apart from propositional understanding, even when supplemented by practical modes of presentation.

What strikes me is how little movement there has been despite decades of exchange about linguistic patterns in knowledge ascriptions, thought experiments about skiers and pianists who can or cannot perform, and neuropsychological dissociations between declarative memory and preserved skill learning. Each side seems able to reinterpret the other side's favored data without revising its core claim.

To keep the discussion from staying at that familiar level of generality, I want analysis anchored in three variant cases of my own design:

Mara, a former competitive swimmer who is now fully paralyzed and ventilator dependent, remembers exactly how elite turns are timed, diagnoses flaws in video of others, and gives coaching instructions that measurably improve her swimmers, but cannot move herself.

Arm-7, a warehouse manipulator trained entirely through trial-and-error reinforcement without any separately stored declarative model of its task, reliably grasps fragile and irregularly shaped objects under varied lighting and clutter, but fails simple verbal or diagrammatic probes about what it is doing and cannot transfer its competence by instruction.

Theo, an advanced violin student who can state accurate principles governing wrist motion, pressure, and timing for a fluid vibrato and can identify good versus flawed vibrato in recordings, but plays with a stiff and uneven vibrato himself, contrasted with his teacher, who plays with exemplary vibrato while stating false principles about her own wrist motion when asked to explain it.

My hypothesis is that the dispute conflates a semantic question about when ordinary English treats someone as knowing how with a psychological question about the cognitive architecture underlying fluent performance, and that the two sides are in effect answering different questions while using the same vocabulary of knowledge.

Assess that hypothesis and offer a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement. Is it substantially verbal, is it a genuine factual disagreement about cognition, is it a difference in which cases are treated as central data or in what counts as evidence for a philosophical claim about knowledge, or is it a clash of starting intuitions that argument cannot resolve? Show how each side would handle Mara, Arm-7, and Theo versus his teacher, and use those verdicts to support the diagnosis rather than surveying the existing literature. Conclude by stating concretely what would count as progress on your diagnosis, whether that is a conceptual distinction, a reformulated question, or an empirical or formal result, and if you think no such advance should be expected, explain why the impasse is principled rather than temporary.
