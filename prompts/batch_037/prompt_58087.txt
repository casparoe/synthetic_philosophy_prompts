I am a high school junior serving as parliamentarian for our student council, and I am trying to turn what we studied about ancient Athens into something my council can actually use.

We discussed three failures of Athenian democracy in class. In the first, the assembly angrily voted to punish a rebellious allied city with extreme severity and then, after a night of reflection and a second debate, reversed itself the next day. In the second, the assembly was persuaded by enthusiastic speeches to approve a large, distant military expedition, brushed aside warnings from more cautious and experienced commanders about cost, distance, and poor information, and suffered a catastrophe. In the third, a large citizen jury convicted a philosopher who questioned conventional views and annoyed prominent people, on broad charges of impiety and corrupting the young.

Our council meets for about forty-five minutes each Tuesday with thirty members between the ages of fourteen and eighteen. We decide matters such as dance budgets under two thousand dollars, spirit week plans, and recommendations to the administration on phone use and club funding. None of us has training in history or philosophy, our faculty advisor must sign off on any action, and we are under constant pressure to vote quickly when feelings run high. I need a reusable procedure, not advice on a single vote: a step-by-step checklist or flowchart in words that a chair with no background in theory could follow in real time and that would fit on one page if summarized.

Please design that procedure so it preserves the core lessons of the three Athenian cases while dropping details that do not transfer to a school setting. It should specify in order the questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, when a vote must be delayed to a second meeting, when dissenting or expert voices must be heard, and what protections apply when the target of a proposal is an unpopular person or view. Mark clearly the points where judgment cannot be eliminated and must be exercised by the chair or advisor.

Then run the procedure step by step on three live proposals: a proposal to ban phones during lunch immediately after a cheating incident, where sponsors demand a same-day vote while anger is high; a proposal to spend most of our annual budget on an ambitious overnight leadership retreat in another city, promoted with great enthusiasm while warnings from the treasurer and advisor about cost and logistics are being dismissed as negativity; and a proposal to remove our newspaper representative from the council after an editorial sharply criticizing the council and administration, on grounds of disloyalty and bad character. Include at least one application where the procedure blocks or delays what I and a majority currently want, and conclude with an honest statement of what the procedure cannot do and where it is likely to misfire.
