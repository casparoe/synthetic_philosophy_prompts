I have been working through the implications of moral uncertainty, understood as uncertainty not about nonmoral facts but about which first order moral theory is true and how good or bad acts are according to those theories, and I keep arriving at a conclusion I cannot accept. To make the issue concrete, consider a stipulated case called Mira Menu: Mira assigns 0.95 credence to a permissive common sense view on which eating factory farmed chicken is at most mildly suboptimal, and 0.05 credence to a stringent suffering focused view on which doing so is gravely wrong, on a par with serious interpersonal violence. Many students report the intuition that humility requires Mira to abstain, and the following argument generalizes that intuition:

(1) Under purely empirical uncertainty, rationality requires expected value reasoning: weight possible outcomes by credence and choose the act with the best weighted sum.
(2) Moral uncertainty is normatively analogous to empirical uncertainty: being unsure whether an act is gravely wrong functions like being unsure whether it causes grave harm.
(3) So under moral uncertainty, rationality requires maximizing expected choiceworthiness: weight each candidate moral theory by credence in that theory and choose the act with the best credence weighted sum, assuming some reasonable way of comparing levels of badness across theories.
(4) On that approach, small credences in high stakes theories can dominate: in Mira Menu, the 0.05 credence in the stringent view outweighs the 0.95 credence in the permissive view, so Mira must abstain.
(5) The pattern in (4) generalizes across ordinary life: for almost any ordinary act such as commuting by car, having a child, voting for an imperfect candidate, or eating chicken, there is some stringent theory to which a thoughtful agent might assign a small but nonzero credence and which condemns that act as gravely wrong.
(6) Therefore an agent who follows (3) and who distributes small credences across such stringent theories will find almost every ordinary act vetoed. Taken seriously, moral humility leads to practical paralysis, where normal human life becomes impermissible.
(7) But a second order norm for handling moral uncertainty cannot reasonably demand paralysis; a norm that forbids living a normal human life is self undermining.
(8) So the expected choiceworthiness approach in (3) must be abandoned, and with it the analogy in (2) that motivated it.

Each step strikes me as plausible, yet (6) and (7) together suggest that being appropriately humble about morality makes ordinary agency impossible, which I take to be unacceptable. I am particularly reluctant to give up (1) and the analogy claim in (2), but I want to know exactly what keeping them would cost.
