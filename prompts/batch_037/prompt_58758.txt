In my upper-level seminar on practical rationality, I introduce the St. Petersburg game as a fair coin tossed until the first heads appears, paying 2 to the nth dollars when heads first appears on toss n, so that its expected monetary value is unbounded. A colleague who visits the seminar each year has defended a stable pattern of judgments about related gambles, stipulating in each case that winnings are added to an ordinary modest bank balance with no fees, delays, or counterparty risk:

1. She will not pay $30 to enter the standard game, reporting a maximum buying price in the neighborhood of $10.
2. She prices a truncated version, capped at $64 even if the coin lands tails beyond the sixth toss, only about a dollar or two below the standard game.
3. She strictly prefers $50 for certain over a 5 percent chance of $1,000, and strictly prefers $40 for certain over a 1 in 200 chance of $8,000.
4. She is indifferent between playing the standard game for free and paying a dollar or two to avoid having to play it when entry is mandatory and nonrefundable at a price of $30.

This year I plan to present her, for the first time, with a choice between two free tickets with no entry fee. Ticket A is the standard game. Ticket B is the standard game plus an additional $4 million if heads does not appear until after the twentieth toss, an event with probability below one in a million, and is otherwise identical.

For purposes of classroom discussion, articulate at least two plausible decision rules that jointly fit judgments 1 through 4, explain what each rule predicts about the choice between Ticket A and Ticket B and about willingness to pay a premium to exchange A for B, offer a best prediction of which option she will favor with reasons for preferring that interpretation of her prior verdicts, and describe one further hypothetical gamble, distinct from the A-versus-B choice, that would most sharply separate the leading interpretations.
