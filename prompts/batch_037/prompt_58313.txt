I am a high school junior completing a final essay for my civics seminar on gun control and the right to bear arms, and I would like help understanding why our class discussion stalled.

We spent two weeks on the topic. My teacher gave us a handout of background facts that both sides in the class accepted for purposes of discussion: that the United States has much higher private gun ownership and much higher rates of gun death than other wealthy democracies, that a large share of gun deaths are suicides rather than homicides, that lawful defensive uses do occur but are difficult to count reliably, and that policing and emergency response are uneven across rural and urban areas. No one disputed those points, yet no one changed their mind.

Roughly, one group argued that the central issue is whether individuals should be able to protect themselves and their families on fair terms, especially when criminals may be armed and help may be far away. On this view, even a policy that reduced total shootings could still be wrong if it left law-abiding people unable to defend themselves. The other group argued that the central issue is which set of rules would produce less overall death, injury, and fear in places like schools and neighborhoods, with attention to suicides, accidents, and arguments that escalate because a gun is present. On this view, an individual interest in owning a particular weapon has to yield if broader restrictions would save many more lives.

My working hypothesis is that we were not really disagreeing about the same claim: one side was answering a moral question about what individuals should be permitted to keep for self-protection, while the other was answering a policy question about which regulations produce the best aggregate outcomes, with the idea of safety doing different work in each argument.

Is that hypothesis correct, or is something else keeping the disagreement in place? Please diagnose the structure of this impasse with reference to the two positions as I have described them — for example, whether the key divide concerns facts, standards for what counts as evidence, the meaning of a right, or deeper moral commitments that argument cannot easily move — and explain what, if anything, would count as progress. That might be a conceptual distinction, a kind of factual finding, or a reframed question that would force the two sides to engage each other, or it might be a reason to think no such move should be expected.
