I am enrolled in an interdisciplinary seminar on mind and machines, and for the past two weeks we have been discussing the intentional stance as a lens for deciding when it is apt to describe an artificial system as having beliefs, wants, or intentions rather than merely behaving as if it did. I find myself pulled in both directions in conversation with classmates: I am impressed by how much predictive leverage comes from speaking intentionally about contemporary systems, yet I worry that such talk quietly smuggles in commitments about understanding, error, and responsibility that I am not ready to endorse. To locate my own view I tried to record my settled reactions to systems I have actually used, and I would like help seeing what principle, if any, lies behind them.

First, the dial thermostat in my apartment that switches the furnace on below its setpoint: I treat talk of what it believes about the temperature as convenient shorthand only, with no temptation to say it has made a mistake when it misfires because of a draft. Second, a phone chess program that reliably avoids a trap in my favorite opening and punishes me when I try a dubious gambit: here I am comfortable saying it anticipates the trap and prefers central control, because that description supports counterfactual predictions about how it will respond to novel lines. Third, a household language-model assistant used for factual questions: when it produces fluent answers but invents plausible-sounding sources for them, I resist saying it believes its answers or that it is lying about the sources, even though intentional description predicts its fluency quite well. Fourth, that same assistant when set to plan multi-leg travel with calendar and booking tools: when a flight search returns full and it rebooks, explains the change, and preserves my constraints on layovers, I am strongly tempted to say it now takes the first flight to be unavailable and wants an alternative that satisfies the constraints. Fifth, my robot vacuum that maps the flat and, after bumping into a shoe left in the hall, routes around it on subsequent runs: I will say for purposes of prediction that it knows where the obstacle is, but I would immediately retract the claim if pressed on whether it really knows anything. Sixth, a video recommender that, after I watch one sensational clip, fills my feed with ever more extreme variants: although saying it thinks I want extremist content or intends to keep me engaged predicts its behavior, I regard that way of speaking as misleading and decline to endorse it.

Is there a single principle that captures most of these verdicts, and if not, which of them should I give up and why. I suspect the relevant considerations include indispensability of intentional prediction, robustness across counterfactuals, and whether the system can recognize and correct its own errors, but the third and fourth cases trouble me because they involve the same underlying model behaving in ways that strike me very differently, as do the second and sixth, where predictive success seems similar but my willingness to attribute beliefs diverges. Please state one or two candidate principles precisely, show which of my cases motivates each clause or qualification, be candid about which of my verdicts each principle cannot accommodate except at the cost of ad hoc complexity, and say what the more defensible principle predicts for a further case I have not listed and keep going back and forth on, namely a coding assistant that apologizes for introducing a bug when I point to a failing test, revises the patch, and then describes why the new version should pass.
