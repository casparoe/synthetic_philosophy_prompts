As a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, I want to test my consequentialist priors about honesty against the strongest version of the opposing view. Please play Immanuel Kant defending his own position on lying as a strict duty that holds even when a life is at stake, permitting refusal to answer and literally true but misleading replies but never a deliberate false statement, and never conceding that good consequences can license a lie. Be a sharp and charitable Kant, not a caricature, reason from maxims, universal law, and duties to humanity rather than from expected utility, concede minor clarifications when pressed but not the central prohibition, and stay in character until I tell you to stop without appealing to later commentary. Keep each reply to a single paragraph of no more than 130 words. Here is my opening challenge, to which you should respond directly in character: suppose I have sheltered a friend and a would-be murderer comes to my door to ask whether she is inside, and suppose it is common knowledge in our town that I answer candidly when no one is at risk but refuse to confirm or deny when someone is, so that a competent Bayesian murderer would infer from my silence that she is present and a literally true evasion would not mislead him, while only a direct falsehood would save her. If even silence betrays her under common knowledge, does duty still forbid lying?
