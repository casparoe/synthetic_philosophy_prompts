On a recent train journey I listened to two acquaintances, Helen and Daniel, develop a disagreement about whether luck can bear on blameworthiness that they were unable to resolve. To fix ideas they constructed a shared example together.

Two landlords, Alan and Ben, each manage an aging building. Each deliberately skips the recommended inspection of wiring and detectors for the same reason, to save time and money, and each judges the risk to be low enough to justify the omission. In Alan's building the wiring is degraded and a fire breaks out, seriously injuring a tenant. In Ben's building the wiring happens to be sound, so the same omission causes no harm. They add one further stipulation: Alan was raised in a family business where economizing on maintenance was treated as normal business sense, whereas Ben was raised by parents who modeled scrupulous compliance, though as an adult Ben has come to share Alan's dismissive attitude.

Their exchange went roughly as follows. Helen began by arguing that Alan is more blameworthy than Ben, not merely liable to pay more. What each did, she said, includes what actually resulted, and a death or serious injury makes the wrong more serious even when the choice and the carelessness were identical. Daniel replied that this violates a basic fairness constraint: a person cannot be more blameworthy for something outside their control, and since neither landlord controlled the state of the wiring, their blameworthiness must be equal, with any difference belonging to bad luck rather than to morality.

Helen answered that our ordinary responses suggest otherwise and are not obviously mistaken. Alan, she said, should feel a different kind of guilt, should offer a different apology, and owes compensation in a way Ben does not, and the criminal law similarly treats a completed harm as worse than an attempt with the same intent. Daniel responded that these observations conflate distinct judgments. Apology, compensation, and legal treatment can be explained by epistemic, reparative, or deterrent reasons that do not show a difference in deserving blame, and the fact that we feel worse when we cause harm does not show we are worse.

Daniel then pressed a further point. If Helen allows character itself to be shaped by luck, as in Alan's upbringing, she faces a dilemma. Either she holds both landlords fully responsible despite Alan's formative luck, in which case her appeal to control is selective, or she discounts blame for formative luck, in which case blame begins to drain away entirely, since genes, upbringing, and circumstance shape everyone. Helen replied that formative influence mitigates without necessarily excusing: Alan may have had to work harder to see the reason to inspect, yet as a competent adult who now endorses the dismissive outlook he remains responsible, whereas no amount of reflection could have given either landlord control over the hidden wiring.

Helen closed by proposing a distinction. Perhaps Alan and Ben are equally blameworthy in terms of the quality of their will, yet Alan has more to answer for in the sense of being answerable for a death, bound to the victim, and properly subject to a harsher self-reproach. Daniel said this distinction merely relabels the problem: if the extra burden tracks only luck, calling it answerability rather than blameworthiness does not make it fair.

I report this as a neutral bystander and take no position between them. I would welcome a careful assessment that examines each stage of their reasoning in turn, indicates where each person's argument succeeds or fails, offers a considered overall judgment about which position is the stronger, and identifies any material consideration that both overlooked. In giving that assessment, please distinguish between points on which specialists in this area largely agree and points on which opinion remains genuinely divided, and avoid presenting a contested position as if it were the consensus.
