I am a retired physical chemist who has taken up philosophy of science in retirement, and I would appreciate a careful assessment of a popular gloss on chaos and determinism that I found puzzling.

An introductory book on physics for general readers, which I will leave unnamed, presents roughly the following line of thought, here paraphrased in my own words rather than quoted: classical systems governed by Newtonian laws are deterministic in the sense that the present state fixes a unique future; systems exhibiting sensitive dependence on initial conditions, such as the weather, amplify minute differences so rapidly that long-range forecasting would demand measurements of unlimited precision; since unlimited precision is unavailable to any finite observer, the Laplacian predictor fails not merely for practical reasons but as a matter of principle; hence such systems are not really deterministic at all, unpredictability and indeterminism amounting to the same thing for all purposes that matter; chaos therefore plays a role in classical physics analogous to uncertainty in quantum physics, showing the future to be open.

I am inclined to accept the early steps about amplification of small differences, which matches my laboratory experience with mixing and reaction instabilities, but I am uneasy about the move from limits on forecasting to conclusions about determinism itself. To test my understanding I programmed in retirement the familiar quadratic recurrence often used to illustrate chaos, starting from two nearby values differing by one part in ten thousand and watching the two trajectories stay close for many iterations before diverging sharply, even though each step was computed by a fully deterministic rule with no randomness added. Does that small exercise illustrate the stronger claim about indeterminism and an open future, or only the weaker claim about limited forecasting from finite precision?
