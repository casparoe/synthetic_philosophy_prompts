Exam Question: Consider Game P defined for class discussion. A fair coin is tossed until the first heads appears. Let N be the total number of tosses. If N is odd, the player receives 2 to the power N divided by N dollars. If N is even, the player pays that same amount, so the payoff is negative. Assume the price of a ticket is fixed in advance.
(a) Calculate the probability of each value of N and its contribution to mathematical expectation. Add those contributions in increasing order of N and contrast the result with the St. Petersburg game, where the payoff for N is 2 to the power N dollars with no sign alternation.
(b) Explain why many theorists treat the expectation of Game P as undefined rather than as equal to the sum found in (a), and assess whether appeal to long-run average winnings across many repeated plays settles the value of a single play.
(c) Consider Game A, which is identical to Game P except that one dollar is added to the payoff in every possible state, so Game A pays one dollar more than Game P no matter how the coin lands. Game A dominates Game P. Does this dominance entail that a rational agent who prefers more money to less must assign Game A a value exactly one dollar higher than Game P? Evaluate this argument and its implications for whether Game P can lack a finite value.

Student Answer:
(a) P(N equals n) equals 1 over 2 to the power n. So the contribution is 1 over 2 to the power n times plus or minus 2 to the power n over n, which is plus 1 over n for odd n and minus 1 over n for even n. Summed in order this is 1 minus 1 over 2 plus 1 over 3 minus 1 over 4 and so on, which converges to natural log of 2, about 69 cents. St. Petersburg is different because each contribution is 1, so the sum diverges to infinity.
(b) Some say the expectation is undefined because the series is only conditionally convergent and rearrangements give different sums. But the natural order is the temporal order in which N increases, so that sum is privileged, and the law of large numbers shows that the average payoff over many independent plays will converge to about 69 cents. With bounded utility for money the paradox also goes away, since large gains and losses matter less at the margin.
(c) Yes. Since A pays exactly one dollar more in every state, preferring more money means preferring A to P and valuing the difference at one dollar. If V is rational value, V of A equals V of P plus 1. This shows V of P cannot be undefined or infinite, because then adding 1 would make no difference, contrary to dominance. So both games have finite values one dollar apart, about 69 cents and about 1.69 dollars.
