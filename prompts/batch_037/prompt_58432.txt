I would welcome a pantoum of four quatrains on quantum immortality and quantum suicide, written in the voice of the experimenter as she addresses, in advance, the assistants who will keep the laboratory record.

Base the poem on the following version of the thought experiment, so readers need no prior specialist knowledge: once per minute, an idealized device measures a quantum superposition with two equally likely outcomes; on one outcome it emits a harmless click, on the other it delivers an instantly lethal event, with no collapse but complete branching of the world. Explain through the poem's development that assistants in most branches will record death within a few trials, while any version of the experimenter who remains conscious will remember only an unbroken sequence of clicks.

Use the pantoum's interlocking repetitions as the conceptual engine: each line that returns in a later stanza should carry a different weight when understood as an entry in the external record versus as a memory in the continuing first-person thread, and the final stanza's return to the opening lines should make that divergence explicit. Include, without breaking the voice or turning didactic, the unresolved question of whether continuity of memory is sufficient for the surviving experimenter to count as the same person who initiated the procedure.

Maintain a sober, reflective register suitable for undergraduates encountering the Many-Worlds picture for the first time: precise enough that a philosopher of physics would recognize the distinction between third-person branch weights and first-person expectation as correctly drawn, yet accessible as a poem about anticipation, survival, and selfhood.
