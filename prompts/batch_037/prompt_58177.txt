For a second-year undergraduate unit on practical rationality, design a decision tree that begins with the standard toxin scenario — a billionaire will pay one million dollars for sincerely forming at midnight the intention to drink a mildly painful but harmless toxin the following day, with no additional benefit from actually drinking — and ends in a stance on whether the prize can be rationally won.

Use at most five yes-or-no questions as branch nodes, each stated as a substantive claim about intention, reasons, or diachronic planning that a student can answer without knowing the label it points toward. Locate at least four stances as leaves: that rational intention requires sufficient reason to act later; that it can be rational to intend while also rational to defect later; that rationality requires sticking to a prior plan once adopted; and that a reason to intend itself creates a reason to act. Force two of your branch questions to turn on the following classroom variants: an escrow variant where the agent may deposit a small sum beforehand to be forfeited if she defects, and an indifferent-fluid variant where the fluid has no pain or pleasure, only a temporary cosmetic effect. Note any combination of answers that yields no coherent stance and explain why it is empty, and flag any two stances your tree cannot separate.
