I volunteer two mornings a week at our uni museum in Manchester and their policy around copies is doing my head in a bit. For the Roman bit they have locked the real coins away and put out these dead perfect 3D printed replicas instead, labels totally upfront about it, and school groups handle them and everyone is chuffed. But last term an alum donated this big moody landscape everyone adored until the conservator realised it was a Victorian pastiche deliberately aged to pass as a seventeenth century Dutch work, fake craquelure and all, and even though it looks exactly the same as when we loved it they hauled it down to storage in disgrace, while this scrappy little genuine sketch that is frankly less interesting to look at stays on the wall. I was arguing with my flatmates about it and we could not sort out what the museum actually beleives about why originals matter.

So what view of authenticity or aesthetic value does that kind of practice presuppose, if any? Does treating an honest replica as fine for display but a deceptive forgery as worthless even when it is more beautiful presuppose that the value is in the contact with the past or the makers intention rather than how it looks, or could you explain both decisions some other way like just caring about not misleading visitors? And is the setup even consistent, given they are happy for kids to get excited about handling a copy in one room but act like the painting in the other room lost all its worth the moment its history changed? Tell me what would have to change in how they label or show things if they actually thought value was just about visual experience, and where the current policy is underdetermined so a couple of different theories could still license it.
