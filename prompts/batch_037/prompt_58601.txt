I spend my workdays building and maintaining distributed backend systems, and I have never taken a philosophy course. Recently I ran into a metaphysical debate about how to talk about possibility that I found both intriguing and opaque.

As I understand it, one side holds that a single individual can itself be present in multiple ways things could have gone, so that when we consider what could have happened to a thing, we are talking about that very same thing located in another possibility. The other side holds that each individual exists in only one possibility, and when we consider what could have happened to a thing, we are really talking about a distinct but sufficiently similar individual in another possibility that serves as its stand-in.

I want to see how these two ideas look when forced into the conceptual tools I use every day. You may assume deep fluency on my part, from about eight years working on production distributed systems, with notions such as identity by persistent identifier versus equality by value, reference versus copy, primary keys versus content hashes, replicas and failover copies, serialization and deserialization, container images instantiated on different hosts, branches and forks in version control, and staging versus production environments, along with everyday operational concerns like deploys, rollbacks, and incident behavior.

Please proceed in order. First, restate each of the two views on possibility with maximal fidelity using only those software systems notions, without quietly importing software assumptions that the original views would reject. Second, apply both restatements to each of the following three cases: a payment authorization worker now running from image payments-v2.3 with a 2GB memory limit on a host in us-east-1b, considered as possibly having been started with a 4GB limit; a user profile row with a stable UUID created on Tuesday, considered as possibly having been created on Thursday with the same email address but a different signup IP and device fingerprint; and myself as a person, considered as possibly having left engineering after college to do woodworking full time. Third, give a candid accounting of what fails to carry over: which elements come across without loss, which elements require bending to fit, and which elements drop out entirely because my vocabulary provides no way to say them.

Finally, take a definite stand on how to interpret that remainder. Argue explicitly for whether it exposes a limitation in the software systems way of thinking about identity or reveals that the original metaphysical disagreement was less substantive than it appeared, and give your reasons. I am not looking for an evenhanded survey that leaves the choice to me; I want a committed conclusion with supporting argument.
