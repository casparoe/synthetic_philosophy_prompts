I just came out of a long argument with a friend about nuclear policy that kept stalling on a single proverb. We were discussing a proposal for mutual de-alerting — taking warheads off missiles to reduce the risk of an accidental launch — combined with a public pledge never to use nuclear weapons first. He opposed both ideas on the ground that any move toward restraint signals weakness and invites aggression, and whenever I pressed him on why restraint must invite aggression, or why a retaliatory threat that would be suicidal to carry out after deterrence has already failed should reassure anyone, he fell back on: if you want peace, prepare for war, as if that settled the case for keeping a large arsenal on hair-trigger alert and refusing to rule anything out.

I suspect that sentence is blurring together several very different claims, and I want to know which of them his argument actually needs. It could mean something empirical about how adversaries perceive vulnerability, something strategic about what makes a threat to retaliate credible when carrying it out would serve no further purpose, something about deliberately cultivating a reputation for recklessness or leaving escalation partly to chance, or something moral about the permissibility of planning for mass killing as a means to avoid it. Peace could mean no nuclear use, no conventional invasion, or no coercion at all, and preparation could mean merely possessing weapons, keeping them visibly ready to fire in minutes, or publicly committing to use them even when doing so would be pointless revenge.

Can you separate out the distinct precise theses hidden in that one line, from the near-trivial to the strongly paradoxical, and for each say whether it is plausible, whether it is true by definition, substantive but defensible, or false, with particular attention to the deterrence paradoxes about the rationality of forming intentions one would have no reason to execute and the logic of brinkmanship? Lay them out as a menu I can bring back to him, and tell me which readings, if any, would actually support the work he was making the slogan do — blocking de-alerting and a no-first-use pledge — and which would support only something much weaker.
