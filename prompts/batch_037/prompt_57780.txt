After a career in experimental physical chemistry, I have in retirement begun to read philosophy of explanation alongside economic history, and I find myself perplexed by a methodological point concerning large-scale historical contrasts.

In laboratory work, a question of the form why did outcome A occur here rather than outcome B there is well posed only when the two cases can plausibly be regarded as instances of the same kind of system, subject to a largely shared set of background conditions, so that the contrast picks out a differencemaker rather than a wholesale difference in kind. I am unsure whether the much-discussed historical problem concerning why industrialization and sustained modern economic growth began in Britain rather than in China satisfies this condition, and similarly for the related problem concerning why China, despite an early technological lead, did not undergo an indigenous scientific and industrial transformation.

Could you give me a careful conceptual explanation of what makes a contrastive why-question in history well posed or ill posed? I would find it helpful if the explanation addressed, in plain but precise terms, the idea of a contrast class and of presupposed background commonality; whether the two historical problems just described have the same logical form or whether one asks about acceleration in one place and the other about absence or delay in another; how the choice of temporal and geographic grain — a few decades around 1800 versus several centuries, Britain versus Europe, the Yangtze Delta versus China as a whole — changes what is being asked; and whether treating Britain and China as two trials of the same process is more like comparing two samples in one apparatus or more like comparing results from two quite different apparatuses.

To anchor the discussion, consider a homely analogy I have been turning over: two ponds in different valleys, one freezing over a week before the other. Under what conditions does it make sense to ask for the reason for the difference in freezing time, and under what conditions does that question dissolve into two separate questions about local microclimate, depth, and shelter? I would like to understand how, if at all, that distinction carries over to the Britain-China case.
