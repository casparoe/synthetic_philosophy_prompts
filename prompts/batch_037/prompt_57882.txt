I serve as the lead reviewer for safety incidents at a regional rail network that carries mixed freight and passenger traffic. Each month my team reviews roughly eight to twelve near misses and minor harms to determine which factors should be recorded as causes for regulatory reporting and for internal reform.

Our current practice is unstable. If we use simple counterfactual reasoning alone, almost every omission that would have prevented an outcome gets listed as a cause, from the dispatcher who did not issue an extra warning to the manager who did not schedule additional training two years earlier. If we instead rely on informal judgments about who should have acted, the results vary by reviewer and conflate causal analysis with blame.

Please provide a reusable decision procedure that my team can apply without training in philosophy, translating the central philosophical difficulty with causation by omission into workable steps: that counterfactual dependence is too permissive on its own, that appeals to norms and reasonable expectations can discipline it but risk turning causal judgments into moral judgments, and that there is a further distinction worth preserving between actively producing an outcome and merely failing to prevent it.

The procedure should be presented as an ordered checklist or flowchart in words, suitable for use in about forty-five minutes per case by engineers and dispatch supervisors, with final sign off by the operations director. For each step, state the question to be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer given the records typically available to an incident team, where the procedure terminates with a finding of no causation by omission, and where the team must stop and mark the issue as requiring evaluative judgment rather than further factual inquiry. Clearly mark the points where judgment cannot be eliminated and specify the hard cases the procedure must be able to handle, including multiple simultaneous omitters, omissions by automated monitoring systems, and cases where the relevant expectation or duty was vague or contested at the time.

Then apply the procedure step by step to the following three cases:

A delayed freight train with degraded brakes enters a downhill section during heavy rain. The dispatcher, who was monitoring three other movements, does not issue a discretionary slow order that was permitted but not required by the rule book. The train overshoots a signal and sideswipes a stabled passenger unit, causing minor injuries. An additional automated advisory that might have prompted the dispatcher was muted for maintenance.

A track inspector shortens a scheduled walking inspection because of heat restrictions and does not detect a cracked joint bar. Two days later the joint fails under a light passenger service, causing a low speed derailment with no injuries. A second inspector assigned to audit paperwork does not notice that the shortened inspection was recorded as complete.

A station team does not clear leaves from a rarely used emergency exit stairwell. During an evacuation drill, the exit is found blocked, but the drill completes within the target time using other exits. No harm occurs, although the safety manager argues the omission could have caused deaths under slightly different occupancy.

Do not adjust the verdicts to fit intuitive expectations about blame. If the procedure yields a result the operations staff will find unwelcome, for example by excluding an omission widely felt to be causal or by including one that spreads responsibility very broadly, retain the result and explain why it follows. Conclude with a brief statement of what the procedure cannot settle and where it is most likely to misfire in future cases.
