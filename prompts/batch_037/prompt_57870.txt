I am a novelist completing a draft whose central figure is a prudent, mathematically literate risk consultant, and I am anxious that her minor wagers and choices remain psychologically coherent across chapters rather than serving merely as plot conveniences. Her defining trait is a settled discomfort with imprecise chances, which I have tried to establish through a series of small, almost incidental decisions that a careful reader could track.

So far I have fixed the following dispositions for her. With an urn containing 30 red balls and 60 balls each either black or yellow in an unknown proportion, she prefers a $100 prize on red to the same prize on black, and she prefers the same prize on black-or-yellow to the same prize on red-or-yellow. With a second pair of urns, one with a known 50 red and 50 black composition and another with 100 balls each red or black in unknown proportion, she prefers a $100 prize on red from the known urn to the same prize on red from the unknown urn, even when the latter is sweetened by a few dollars. Offered two wholly ambiguous wagers on the first urn, $100 on black versus $100 on yellow, she is indifferent and would not pay even a trivial sum to exchange one for the other. In the chapter now under revision she must choose between retaining a $100 ticket on red from the first urn and exchanging it for a $100 ticket that pays if the ball is black and an independent fair coin lands heads, or if the ball is yellow and the coin lands tails. Predict which she would choose if she is to remain consistent, articulate the two or three distinct rules that each fit her earlier dispositions equally well, explain what each rule predicts about this coin-hedged exchange, offer a best guess with reasons for preferring one interpretation of her character, and describe one further wager I could stage in a later scene that would decisively separate those rules.
