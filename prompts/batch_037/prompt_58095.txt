This term I am leading an interdisciplinary undergraduate seminar on the limits of reason, and I have found that Gödel's incompleteness results function for many students as a kind of universal solvent, invoked to settle debates about machine minds, legal codification, ethics, and theology. I want to give them something more disciplined than a lecture on what the theorems actually say, namely a diagnostic decision tree handout that starts from an ambitious claim to the effect that Gödel shows something important outside mathematics and leads, through a series of neutral questions, to a verdict on that use.

To keep the exercise anchored, I have distilled four claims that came up in recent class discussions and that the tree must sort correctly: one holding that no AI system could ever match human mathematical insight because any consistent formal system leaves truths it cannot prove; one holding that any fully codified system of law must contain genuine legal questions it can neither decide nor leave undecided; one holding that a self-updating AI ethics framework escapes Gödelian worries entirely because it is not a fixed set of axioms but learns from experience; and one holding that theology is vindicated because there are profound truths that reason cannot establish. The tree should be built from the logical space rather than from labels, with early branches turning on whether the target domain even purports to be a formal system that is consistent, effectively axiomatizable, and expressive enough to encode basic arithmetic, whether the claim concerns provability inside the system versus truth or knowability in some broader sense, whether it depends on the system proving its own consistency, and whether it trades on a confusion with uncertainty, vagueness, or ordinary fallibility.

Please produce a tree with six to eight branch questions and six to seven leaves, including at least a legitimate or analogically careful extension, a philosophically serious but contestable use centered on minds and machines, a clear category mistake where formal notions do not apply, a confusion with a different limitative idea, and a trivialized use that is technically correct but philosophically empty. Phrase each branch as a concrete question a sophomore without prior logic could answer without knowing which leaf it leads to, so the instrument sorts by commitments rather than slogans, and show how each of my four test claims travels through the tree. Keep the whole handout self-contained with brief explanations of the logical ideas it uses and no reading lists or citations, flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate, and add a short note on at least one combination of answers that yields no coherent position, with a diagnosis of whether that corner is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
