As part of a project on designing a domestic assistant that learns household preferences from observed behavior, I have been attempting to systematize my own judgments about when an inference from behavior to underlying preference is warranted. The following cases illustrate my current views.

Case 1. Elena commutes to work by one of two routes, a 25 minute highway route and a 40 minute riverside route. When she must arrive by a fixed hour she takes the highway. When she leaves with ample time she consistently takes the riverside route, even when traffic reports indicate the time difference will hold. Verdict: it is warranted to infer a conditional preference for the scenic route over time saving when arrival time is not pressing.

Case 2. Marcus shops for dried beans. Both store brand and national brand are fully stocked. He pauses, compares unit prices, and selects the store brand. He does so on repeated visits. Verdict: it is warranted to infer a preference in this category for lower price over brand loyalty.

Case 3. Priya cares about limiting sugar intake. She selects a regular soda that carries a shelf label indicating low sugar content, having misread the label and having a false belief about the sugar content of the item she selected. Verdict: it is not warranted to infer a preference for high sugar content as such.

Case 4. David remains at the office until late evening on days when colleagues remain late, and joins them without protest. On days when he works remotely he logs off early and reports satisfaction with the shorter day. Verdict: it is not warranted to infer a preference for long work hours.

Case 5. Tomas pays a monthly pool membership, states a firm aim to swim before work, and installs phone blocking applications to prevent late night scrolling. He then repeatedly disables the blockers, remains in bed when the alarm sounds, and misses the morning session. Verdict: it is warranted to infer that at that hour he prefers additional sleep to swimming, notwithstanding the avowed longer term aim.

Case 6. Lena remains in a noisy apartment although quieter units at comparable rent are available nearby. She has investigated one alternative but has not pursued a move, largely because searching, coordinating schedules, and persuading a reluctant partner would require substantial effort. Verdict: it is not warranted to infer a preference for noise, or for the current building as such.

Cases 4 and 5 trouble me in combination, since both involve divergence between an avowed aim and situated choice, yet I treat only one as revealing preference. Cases 5 and 6 trouble me in a parallel way, since both involve repeated selection of the status quo where exit would be costly, yet I treat only one as revealing preference.

I would be grateful for an analysis that proposes a principle, or a small set of rival principles, stated with sufficient precision to explain the pattern above. For each qualification or clause in the proposed principle, please indicate which case motivates it. Please also state what the principle predicts for cases not listed here, in particular for a neighbor who donates readily when asked in person but never gives when unobserved, and for a traveler who always follows navigation system rerouting even on occasions when the rerouting adds delay without clear benefit. Where the principle cannot accommodate one of my provisional verdicts, please identify the verdict candidly and explain whether the cost of preserving it through added complexity is worth paying or whether the verdict should be revised.
