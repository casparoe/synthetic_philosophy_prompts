In my comparative politics seminar our lecturer insists that democratic backsliding can be applied as a nonpartisan concept, and she defends four judgments: Hungary after 2010 counts as backsliding because constitutional rewriting, court curbing and media capture durably weakened independent checks; Poland under Law and Justice counts as backsliding even though the opposition won in 2023, because the judicial overhaul attempted the same kind of capture whether or not it ultimately entrenched power; Turkey under Erdogan counts as backsliding, especially after the post-2016 emergency measures; the United States in 2017-2021 does not count as backsliding, despite norm-breaking and the attempt to overturn the 2020 result, because courts, press and electoral administration remained operationally independent and turnover occurred.

She has set as a test a hypothetical Republic of Arcadia: a prime minister re-elected with 64% of the vote enacts a severe defamation statute used to bankrupt two opposition newspapers and a ban on foreign funding that closes most election-monitoring NGOs, while also creating an independent electoral commission with an opposition veto, observing a two-term limit she herself proposed, and accepting opposition victories in recent mayoral races.

Predict what she would say about Arcadia if she is to remain consistent with her four prior verdicts. Identify at least two general rules that fit those verdicts equally well but diverge on Arcadia, state what each predicts and which prior case is the closest analog for each, give a best guess as to her implicit rule with reasons, and describe a further hypothetical case that would most cleanly separate the candidate rules.
