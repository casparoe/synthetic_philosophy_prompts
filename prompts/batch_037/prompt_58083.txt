A friend enthusiastic about the claim that the world has become less violent summarized the case for me roughly as follows: premodern societies had far higher rates of homicide and death in warfare, and the rise of centralized states, commerce, and wider circles of sympathy has produced a sustained decline across killing, war, and cruel punishments, so that even the large absolute death tolls of the twentieth century remain low once adjusted for population, which shows that contemporary life is less violent in the sense that matters morally. I am inclined to accept the population adjustment for comparing wars across eras with very different population sizes, but I am unsure whether that adjustment settles the evaluative question, or whether the evidence for fewer battlefield deaths carries over to violence more broadly. Which parts of this summary would defenders of the decline thesis accept as accurate, which conflate distinct claims they keep apart or overstate what the evidence shows, and does the moral conclusion follow from the corrected version?
