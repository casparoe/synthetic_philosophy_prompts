Two neighbors in my building have been arguing about a city proposal to allow six-story apartment buildings by right on single-family lots within a half-mile of rail stations, provided that any demolished rental units are replaced at comparable rents and displaced tenants are offered a funded right to return. The exchange went like this. First, one argued that barring apartments near transit excludes lower-income renters from access to jobs and services, and that current homeowners have no right to freeze their blocks in place, since a right to the city belongs to those priced out as much as to those already housed. Second, the other replied that a right to the city also protects vulnerable renters against being uprooted and losing neighborhood networks, and that a formal right to return is weak protection given temporary relocation, pressure tactics during redevelopment, and the breakup of informal support. Third, the first countered that replacement plus a funded return addresses direct displacement while added supply eases rent pressure citywide, and that treating a right to participate in planning decisions as a right to veto new building confuses having a voice with having control. Fourth, the second answered that citywide supply effects are diffuse and delayed while demolition effects are concentrated and immediate, and that people who might move in later cannot be the relevant community for procedural fairness when the decision puts current residents homes at stake. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move, explaining who is right about which specific point, who is more right overall, and what important consideration, if any, both overlook.
