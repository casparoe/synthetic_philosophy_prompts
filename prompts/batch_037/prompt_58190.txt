Consider the following finite two-player game. Row chooses among strategies A, B, and C, while Column chooses among L, M, and R. The entries give first the Row payoff, then the Column payoff:

If Row plays A: against L the payoffs are 10, 0; against M they are 0, 10; against R they are 6, 4.
If Row plays B: against L the payoffs are 0, 10; against M they are 10, 0; against R they are 6, 4.
If Row plays C: against L the payoffs are 4, 0; against M they are 4, 0; against R they are 5, 5.

Assume both players maximize expected utility, both know the payoff matrix, and each knows that the other is rational, knows that the other knows this, and so on.

Write a conceptual explanation, suitable for an advanced undergraduate who already understands strict dominance by a pure strategy but has not yet encountered dominance by a mixed strategy, that resolves three linked puzzles about this game.

First, explain why C is an unreasonable choice for Row even though neither A nor B always yields a higher payoff than C. Describe in terms of Row beliefs about Column play what belief would be required to make C attractive, why no such belief exists, and how a randomized mixture of A and B improves on C against every possible Column choice. Give the geometric or averaging intuition behind the dominating mixture rather than only checking inequalities.

Second, explain why R is an unreasonable choice for Column, but only after C has been removed from consideration. Show why R initially survives a strict dominance test, including by mixed strategies, yet becomes strictly dominated within the reduced game containing only A and B for Row. Clarify whether a strategy eliminated at one round may still be used as part of a mixture that dominates another strategy at a later round, and why the resolution of this question matters for the coherence of iterated elimination.

Third, explain what depth of mutual knowledge of rationality is needed to reach the final reduced game, and what fails if that depth is lacking. In particular, distinguish what follows from Row rationality alone, from Row rationality plus knowledge of Column rationality, and from further iterations, using the fates of C and R to illustrate the difference between a single round of elimination and common knowledge of rationality.

Throughout, emphasize intuition about beliefs, best responses, and expected payoffs over formal computation, but keep the reasoning precise enough that a reader could see why iterated elimination by mixed strict dominance captures something that elimination by pure strict dominance alone would miss.
