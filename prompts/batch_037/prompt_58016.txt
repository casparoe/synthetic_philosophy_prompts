I serve as vice-chair of the Planning Commission of a mid-sized coastal city that must decide Application 24-18 at our meeting next month.

The application seeks to rezone twelve contiguous single-family lots along the Elm Corridor, currently limited to detached homes under a 1974 downzoning, to allow four-story mixed-use buildings with 180 homes, ground-floor retail, and no minimum parking requirement. Under our inclusionary rule, 40 percent of the homes would be deed-restricted for households earning below the area median, with the remainder at market rates. Staff analysis finds the project would add an estimated 320 daily transit trips, comply with our climate action plan by locating housing near jobs and rail, and modestly reduce average rents citywide by adding supply, while increasing shadows and traffic on two adjacent blocks.

The Elm Neighborhood Association opposes the rezoning and offers a counterproposal: retain current zoning and create a small community land trust of eight permanently affordable homes on two donated lots. Its central arguments are that long-term owners reasonably relied on the existing zoning when purchasing, that neighborhood character and the scale of owner-occupied streets constitute a legitimate interest in self-determination, and that only residents most affected should have decisive voice.

Citywide tenant and labor groups support the application. They argue that the 1974 downzoning foreseeably excluded lower-income and minority households, that prospective residents who cannot currently afford to live in Elm have an equal moral claim to shape the city, and that allowing each block to veto new housing concentrates benefits on incumbents while dispersing costs of scarcity onto renters elsewhere.

Please draft a memorandum of 700 to 900 words for circulation to the full Commission recommending that we approve the application, approve it with specified conditions, or deny it in favor of the counterproposal. The memorandum should state a clear bottom line with implementable next steps, give principled reasons that address the competing claims about who is entitled to decide the future of the neighborhood and on what grounds, and engage seriously the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than dismissing it.
