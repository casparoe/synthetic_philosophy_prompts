I am a software engineer without formal philosophy background. In trying to state a rule for when our dashboard may list a service as healthy, I arrived at an argument with a conclusion I cannot accept, and I cannot see where it fails.
(1) If my evidence makes a claim at least 99% likely, I am rationally permitted to believe it outright.
(2) Rational outright beliefs must be consistent and closed under obvious entailment: if I am permitted to believe each of a set of claims, I am permitted to believe their conjunction.
(3) Suppose I monitor 200 independent services, with evidence making it 99.5% likely of each one that it is currently healthy.
(4) By (1), I am permitted to believe, of each service, that it is healthy.
(5) By (2), I am therefore permitted to believe that all 200 services are healthy at once.
(6) But the likelihood that all 200 are healthy at once is only about 37%, so by (1) I am not permitted to believe that conjunction, and indeed should suspend judgment on it.
(7) Hence (1) and (2) together both permit and forbid the same belief. The only consistent resolutions seem to be to require certainty for outright belief, or to abandon deductive closure entirely.
(8) Either resolution seems to prove too much: the first would forbid outright belief in almost everything I take myself to know about our systems, and the second would permit believing the premises of a valid argument while rejecting its conclusion.
Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost for ordinary reasoning of this kind? I would prefer to retain (1), since thresholds are how we gate releases in practice, but I want to understand what retaining it commits me to.
