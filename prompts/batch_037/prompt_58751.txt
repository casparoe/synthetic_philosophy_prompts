Please compose a deadpan parody, approximately 600 to 700 words, presented as a corporate job advertisement with an appended internal interview scoring rubric, issued by a fictional startup that claims to hire exclusively for stable moral character, understood as cross-situational honesty, compassion, and conscientiousness.

Adopt the voice of earnest human-resources optimism. The public advertisement should promise to identify genuinely virtuous candidates and to filter out mere situational compliance. The confidential rubric, printed below it, should quietly attempt to control for situational influences while revealing that the designers have only half understood them.

Take as given background for the piece the following situationist worries, which the satire should dramatize rather than explain: that a single observed act predicts later acts only weakly; that apparently trivial features of a situation such as hurry, mood, ambient noise, or the behavior of a confederate can substantially shift helping, honesty, and obedience; that behavioral consistency appears more clearly in patterns aggregated over many occasions than in any one occasion; and that virtue ethics traditionally responds by appealing to habituation, to practical wisdom as sensitivity to morally relevant features of situations, and to the rarity of complete virtue.

Stage the assessment around three standardized micro-situations you invent, for example a rushed hallway encounter, a tedious task with an opportunity for small dishonesty, and an ambiguous instruction from a supervisor, and let the comedy arise from the rubric's increasingly elaborate efforts to hold the situation constant while manipulating it.

Remain legible without winking: a reader unfamiliar with the debate should sense that something has gone wrong with the hiring model, while a reader familiar with it should recognize a fair exaggeration of both the critique and the standard replies.
