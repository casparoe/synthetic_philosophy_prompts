I would appreciate a careful adjudication of a sustained disagreement I witnessed between two members of our university sustainability committee concerning individual abstention from factory-farmed meat.

The committee was discussing a proposed voluntary pledge to forgo meat on weekdays. I will call the two participants A and B. Their exchange unfolded in four stages.

A opened by arguing that an individual decision to forgo a single packet of chicken breasts at a large supermarket is causally inert. Orders, A said, are placed in large lots on the basis of forecasts with substantial slack, and shortfalls in demand are absorbed by spoilage, markdowns, and buffer stock rather than by reduced production upstream. Hence, while collective regulation of production could make a large difference, one person's abstention makes no difference to the number of animals raised or to emissions, and cannot be morally required on the basis of its consequences.

B answered with an expected-effect argument. B conceded that most abstentions change nothing observable, but maintained that this is compatible with a significant expected benefit. On B's illustration, if roughly one in several thousand forgone purchases is the one that tips the retailer below a reorder threshold for a case representing several hundred birds, then averaged over many such decisions each abstention prevents a fraction of a bird's suffering and associated emissions. A small probability multiplied by a sizable harm averted, B claimed, still yields a morally non-negligible expectation.

A rejoined that real supply systems do not present the clean thresholds B's arithmetic assumes. The store, A noted, reorders on a rolling average with a standing upward buffer and rounds up, so the chance of being the pivotal purchase is far lower than one divided by lot size, and a foregone order that does reduce a shipment often merely reduces downstream waste rather than upstream placements. A added that purchases are socially correlated rather than independent, which complicates simple multiplication of a per-person probability, and that once highly uncertain thresholds are admitted, the reasoning becomes unstable, since tiny chances of very large shifts could dominate the calculation in implausible ways.

B then supplemented the causal claim with a non-causal one. Even granting that the supply-chain effect is negligible, B argued, buying the product still involves participating in and normalizing a practice one judges to be seriously wrong, whereas abstaining expresses that judgment and helps shift social norms. B suggested this is why we recognize reasons to vote or not to litter despite negligible individual causal power: fairness and integrity require doing one's share in a collective pattern, not merely producing a detectable difference alone.

A closed by contending that the expressive and participatory considerations either collapse back into causal claims about influencing others, which face the same inefficacy worry at one remove, or amount to concern for the agent's own cleanliness rather than for animals or the climate. Doing one's share, A said, has clear content within an organized cooperative scheme with assigned roles, but an isolated consumer choice without such a scheme does not create a share in that sense.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move, explaining for each stage whether the reasoning is sound, where it equivocates or overreaches, and who has the stronger point. Conclude with an overall assessment of which position is better supported as stated and identify any important consideration bearing on individual contributions to collective harms that neither participant raised.
