My friend and I just spent several hours arguing about whether the AI alignment problem should be understood narrowly, and I am hoping to get some distance on the disagreement by thinking it through in connection with fiction. My friend defends a narrow, intent-centered view, roughly that alignment is a matter of getting an AI system to do what a particular principal intends or instructs it to do, with all the hard work located in correctly inferring instructions, handling underspecification, and remaining obedient without overstepping latitude. I was pushing a broader, value-centered view, roughly that even a system that perfectly captures immediate intent can be badly misaligned if it helps bring about an outcome the person would reject on reflection, when calm, informed, and thinking clearly about what matters to them. My friend insisted that classic wish-gone-wrong stories vindicate the narrow view, because the tragedy looks like a failure to specify intent precisely enough, while I came away thinking those same stories show why intent is not enough.

This is where I would like a philosophical reading of Jacobs's story about the monkey's paw and the White family, which I take to be familiar enough that we can discuss it from memory without needing exact wording. As I remember the central beats, Sergeant-Major Morris warns the Whites about a talisman that grants wishes with terrible side effects, Mr. White wishes for a sum of money to finish paying for the house, his son Herbert dies in a factory machinery accident and the company pays exactly that sum as compensation, Mrs. White in grief persuades her husband to wish her son back, something knocks persistently at the door late at night, and Mr. White uses the final wish to undo the second before his wife can open the door. My friend says the paw is actually a perfect narrow-alignment agent that does exactly what is asked and that the fault lies entirely in the Whites stating their wishes badly, so a more careful specification plus better intent inference would have solved everything. I suspect the story complicates that, since the Whites arguably get the literal content they request each time while missing what they care about most, avoiding grief, keeping their son alive in a recognizable form, not being haunted by a reanimated corpse, and the forewarning and dread throughout suggest they lack the information and emotional clarity to form the instructions a narrowly obedient agent would need.

Please act as a professional philosopher with expertise in both AI alignment theory and philosophy of literature, and help me sort this out for an essay I am planning. Start with brief brainstorming in which you lay out two or three competing ways to map intent versus value alignment onto the three wishes, then think carefully step by step through which mapping is most faithful to both the philosophy and the story. I want a reading that takes the details seriously, explains where the concepts illuminate particular choices by Mr. and Mrs. White and Morris, acknowledges where the story resists being treated as an AI thought experiment and where the analogy to obedience, loyalty, latitude, and informed preferences breaks down, and concludes with what the story contributes to the narrow versus broad debate that abstract argument alone does not capture.
