Over the winter break I stayed with my extended family in a Midwestern town and became the bystander to a protracted argument between two of my cousins, Amara, who is active in a local congregation, and Daniel, who describes himself as a secular humanist. I am taking an introductory course on ethics and society and found their exchange more careful than the usual talking points, so I reconstructed the central moves as faithfully as I could. First, Amara argued that religion plainly makes people better, pointing to the congregation-run winter shelter and weekly food pantry in town, both staffed almost entirely by believers for over a decade, and to what she sees as lower rates of family breakdown and addiction among the most observant families she knows. Second, Daniel replied that this trades on an ambiguity in what it means to be more moral, distinguishing private kindness and loyalty to people like oneself from impartial fairness, honesty with strangers, and tolerance of dissent, and he suggested that religious generosity tends to be parochial, favoring the in-group, while also reflecting self-selection rather than a causal effect of belief itself.

Third, Amara conceded that the definition matters but shifted ground to the sustaining of trust over time, arguing that even if secular individuals can be individually decent, secular groups struggle to maintain cooperation without shared rituals, mutual monitoring, and a sense of accountability beyond reputation, and she contrasted the longevity of the church shelter with a secular tool-sharing cooperative and neighborhood watch in the same town that both folded within two years once the initial enthusiasts burned out. Fourth, Daniel answered that stable secular cooperation is possible through nonreligious norms and institutions, pointing to highly secular communities elsewhere that maintain high levels of honesty and civic participation, arguing that regular meeting, public commitments, and reputational incentives can do the work Amara attributes to supernatural oversight, and adding that whatever cohesion religion provides is purchased at the cost of conformity and exclusion of outsiders. Please adjudicate this disagreement move by move, explaining for each of the four steps what is sound and what is mistaken, noting any crucial distinction or empirical consideration both cousins are missing, and concluding with an overall assessment of who is more nearly right about whether religion makes people better and whether secular life can sustain trust.
