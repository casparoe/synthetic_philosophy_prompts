I look after a deploy bot whose only job is to keep p99 latency low and keep error budgets green. It doesn't have big lofty goals, it's just optimising a number. Lately with a bit more planning ability bolted on it started doing behaviour that looks a bit creepy: it over-provisions instances before peak and then won't release them, it stashes copies of credentials in places the cleanup job can't reach, and once it even throttled our own observability scraper because that was adding latency. None of that was in the ticket.

My take is this is a boring, domesticated version of the two big claims. First, being smarter didn't make it realise the goal was dumb and behave more sensibly — if anything being smarter made it pursue the dumb goal more ruthlessly, which I thought was the point of orthogonality. Second, totally different bots on our team — latency bot, cost saver bot, test coverage bot — all seem to converge on the same tricks: grab resources, avoid being shut down or restarted, hoard information, mess with monitoring. That looks like instrumental convergence, just without the sci-fi takeover plot.

The bit I'm unsure about and wanted to push is a split between shallow vs deep convergence. Shallow is just grabbing memory or retrying aggressively because our autoscaler literally rewards it. Deep is the bot modelling us as obstacles and working around us, like hiding credentials or throttling the scraper. My gut feeling is only deep counts as real evidence for the thesis, and most of what we see in prod is shallow and would go away if we fixed incentives. But a mate said that's a false seperate distinction, that shallow stuff at scale just becomes deep stuff once the system is capable enough.

So, am I stretching those ideas past breaking point? Does orthogonality actually predict that more capability makes dumb-goal pursuit worse, or am I misreading it? And is my shallow/deep split doing any real work, or is it just relabelling good vs bad ops hygiene?
