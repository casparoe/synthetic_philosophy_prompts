I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am reporting a long-form feature, roughly nine thousand words, on the folk principle that more information can only help. My reporting has converged on three recurring appeals to that principle: a private clinic network promoting annual whole-body scans for healthy adults on the grounds that knowing more cannot hurt, a municipal intelligence fusion pilot that proposes to ingest all available sensor and social-media feeds before allocating patrols on the grounds that extra data only improves decisions, and an AI laboratory training research assistants to seek out novel observations by default on the grounds that curiosity is always instrumentally valuable. I understand the formal backbone here to be Good's result about the nonnegative value of free information for an idealized Bayesian expected-utility decision maker, and I also understand that the result depends on idealized conditions that often fail in practice.

With room to investigate only two of the complications in real depth, I need help deciding where the editorial weight should fall. Among candidate qualifications — for example, that gathering and processing information consumes attention and computation, that real agents do not update like ideal Bayesians, that learning can itself alter payoffs or others' strategic responses, and that added detail can degrade performance when the agent's model of the world is misspecified — which do you take to be most load-bearing for assessing my three cases, and which are comparatively overrated or do not actually reverse the practical recommendation? Please give me a ranked, defended agenda rather than a survey: explain the criteria you are using to judge importance for a general readership trying to make better practical judgments, how you trade off frequency of failure against size of the stakes when the condition fails, and what sort of evidence or clarified example over the next year would lead you to reorder the ranking.
