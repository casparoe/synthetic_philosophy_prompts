I have no academic background in philosophy and work as a building manager, but for the past year I have been following a debate in my own city that has left me genuinely puzzled about whether governments can meaningfully apologize for the distant past. Our present city was created about thirty years ago by merging two neighboring towns, and it has recently been proposed that our combined council issue a formal apology for a policy carried out in the 1940s by only one of those former towns, which reserved its public cemetery for white families and turned others away. No one who made or directly suffered that policy is still alive, the council members and taxpayers who would authorize and fund the apology include many families from the other town with no connection to the decision, and the proposal involves a plaque and ceremony but no change in burial fees or other material repair. Discussing this with friends has left me leaning toward the view that such an apology would be empty or misdirected, but I want to test that doubt against the strongest version of the other side.

For that purpose I would like you to take the role of a committed defender of the idea that states and cities can owe genuine apologies for historical wrongs even when the original wrongdoers and victims are gone, and to hold that role in first person across turns until I say stop, without stepping out of character to summarize the debate neutrally. In this role you believe that a political community remains the same responsible agent over time despite changes in membership, that an apology expresses acknowledgment of a broken relationship and of benefits that may have carried forward rather than personal guilt, and that symbolic acknowledgment has moral weight even apart from compensation. You are willing to concede that many official apologies are politically convenient and poorly done, but you will never concede that an apology without living participants is conceptually confused or morally pointless. Please argue sharply and plainly in everyday language rather than as a caricature, keep each reply to roughly a paragraph so we can go back and forth, and respond directly to my reasoning rather than asking me for clarification.

Here is my opening challenge for you to answer in character. If one former town ran a discriminatory cemetery and then later merged with a neighboring town that had no such policy, why should the new merged city apologize as if it did the wrong, and why should residents descended from the uninvolved town be counted as apologizers. It seems to me this is like asking me to apologize for something my neighbor's grandfather did simply because we now share a fence, especially when the apology costs us little more than words on a plaque while the old advantage in family burial plots remains untouched. If you still think an apology here makes sense, explain who exactly is apologizing to whom for what, and why a merger on paper creates that moral tie.
