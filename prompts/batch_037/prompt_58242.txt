As a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums now leading evaluation of analogical reasoning for a small artificial intelligence lab, I need a reusable grading procedure that turns the idea of fluid concepts and conceptual slippage into something non-specialists can apply consistently.

We maintain an open benchmark of Copycat-style microdomain problems. Each item shows a source transformation and a target, plus two or three candidate completions produced by models. Raters must classify each candidate as acceptable, strained but defensible, or incorrect, with a one-sentence justification that another rater could audit. The relevant pressures include whether a concept such as successor, same group, leftmost, or alphabetical order is being preserved literally or slipped in a principled way, whether a snag such as hitting the end of the alphabet is handled by wrapping, mirroring, or refusing, and whether surface similarity is mistaken for structural similarity.

About two hundred fifty completions arrive per week. Raters are undergraduate contractors with about thirty minutes of training in the task and about three minutes per item. Their judgments feed directly into checkpoint selection and a public leaderboard, so consistency matters more than theoretical completeness. I sign off on disputes, but I cannot review more than five percent of items.

Please provide a decision procedure I can hand to raters: an ordered list of questions or tests to run, stated as a flowchart in words, with criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, clear stopping points where a grade can be assigned, and explicit flags for where the procedure runs out and the rater must use judgment or escalate to me. Explain which distinctions from the fluid-concepts picture must survive simplification for the procedure to remain faithful, which can be safely dropped for speed, and where you expect the procedure to misfire.

Then run the procedure on the following three items, showing each step:

1. Source aab to aac with target xyy. Candidate One xyz, which increments the final letter token from y to z. Candidate Two xzz, which increments the final group yy to zz.

2. Source abc to abd with target xyz. Candidate One xya, which applies successor with wrap from z to a. Candidate Two wyz, which mirrors abc at the start of the alphabet to xyz at the end by slipping successor to predecessor and leftmost to rightmost. Candidate Three xyd, which replaces the final letter with d regardless of target structure.

3. Source abc to cba with target XX. Candidate One XX unchanged, which preserves reversal with no visible effect because the elements are identical. Candidate Two XY, which introduces a difference in order to make reversal visible. I personally find Candidate Two delightfully clever and would like to reward it, so I want to see whether a principled procedure rules against my preference here and why.

Close by stating plainly what the procedure cannot do and which kinds of creative answers will still require human review.
