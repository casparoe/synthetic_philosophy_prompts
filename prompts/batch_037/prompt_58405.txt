Suppose you are advising a small interdisciplinary research group with one year of support to clarify anthropic reasoning for use in practical forecasting about technological risk and the search for extraterrestrial life. The group begins from two competing ideas, often called the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption. The former holds that you should reason as if you are randomly sampled from among all observers with your evidence, while the latter holds that hypotheses predicting more observers deserve proportionally higher prior probability. The two agree in many cases but diverge when rival hypotheses predict very different numbers of observers, as in the familiar presumptuous philosopher thought experiment in which theorists are asked to favor one of two cosmological theories largely because one predicts vastly more observers than the other.

The group is considering three lines of conceptual work: first, developing a nonarbitrary way of deciding which entities count as relevant observers when candidates include present humans, future humans, digital minds, nonhuman animals, and extraterrestrials; second, determining whether either idea genuinely licenses confident conclusions about cosmology or about the long-run size of civilization ahead of empirical evidence, and what principled limits would block such armchair inference; third, clarifying how anthropic reasoning should interact with decisions that themselves influence how many observers will exist, such as funding projects that would create many simulated observers or interpreting the fact of our own existence as evidence about extinction risk.

Which of these lines of work should the group prioritize, and in what order? Defend your ranking by explicit criteria such as importance for forecasters, tractability with mainly philosophical methods, and how much progress on one question would unblock the others, noting trade-offs between them and what evidence or argument over the coming year would lead you to revise the ordering.
