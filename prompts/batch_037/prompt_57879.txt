I am a third-year undergraduate majoring in philosophy and journalism, currently enrolled in a seminar on the ethics of speech where we have been examining the distinction between outright lying and merely misleading, and whether that distinction carries moral weight.

Alongside my coursework, I serve as a section editor for our university student newspaper, where I review roughly a dozen news summaries, headlines, and opinion drafts each week under significant time pressure. Many of the problems I encounter do not involve clear falsehoods but involve technically true wording that still creates a false impression through selective emphasis, omission of relevant context, misleading implicature, or presupposition.

I would like a reusable decision procedure that undergraduate editors without philosophical training could apply in about fifteen minutes per piece to determine when a draft crosses from acceptable framing into deceptive communication, and what to do about it. The procedure should distinguish, in practical terms, asserting as fact something the writer believes to be false while expecting to be taken sincerely from conveying a false impression while staying literally true, and it should explain how that distinction should matter for editorial action. Please structure it as an ordered sequence of questions or tests, specify what would count as a satisfactory resolution at each step, indicate where the process should stop with a requirement to revise, and identify explicitly where further judgment by a senior editor is unavoidable rather than resolvable by rule.

Please then demonstrate the procedure on three fictional drafts I have constructed for this purpose: first, a news brief stating that reported bike thefts fell by a third after expanded patrols, which omits that the reporting window was shortened; second, an opinion column that repeatedly asks when an administrator will finally account for misused activity fees, without ever asserting that any misuse occurred; third, a headline that is literally accurate but strongly suggests a more dramatic finding than the article supports, of the sort our staff is reluctant to change because it increases readership. The third case should test whether the procedure can deliver an unwelcome verdict. Conclude by noting the central limitations of the procedure and the kinds of cases it cannot settle on its own.
