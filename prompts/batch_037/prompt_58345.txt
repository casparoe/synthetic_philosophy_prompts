Please develop a reusable decision procedure grounded in updateless decision theory for the following operational context. I chair a weekly deployment review board at a regional freight company that operates a fleet of autonomous dispatch and negotiation agents, all running copies of the same decision code. Several times per month we must approve behavioral policies for situations in which outside parties can anticipate our behavior, or in which many copies of our own system will face the same choice. Board members are software engineers and operations managers with no training in formal decision theory, we have roughly twenty-five minutes per proposal, and I provide final sign-off where stakes typically involve costs in the low thousands of dollars plus reputational effects.

Provide a procedure of no more than seven ordered steps that translates the central idea of updateless reasoning — selecting a prior policy by considering what would happen if all predictably similar instances followed it, rather than optimizing afresh upon arrival in the situation — into plain language an engineer can apply. For each step specify the question to be answered, what kind of evidence or modeling counts as a sufficient answer under our time constraints, and when to stop with a refusal to deploy versus escalate for further analysis. Mark explicitly the points where factual estimation ends and value judgment or tolerance for counterintuitive risk must take over.

Then demonstrate the procedure end to end on the three cases below, showing the reasoning at each step:

Case A: Two trucks from our own fleet, running identical code, converge on a single narrow loading bay where mutual insistence causes costly delay for both, while unilateral yielding costs only the yielder a smaller delay.

Case B: A fuel vendor offers our fleet a priority contract that is profitable for the vendor only if we will refrain from diverting to a competitor during rare price spikes. The vendor decides using a statistical model trained on our published policy and past behavior, and its prediction will be available before we face an actual spike where diversion would save us money.

Case C: A municipal regulator runs a high-fidelity simulation of our dispatch code to determine whether we would have violated an overnight routing restriction last quarter, and will waive a substantial fine only if the simulation shows consistent compliance. We now have the opportunity to patch the code to be compliant going forward, knowing the patch will also affect what the simulation reports about the past version.

Include among the demonstrations at least one verdict that will strike the board as ex post irrational, and explain why the procedure nevertheless endorses it. Close with a concise statement of what the procedure cannot settle and where it is most likely to misfire in our setting.
