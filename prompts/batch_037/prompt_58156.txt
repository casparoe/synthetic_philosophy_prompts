I'm a backend software engineer, I mostly do Go and on-call rotations, no formal philosophy background at all, and this has been bugging me since a rough incident week. On my team we have like 180ish cron jobs and data pipelines, and before every big release each owner has to click approve to say yeah I beleive my job will run clean. But then in the same quarterly plan we explicitly set aside error budget and pager time because we all expect that two or three of those jobs will silently fail or need a rollback. I was venting about this to friends over discord and one of them said it sounds like that lottery paradox thing where you think each ticket will lose but also that someone will win, plus that preface paradox where an author thinks each chapter is correct but also that the book as a whole has a mistake in it somewhere.

So what are we actually assuming about belief when we do both of those things at once, and are the two halves of our process even assuming the same thing? Like does our approve-each-job practice only make sense if you can rationally beleive a bunch of things that don't all fit together, or is there a way to redescribe what we're doing so it's consistent, maybe that approving just means probable enough to ship and not actually true? And if we ditched the yes-or-no approve button entirely and just made everyone put a number on it and act purely on probabilities for paging and rollbacks, what would have to change in practice to keep it workable?
