After a semester of journal clubs on failed replications in social psychology and cognitive neuroscience, and after watching my own lab debate whether to ban p-values in favor of Bayes factors with default priors, I have become sympathetic to a strong diagnostic claim: that the replication crisis is fundamentally a frequentist artifact, in that reliance on null hypothesis significance testing, with its sharp thresholds, optional stopping in practice, and confusion of statistical with substantive significance, manufactured a literature of fragile positives that a routine Bayesian workflow — explicit priors, posterior intervals, and graded evidence — would have prevented from counting as discoveries in the first place.

I want to pressure-test that diagnosis rather than rehearse its virtues. Suppose this Bayesian-switch story is wrong or overstated: what kinds of findings, arguments, or developments should I take as telling against it, and how should I weight them? Please lay out the considerations that would most seriously undermine the claim alongside those that would merely complicate it, including whether there are already known patterns — for instance, Bayesian analyses that replicate no better, ways that priors and Bayes factors can be gamed or can hide the same researcher flexibility, or cases where preregistered, high-powered frequentist work does replicate reliably — that its advocates should admit as genuine costs. I am especially moved by head-to-head comparisons and by demonstrations that the proposed cure preserves the disease under a new name, more so than by abstract philosophy of probability, and I would like to know what, if anything, the diagnosis uniquely predicts that rival explanations centered on incentives, theory weakness, or low base rates do not. Finally, give a candid judgment on whether defenders have rendered the claim effectively immune to counterevidence by retreating to a vaguer appeal to good research practices, and whether such insulation, if present, actually undermines this sort of methodological position.
