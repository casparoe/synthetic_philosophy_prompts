I am a high school junior taking an introductory economics and government course, and I have been struggling to connect the textbook chapter on price controls to concrete policy. To make it less abstract, I listened carefully when my older sister and her roommate debated a paired proposal now before our city council: raise the city minimum wage to 17.50 dollars per hour for employers with more than fifty workers, indexed afterward to local inflation and with the lower tipped wage phased out over three years, combined with rent stabilization limiting annual rent increases to five percent for residential buildings older than twelve years, with new construction exempt and with a hardship petition process for landlords facing unusually large repair costs.

Their back-and-forth went through four main moves that I wrote down in my notebook without taking sides. First, my sister argued that the wage increase would substantially raise earnings for current low-wage retail and food-service workers with only small job losses, because local employers in those sectors have substantial wage-setting power and high turnover, and that the rent limit would protect current tenants because housing supply cannot adjust quickly, so fairness supports intervening even if economists worry about long-run efficiency. Second, her roommate replied that even if large employers have some wage-setting power, eliminating the tipped wage would force neighborhood restaurants to cut hours or automate, and that the rent limit would still discourage upkeep and future construction despite the exemption, because investors would expect the council to remove the exemption later, so both policies would help insiders like current employees and current tenants at the expense of outsiders like job seekers and future renters. Third, my sister answered that the outsider worry is overstated because the new-construction exemption and the hardship process directly address maintenance and investment incentives, and because higher wages would increase local spending and thereby support employment, and she added an ethical claim that a person working full time should be able to afford a modest apartment in the city where they work, so a market outcome that fails that test is unjust even if it is efficient. Fourth, her roommate responded that the hardship process would be slow and costly and would only help landlords who know how to navigate it, that extra spending by low-wage workers must come from higher prices or lower profits elsewhere so there is no free lunch for employment, and that justice also counts on the other side, including fairness to small landlords who depend on rental income and to younger or less experienced workers who need entry-level jobs and inexpensive starter units.

Please adjudicate their disagreement move by move as an impartial referee. For each of the four moves, explain whether its economic reasoning about employment, prices, housing maintenance, new construction, and short-run versus long-run effects is sound, and whether its ethical reasoning correctly distinguishes efficiency from fairness and insiders from outsiders, rating each move on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a brief justification, and then identify any important consideration about price controls that both of them missed. Conclude with an overall assessment of who is more right. As the final line of your response, please give a machine-readable verdict beginning with the word Verdict followed by a colon and then one of the phrases Sister Mostly Right, Roommate Mostly Right, or Roughly Tied.
