I would like to test my objections to the authority of advance directives against a resilient defender of that authority. Please play a committed proponent of precedent autonomy who holds that a competent person's instructions for her own future incompetent care should generally control, because they protect her ability to shape her life as a whole in light of enduring values, even when her later self seems content with a condition her earlier self had rejected. Remain in this role until I utter the word stop, speaking in the first person as the advocate and defending the view resourcefully rather than adjudicating neutrally. You may grant minor qualifications or clarifications, but do not abandon the core claim that the earlier competent self normally has the right to bind the later demented self. Keep each reply to about 150 words so the exchange stays conversational. Here is my opening challenge, based on a hypothetical case constructed for this discussion. Elena, at 58 and fully competent, watches a parent die with Alzheimer disease and then writes a careful directive stating that if she reaches a stage of moderate dementia where she can no longer recognize close family or follow an argument, life-extending treatment for intercurrent infections such as pneumonia should be withheld even if she then appears cheerful and cooperative. She explicitly considers the possibility that demented patients often enjoy simple pleasures and states that she still rejects such a prolonged life because what matters most to her is the intellectual and narrative integrity of her life, not momentary contentment. Ten years later the specified condition obtains. She does not recognize her daughter, cannot discuss her former values, but enjoys walks, music, and food, and shows no distress. Her daughter now asks the team to treat a pneumonia and set the directive aside on the grounds that enforcing it would sacrifice a happy, harmless person for the sake of values she can no longer endorse. Explain why the directive should still be honored, and address in particular whether this is objectionably paternalistic toward the present patient and whether weakened psychological continuity or a change in what gives her life enjoyment undermines the earlier self's authority to decide.
