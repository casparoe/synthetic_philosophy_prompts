Over the past eight years building distributed backend systems, I have repeatedly encountered a form of uniformity that puzzles me, and I have no formal training in philosophy to place it. I would like to understand whether this pattern has been isolated and analyzed elsewhere, and if so how it is properly bounded.

A first instance arose within a team of about a dozen engineers maintaining separate microservices. Over roughly a year, our error responses converged on the same envelope: a numeric status, a stable machine-readable code, a human-readable message, and an additional field indicating whether the caller should retry and after what delay. No architecture document initially required this shape, and several leaner shapes would have worked equally well from a purely technical point of view. Early services happened to include the retry hint, later services copied them to keep client code uniform, and after a point a new service that omitted the hint would still build and pass tests yet would create integration friction and attract critical review comments. What struck me is that each team seemed to follow the shape mainly because it expected the other teams to keep following it, and each would have been content to move together to a different equally workable shape if the others moved.

A second instance spans organizations. Different client and server teams with no shared management settled on compatible practices for safe retries, including sending a client-generated idempotency key on certain requests and backing off with randomized delays after failures. Again the exact details, such as where the key travels and how it is formatted, could have been settled differently without loss of function, and conformity seems sustained by mutual anticipation rather than by any single decision.

These cases feel different to me from two other kinds of uniformity. One is uniformity imposed by automated checks, such as a formatter enforced by continuous integration that rejects nonconforming code regardless of what anyone expects. The other is uniformity required by technical necessity, where only one shape will parse or only one protocol sequence will succeed. They also feel different from mere personal habit, since the reason to continue seems essentially social: unilateral deviation is costly even when everyone privately agrees an alternative would be just as good, while coordinated deviation would be unproblematic.

I am also interested in the emergence over time. No vote is taken, yet a small early precedent gets copied, copying creates an expectation of further copying, and the practice then locks in without anyone having chosen it.

A bare label would not help me. I wonder whether philosophers or students of social order have distinguished this expectation-sustained, could-have-been-otherwise kind of regularity from nearby phenomena such as tool-enforced compliance, technically forced uniformity, and private habit. If such a distinction exists, what is the closest characterization, where does my pattern fit imperfectly, and what are the precise inclusion and exclusion criteria with difficult boundary cases made explicit. If no existing characterization fits, what definition would you propose that captures the two engineering instances while excluding the contrast cases.
