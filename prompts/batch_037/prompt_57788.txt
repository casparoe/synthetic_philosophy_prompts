As a retired plant physiologist who has taken up philosophy in retirement, I find myself perplexed by the long-running dispute over causation by omission.

In my former greenhouse work I treated a technician forgetting to water seedlings, a sensor failing to trigger shading, and an applied heat shock as similarly explanatory of crop outcomes, yet I now encounter the view that only positive happenings can be causes, while omissions merely reflect norms or explanatory interests.

To sharpen the issue consider a pair of cases of my own devising: in one, an automated chamber is programmed to mist at noon and a power cut prevents it, killing the cuttings; in the other, the misting routine is never written in the first place, with the same result.

Is the continued disagreement here chiefly verbal, turning on how broadly to use causal language, or substantive, turning on what there is or what does explanatory work, or does it persist because each side privileges different paradigm cases?

Please defend a diagnosis with reference to how each side would treat my two cases, and close by identifying where your argument is most vulnerable and what consideration would cause you to change your assessment.
