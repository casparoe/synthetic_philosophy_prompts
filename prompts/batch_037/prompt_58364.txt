In early June I observed a prolonged disagreement between two acquaintances, Lena and Marco, who each coordinate a different neighbourhood association bordering the same system of municipal parks. For context, the city public health department has announced a seasonal program involving the park-wide release of lab-reared sterile male mosquitoes intended to suppress the local disease-carrying mosquito population. Under the program rules, a formal request from any one of the six eligible associations bordering the parks is sufficient to trigger the release across all of the parks, and once triggered the release cannot be recalled until next season. Both associations have access to the same entomologist report and pilot trial data, and both currently estimate roughly a two-in-three chance that the release would do more good than harm, with the main downside risk being poorly understood effects on pollinators.

Their exchange went through four stages. Lena began by arguing that a positive expected value on shared evidence is a sufficient reason for either association to file the request if it has the opportunity, and that it would be odd to call it rational for each group in isolation but irrational for them together. Marco replied that this ignores the selection effect created by unilateral power: when six groups will each act on a favorable private assessment, the group with the most optimistic assessment determines the outcome, so even well-calibrated groups will trigger too many interventions that are in fact net harmful.

Lena responded that the selection effect assumes independent judgments, which is unrealistic here because the groups are reading the same report and trial data, so their assessments will rise and fall together; she added that caution creates a symmetric error, since if a beneficial release is delayed because each group holds back for fear of being the optimist, the collective misses a good outcome. Marco answered that shared evidence attenuates but does not remove the bias unless the correlation is perfect, since any residual private judgment still makes the maximum more optimistic than the average, and that symmetry fails because the errors are not symmetric in this case: an ill-advised release is season-long and ecologically irreversible, whereas a delayed release can be revisited next year, so the threshold for action should be higher than bare positive expected value.

Lena closed by arguing that a general norm of raising the threshold hands disproportionate power to the most cautious or most misinformed actor, effectively allowing a single pessimist to veto a beneficial intervention, and that since the associations can speak to one another, the appropriate remedy is an explicit coordination mechanism rather than unilateral restraint exercised in the dark, which merely privileges inaction without justification. I found both lines of argument plausible and could not determine who had the better of each exchange.

Please adjudicate the disagreement move by move, explaining who is correct on each specific point and identifying any shared blind spot.

Shared Oversights, about 200 words, describing any important consideration neither party adequately addresses; 4.
