I am a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar on practical rationality and decision theory, and I am trying to systematize my judgments about cases in which unrecoverable past investments seem to matter for what it is rational to do now.

The simple view presented in class holds that rational choice should depend only on prospective costs and benefits, so that bygones are bygones. I find this compelling in the abstract, but my reactions to particular cases do not line up neatly, and I would like help finding a principle that captures them or showing where they conflict.

Consider the following cases and my provisional verdicts:

First, a concert case. I have purchased a nonrefundable ticket for an evening performance. When the evening arrives I am exhausted, and I judge that, setting aside the money already spent, I would clearly prefer to stay home and rest. If I go only in order not to waste the money, I take this to be irrational, a paradigm instance of the mistake.

Second, an entrepreneurial case. A founder has invested three years and nearly all of her savings in a startup. Market feedback is consistently negative, with no new evidence that prospects have improved. She continues to invest further time and money primarily to avoid rendering the earlier investment pointless. I take this continuation to be irrational as well.

Third, a doctoral case. A student is in the fourth year of a demanding program. She has discovered that she does not enjoy research, but finishing would require only one further year, and her employment prospects if she finishes are at least as good as if she leaves now for work she finds equally unappealing. She is inclined to finish in part because she does not want the previous years to have been for nothing. Here I am inclined to say that allowing the past investment to tip the balance is permissible, perhaps even sensible, and not the same mistake as in the first two cases.

Fourth, a relationship case. Two partners have built a life together over eight years. They judge that their expected future well-being together is roughly on a par with what they could expect if they separated and eventually formed new partnerships, yet they remain together in part because of their shared history and the effort already invested. I am inclined to regard this history-sensitivity as permissible and even admirable, rather than as a rational error.

Fifth, a self-binding case. Anticipating that I will be tempted to skip workouts, I deliberately purchase a block of nonrefundable training sessions precisely so that the aversion to waste will motivate me to attend. When I later attend a session I would otherwise skip, motivated mainly by the thought that I have already paid, I am inclined to regard this as rational execution of a sensible prior plan, even though the immediate reasoning looks identical to the reasoning I condemned in the concert case.

My questions are these. Is there a precise principle governing when sensitivity to sunk costs is rational that can accommodate most or all of these verdicts, or must some of them be abandoned? In particular, how should such a principle distinguish mere aversion to waste from legitimate considerations with which sunk costs are often entangled, such as acquired skills, evidence of commitment, identity-constituting projects, or the rational use of waste-aversion as a motivational tool? If a unified principle requires qualifications to handle the doctoral, relationship, and self-binding cases, what motivates each qualification?

Please state one or two candidate principles with sufficient precision to apply, explain which case motivates each clause or distinction, indicate what the preferred principle predicts for two further cases I have not listed, and conclude with an explicit assessment of which of my five provisional verdicts, if any, cannot be preserved together.
