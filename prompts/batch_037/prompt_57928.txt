For the past seven years I have managed an applied-physics instrumentation laboratory centered on precision measurement, so I reason most fluently in terms of test articles and fixtures, controls and confounds, calibration chains, idealizations that we later de-idealize, error budgets, and questions of construct and external validity. I am currently taking an evening course in philosophy of science, and a seminar discussion with classmates about whether scientists can genuinely learn about the world without gathering new data left me unsatisfied, in part because the philosophical proposals felt detached from how my technicians and I think about what makes any experimental arrangement informative.

Could you systematically recast three familiar proposals about how scientific thought experiments yield knowledge into that bench-experimental vocabulary, without smuggling in laboratory assumptions the philosophy does not itself make: first, that such thought experiments work as compact arguments that draw out consequences of principles we already accept; second, that they afford a kind of direct intellectual insight into natural necessities; third, that they work by building and running an internal model offline much as we would run a simulation to see how it behaves. Please carry the same invented illustration through all three recastings so I can reuse it with my students, namely a sealed and perfectly insulated chamber housing a delicate torsion balance, where we imagine attaining an unattainable perfect vacuum and eliminating all seismic noise in order to consider whether thermal fluctuations alone would still set a limit to measurement, and for each proposal explain what would count as the apparatus, the control, the idealization, and the result.

Conclude with a careful audit of what the recasting preserves and what it drops, distinguishing claims that carry over straightforwardly from claims that can be carried over only by stretching either the philosophy or the laboratory notions and from claims for which my vocabulary offers no counterpart at all, and in each case of mismatch offer a judgment about whether the gap exposes a limitation of bench-experimental language or suggests that the original philosophical claim was less precise than it appeared. Precision about the points of failure matters more to me than a smooth analogy, since I hope to use your answer both to decide how to present thought experiments to new technicians and to clarify my own sense of when imagining an experiment can stand in for performing one.
