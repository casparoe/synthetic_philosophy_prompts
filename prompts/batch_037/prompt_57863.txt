I am a journalist who covers science and ideas. While doing background research for a piece on whether human extinction would be bad for reasons beyond the premature deaths it would cause, I attended a forum on long term futures where two panelists developed an extended disagreement that I would like help assessing.

The shared starting point was the afterlife conjecture associated with Samuel Scheffler, namely the proposal that the meaning of many present activities, from scientific research and education to political reform and artistic work, tacitly depends on the assumption that humanity will continue after we are gone, with future generations to take up, use, and appreciate what we do now.

The exchange proceeded in four steps.

1. The first panelist argued that a universal and painless loss of fertility, in which no further children are conceived but all people now alive live out natural lifespans in good health, would leave many current projects feeling hollowed out and pointless. Since by stipulation the scenario involves no premature death, the sense of loss reveals a distinctive badness of the end of humanity that cannot be reduced to a count of lives cut short.

2. The second panelist replied that the reaction can be explained without positing such additional badness. People now alive would be harmed by living in a world without children, mentorship, and open ended institutions, and the final generation would face the sorrow of knowing itself to be last. Researchers would also correctly anticipate aiding fewer future people. On this view, apparent concern for humanity in the abstract is concern for the quality of lives of contemporaries under a truncated future.

3. The first panelist responded with two variants intended to hold fixed the effects on contemporaries. In the archive variant, automated systems preserve all books, data, and artworks indefinitely, although no conscious successors ever use them. In the successor variant, capable artificial agents continue science, read literature, and maintain institutions after human beings are gone. The claim was that the archive variant would remain almost as bleak as outright extinction, while the successor variant would provide only partial consolation, which suggests that what matters is the continuation of a specifically human form of life rather than benefits to those now alive or mere preservation of information.

4. The second panelist answered that these intuitions support the opposite conclusion. If preservation without readers seems bleak, that shows that usefulness to future agents was part of the value all along. If continuation by artificial agents who carry on everything we value would still leave us unmoved, that indicates a preference for our own species rather than evidence of objective value. To treat lack of consolation as proof of greater badness, the reply concluded, is to assume what is at issue, namely that only human continuation can sustain present meaning.

Please work through the exchange step by step, explaining for each of the four moves whether the reasoning succeeds, fails, or is partly correct, and why. Then give an overall assessment of which panelist has the stronger case, and identify any important distinction, objection, or alternative explanation that both panelists have overlooked.
