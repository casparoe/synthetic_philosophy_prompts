After a joint reading group with philosophy and economics students on practical rationality, I have been stuck on a puzzle about my friend Lena, a graduate student who resolved on Sunday to spend next Saturday drafting a chapter, and to make it stick gave a trusted friend 150 dollars to keep and forfeit if she binge-watches a series instead of writing. Please act as a professional philosopher with expertise in action theory and behavioral economics, and work through this slowly and carefully, beginning with a brief brainstorm of candidate diagnoses before developing your best one in detail.

A friend in the group offered the following argument that Lena cannot be weak-willed, only changeable. (1) An agent acts akratically only if she acts against what she judges best, all things considered, at the very moment of action. (2) What an agent judges best at a moment is shown by what she sincerely prefers when both options are concretely in front of her to choose between. (3) Hyperbolic discounting means evaluations reverse predictably with proximity: Lena on Sunday sincerely prefers finishing a draft on Saturday to binge-watching that day, yet on Saturday afternoon, with both options immediate, she sincerely prefers binge-watching now to writing now. (4) So by (2) and (3), what Lena judges best on Saturday afternoon is to binge-watch. (5) So when she binge-watches, she acts in line with her then-current best judgment, and by (1) she is not akratic but simply someone who changed her mind. (6) Since nearly every stock case of weakness of will — dieting, procrastinating, addictive behavior, breaking a resolution — can be redescribed as such a proximity-driven reversal, there is no weakness of will left to explain, and commitment strategies like handing over the 150 dollars look like irrational distrust of her better-informed later self.

That conclusion seems to prove too much, since it would dissolve the difference between revising a plan for a good reason and caving, and make ordinary commitment devices look confused. Which premise or inference should I give up, why did it look plausible in the first place, and what must I pay for each possible way out in terms of how I understand judgment versus preference, rationality over time, and the point of resolutions? I am especially reluctant to give up the ideas that Lena can be genuinely weak-willed on Saturday and that her Sunday commitment device can be rational, but I want an honest accounting of the costs of keeping them.
