I am preparing to defend my doctoral thesis in moral philosophy next month and seek help preparing for the question period.

My central thesis is that in morally loaded contexts there is no morally neutral way to distinguish respecting a persons preferences from correcting for bias, because any such classification already presupposes a substantive moral judgment about which influences are legitimate. I illustrate this with three stylized cases that run through the thesis: a caregiver from a community that valorizes familial self-sacrifice who declines a preventive treatment that would prolong her own life in order to avoid burdening her children; a hiring manager who sincerely endorses egalitarian principles yet systematically downgrades equally qualified candidates from a stigmatized group when under time pressure; and a hospital proposal to set advance-care defaults in a way described as debiasing status-quo bias rather than overriding patient values. My argument is that standard strategies for drawing the line, such as appeal to what the agent would want if fully informed, to procedural independence, or to the distinction between content and process, all smuggle in the very moral evaluation they claim to avoid. I conclude with a procedural proposal that those who intervene must openly state the moral premise licensing the description of an influence as bias.

My audience will consist of a behavioral economist committed to revealed-preference welfare economics and to libertarian paternalist nudging, a Kantian ethicist focused on autonomy as rational self-legislation, and a feminist philosopher whose work concerns adaptive preferences formed under oppression, plus a chair from clinical ethics concerned with practical guidance for surrogates and policy makers.

Please draft a preparation sheet for the defense. Identify the seven or eight objections I am most likely to face from this specific audience, ordered by a combination of likelihood and potential damage to my thesis, phrased in the vocabulary and concerns each examiner would naturally use. For each objection, provide a concise reply suitable for delivery in about two minutes, indicate what I should concede and what I should hold firm on, and add a brief note on where the reply remains vulnerable. Conclude by identifying the single most difficult question I should hope is not asked and explaining why it threatens the core of the argument.
