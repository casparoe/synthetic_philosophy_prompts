I work as a backend software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I volunteer on the steering committee for a large open-source infrastructure library that uses formal votes of its members to set roadmap priorities, licensing changes, and enforcement policy. To stay consistent we try to follow our own past decisions about who counts as a member, and I am finding those decisions hard to interpret. So far we have granted full voting membership to volunteer developers living outside the United States who have maintained at least six months of sustained commits, even when they do not use the library themselves; we have granted temporary voting membership to contracted technical writers for the duration of their twelve month contracts, during which they are assigned work, subject to review, and bound by our code of conduct; we have declined membership to a venture firm that made a substantial financial donation but never contributed code, documentation, or maintenance effort; and we have declined membership to a logistics startup whose entire fleet management stack depends on our releases but which has never contributed upstream, on the ground that heavy reliance alone does not make one a builder. I had assumed these pointed in the same direction until our current dispute.

Now we must decide a new proposal to grant membership to a network of small regional hospitals that deploy our library inside patient record systems without contributing code or documentation, but that under our new certified build program would be required to install our mandated security patches within fourteen days, submit to periodic audits of their deployments, and lose the right to use the certified name if they fall out of compliance, which for them would create significant procurement and liability risks. Some committee members say this is just like the logistics startup, namely affected users asking for votes, while others say it is like the technical writers, namely outsiders who have placed themselves under our binding rules and therefore deserve a say. Please recommend what we should decide if we want to remain most consistent with our own precedents, identify which prior decision is the closest analog and why the others are less similar, lay out at least two different general rules that each fit all four earlier verdicts but point in opposite directions here, explain which rule you think best captures what we have implicitly been doing and why, and describe a further hypothetical membership request that would separate those rules more cleanly going forward.
