I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, currently serving as an engineering manager in a mid-sized technology organisation. Next week I have been asked to give the closing remarks at a short internal sunset event for a platform rebuild my group has worked on for nearly four years and that senior leadership has now decided to discontinue in favour of an external vendor solution. Please draft the remarks in American English, suitable for reading aloud in about five minutes.

The audience will be mixed. About a dozen engineers who designed and maintained the system will be there, along with product colleagues who helped secure its funding, several recent hires who were not involved in the build and who will lead the migration, and the senior manager who made the decision to stop the work. The mood is a mixture of pride, exhaustion, and frustration, and people are looking to me to acknowledge what was achieved without reopening the decision.

I want the speech to take seriously a difficulty I cannot quite articulate. I accept that the sheer amount of time and budget we have already spent cannot by itself justify carrying on, yet I do not want to suggest that a rational team should simply behave as if those four years never happened or that everything we built was wasted because it will not launch. Some of what remains seems worth honouring for forward-looking reasons: a testing harness we intend to reuse, on-call and review practices that have improved reliability behaviour in neighbouring teams, and a commitment we made to pilot users in customer support to give them a stable and well organised transition rather than an abrupt switchover.

Please help me make that distinction clear in ordinary, non-technical language without turning the speech into a lecture on decision theory. The remarks should recognise the loss honestly, explain why discontinuing the project can be the right choice even while the work mattered, and give the team a credible, non-sentimental reason to invest energy in the migration. The tone should be candid, respectful, and collegial, and it should close with a finalised next step the whole room can support.
