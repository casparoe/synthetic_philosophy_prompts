I chair a university examinations panel that adjudicates requests for cognitive accommodations, roughly thirty to forty cases per term. The stakes are significant: we must preserve fairness across closed-book assessments while avoiding discrimination against students whose effective memory and reasoning depend on external resources. Administrators who will first apply any framework have no training in philosophy, have about twenty minutes per case, and must produce a documented rationale for my final sign-off.

Please develop a reusable decision procedure grounded in the extended mind thesis for determining when an external resource should be treated as constitutive of a student's cognitive process rather than as a mere aid or potential source of unfair advantage. The procedure should translate the central philosophical distinctions into terms a non-specialist can apply, including the parity consideration that an external process that plays the functional role we would count as cognitive if performed in the head should presumptively count as cognitive, the further conditions often discussed concerning stable availability, typical reliance, automatic endorsement without routine critical vetting, and ready accessibility, the concern that mere causal coupling is not constitution, and the distinction between an external resource that replaces an internal capacity and one that complements it by doing something different.

Deliver the framework as an ordered checklist or flowchart in words, with the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a denial, where it terminates in a grant, and where it requires escalation to individual judgment because the issue turns on values or underdetermined concepts rather than observable facts. Mark explicitly which steps involve unavoidable judgment calls and which distinctions from the philosophical debate you have simplified or set aside to gain usability, with a brief justification for each simplification. Conclude with an honest statement of what the procedure cannot settle and where it is most likely to misfire.

Then demonstrate the procedure on three cases:

A second-year biology student with a documented working-memory limitation who has carried the same paper notebook for four years, containing densely cross-referenced diagrams in her own shorthand, consulted reflexively many times per day and relied upon for recall of material she cannot otherwise retain, who requests to bring it into a closed-book examination.

A first-year law student who relies dozens of times per day on a commercial AI assistant trained on his own notes and accessed by phone, whose summaries he generally acts on after a brief skim but which periodically invents authorities, so that he has learned to verify citations before filing any work, who requests exam access to a locked-down version of the assistant.

An engineering student without a diagnosed impairment who uses augmented-reality glasses that overlay standard formulae and stepwise prompts authored collaboratively with her study group, used fluently when connectivity permits but unavailable in some campus buildings and routinely double-checked against her own calculations, who requests to wear the glasses in examinations as an accommodation.

Work through each case stepwise under your procedure to a determinate recommendation, even where the recommendation will be unwelcome to the panel, for example by requiring us to permit something we would prefer to ban or to deny something we would prefer to allow, and explain why the unwelcome result follows.
