In my seminar on personal identity we have spent several weeks on Parfit's teletransporter thought experiments concerning what matters in survival, and after an extended discussion with my study group I attempted to systematize my own reactions by writing down verdicts on several variants we devised.

My provisional verdicts are as follows. When a scanner destroys my body on Earth while creating on Mars a fully functioning replica with apparent memories of my entire life, my character and intentions intact, I am inclined to say I survive and that this is as good as ordinary continued life. When the same scanner creates the same Mars replica but through malfunction fails to destroy the original, so that I wake up on Earth fully conscious while the replica wakes on Mars, I am inclined to say I am the person on Earth and the Mars person is someone else, a kind of psychological twin but not me. When a destructive scan produces two simultaneous replicas, one on Mars and one on Venus, each fully continuous with me, I am inclined to say neither is uniquely me but that both preserve what matters about survival in a divided form, which feels different from both the first and second verdicts. When my neurons are gradually replaced over many months by functionally equivalent artificial components while I remain conscious throughout, with no scan or duplication involved, I am inclined to say I survive without qualification. When a destructive scan produces a replica whose psychology has been edited to remove the last six years of memories and whose body will fail after only a few hours due to a transmission error, I am still inclined to say something important about survival is preserved, though to a diminished degree. Yet when a scan is taken of me now, stored for forty years while I live out a full life and die naturally, and only then used to build a replica from the decades-old pattern, I am inclined to say this is not my survival at all but the creation of a memorial copy, even though the psychological pattern is much the same as in the first case.

Is there a single principle about personal identity or about what matters in survival that captures most of these verdicts, and if so, how should it be stated precisely? Please show how each clause of the proposed principle is motivated by particular cases above, explain what it predicts for further cases I have not listed such as fusion of two scanned persons into one replica with merged memories and a case of destructive scanning with substantial but haphazard memory corruption, and be candid about which of my verdicts the simplest coherent principle would require me to abandon and why retaining all of them would come at the cost of theoretical complexity.
