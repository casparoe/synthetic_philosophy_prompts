For an undergraduate workshop in formal epistemology, I need a taxonomy of responses to Dutch book arguments that sorts holders by their verdicts on concrete cases rather than by labels they already prefer.
Base the taxonomy on the following three invented test cases, which must shape the branching questions: a weather forecaster who only ever posts one-sided odds set by an opposing bookie and never faces a complete package of bets at once; a research collective whose members are each probabilistically coherent but whose pooled odds permit a sure loss against the collective as such; and a traveler who knowingly accepts a diachronic sure loss on a road-trip bet in order to prevent an eavesdropper from inferring her itinerary from her refusal.
Deliver the taxonomy as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion. Structure the first four items as occupied leaves that must include a classical pragmatic vindication of synchronic coherence, a symptom or indicator reading on which bookability merely reveals a defect vindicated on other grounds such as accuracy, a synchronic-only view that rejects diachronic books for updating, and an imprecise-credence view that rejects identification of belief with a single betting price. Formulate each branch as a yes-or-no question about one of the test cases or about whether vulnerability to sure loss constitutes irrationality itself, stated so a student can answer it without knowing the label it leads to. Use the fifth item to name an unoccupied combination of answers in this logical space and diagnose in one sentence whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and within the relevant items flag one pair of leaves the tree cannot separate and state why.
