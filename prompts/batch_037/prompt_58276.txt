Contractualism as developed by T. M. Scanlon holds that an act is wrong when it is disallowed by principles that no individual could reasonably reject in favor of alternative principles, with each person's objection to be assessed on its own rather than added together across persons. This individualist restriction generates a familiar controversy about aggregation: many find it compelling that a very large number of modest benefits can outweigh a single grave harm, while the restriction appears to forbid such tradeoffs.

Consider in this connection three stylized allocation problems for a public decision-maker with fixed resources. First, a one-off rescue choice between preventing the early death of a single identified young adult and preventing a two-day bout of moderate influenza for each of two million people. Second, a standing policy choice for an agency that will face a thousand structurally similar decisions over a decade, where in each instance the same kind of tradeoff recurs between one severe impairment and many minor ailments. Third, a risk-imposition choice made before anyone knows who will be affected, where one rule imposes a tiny increased risk of death on everyone and another imposes a substantial risk of a short hospital stay on a smaller group, with the expected outcomes equivalent in the aggregate.

Please compose a sustained philosophical essay that examines whether the contractualist can handle these cases without either abandoning the individualist restriction or endorsing highly counterintuitive verdicts. The essay should reconstruct the strongest contractualist strategies for resisting aggregation, including appeals to the separateness of persons, to what individuals could reasonably reject ex ante as well as ex post, and to the relevance of intrapersonal aggregation over a lifetime, alongside the strongest reasons for thinking some interpersonal aggregation is morally required. It should apply that general discussion to each of the three allocation problems in turn, showing how the considerations favor or disfavor particular verdicts. It should conclude with a defended overall verdict about the aggregation controversy, whether revisionary, conciliatory, or uncompromising, with explicit reasoning about why the opposing position fails.
