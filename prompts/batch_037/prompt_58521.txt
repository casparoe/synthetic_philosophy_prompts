I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, working on backend infrastructure for a payments company, and I would like practical guidance on how to think about responsibility for a recent incident and how to argue for a fair postmortem process.

Three months ago I was assigned a ticket to add an idempotency check to our charge service to guard against duplicate processing on client retries. To meet a release deadline I shipped the feature without it, leaving a TODO in the code. A teammate approved the pull request without flagging the missing check, even though our review checklist mentions it. Around the same time, our platform team temporarily disabled a separate deduplication sidecar for maintenance and did not re-enable it afterward, without notifying service owners.

Last week, during an unusual traffic spike combined with aggressive retries from a large merchant, customers were double-charged. The draft postmortem lists me as the root cause on the grounds that my service allowed the duplicates through.

Part of the team now proposes a general rule for future postmortems: only active triggers, such as the retry storm or the decision to disable the sidecar, should be listed as causes of an incident, while missing safeguards should go in a separate lessons-learned section and not be described as having caused anything. Others object that this would let owners of safeguards off the hook.

There are two further complications. First, my ticket explicitly required the check, whereas re-enabling the sidecar was customary for the platform team but not documented as their duty. Second, we disagree about what would have happened otherwise: our QA engineer argues that even if I had added my check, some duplicates would still have gotten through because my planned implementation had a known race condition and the sidecar was off; I argue that if the platform team had re-enabled the sidecar, there would have been no duplicates even without my check.

I did not write code that created duplicate charges; I failed to write code that would have blocked them. Should I accept that I caused the harm to customers, or is there a meaningful difference between triggering it and failing to prevent it? How should responsibility be apportioned when several people each failed to act, and does it matter that my duty was explicit while theirs was informal? Should I support the proposed rule distinguishing triggers from missing safeguards, and what would be a fairer way to write the postmortem?
