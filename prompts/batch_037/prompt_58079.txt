I am developing teaching material on organizational decision-making under severe time pressure and uncertainty, and I would like a two-directional case analysis that uses the Johnson and Johnson response to the 1982 Tylenol poisonings in the Chicago area as a test of ideas about satisficing and bounded rationality.

For purposes of this exercise, please take the following broad outline as given and do not make your analysis depend on exact figures or disputed details: in early autumn 1982 several deaths in the Chicago area were linked to cyanide contamination of retail Tylenol capsules, with tampering believed to have occurred after manufacture; while the full scope of contamination, the likelihood of further tampering or imitation, and the forthcoming regulatory expectations remained highly uncertain and no nationwide withdrawal had yet been legally required, the company withdrew Tylenol products nationally at great financial cost, communicated openly with authorities and the public, and later reintroduced the product with tamper-evident packaging and a revised dosage form.

Please provide an analysis with three main parts. First, reconstruct the decision as a boundedly rational agent would have faced it. Specify who the relevant decision-maker is for your analysis, the options plausibly on the table at the moment of choice such as nationwide withdrawal versus a Chicago-area limited withdrawal versus waiting for more evidence or regulatory direction, the information, time, and computational limits bearing on the choice, and what would count in this context as an aspiration level, a search procedure, and a stopping rule for inquiry. Explain what a thorough optimizing account that assumes extensive search and expected outcome calculation would have recommended, and contrast it with a satisficing account.

Second, distinguish within satisficing between two readings I want tested against the case: satisficing as constrained stopping, where the actor stops searching because further search is infeasible in the time available, and satisficing as principled modesty, where the actor could have continued to gather information or chosen a narrower intervention but deliberately sets a high threshold for public safety and decisiveness in order to protect trust and simplify later decisions. Show which reading better fits the choice of a national withdrawal, which features of the episode each reading illuminates and which it misdescribes or leaves unexplained, and where the bounded rationality framework as such is silent and should be marked as silent rather than stretched to cover everything. Consider briefly whether a neighboring account such as recognition-primed choice, reliance on organizational routines, or a simple moral heuristic does better on the parts that satisficing handles poorly.

Third, reverse the direction: say what this episode in turn shows about the theory. Does it confirm the theory, present an anomaly for it, or present a kind of case the classic formulations did not anticipate, for example a case where acting as if one has lower standards for analysis enables higher standards for safety. Throughout, keep the mapping disciplined about who is deciding, what the alternatives are, and what counts as evidence for safety, and keep the focus on the structure of the decision rather than on praise or blame for the company.

Close with an explicit note that separates what is broadly settled among researchers who study bounded rationality and satisficing from what is genuinely contested among them, and do not present a contested interpretation as if it were the consensus view.
