I am a junior in high school taking an elective in ethics, and I would appreciate help diagnosing an argument my brother and I developed that now seems to prove too much.

In class we contrasted two communicative styles. One, illustrated by Dutch directness, favors stating an unwelcome judgment plainly even when it stings, on the grounds that clarity treats the listener as capable of handling the truth. The other, illustrated by Japanese indirectness, favors avoiding a flat refusal or harsh assessment and instead offering a vague, softened, or encouraging response in order to preserve harmony and let the other person adjust without embarrassment. We then discussed whether white lies told out of kindness are morally wrong.

The combined argument runs as follows:

(1) If telling a white lie to spare feelings is morally wrong in one society, it is morally wrong in every society. Whether an act is wrong does not change merely because local customs about directness are different.

(2) Highly direct communication shows that social life can function without white lies. People can hear an unwelcome true evaluation of their work and still cooperate and remain friends afterward, so white lies are never genuinely necessary to keep relationships or cooperation going.

(3) Therefore, if white lies are wrong anywhere, there is no cultural defense that makes them permissible elsewhere for the sake of politeness.

(4) A white lie still involves putting forward as true something the speaker believes to be false in order to be kind, for example reassuring a friend that a weak rehearsal went well when one believes it went poorly.

(5) To put forward as true something one believes to be false is to lie, and to lie is always morally wrong because it interferes with the other person's ability to grasp the situation and decide for herself what to do.

(6) Therefore every white lie is morally wrong in every culture.

(7) The same reasoning condemns indirect polite moves that avoid a direct no while creating a false impression, such as answering vaguely when one has already decided to decline, or stressing a minor positive point while withholding a larger negative judgment one holds, when one expects the listener to come away with something one believes to be false.

(8) But that implies that a large part of ordinary tact in every culture is morally forbidden, including much indirect politeness and even many careful word choices within direct cultures. That seems impossible, since some degree of tact seems required for living together respectfully.

I am inclined to keep (1), because I do not want morality to become mere custom, but I also do not want to conclude that all politeness is immoral. Please tell me which premise or inference I should reject, explain why that step looked plausible at first, and explain what it costs to give it up compared with giving up a different step.
