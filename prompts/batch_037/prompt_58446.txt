I coordinate grantmaking for a small foundation that funds chronic pain interventions through rural clinics, and we are currently torn between an intensive program that would substantially relieve severe neuropathic pain for about two hundred patients and a low-cost program that would relieve moderately severe back pain for several thousand patients, with colleagues extending the comparison stepwise toward relief of very mild discomfort for very large numbers. I am taking an evening course in practical ethics and find the philosophical discussion fascinating but difficult to connect to our budget decisions, especially since our trustees disagree about whether helping more people with smaller benefits can eventually outweigh helping a few people with very large benefits.

As I understand it, philosophers disagree about cases of this kind where each adjacent comparison seems to favor the larger group with slightly milder pain, while comparison of the endpoints seems to favor the small group in severe pain, raising the question whether one outcome being better than another must always be transitive and how small benefits should be aggregated. Please describe the most important implications of that debate for someone in my position: how should it affect our choice of decision procedure, our handling of consistency across many funding cycles, and our explanation to donors and clinicians if we decline to follow simple aggregation? I am not looking for scholarly references, only for practical and conceptual lessons I can apply without further research.
