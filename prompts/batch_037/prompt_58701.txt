Next Tuesday our regional health system's Technology Assessment Committee will take a binding vote on whether to authorize routine primary-care use of an internally developed statistical tool for stroke prevention, and I need a draft decision memo I can circulate in advance as a voting member.

The tool estimates five-year stroke risk to guide offers of a preventive regimen that modestly reduces stroke incidence but imposes burdensome side effects and monitoring costs. The dispute is not about average efficacy but about how to apply population statistics to borderline individual patients.

For the patients most affected by the decision — for example, a 62-year-old former smoker with controlled hypertension, no prior vascular event, and a physically active lifestyle without diabetes — the choice of reference class changes the estimate dramatically: around 3 to 4 percent when the patient is grouped with all adults in their sixties with controlled hypertension, around 8 to 9 percent when grouped with former smokers in their sixties with hypertension, and around 2 percent when grouped with physically active non-diabetic adults in their sixties regardless of smoking history. Each grouping comes from a well-calibrated model, and each privileges one causally relevant similarity while discounting another. The developer proposes resolving the conflict by rule, always using the most specific grouping that contains at least two hundred prior cases in our system, while our clinical ethics consult proposes reporting the full range across medically defensible groupings and requiring a structured shared decision-making conversation before prescribing for patients whose classification is grouping-sensitive.

One faction on the committee argues that without a principled basis for privileging one grouping, routine use would make treatment turn on an arbitrary administrative choice and undermine informed consent; the other argues that refusing to deploy until the grouping question is settled in the abstract would itself harm patients who would clearly benefit under any reasonable grouping.

Please draft a memo of roughly 800 to 1200 words that the committee could act on. It should recommend a specific course — authorize unrestricted use, decline authorization, or authorize subject to concrete and enforceable conditions on which estimate is displayed, how variation across groupings is communicated to clinicians and patients, and what re-review or monitoring is required — together with the reasoning that supports it and a candid treatment of the strongest consideration against the recommended course. Take a definite position and defend it with reasons rather than merely surveying possible views, so that the memo commits to a bottom line we can vote on.
