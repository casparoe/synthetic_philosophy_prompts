Having spent my working life as a physical chemist and having turned to philosophy only since retirement, I have been invited to address our local university philosophy society on reduction and emergence in science.

My thesis is that what philosophers call strong or ontological emergence is never needed in chemistry and physics, and that the appearance of novelty is always a matter of computational limitation and coarse-grained description. I plan to argue from three steps. First, the textbook reduction of temperature and the gas laws to molecular motion shows how a macroscopic regularity can be derived once suitable averaging assumptions are in place. Second, even phase transitions, often presented as the best case for irreducibility, become tractable when one considers how renormalisation methods show microscopic details washing out near the critical point, leaving universal macroscopic behaviour. Third, an illustration from my own laboratory career modelling freezing-point depression in binary salt solutions, where our molecular model recovered the macroscopic behaviour to within experimental error once computing power allowed, the only obstacle having been practical rather than principled.

The audience will number about fourteen, mostly academic metaphysicians and philosophers of mind with a broadly non-reductive outlook, plus one historian of science interested in past failures of reduction. They are comfortable with multiple realisability and with the distinction between epistemic and ontological emergence, they care a good deal about explanation and causal powers, and they will have little patience for the suggestion that difficulties of derivation are merely a matter of waiting for faster computers.

Please prepare me for the discussion period following a twenty minute talk. I need a prep sheet setting out the six or seven objections this particular audience is most likely to press, ranked by a combination of likelihood and seriousness, each formulated in the idiom a metaphysician or philosopher of mind would naturally use. For each objection, give a concise response suitable for use in the room and a frank note on where that response remains vulnerable and what I should concede rather than contest. Conclude with the one awkward objection I should most hope is not raised, and why it is difficult for my position. Please be committed rather than even-handed throughout: take a definite view on which objections have genuine force and which may properly be set aside, with reasons for favouring that assessment, rather than leaving the choice to me.
