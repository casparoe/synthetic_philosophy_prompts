As a novelist drafting a book around a fictional slur for the canal-dwellers of my invented city, I have adopted as a working hypothesis that the slur and its neutral counterpart make the same claim about who someone is, differing only in the contempt the former displays, so that reclamation among friends, innocent repetition by a child, and careful reportage in court can all be explained as changes in who is licensed to display that attitude rather than changes in meaning. Where is this hypothesis exposed? Describe the observations, cases, and arguments that should make me revise or abandon it, distinguishing failures I could accommodate with a small repair from those that would require me to discard it, noting what it leads me to expect that rival pictures do not, what even its sympathetic defenders would admit tells against it, and whether my provisos about context and licence have insulated it to the point of emptiness.
