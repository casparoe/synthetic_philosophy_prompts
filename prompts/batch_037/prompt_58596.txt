I am a high school junior currently taking an elective on storytelling across novels, film, and games, and a recent discussion with my friends after class left me confused about something I keep experiencing. Last month our teacher showed us an animated short about an elderly lighthouse keeper who loses his dog in a storm, rendered in a style so simplified that no one could mistake it for real footage, and I still found myself tearing up and feeling anxious during the storm scene even while I was fully aware that neither the keeper nor the dog ever existed. I have had similar reactions while reading a novel for homework, feeling angry at a cruel stepmother character and then feeling slightly embarrassed afterward because I knew there was no actual person to be angry at, unlike when I feel angry at someone at school.

What puzzles me more is that the feeling seems to change when I am given a role in the story. In our after-school game design club, we played a student-made, choice-based game where I had to decide which of two villagers in a mountain village to warn about a flood, knowing I could only save one, and for days afterward I felt guilty and kept wanting to replay to undo my choice, almost as if I had harmed someone, which feels different from the sadness I felt watching the lighthouse keeper. Is there an established name for this pattern of having genuine-seeming emotions toward people and events we know are invented, and especially for the difference between merely witnessing such events and feeling responsible for them as a player or reader who must choose? I am not looking for just a single label, but for an explanation of what the closest existing ideas are, how my examples differ from simply pretending, being temporarily fooled, or caring about real people, and where the boundaries lie, and if there is no precise term, I would appreciate a clear proposed definition that separates the passive case from the interactive guilt case.
