I am taking an introductory module in moral philosophy and have been puzzled by the idea of supererogation, understood roughly as acting well beyond what duty requires in a way that is praiseworthy when performed but not blameworthy when omitted. At a recent family dinner, two of my cousins who are both medical trainees fell into a sustained disagreement about the case of a junior doctor colleague who had volunteered for repeated extra night shifts during a winter staffing crisis at significant cost to her own health and exam preparation, and I listened throughout as a neutral bystander without taking sides.

Their exchange went roughly as follows. First, one cousin said the colleague had acted beyond duty precisely because her rota and contract did not require the extra shifts and declining them would not have been fair to criticize, which seemed to fit the definition of praiseworthy but optional. Second, the other cousin replied that contractual minima are the wrong baseline for role-based obligations, arguing that by becoming a doctor one assumes a broader duty of care that intensifies in emergencies, so the extra shifts, while admirable and demanding, were still the fulfillment of a professional duty rather than something merely optional. Third, the first cousin answered that if duty expands to absorb every admirable response to need, then people with demanding roles such as doctors or parents could almost never act beyond duty, which would make the category empty for exactly those lives where it seems most relevant, and insisted there must be some limit grounded in personal cost beyond which duty ends. Fourth, the second cousin rejoined that the absence of blame upon omission is an unreliable test for optionality, since we often withhold blame from people who fail to do what is genuinely required when failure is understandable, as with exhaustion or competing obligations, so being excused is not the same as being permitted, and added that if the extra shifts were genuinely the morally best course it is puzzling why morality would merely allow rather than require them.

Could you adjudicate their disagreement point by point, explaining for each of the four moves where the reasoning succeeds and where it goes wrong, identifying any crucial distinction or consideration that neither cousin noticed, and concluding with a judgment about who is more right overall and why.
