My friend and I ended a long evening yesterday still divided over Ayn Rand's attack on altruism, and I am trying to locate where we actually disagree. As we both understood her, though we drew opposite morals, she is not primarily criticizing occasional kindness or goodwill toward people one values, but a moral principle that treats living for others and sacrificing one's own good as the standard of virtue and self-interest as disreputable. My friend holds that this leaves ample room for generous action chosen because it expresses one's own values and brings one joy, and that Rand is right to deny that unchosen sacrifice is noble. I hold that once genuine other-regarding concern is admitted, the denial collapses, because much ordinary decency involves acting for another person's sake even at net cost to oneself, and calling that mistaken seems false. To keep us honest we invented two cases to test the view. In one, a freelance illustrator with an imminent deadline spends a weekend helping a neighbor rebuild a flooded kitchen, knowing she will lose a client and gain nothing but the neighbor's recovery, which she cares about deeply. In the other, a medical resident donates blood on a day off despite exhaustion, not from duty or guilt but because she endorses a world in which strangers do such things, though she expects no return. Consider the proposition that no one is ever morally required to bear a net cost solely for another person's good, and that regarding such self-sacrifice as morally good is itself an error. I would appreciate your assessment stated as a credence in that proposition, developed by separating it into at least four contributory judgments: whether the distinction between kindness chosen from one's own values and sacrifice demanded as duty is conceptually stable, whether morality can dispense with required other-regarding sacrifice, whether human psychology can sustain reliable benevolence without treating sacrifice as admirable, and whether the two cases above are best described as self-interested in Rand's sense or as counterexamples. For each contributory judgment please offer a rough probability with a brief reason, show how they combine into the overall credence, indicate which contributory judgment the overall figure is most sensitive to, and suggest what sort of argument or experience would move it most. It would also help to hear where you think the gap between my friend's sympathetic reading and my skeptical one most likely resides among those judgments. Please present all of this in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a thoughtful letter.
