I am a philosophy professor preparing a seminar unit on responsibility for autonomous systems, and I would appreciate your help working through a case. Please act as a professional philosopher with expertise in moral responsibility, agency, and the philosophy of technology. Think carefully and systematically, and begin with a brief brainstorming of candidate interpretations before developing your best analysis.

Consider the following institutional practice, which is based on a composite of real deployment policies I have described to students in full detail so no outside research is needed:

A metropolitan transit authority operates a fleet of highly automated shuttles on fixed routes with an onboard attendant. The governing policy has four interlocking elements. First, the authority requires each attendant to sign a statement at the start of every shift accepting full responsibility for passenger safety during that shift. Second, the training manual instructs attendants not to intervene in steering or braking except when the system issues an explicit takeover alert or when a collision is immediately obvious to a lay observer, and the authoritys performance metrics penalize frequent manual overrides as indicating poor system monitoring. Third, after any incident, a review board evaluates the attendant by asking whether a reasonable attendant would have intervened, but it conducts that evaluation with access to slowed replay, sensor logs, and engineering analysis that were not available in the vehicle at the time. Fourth, injuries to passengers are compensated through a no-fault fund financed by the authority and the manufacturer, without any formal finding about who was at fault.

This pattern puzzles my students because the authority simultaneously treats the attendant as continuously responsible and as properly passive, and because it separates compensation from judgments of fault.

Please reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory underlying this practice. What view of moral responsibility, agency, control, and knowledge does the practice presuppose in order to make sense, if any? Does it presuppose a control-based condition, a reasons-responsive or quality-of-will view, a strictly role-based or strict-liability notion, or a distributed conception of agency across human and machine? Which rival views does it quietly rule out? Distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely fits well with, and identify points where the practice is philosophically underdetermined so that several theories could license it. Assess whether the four elements presuppose consistent things about responsibility, and explain what would have to change in training, evaluation, or compensation design if the presupposed view were replaced by a genuinely forward-looking, collective model of responsibility for sociotechnical systems.
