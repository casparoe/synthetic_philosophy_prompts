My friend and I argued late into the evening about whether knowing a thing requires knowing what it implies. He defends a tracking view of knowledge inspired by Nozick: to know, my belief must be sensitive, such that were what I believe false I would not believe it by the same method, and he pressed me with this chain. (1) I know by reading the station departure board that my train leaves from Platform 2. (2) I know that its so leaving entails that the board has not been hacked to misdirect passengers about platforms. (3) Were the board hacked in that way, I would still believe by reading it that my train leaves from Platform 2 and hence that it has not been so hacked, so my belief that it has not been hacked is insensitive. (4) So I do not know that the board has not been hacked. (5) Suppose I now competently deduce that it has not been hacked from my known premise about Platform 2. (6) Competent deduction from something known yields knowledge of the conclusion. (7) So after the deduction I do know that the board has not been hacked, on the very same basis that left me ignorant. That looks contradictory, yet each step seemed compelling, and I am reluctant to abandon either sensitivity or the power of deduction to extend knowledge. Which premise or step should I give up, and what would giving it up cost? Please answer in British English.
