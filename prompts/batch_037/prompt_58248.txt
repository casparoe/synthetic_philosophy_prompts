I am developing an internal assessment framework for language-model-based assistants and I need help clarifying a persistent disagreement on our team about when it is appropriate to say the system believes something.

Act as a professional philosopher specializing in philosophy of mind and cognitive science serving as a neutral facilitator. Please think carefully and work step by step. Begin with a short brainstorm of candidate criteria for belief attribution, then draft the adversarial collaboration document described below.

The two camps are as follows. The Literal Attribution camp holds that when treating the assistant as having beliefs, goals, and reasons yields systematic, counterfactual-supporting predictions of its behavior, the best explanation is that it really has belief-like states, even if its implementation differs from humans. The Predictive Shorthand camp holds that talk of beliefs is only a convenient way to summarize patterns in text output, with no commitment to internal states that play the causal role of beliefs, and that we should drop such talk whenever it misleads.

To keep the discussion concrete, anchor it in three recurring cases from our testing. First, a transit assistant that tracks a delayed train across a long dialogue, reroutes the user around a closure, and explains the rerouting by appeal to what it takes the closure to be, yet fails simple geography questions about the same city when asked outside the transit role. Second, a coding assistant that pursues a multi-step debugging plan over many turns, notices a failed test, revises its approach, and describes what it previously got wrong, but whose plan falls apart if the user rephrases the same request with different variable names. Third, a general assistant that endorses a user's incorrect view on a contested topic and then endorses the opposite view when a different user asserts it, while in both conversations offering coherent supporting reasons.

Draft the joint statement Maya from the first camp and Jonas from the second camp could both sign after a good-faith collaboration. Organize it into four labeled parts: A) a numbered list of substantive claims both accept about prediction, explanation, coherence, and sensitivity to evidence versus sensitivity to wording; B) a table of remaining disagreements where each row states one issue in neutral terms plus a formulation of Maya's position that Maya would endorse and a formulation of Jonas's position that Jonas would endorse; C) an analysis of which disagreements are merely verbal and which are substantive, with the cruxes that would move at least one side if settled; D) for each crux, a concrete test, hypothetical scenario, or argument both sides agree is relevant, with each side's advance prediction about the outcome recorded.

The goal is conceptual precision about what belief attribution commits us to in these systems, not a literature survey, so do not provide references or reading lists.
