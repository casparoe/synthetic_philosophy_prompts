I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy and I oversee backend infrastructure for a persistent multiplayer virtual environment where users build, trade, and inhabit shared spaces through avatars. Two design debates have made me realize I lack a clear conceptual framework. One debate is whether the persistent, tradable, server-enforced items in our system, such as player-forged tools that require hours of coordinated effort to produce, retain stable properties across sessions, and can be sold for real currency, should be regarded as genuinely real objects rather than mere illusions or interface conveniences. The other debate is whether disruptive actions directed at avatars, such as a coordinated group surrounding and simulating violence against another user's avatar in a way that leaves database records intact but causes the targeted user persistent distress and avoidance of the platform, should be regarded as genuinely real harms rather than only fictional events with off-platform psychological side effects. I would appreciate a systematic comparison of these two realism claims. Please articulate the strongest basis for each, examine whether persistence, causal influence within and beyond the system, social recognition among users, and dependence on human experience support them in the same way, and assess which claim rests on firmer ground and where the apparent parallels between them break down.
