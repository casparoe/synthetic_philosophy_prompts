Last night I had a long and unexpectedly heated argument with a close friend about the death penalty, and we ended precisely where we started despite agreeing on most of the relevant facts. His position, refined over the course of the evening, is that he concedes the system as currently practiced in the United States is indefensible because of racial and economic bias and demonstrated exonerations, but he defends a narrowed, failsafe version restricted to intentional homicides of exceptional cruelty where guilt is established by multiple independent kinds of evidence such as video, DNA, and corroborated confession, with mandatory extended review, and where life without parole is available as the alternative. On that narrowed ground he argues that retributive desert still calls for death in at least some cases because only death adequately marks the gravity of the wrong and vindicates the victims, that even a small additional deterrent effect over life without parole for prison murders and murders of witnesses would be worth preserving, and that a residual risk of error made vanishingly small by the heightened standard is a tragic cost morality must tolerate, just as it tolerates foreseeable deaths from other justified institutions.

I argued that the narrowing does not dissolve the objections but only relocates them: that retributive proportionality can be satisfied by a severe nonlethal punishment and that the claim that only death expresses adequate condemnation confuses what a community currently feels with what justice requires, that the deterrence question must be marginal deterrence over life without parole rather than over impunity and that the existing evidence cannot isolate such an effect from broader social conditions, and that irreversibility changes the moral arithmetic so that even a tiny, unevenly distributed risk of executing an innocent person outweighs any expressive or incremental deterrent gain, especially when the selection of the supposedly failsafe cases will itself be shaped by the same discretionary judgments that produce bias. We both accepted that exonerations have occurred, that life without parole is itself severe and fallible, and that murder inflicts a distinctive and irreparable harm, yet neither of us moved.

My hypothesis is that we are not really disagreeing about any pending factual finding but about deeper structural matters: whether avoiding the wrongful execution of an innocent has lexical priority over giving the guilty what they are due or is merely one very weighty value to be balanced, what should count as data for deterrence and for safety, and whether we are even answering the same question, with him asking what punishment would be fitting under ideal epistemic conditions and me asking what institution we can justify given our actual fallibility and history. Is that the right diagnosis, or is this a substantive factual disagreement in disguise, a verbal dispute about words like desert and deterrence, or a clash of bedrock intuitions about irreversibility and the meaning of justice that argument cannot resolve? I want an analysis grounded in the positions as I have stated them, not a general overview of the death penalty debate, and a clear account of what, if anything, would count as progress from here — a conceptual distinction, a kind of evidence, a reformulated question — or a reasoned case that no such progress should be expected.
