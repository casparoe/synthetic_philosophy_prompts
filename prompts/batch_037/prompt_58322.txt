I have been asked to give the opening remarks at our town autumn film weekend before the premiere screening of a biographical drama based on the life of my late mother, who worked for thirty years as a district nurse and led a prolonged campaign for a rural clinic. With our family consent the production condenses a campaign that unfolded over several years into a single winter, combines several of her actual colleagues into a single invented coworker, and stages a decisive kitchen table argument with union officials that in reality took the form of months of correspondence and committee meetings. The audience will include relatives and former patients who knew her personally, local historians who have documented the clinic struggle in detail, the director and cast seated in the front row, and many neighbours who know little of the history and have come chiefly for an evening story.

I am seeking help in articulating a distinction that will have to govern the entire speech, between fidelity to checkable facts and a different kind of faithfulness to a person character and commitments, without treating the second as a license to rewrite the first. I want to acknowledge openly what has been altered or imagined, explain why such invention might still have value for understanding a life rather than merely for entertainment, and at the same time set a clear limit that prevents affectionate invention from hardening into false public memory, especially where the film attributes motives and decisions to my mother that simplify her actual deliberations. The remarks must remain gracious toward the filmmakers while making plain to the historians that their concern for accuracy is legitimate, and must give ordinary viewers a way to watch the film with both pleasure and appropriate reserve.

Please write the remarks as a six to eight minute address suitable for delivery by her son, in a tone that is warm, sober, and civic minded rather than elegiac or academic, and that will work for listeners with no philosophical background while still giving the historians reason to feel that a substantive question about our obligations to the dead has been addressed rather than evaded. The speech should prepare the audience to view the dramatization attentively, equip them to carry away a truer rather than a merely more vivid impression, and close by inviting them to remember the real person as distinct from the character they are about to meet.
