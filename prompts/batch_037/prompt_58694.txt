I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I am building an internal tool that combines individual engineers' judgments into a single team judgment. In testing the logic I ran into a pattern I do not have good vocabulary for, and I would like help finding the principle behind my own reactions to cases. Please write your answer in American English.

The tool asks each engineer to vote yes or no on two factual premises and also on a conclusion that follows from them, then it has to publish a team position that is logically consistent. The puzzle is that majority votes on the premises and majority votes on the conclusion can point in opposite directions.

Here are five test cases I ran through, with what felt right to me in each:

Case 1, bug triage. Three reviewers vote on whether a bug is release-blocking. Premise A is the bug reproduces reliably, Premise B is the behavior violates the specification, Conclusion C is the bug blocks release, where C follows if A and B are both true. The votes are Engineer 1: yes, yes, yes; Engineer 2: yes, no, no; Engineer 3: no, yes, no. So a majority says yes to A, a majority says yes to B, but a majority says no to C. My verdict: the tool should publish yes to A, yes to B, and yes to C as the team view, overriding the majority no on C. Publishing an inconsistent set, or letting the conclusion vote defeat two premise majorities, seems worse.

Case 2, deploy decision. Same voting pattern and same logical structure. Premise A is the migration will finish in the maintenance window, Premise B is the rollback plan has been tested, Conclusion C is proceed with tonight's migration, where C follows if A and B are both true. Votes are identical to Case 1. My verdict here is the opposite: the tool should publish do not proceed. Even though premise majorities support proceeding, a direct majority against proceeding on a high-stakes action feels controlling, and auto-overriding it to force a deploy feels wrong.

Case 3, incident review. Premise A is Alice authored the configuration change, Premise B is that change caused the outage, Conclusion C is Alice is accountable for the outage under our review policy, where C follows if A and B are both true. Votes are again Engineer 1: yes, yes, yes; Engineer 2: yes, no, no; Engineer 3: no, yes, no. My verdict: the tool should not publish either a yes or a no on C. It should publish the premise majorities and flag C as unresolved for human discussion. Forcing a team verdict that someone is accountable when most reviewers voted not accountable seems unfair, but so does manufacturing accountability from premise votes.

Case 4, failover. Now the logic is a disjunction. Premise A is primary database A is unhealthy, Premise B is primary database B is unhealthy, Conclusion C is route traffic to the backup region, where C follows if A or B is true. Votes are Engineer 1: no, no, yes; Engineer 2: no, yes, yes; Engineer 3: yes, no, yes. So a majority says no to A, a majority says no to B, but everyone says yes to C on their own ballot. My verdict: the tool should publish yes to C and trigger the routing, even though that combination is inconsistent if you take the premise majorities literally. Unanimity on the conclusion seems to carry special weight here.

Case 5, expertise. Back to conjunction for a launch check. Premise A is load testing passed, Premise B is the cryptography implementation was audited, Conclusion C is the service is ready. Engineer 1, our only security specialist, votes yes, yes, yes. Engineer 2 votes yes, no, no. Engineer 3 votes yes, no, no. So there is unanimity on A, a 2-1 majority against B, and a 2-1 majority against C. My verdict: the tool should defer to the specialist on B and publish yes, yes, yes. I am uneasy about this because it abandons one-person-one-vote, but counting uninformed votes equally on an audit question seems worse.

My tentative rule was something like follow premise majorities for factual questions, follow conclusion majorities for actions, and defer to experts when recognized, but Cases 2 and 3 both involve actions and I treat them differently, and Case 4 suggests unanimity matters in a way my rule does not capture.

Is there a precise principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures most of these verdicts? For each candidate, please state it exactly, explain which case motivates each part of it, say what it predicts for two further situations I have not resolved — first, a case where one voter abstains on a premise but votes on the conclusion, and second, a chained case where the conclusion of one vote becomes a premise for the next — and give an honest assessment of which of my five verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and whether I should revise that verdict or accept a more complicated rule to save it.
