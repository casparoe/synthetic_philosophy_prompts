I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I am preparing a short lunch-and-learn for smart coworkers who also have no philosophy background on commitment, intentions, and rational choice.

Please act as a professional philosopher specializing in decision theory and practical rationality. Please think carefully through the design, beginning with brief brainstorming of two or three candidate ideas and explaining why you reject the weaker ones before presenting your final version.

As I understand it, Kavka's toxin puzzle involves a structure where a person is offered a large reward this evening for intending this evening to perform a mildly costly action tomorrow, where the reward will already be secured before tomorrow arrives and the person will remain fully free tomorrow to reconsider, so that it can seem rational to form the intention tonight and also rational not to follow through tomorrow. I want a fresh version of that same structure without the usual billionaire, perfect mind-reader, or toxin.

Please construct a thought experiment to the following specifications:

1. Set the case in a realistic modern software workplace or in ordinary adult life, with no science fiction, no perfect predictors or lie detectors, no eccentric benefactors, and no poison or physical harm.
2. Keep the stakes at a level a normal person could face, for example a free team lunch, avoidance of a tedious documentation shift, or a small bonus on the reward side, balanced against a mild inconvenience on the cost side such as drinking something very bitter, staying an hour late to clean up a backlog, or volunteering for weekend on-call triage.
3. Provide an ordinary and plausible mechanism by which the earlier intention can be checked or made credible, for example a sincere avowal that teammates have good reason to trust based on track record, a visible calendar block or written plan, or an automated log of a stated commitment, but the mechanism must not force the later action, must not add a further penalty for backing out beyond the original inconvenience itself, and must not rely on forgetfulness, self-deception, drunkenness, or coercion.
4. Present the scenario itself in a single paragraph of about 150 to 200 words, suitable to read aloud to engineers with no philosophy background.
5. After the scenario, demonstrate that it meets each specification, spelling out step by step what two simplified views would say and why: first, an act-only view on which rationality is assessed action by action at the time of acting to maximize expected benefit then, and second, an intention-permitting view on which it can be rational to form an intention earlier because having it brings a benefit even if carrying it out later would be costly.

Please be explicit about which structural feature of your case is doing the work in generating the tension, what happens if that feature is removed or weakened, and how someone who thinks the original puzzle is incoherent or practically impossible would attack your new version.
