As a novelist preparing a collection of stories that treat moral questions with philosophical precision, I am developing a narrative that explores the ethics of space colonization through a single, tightly focused case. You are a professional philosopher specializing in intergenerational and environmental ethics and also an experienced editor of literary science fiction, and I would like you to think carefully through the philosophical stakes before writing, considering internally how each position could be held for internally coherent reasons.

The premise to work from is this: after a 140-year voyage, a generation ship arrives at a habitable but not yet human-habitable world whose shallow seas contain extensive microbial mats that cycle carbon and build mineral structures, but show no sign of sentience or pain. The ship-born crew, none of whom chose to be born on the voyage and none of whom can return, must decide whether to proceed with a terraforming release that will oxygenate the atmosphere for human children but will unavoidably sterilize much of the native mats, or to remain in enclosed habitats and study the world without remaking it, accepting higher risks of long-term failure and dependence on failing ship systems. The ship charter contains two principles in unresolved tension, one directing the crew to secure a self-sustaining human presence as a hedge against catastrophe on Earth, with which contact has been lost, and another directing them not to supplant an existing biosphere where one is found.

Please write a self-contained short story of about 1,200 to 1,500 words, in third-person limited perspective centered on the ship-born ecologist tasked with certifying the survey, in which the decision turns not on technical error or villainy but on how the characters weigh consent of the unborn, responsibility to future humans, and responsibility to non-sentient alien life, including whether a backup rationale can justify irreversible ecological replacement. Let the ending remain dramatically resolved but philosophically open, without authorial moralizing.

Begin directly with the story itself, with no preface, commentary, or explanatory note.
