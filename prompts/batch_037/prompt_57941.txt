I am a novelist drafting a parable about a famine village that must divide its grain between many ordinary villagers and one child, Jun, whose rapture from a single loaf is stipulated to exceed by a vast multiple the total comfort that loaf would provide to any number of others. I distilled the lesson I meant the case to carry into the following chain, but the result seems to prove too much: (1) An aggregative morality holds that one distribution is better than another when it contains a larger sum of well-being. (2) By stipulation, giving all the grain to Jun produces the largest sum. (3) So aggregative morality requires letting the village starve to feast Jun. (4) That requirement is morally unacceptable. (5) If a moral view has an unacceptable requirement in a coherent hypothetical case, the view is false and not merely difficult to apply. (6) Any moral view that gives any positive weight to aggregate well-being will, for some sufficiently large multiple on Jun's rapture, require feasting Jun. (7) Therefore no acceptable morality may give any weight at all to aggregate well-being. I cannot accept (7), since it would condemn even modest efforts to produce more rather than less good, yet each step seems forced, and I am reluctant to surrender (4). Identify the step to reject, explain why it appeared compelling, and state what must be paid to reject it rather than the alternatives. Conclude with a final line of the exact form Verdict: Step N, where N names the rejected step, or Verdict: Valid if you endorse the argument.
