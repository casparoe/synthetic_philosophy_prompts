I am a doctoral candidate scheduled to present my dissertation chapter on Frege at our department colloquium next month, shortly after a graduate petition to add a contextualizing statement about his politics to our introductory logic syllabus. My thesis is that his late political remarks, including his hostility to universal suffrage and parliamentary compromise, extend the same antimajoritarian conception of truth found in his critique of psychologism, but that this continuity leaves the correctness of his logic and semantics untouched. The audience will be split between senior specialists in logic and philosophy of language who regard the politics as biographically unfortunate but philosophically irrelevant, and political theorists and graduate students who suspect the politics reveals something authoritarian in the ideal of objectivity itself. Please prepare a prep sheet for the question period: the six or so objections I am most likely to face from this particular mix, ordered by a combination of likelihood and seriousness and voiced as each faction would voice them, each paired with a concise reply I could deliver in about two minutes and an honest note on where that reply remains vulnerable. Also advise what I should concede at the outset, what I must not concede, and identify the one question I should most hope is not asked.
