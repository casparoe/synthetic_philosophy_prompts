I am a second-year PhD student in philosophical logic working on the dynamics of public announcements, and my reading group has just spent a full term on the unexpected examination scenario where a teacher tells a class that there will be an exam on one day next week on a day they will not be able to predict in advance, the students then reason backwards from Friday to rule out each day, and the teacher then gives the exam on, say, Wednesday to apparent surprise. I came in expecting the recent formal work to have settled things, but instead we found the same stalemate repeating: one camp treats the backward elimination reasoning as sound and concludes the announcement could not have been known and so was self-undermining, a second camp formalizes knowledge and surprise in epistemic temporal logic and argues the paradox turns on illicit principles like luminosity or unrestricted KK or closure under known entailment, and a third camp insists the whole problem trades on shifting between an epistemic reading of unpredictability and a more psychological or pragmatic reading tied to how students would actually reason or feel.

My working hypothesis, which I would like tested rather than assumed, is that these are not three rival solutions to one puzzle but three different precisifications of surprise and of what the announcement is supposed to do, and that much of the impasse comes from disagreeing implicitly about which formalization preserves the intuitive datum versus explaining it away. To make this concrete I have been playing with two variants for the group: in Variant A the teacher openly rolls a die on Sunday to select the exam day while still announcing unpredictability, so unpredictability is secured by chance rather than by student ignorance; in Variant B the students must each write down a predicted day every evening and surprise means their written prediction the night before was wrong. Standard moves that seem plausible for the original case appear to diverge on these variants, yet the literature rarely says which variant it takes as canonical. I want to know whether the stalled character of the debate reflects a verbal slide over surprise, a substantive split over epistemic principles, sides answering different questions about idealized knowers versus real reasoners under one label, or an ill-posed original scenario that needs replacement by sharper variants.

I am less interested in another verdict on which solution is correct than in a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement itself, supported by what proponents of each approach actually claim and why their arguments fail to engage the others, plus an account of what would count as progress on your diagnosis, whether a conceptual distinction, a formal result, a revised scenario, or a change of question, or else a reasoned case that no progress should be expected because the puzzle is hard in a particular way.
