I clerk for a mid-sized city's Community Standards Review Panel, which operates under a locally adopted public reason guideline holding that coercive municipal measures should be justifiable in terms that residents of all faiths and none can in principle engage with, with heightened scrutiny whenever explicitly religious premises are doing justificatory work. I find the guideline abstract in isolation and would like guidance grounded in our own track record rather than in general theory, since consistency with what we have already done matters more to the panel than abstract correctness.

Our unanimous rulings over the past two years include four relevant precedents. We approved funding for a winter shelter expansion where sponsors invoked both a faith-based duty of hospitality to strangers and a city auditor's findings on unsheltered counts and emergency service savings, stating on the record that the audit alone would have persuaded them to vote the same way. We rejected a proposed ordinance mandating retail closure on a weekly holy day where sponsors invoked only obedience to divine command and where staff analysis found no credible independent benefit for worker well-being or traffic that sponsors were willing to defend as sufficient on its own. We approved a temporary flood-relief levy championed by an interfaith coalition that cited both scriptural duties of stewardship and an independent engineering report on levee failure risk, with coalition witnesses affirming that they regarded the engineering case as compelling in its own right. We rejected an attempt to deny a parade permit to a minority faith on the basis of theological disapproval joined with asserted congestion concerns that the transportation department study directly contradicted, leaving no intact secular rationale for denial.

We now face a proposal to sharply cap fees and rollovers on payday and auto-title loans within city limits. The principal sponsors are congregations appealing to doctrinal prohibitions on usury and exploitation of the vulnerable, and the file also contains a housing-office analysis documenting cycles of rollover debt, correlations with eviction filings, and net fiscal costs to the city. The sponsors state candidly, however, that they find the statistical case methodologically unpersuasive and would press the cap even if the data pointed the other way, regarding the doctrinal prohibition as alone decisive for them. Please recommend the decision that best coheres with our precedents, identify which of the four prior rulings is the closest analog and why, lay out at least two candidate principles that both fit our track record but diverge on this case, explain what each predicts here, give your best-supported recommendation with reasons for preferring one reconstruction, and describe a further hypothetical case that would most cleanly separate the candidate principles going forward. Close with a final line that reads exactly Verdict: Approve or Verdict: Reject or Verdict: Approve with conditions, with nothing after it.
