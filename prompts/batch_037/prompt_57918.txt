I am leading a seminar section on democratic theory for undergraduates with mixed majors, and after covering the standard clash between epistemic defenses of a knowledge-restricted franchise and procedural plus epistemic defenses of universal franchise, my students keep assuming the positions line up neatly with policy conclusions, with the epistocrat always favoring exclusion and the democrat always favoring inclusion. To unsettle that assumption, I would like a thought experiment that reverses the usual alignment for pedagogical purposes.

Could you create a realistic, present-day scenario, describable in a single paragraph and suitable for non-specialists, set in a familiar institution such as a city zoning hearing, a public school parent council, or a county public health advisory board, in which a consistent epistemic epistocrat, someone who holds that political power should track knowledge because that produces better outcomes, is compelled on strictly epistemic grounds to defend universal inclusion, while a consistent procedural democrat, someone who holds that universal inclusion is required for legitimacy and also tends to produce better collective inquiry, is compelled on their own grounds to defend a temporary and narrowly drawn exclusion. To keep the case honest, please observe the following constraints: no voter may be portrayed as cartoonishly irrational, bigoted, or deliberately destructive; no perfect or near-perfect test of competence or predictor of correctness may be assumed; the epistemic benefit of inclusion must arise from the structure of the decision itself, such as dispersed local knowledge, correlated blind spots among experts, or feedback effects from implementation, rather than from a strategic concession or from smuggling in the intrinsic value of equality; and the reason for exclusion must likewise arise from the democrat's own commitments, not from abandoning democratic principles.

After the scenario, please walk through the reasoning step by step for each of the two positions to show that the verdicts genuinely follow from their premises rather than being stipulated, identify precisely which feature of the case is doing the work in flipping each verdict, explain what happens if that feature is removed and whether the case then collapses back into the standard disagreement, and close with how a committed epistocrat who dislikes the outcome for their side would most plausibly attack the case. I intend to use the case to test whether students can separate a theory from its usual policy conclusion, so what counts as cheating here is simply stipulating that each side changes its mind without deriving that change from its stated premises.
