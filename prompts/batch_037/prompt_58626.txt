You are a professional philosopher specializing in animal minds and applied research ethics. Think carefully through the trade-offs before you write, beginning with brief private brainstorming in which you map the main moral risks on each side.

I serve on my university's Animal Care and Oversight Board. We must issue a binding written opinion by next Friday on Protocol 24-17, a year-long comparative learning program proposed by our marine laboratory. Please draft the short opinion memo I can circulate to the Board, with a concrete, actionable recommendation.

The protocol has three components using the same facility staff and budget:

1. Problem-solving assays with 12 common octopuses: animals are housed individually, presented daily with transparent puzzle boxes baited with crab meat, then returned to a display tank at year's end. No surgery or noxious stimulation is involved, but housing is confined and handling is frequent.

2. Avoidance-learning assays with 200 green shore crabs: animals receive brief low-voltage shocks in one arm of a two-chamber tank to test whether they will forgo a preferred dark shelter to avoid the shock, and whether that avoidance is modulated by hunger and by prior exposure to a local anesthetic. A subsample are euthanized for neural tissue analysis.

3. Reflex assays with fruit flies as a comparison control: brief heat exposure to measure withdrawal, with no additional housing changes.

For purposes of this decision, take the following summaries as stipulated by our veterinary consultants and do not look for further evidence:

- Octopuses in this lab show rapid learning, individual strategies that persist across weeks, escape attempts targeted at weak points in lids, prolonged guarding of injured arms, and dose-sensitive responses to general anesthetics used in cephalopods.

- Shore crabs in this setup show avoidance of the shock-paired chamber, willingness to leave the preferred shelter when shock intensity rises, reduced avoidance after anesthetic, and resumption of feeding and mating behavior afterward, but no wound-directed behavior of the kind seen in the octopuses and no validated anesthetic dose-response curve for this species.

- Flies show rapid withdrawal and sensitization but no modulation by hunger or competing reward in this setup.

Our current policy assigns rodent-level protections — required anesthesia review, humane endpoints, enrichment minima, and full-board re-review if mortality exceeds 5% — to all vertebrates plus cephalopods housed in the primate center, but not to cephalopods in the marine lab and not to decapod crustaceans or insects anywhere. Two Board members argue the evidence above warrants extending rodent-level protections to the octopuses and to the crabs in this protocol on precautionary grounds. The researchers argue that extension to crabs would make the sample size unworkable, delay a study relevant to fisheries handling, and set a precedent for regulating all invertebrate work, while extension to octopuses alone would be workable if phased in over six months.

Your memo should recommend one concrete course of action with a time frame — approve as proposed, approve with specified conditions, approve in part and remand in part, or reject — and state which protections apply to which group, what monitoring and review triggers apply, and how to handle precedent for future invertebrate protocols. It must give reasons the researchers could actually respond to and engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than dismissing it.

After the memo, append a brief structured summary in the form of a JSON object with exactly these fields: recommendation (one sentence stating the bottom line), protections_by_group (what level of protection for octopuses, crabs, and flies), key_grounds (two to three sentences on the central reasons), strongest_counterconsideration (two to three sentences stating the best point against your recommendation and your reply to it), and review_trigger (concrete condition and date for re-review).
