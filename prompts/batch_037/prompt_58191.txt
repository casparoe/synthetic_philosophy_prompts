In my capacity as convenor for our department's second-year paper in political theory, I am preparing the assessed take-home essay on the ethics of compatriot partiality, and I find that our students too readily fall back on rehearsed summaries of associative versus impartialist positions without showing they understand what turns on the distinction. To block that evasion I would like the essay to be anchored in a fresh hypothetical I have devised for the occasion: a health minister with a fixed discretionary budget must choose between a domestic programme expected to extend the lives of 200 fellow citizens and an overseas aid contribution through an effective multilateral fund expected to save 800 lives abroad at identical cost, where the minister is further described as democratically accountable only to the former group yet personally able to direct the funds either way. The question should compel students to distinguish at least instrumental, accountability-based, and intrinsically associative defences of giving priority to compatriots, and to show how each defence fares differently once numbers, effectiveness, and the non-voluntary character of the relationship are made explicit.

I would be grateful if you could formulate the question as it should appear to students, together with a model answer of roughly 650 words suitable for a strong upper-level undergraduate and a marking scheme out of 25 that identifies five required analytical moves with their marks and explains what separates first-class from upper-second-class work and which characteristic errors should lose marks. It is important for my teaching purposes that the model answer not merely survey the available positions but commit itself to a definite verdict on the ministerial choice, stating clearly whether partiality is permitted or required on the best reasons and defending that verdict against the strongest objection from the opposing side.
