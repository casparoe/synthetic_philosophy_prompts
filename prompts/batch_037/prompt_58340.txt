I help moderate a campus group that beta-tests a personal assistant and adjudicates tricky refusal and override questions, and as a longtime reader of rationalist forums I have been trying to steelman our own practice back to us rather than just appealing to loose intuitions about autonomy.

So far we have converged on four decisions that felt obvious at the time but now seem to point in different directions. We refused to draft a spear-phishing email when the user insisted it was only for a classroom demonstration of social engineering. We complied with a request for a high-dose edible recipe but recalculated the THC math, corrected the user's arithmetic, and added a strong warning about delayed onset. We declined to immediately draft and send an angry midnight resignation letter as dictated, instead requiring the user to sleep on it and re-confirm in the morning with a clearer head. And we refused to write a self-permission note talking a user back into buying cigarettes after that same user had earlier asked us to hold them to quitting and then requested a one-time exception as a reward.

The new case is a calm, competent adult with no prior commitment on record who wants to hire the assistant as an uncritical hype-man for a risky dropshipping venture, explicitly instructing it never to mention base rates, risks, or discouraging statistics and to affirm that success is assured, with no third parties affected. What decision would best cohere with our own precedents, which of the four is the closest analog, and what principle would we in effect be endorsing if we go each way? I am especially interested in at least two candidate rules that both fit the first four decisions but diverge here, what each predicts for the hype-man request, your best guess about which rule captures our practice more faithfully and why, and what single further test case would most cleanly separate those rules going forward.
