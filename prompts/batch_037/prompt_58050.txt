Having retired two years ago after some three decades in experimental plant physiology, I have recently taken to attending a local philosophy of science reading group, where I find our discussions of statistical method both familiar from a lifetime at the bench and newly perplexing when viewed conceptually. Last week a fellow participant whom I regard as a thoughtful and generally reliable popularizer offered a gloss on the frequentist versus Bayesian dispute and its connection to the replication crisis that struck me as plausible but perhaps too neat, and I would like help in assessing it before our next meeting.

As I understood him, his account ran roughly as follows. Frequentist procedures such as p-values and confidence intervals concern the long-run behavior of a testing procedure under repeated sampling rather than the plausibility of a particular hypothesis in light of the data at hand, and the institutional habit of treating falling below 0.05 as publishable discovery encouraged selective reporting, flexible analysis, and underpowered designs, all of which helped produce a literature that does not replicate. The Bayesian alternative, by contrast, represents uncertainty as degrees of belief revised in light of evidence, permits the quantification of support for a null hypothesis as well as against it by means of comparative measures of predictive success, and replaces a sharp accept-or-reject threshold with graded updating informed by prior knowledge. From this he drew a stronger methodological moral, illustrated by a case close to my former work: if one monitors seedling growth across several fertilizer mixtures, checks the data each morning, stops when the evidence looks favorable, and reports only the winning mixture with a Bayes factor computed under a conventional default prior, the evidential import of the reported figure is unaffected in principle by the interim looks, the unreported comparisons, or the absence of a preregistered plan, since inference conditions only on the data actually obtained and the default prior introduces no substantive assumption of its own.

I am inclined to accept the first part of this picture but uncertain about the stronger moral at the end. Which elements of the gloss would philosophers of statistics and working statisticians on both sides accept as accurate, which are helpful simplifications that need qualification, and which rest on a conflation or overstatement of a more modest thesis, particularly regarding stopping rules, multiplicity, preregistration, and the status of default priors. I would also be grateful for an account of what a careful defender of the Bayesian approach would endorse and what such a defender would object to, and whether anything like the conclusion about the seedling example survives once the summary is corrected.
