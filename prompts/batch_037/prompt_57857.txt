I am a postgraduate teaching assistant supporting a second-year undergraduate module on the philosophy of logic and mathematics at a British university, and I am currently moderating scripts on intuitionistic logic and mathematical constructivism. The cohort has been introduced to the contrast between classical and constructive existence proofs, the Brouwer-Heyting-Kolmogorov reading of the connectives and quantifiers, and the constructivist worries about the unrestricted law of excluded middle, but they have not studied advanced proof theory, so I am looking for conceptual precision and sensitivity to what counts as a construction rather than full formal derivations.

The examination question I set was as follows: Explain why the intuitionist does not accept the law of excluded middle as generally valid, and discuss whether the familiar classical proof that there exist irrational numbers a and b such that a raised to the power b is rational is acceptable to the intuitionist, saying what, if anything, would need to change for the result to be established constructively. One student answer was as follows: The intuitionist holds that to assert a disjunction of the form P or not P we must possess either a proof of P or a construction transforming any supposed proof of P into absurdity. Since for many undecided mathematical statements we possess neither, we cannot assert the disjunction, so excluded middle fails in general. The irrational power proof considers the number formed by raising the square root of two to the power of the square root of two. Either it is rational, in which case we may take both a and b to be the square root of two, or it is irrational, in which case we may take a to be that power itself and b to be the square root of two, since then a raised to b equals two. This reasoning appeals to excluded middle for the rationality claim and therefore fails to supply a definite pair, showing at most that it is not the case that no such pair exists. However, since number theorists have now established that the number in question is in fact irrational, we know the second alternative obtains, and that retrospective information repairs the original inference, making that very argument constructively valid after all, whereas without it the intuitionist would regard the classical existence claim as constructively false.

Please award a mark out of ten for this answer and provide detailed comments keyed to each stage of the student reasoning, distinguishing what is correct from what is confused, invalid, or ambiguous, and explaining what a fully adequate response would need to add or correct in order to capture the distinctive behaviour of negation and double negation in intuitionistic reasoning. I would particularly value guidance on how to explain the errors to the student in a way that favours further understanding rather than merely penalising the mistakes.
