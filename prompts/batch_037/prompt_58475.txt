Here is the homework question I set: Consider a finite state space Omega = {a,b,c,d} with a uniform common prior, so each state has probability 1/4. Compute Alice's and Bob's posteriors for H conditional on each of their possible signals, and say at which states, if any, a disagreement about H is common knowledge. Then explain what Aumann's agreement theorem says about this setup, distinguishing carefully between mutual knowledge and common knowledge of posteriors. Finally, suppose Alice and Bob are two forecasting assistants that publish their posteriors for H to a shared dashboard visible to both. Does the theorem imply that once they have announced once they must agree, and what breaks if we drop the assumption that they started from the same prior?

Here is one student's answer I need to grade: At a, Alice sees {a,b} so P(H)=1 and Bob sees {a,c} so P(H)=1, so they agree. At b, Alice sees {a,b} so P(H)=1 while Bob sees {b,d} so P(H)=1/2, since only b of {b,d} is in H. Similarly at c Alice has 1/2 and Bob has 1, and at d both have 1/2. The disagreement at b is not a counterexample to Aumann's theorem because the theorem only rules out common knowledge of disagreement under a common prior, and here the posteriors 1 and 1/2 are not common knowledge. Common knowledge in this case just means that Alice knows Bob's posterior and Bob knows Alice's posterior, which fails at b because Alice considers a possible where Bob's posterior is 1. If they now publicly announce their posteriors on the dashboard, those announcements immediately become common knowledge, so by the theorem they must revise to the same number in one step. In general this shows that ideal Bayesians with a common prior cannot really disagree at all, and any persistent disagreement we observe must mean they started with different priors.
