I am the safety review lead at a mid-sized startup shipping LLM-based assistants for customer support triage and code review, clearing roughly half a dozen claims each week about what a system knows, believes, intends, or is trying to do. As a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums I am comfortable thinking of belief-talk as a predictive instrument in Dennett's sense, moving between physical, design, and intentional ways of explaining a system depending on payoff, but the engineers and marketers I work with are not, and I have final sign-off under time pressure where loose mentalistic language risks user overreliance on the one side and strategic denial of obvious capabilities on the other.

Please turn the intentional stance into a practical review procedure my team can run in twenty to thirty minutes per claim without philosophical training. Lay out the questions to ask in order, what would count as a sufficient answer to each from logs, evals, or ablations we actually have, where the procedure stops with a do-not-attribute verdict, where it licenses qualified attribution, and where it must hand off to human judgment because the issue is about our tolerance for error or our communicative responsibilities rather than about the system. Be explicit about which distinctions from the theory must survive simplification for the procedure to remain honest, which can be dropped, and where you expect the procedure to misfire.

Then run the procedure on three live disputes: whether our support bot, accurate on routine refund queries but prone to confabulation on edge cases, can be described in the system card as knowing the refund policy; whether our coding agent, which reliably preserves backward compatibility across multi-step refactors except when the test suite is sparse, can be described as intending to preserve compatibility; and a case of your choice where the procedure forces an attribution my marketing colleagues will dislike, such as having to say the system acts as if it believes a falsehood or intends an outcome we would rather disown. Close by stating plainly what the procedure cannot settle.
