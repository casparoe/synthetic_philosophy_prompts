Consider two distinct arguments for the view that large-scale human-caused habitat destruction may on balance reduce wild animal suffering.

The first, the fewer-lives argument, holds that destruction mainly matters by shrinking the total number of wild animals who ever live. If the average wild life contains more suffering than positive well-being, then preventing those lives, even without improving the lives that remain, lowers aggregate suffering.

The second, the better-lives argument, holds that destruction mainly matters by changing what kinds of lives remain. On this view, simplified or human-proximate habitats favor species with slower life histories, lower predation risk, and more stable nutrition, so average welfare per individual rises even if total abundance does not fall much.

Please compare these two lines of thought as alternative defenses of the same practical conclusion. Address their different empirical presuppositions about fecundity, density compensation, and trophic structure; their different ethical presuppositions about total versus average welfare, the asymmetry between pain and pleasure, and whether a life barely worth living still adds value; and their vulnerability to standard ecological counterarguments such as mesopredator release, increased edge-related parasitism, and chronic anthropogenic stressors.

To make the contrast concrete, apply both arguments to two stylized transitions: the conversion of a highly productive ephemeral wetland dominated by highly fecund amphibians and insects into sealed pavement, and the fragmentation of continuous forest into suburban edge supporting dense omnivores with few apex predators. Explain where the two arguments agree, where they diverge in prediction, and which provides the more resilient case for net suffering reduction under uncertainty about whether most wild lives are net negative.
