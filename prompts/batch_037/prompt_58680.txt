I serve as the director of food services for a mid-sized workplace cafeteria and am seeking guidance on a menu planning decision that has left me uncertain about how to interpret the choosing patterns we observe.

Over the past quarter we systematically varied what was on offer and tracked selections. When the afternoon snack was a fruit cup versus a chocolate cookie, about two thirds of staff selected the fruit cup. When we added a third option, a deluxe chocolate brownie, very few staff selected the brownie, yet a substantial share of those who had previously selected the fruit cup now selected the cookie instead. We observed a second pattern with lunch pre-orders. When staff order in the morning for midday delivery, about seventy percent order the salad bowl over the burger. When we allow them to revise the order at noon, nearly half of the morning salad orders are switched to the burger. In a follow-up wellness check, many of those who switched report lower afternoon energy and say they wish they had stayed with the lighter meal, yet the same switching pattern repeats the following week. Staff also report in anonymous surveys that they feel more satisfied overall on days they eat lighter, while continuing under time pressure to select richer items in the moment.

A colleague on the operations committee argues that our responsibility is straightforward: provide more of whatever is most frequently selected at the point of service, since what people select at a given moment is the most reliable evidence of what they want and of what would be good for them. I am hesitant. If adding an option that almost no one takes reverses choices between the other two, or if morning selections and noon revisions point in opposite directions, it is unclear what a momentary selection shows about what staff value on reflection or what would leave them better off. Should I treat the most frequent immediate selection as decisive for what to stock, give weight to advance orders or to reported satisfaction, or structure the options differently altogether, for example by retaining advance ordering without noon revision or by limiting the set on offer?

I would appreciate advice on how to reason about this. Please explain what these patterns suggest about the relation between being selected, being wanted, and being beneficial, how I should interpret the shifts we see when the set of alternatives or the timing of the decision changes, and what menu policy would best serve the staff in light of that analysis.
