After a career in field ecology, now retired, I have begun to study philosophy of biology with particular interest in the debate over adaptationism and the critique of Panglossian explanation associated with Gould and Lewontin.

I grasp the general idea that not every useful trait was built by natural selection for its present effect, and that some features may persist as structural byproducts. Where I remain uncertain is the conceptual boundary that is often drawn between a feature that is merely an incidental correlate of an adaptive design and a feature that began as such a correlate but has subsequently been maintained because it now contributes to fitness.

Could you give me a detailed hypothetical example, constructed for purposes of illustration rather than drawn from a familiar textbook case, that makes this distinction vivid? It would help me if the example involved a single imaginary organism with two different traits, for instance aspects of leaf geometry, shell form, or nesting behavior, one illustrating a persistent byproduct with no present adaptive maintenance and the other illustrating a former byproduct now under stabilizing selection for its secondary effect.

Please explain, for each trait, what pattern of evidence from manipulation experiments, cross-species comparison, or response to artificial selection would lead a biologist to place it on one side of the distinction rather than the other, and why conflating the two cases would misdirect the design of such tests.
