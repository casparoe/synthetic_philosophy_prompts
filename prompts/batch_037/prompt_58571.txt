I am leading a discussion section for first-year undergraduates on reasoning and inference, and several students have dismissed the old story of Achilles and the Tortoise as a word game about endlessly adding premises. I want to show them why the episode raises a live difficulty about what justifies moving from premises to a conclusion, even for a careful reasoner who makes no factual or linguistic mistake.

Please design a fresh, realistic thought experiment for this teaching purpose, set in an ordinary professional context such as a hospital safety check, a building inspection, or a contract review, with no science fiction, no idealized predictors, and no appeal to fatigue, forgetfulness, shifting word meanings, or general distrust of reason. The central figure should be competent and conscientious, should explicitly accept the relevant particular premises and should sincerely agree in the abstract that modus ponens is a valid way to proceed, yet should withhold the conclusion in a way that initially seems principled rather than confused, and the case should turn precisely on what happens when the validity of the step is itself written down as one more item for the person to accept. Keep the scenario itself to roughly a paragraph in plain language accessible to students with no formal logic background.

After presenting the scenario, explain why it satisfies each of my constraints rather than merely stipulating that it does, lay out how a proponent of the Tortoise challenge and how a defender of the view that deductive moves need no propositional backing would each diagnose the protagonist, identify which structural element is doing the philosophical work and whether the puzzle dissolves if that element is removed, and close with the strongest objection the defender could raise that the case begs the question and how the challenger should reply.
