For the past twelve years I have worked as a writer of literary fiction, and the conceptual tools most alive for me are those of craft: scene as against summary, close against distant point of view, dilation against compression of narrated time, the accumulation of concrete sensory detail, degrees of interior access, and the distribution of sustained attention between one figure and a multitude.

I am unsettled by the familiar moral puzzle that sets prolonged and severe suffering imposed on a single person against an almost imperceptible discomfort or delay imposed on each member of an enormous population, together with the dispute over whether enough instances of the latter can ever outweigh the former, and over the relevance of distinctions between what is suffered after the fact and what is risked beforehand, between persons kept separate and harms merely summed, and between smooth tradeoffs and sharp thresholds.

To anchor the exercise, consider two passages I am drafting: one holds a single woman in a cell through three hours of interrogation rendered moment by moment from just behind her eyes, the other surveys three hundred million commuters each losing three seconds at a turnstile in a paragraph of distant, accelerated summary.

Render the principal competing stances on the puzzle — straightforward summation, refusal of summation across persons or across kinds of harm, and intermediate views that allow summation but with diminishing or capped weight — into the craft terms described above, showing how each stance would direct the handling of duration, detail, point of view, and sympathy in cases like my two passages. Then conclude with a candid ledger of loss: which moral distinctions survive the rendering without strain, which survive only by stretching craft notions beyond their proper use, and which have no counterpart in narrative technique at all, together with your judgment whether each failure reveals a limitation of novelistic thinking or exposes an obscurity in the moral idea itself.
