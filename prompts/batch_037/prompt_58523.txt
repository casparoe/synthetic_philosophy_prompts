I facilitate a community reading and theatre group for adults returning to literature after long breaks, and a puzzle about our emotional lives has stayed with me through several discussions with friends after rehearsals. During a recent stage thriller, my pulse raced through an extended chase scene even as I kept in mind that the performers were colleagues I had chatted with at intermission and that nothing was at stake, and silently rehearsing that fact did almost nothing to slow my breathing. A similar stickiness appears after novels: after spending a weekend absorbed in a long story about a family losing its home to a flood, I felt low for the rest of the afternoon while fully aware that no such family existed and that no loss had occurred. This contrasts sharply with everyday deception: when a friend recently told a vivid story about a coworker being dismissed and then admitted he had invented it to illustrate a point, my concern evaporated almost instantly and was replaced by mild irritation.

Has philosophy or psychology settled on a name for this kind of lingering affective momentum that survives clear-eyed acknowledgement of fictionality, and if so, what are the closest neighboring ideas and how does this pattern differ from each in a way that lets me tell which label fits best? I do not want only a label; I want a careful carving of what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie, including why deliberate reminders fail here but succeed when a supposed real report is exposed as invented. If no established concept captures it exactly, please coin a concise term and define it with positive and negative boundary cases. Please answer in no more than 300 words.
