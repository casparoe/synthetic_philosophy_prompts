For the past two terms I have led a tutorial on distributive justice for second-year undergraduates, and I keep running into the same pedagogical difficulty: students readily affirm that society should treat people as equals but cannot articulate whether they care about equal happiness, equal means, or equal real freedom to achieve valued functionings, especially once expensive tastes, adaptive preferences, and differential needs for conversion are introduced. To make the disagreement vivid before we discuss institutional implications, I would like to use a diagnostic exercise in class that reveals underlying commitments through judgments about persons rather than through theoretical slogans.

Please construct a nine-item forced-choice quiz for this purpose, pitched at students with no prior background in economics or moral philosophy. Each item should present a brief pairwise comparison involving two fictional individuals and ask whom a just distributor should favor, or whether no favoring is required, with two or three response options stated in plain language without naming any egalitarian doctrine. The set as a whole must include the following situations recast in your own concrete detail: a musician who needs costly equipment to experience ordinary contentment compared with a neighbor who finds great joy in costless walks; a wheelchair user who reports high subjective satisfaction yet cannot enter the only public library; a woman in a community where custom prevents her from using an equal share of communal grain in company, so that formal parity does not yield effective nourishment; and a contrast between an expensive taste that was deliberately cultivated and one that was unchosen. Additional items should probe responsibility for imprudent choices and adaptation to longstanding deprivation.

Conclude with a scoring guide that maps characteristic response profiles onto four broad families — equalization of felt satisfaction, equalization of transferable means with responsibility for tastes, equalization of effective freedom to be and do valuable things, and a threshold view on which equality matters only below sufficiency — indicating which combinations of answers would be unstable or unoccupied and explaining why, and noting explicitly where your nine vignettes cannot separate two families and would need a further case to do so.
