here is what I currently hold:

1. Hume was right about the gap — you cant get a valid argument to a moral conclusion from purely nonmoral premises alone, there has to be a moral premise in there somewhere doing work.

2. Moore was right about the open question intuition — for any purely natural description like pleasant or adaptive or desired by informed people, it still seems sensible to wonder whether that thing is actually good, which suggests goodness isnt just the same thing as any of those natural features as a matter of meaning.

3. Supervenience seems obviously true to me — if two acts or people are exactly alike in all their natural features, brain states, behavior, consequences, history, whatever, they have to be alike morally too. No free-floating moral difference without a natural difference.

4. I also do ethics flourishing-first — I think we figure out what we should do and what counts as a virtue by looking at what humans need to develop well and stay non-miserable, stuff about sleep, attachment, play, agency, stress, that kind of psychology and child development work. Virtues are just the traits that support that package, so facts about flourishing basically settle what we have reason to cultivate. Like last month I argued we should change our lab authorship rules purely because our survey data showed juniors burn out and quit under the old system, and I took that to settle that we ought to change it, no extra moral premise needed.

5. I rely a lot on thick ideas like cruel and considerate and exploitative and bullying — I think they describe how the world is and at the same time give you a reason to stop or keep going, and moving from that description to the evaluation isnt cheating or smuggling anything in.
