In an interdisciplinary undergraduate seminar on minds and culture that I convene, with 18 second-year students drawn equally from philosophy and biology meeting for 75 minutes, we have spent several weeks on natural selection and on trial-and-error learning, and the group now treats external supports such as diagrams, maxims, and inherited designs as helpful but essentially optional aids to thinking that goes on inside the head. I want them to leave with a more unsettling appreciation, drawn from the framework of Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian and Gregorian creatures, that certain tools reorganize the cognitive task itself so that a community equipped with them can solve problems no isolated brain could anticipate, while also seeing the costs of dependence on such inheritance.

Could you design a hands-on team challenge around an invented case, perhaps the charting of safe foraging routes across a reef grid patrolled by unpredictable predators and seasonal currents, to be played in four successive regimes with tightening and then loosening constraints on guessing, feedback, internal modeling, and borrowing? Please provide complete facilitator instructions for groups of four to five, ordinary classroom materials, a scoring system using tokens rather than money, and a way of staging the four regimes so that participants confront the limitations of blind variation, bare reinforcement, solitary foresight, and finally culturally scaffolded design through their own failures and successes before the fourfold terminology is introduced. Conclude with guidance for a fifteen-minute debrief that connects specific turning points in play to the conceptual distinctions, anticipates two or three practical difficulties a first-time facilitator is likely to encounter with this cohort and how to address them, and acknowledges candidly one central feature of the philosophical view that the exercise necessarily simplifies or omits.
