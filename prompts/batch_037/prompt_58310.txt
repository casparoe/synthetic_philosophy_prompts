I help facilitate a campus discussion group composed largely of longtime readers of rationalist and effective altruist forums, and after several meetings on whether to prioritize donations to projects aimed at reducing risks of astronomical future suffering, we found ourselves talking past each other with labels like s-risk minimizer, longtermist, and skeptic. I would like a one-page decision tree handout that sorts participants by their substantive commitments rather than by which label they antecedently prefer, suitable for undergraduates with some familiarity with expected-value reasoning but no background in population ethics.

Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names. Start from the shared starting point that future technologically created minds are at least possible, then branch on diagnostic questions participants can answer without knowing where the branches lead, including whether simulated or artificially trained minds with aversive signals would matter morally if they were sentient, whether preventing severe suffering takes lexical or otherwise strong priority over promoting happiness or other goods, and whether large-scale future suffering is sufficiently likely and tractable to outweigh near-term animal-welfare work. Each leaf should correspond to a substantive stance I can recognize among my friends: a dedicated focus on minimizing s-risks even at cost to other longtermist goals, a broader impartial welfarist view that treats s-risks as one consideration among many, a near-term suffering-focused view skeptical of longtermist speculation, a technology-led optimism that future abundance will dissolve the worry, and a skeptical view that denies the coherence or decision-relevance of the whole framing.

Use six to eight branch questions in total, illustrate each with a concrete test case such as a proposal to pause large training runs that rely on punishment-like signals, a proposed insect brain-emulation project for agricultural pest control, and a choice between funding a far-future governance project versus a present-day shrimp-welfare campaign, and for any combination of answers that no one in practice holds, add a one-sentence diagnosis of whether the gap reflects incoherence, unattractiveness, or merely an unexplored position. Where your tree cannot separate two positions without adding further machinery, explicitly flag that limitation rather than forcing a distinction.
