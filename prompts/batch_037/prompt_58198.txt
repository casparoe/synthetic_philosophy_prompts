I am preparing to evaluate competing accounts of how a conscientious agent should act while uncertain about basic moral questions, and I wish to establish the standards of assessment before considering any particular proposal.

For this purpose, take moral uncertainty to mean uncertainty about which moral principles are true, how different values compare in importance, and how central moral concepts should be understood, as distinct from uncertainty about empirical facts or about the likely consequences of action. The theory in question should be practical, in the sense of offering action guidance to an agent who must decide now and cannot defer judgment until philosophical disagreement is resolved.

Please develop a set of desiderata that such a theory must satisfy in order to count as adequate. For each item on the list, explain the underlying rationale, drawing on a concrete scenario, a conceptual boundary that must be preserved, or a finding from psychology, economics, or decision theory with which the theory must remain compatible. Among other matters, the list should address how to compare the demands of rival moral outlooks, how to separate the influence of confidence in an outlook from the influence of what is at stake if the outlook is true, how to avoid both excessive caution and objectionable indifference to moral risk, and how to account for the difference between acting well and being able to justify the act to others who hold different outlooks.

To make the discussion concrete, apply each desideratum to the following two situations constructed for this exercise. First, a director of a municipal relief fund must allocate a fixed budget and is torn between an impartial outlook that treats distant strangers and near neighbors as equally deserving and an associative outlook that permits strong priority to members of the local community, with the two outlooks also disagreeing about whether wastefulness is a minor inefficiency or a serious moral failure. Second, a clinician must decide whether to follow an advance instruction written by a patient in an early stage of cognitive decline, where one plausible outlook treats precedent autonomy as decisive and another treats current experiential interests as decisive, and where the clinician has moderate confidence in each outlook but the burdens of error are highly asymmetric.

Conclude by indicating which desiderata you take to be central to any acceptable view and which reflect the preferences of a particular ethical or decision theoretic camp and may therefore be set aside, by identifying any tensions where full satisfaction of one desideratum appears to prevent full satisfaction of another, and by naming the single desideratum that places the strongest restriction on the space of viable theories. A brief illustration of how two broad families of response, one that selects action by weighting outlooks according to confidence and stakes and another that selects action by following the single most credible outlook, fare against the central desiderata will be sufficient, without extended exposition of those families.
