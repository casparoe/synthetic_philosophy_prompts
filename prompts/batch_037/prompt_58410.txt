I am endeavoring to bring my judgments concerning reliance on the moral judgments of others into reflective equilibrium and would appreciate assistance in articulating the principle that best systematizes them.

My verdicts on six concrete situations are as follows. First, a young adult with little prior moral reflection asks a morally sensitive mentor whether eating meat produced under standard industrial conditions is wrong, accepts an affirmative answer, and becomes vegetarian without ever engaging the reasons, though her action is morally correct. This strikes me as deficient in a way that goes beyond mere lack of curiosity. Second, a busy physician follows her hospital ethics committee protocol for obtaining consent from patients with limited English proficiency, without independently working through the autonomy-based rationale, because she must act promptly and the protocol is trustworthy. This strikes me as entirely unobjectionable, indeed responsible. Third, a voter with no prior view on a ballot measure concerning prolonged solitary confinement reads a detailed account by a formerly incarcerated activist, becomes convinced the practice is cruel, and votes accordingly while later unable to reconstruct more than the gist of the argument. This strikes me as permissible, even exemplary. Fourth, a visitor in an unfamiliar community asks a local guide whether photographing a funeral procession passing in the street would be disrespectful, is told that it would be, and refrains. This strikes me as unobjectionable. Fifth, a person who finds moral deliberation tedious subscribes to a highly reliable artificial advisor for everyday dilemmas concerning matters such as whether to report a coworker minor misconduct or how to allocate childcare duties between partners, and simply does whatever the system outputs without considering why. Even stipulating excellent reliability, this strikes me as deeply troubling, more so than the physician case. Sixth, parallel nonmoral deference to a reliable instrument or specialist for predictive or technical matters, such as following a weather model for storm preparation or a laboratory result for diagnosis, strikes me as unobjectionable.

My tentative hypothesis is that deference is acceptable when it is constrained by role-based responsibilities, time pressure, or local ignorance, but objectionable when it functions as a standing substitute for the exercise of moral agency. I am unsure whether this can distinguish the physician from the artificial advisor subscriber, or the voter from the new vegetarian.

Please propose one or at most two candidate principles, stated precisely, that might capture this pattern. For each principle, indicate which case motivates each qualification or clause, explain what it predicts for two further situations about which I am undecided — retrospective deference about whether my own past conduct was wrong and warranted guilt, and prospective deference about whether to forgive another person who has apologized — and state candidly which of my six verdicts, if any, the principle cannot accommodate and should therefore lead me to revise.
