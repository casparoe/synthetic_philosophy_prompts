I would like help bringing my judgments about internal versus external reasons for action into reflective equilibrium. I am attracted to the view that having a reason to act must connect in some way to one's own motives, yet my verdicts on cases do not line up neatly.

A health-conscious commuter who drinks sugary energy drinks because he mistakenly believes they are healthy: I think he has reason to stop, though his present motives favor continuing.

An exhausted friend who would notice her friend's distress and want to help if she were less depleted: I think she has reason to check in now.

A hobbyist who finds deep satisfaction in cataloguing pavement cracks, harming no one: I think he has reason to continue, even though no fully informed or virtuous person would care about this.

A cruel manager who enjoys humiliation and would still endorse it after calm, fully informed reflection: I still think he has reason not to act cruelly.

An ambitious scholar who would, after prolonged reflection, convert to values that condemn her current life as worthless: I think she does not now have reason to live by those future values.

Can a single principle capture these five verdicts, and if not, which verdict should I abandon? Please defend a definite principle, show how each clause earns its keep from a case above, state what it predicts for a further case I have not listed involving a person whose core commitments would be transformed by vivid acquaintance with distant suffering, and be explicit about where you bite the bullet.
