My current thinking about imperfect recall favors a resolute treatment of the following driving case, and I would like to understand its vulnerability.

Consider a driver traveling at night who must leave a highway at the second of two exits to reach a destination. The two intersections appear exactly alike in the dark, and the driver will not be able to tell while driving whether an intersection encountered is the first or the second. If the driver exits at the first intersection, the result is a long detour with a low payoff, which we can set at zero. If the driver continues past both intersections, the result is a missed destination with a modest payoff, which we can set at one. If the driver exits at the second intersection, the result is arrival with a high payoff, which we can set at four. Before the trip the driver can settle on a general tendency to exit when an intersection is encountered, including a tendency implemented by a chance device consulted at each intersection, but during the trip the driver cannot condition differently on the two locations because they are indistinguishable. The ex ante calculation favors exiting with a one-third tendency at any encountered intersection. The resolute position I am drawn to says that rationality consists in selecting that ex ante optimal tendency before the trip and then adhering to it on the road without reconsideration, and that a theory of rational agency that licenses the driver to revise the tendency upon arriving at an unidentified intersection is thereby disqualified as an account of agency under imperfect recall.

I would like a careful account of what speaks against this resolute position and how seriously to take each concern. Please develop variant cases I can follow from the description above, such as a version where the driver may pay a small amount of the payoff to leave a mark that distinguishes the intersections, a version with three indistinguishable intersections and a suitably adjusted payoff structure, and a version where a reconsidering driver can anticipate a predictable pattern of future reconsideration. Explain for each whether it reveals a structural failure, an implausible prediction about what the driver will do or feel, or only a cost that a resolute theorist can accept. Also address whether formal work on imperfect recall, intuitions about personal persistence and responsibility over time, and comparisons with ordinary cases of planning and temptation tell for or against resoluteness, and say which objections defenders of resoluteness would themselves regard as genuine liabilities rather than misunderstandings. Conclude with a judgment about whether the resolute view as I have stated it actually stakes anything on how such cases turn out or whether it can be restated to absorb any outcome, and whether that flexibility should worry someone attracted to it. I am most likely to be moved by concrete reasoning about the variants described here rather than by general methodological remarks.

Please present your assessment in continuous prose only, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of an email or letter.
