I serve on an advisory group for a country emerging from twelve years of authoritarian rule marked by several thousand disappearances and systematic torture by security forces, now governed by a fragile coalition that must decide how to address past abuses while preserving a precarious stability.

For the past eighteen months our deliberations have circled without movement between two camps. One camp favors a commission that offers individualized, conditional amnesty from criminal prosecution in exchange for full public disclosure of politically motivated crimes, combined with a reparations program and a narrow prosecutorial backstop for those who refuse to participate or fail to disclose fully. Its advocates argue that this arrangement maximizes truth recovery, gives public acknowledgment to victims, and makes democratic transition viable under constraints where the former security apparatus remains largely intact. The other camp favors prioritizing criminal trials for senior and mid-level perpetrators, with sentence reductions rather than amnesty available in exchange for cooperation, on the grounds that punishment is required by justice and the rule of law, that amnesty entrenches impunity, and that reconciliation without accountability is hollow.

Both camps invoke the broad experiences of South Africa, Argentina, and Rwanda, both claim to value truth, justice, and reconciliation, and both concede considerable uncertainty about deterrence, truth recovery, and social healing, yet neither has shifted.

My hypothesis is that the persistence of the impasse reflects not primarily a factual dispute about consequences but a deeper divergence over what justice fundamentally requires in transition, compounded by the two sides addressing different practical problems, one focused on securing a workable political transition and the other on vindicating the moral status of victims, while using a shared vocabulary that obscures this difference.

Assess whether this hypothesis is correct and provide a more general diagnosis of the structure of this impasse. Consider whether the disagreement is principally empirical, normative, or conceptual, whether central terms are being used in different senses, whether the camps are answering different questions under a common heading, and whether unargued starting commitments are doing the decisive work. Ground your diagnosis in the arguments each camp actually advances as described above, and conclude by specifying what, if anything, could constitute progress given your diagnosis, whether a conceptual distinction, a feasible empirical inquiry, an institutional redesign, or a reframing of the question, or explain why no such progress should be expected.

Please answer in American English.
