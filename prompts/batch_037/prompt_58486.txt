As a retired physical chemist who has lately taken up philosophy by attending our town's discussion circle, I should be grateful for assistance in interpreting a pattern of judgments offered by our convenor on vagueness.

Over several meetings, the convenor has endorsed the following positions, which I record as faithfully as I can:

1. A scalp with no hairs at all is bald, and one with dense coverage of some 150,000 hairs is not bald, yet no amount of counting, microscopy, or improved physiology could in principle locate a dividing number.

2. Beginning with a pile of 10,000 grains that is a heap, removing one grain cannot by itself turn a heap into something that is not a heap, and the same holds for each successive single removal considered on its own; still, one grain, or two or three, does not make a heap. The reasoning that chains these steps together to reach an absurd conclusion must fail, though not at any step one can point to.

3. When led through a forced march of colour chips running by very small steps from a clear red to a clear orange, where neighbouring chips are indistinguishable to the normal eye, a competent speaker who answers red for a while and then declines to give a yes-or-no answer for a stretch in the middle commits no linguistic mistake by so declining, and two such speakers may begin declining at different places without either being at fault.

4. Greater scientific information does not remove the difficulty: detailed knowledge of follicles, angles of repose, or retinal responses leaves the classifications just as unsettled.

5. The unsettled region itself has no sharp edges: there is no precise point where clear baldness ends and borderline baldness begins, nor where borderline baldness ends and clear non-baldness begins.

For next month I have proposed, in a laboratory spirit, a construction that requires a bivalent response. Take sand piles descending grain by grain, or water samples ascending in tenth-degree steps from clearly tepid to clearly hot, and require an indicator lamp to display either HEAP or NOT HEAP, or HOT or NOT HOT, for each exact total or temperature, with no third response or suspension permitted. I asked where the lamp must change over in order to be correct, and whether exactly one setting among the many possible ones uniquely gets the facts right, the others getting them wrong.

Please predict what our convenor would say about this lamp case in light of the five earlier verdicts. Articulate at least two general rules or principles that each fit those verdicts about equally well, explain for each what it predicts the convenor will say about the lamp, offer a reasoned estimate of which prediction is the more likely with the reasons favouring it, and describe one further hypothetical case, distinct from the lamp, that would best tell the candidate rules apart.
