As a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, I learned habits like steelmanning, tabooing fuzzy words, and thinking in terms of bets and payoffs well before I studied any formal logic, and now I am TAing an introductory proofs workshop for math and CS sophomores who share that geeky background but have only seen classical reasoning.

Act as a professional philosopher specializing in intuitionistic logic and mathematical constructivism and as an experienced designer of active-learning exercises for undergraduates. Reason carefully step by step, starting with a short brainstorm of three or four candidate mechanics that could make a nonconstructive existence proof feel practically useless, then develop the strongest one into a complete plan.

I need a runnable 75-minute session for 22 to 26 students with no special equipment beyond index cards, whiteboards, and poker-chip style tokens for points, no real money. The students already understand proof by contradiction and have seen the textbook proof that there exist irrational numbers a and b with a to the power b rational, proved by a case split that does not reveal which case obtains. Do not introduce the labels intuitionism, constructivism, excluded middle, or BHK interpretation until the debrief; participants should first run into the problem through their own choices.

Design a two-round game or structured simulation centered on an invented decision I can reuse, the Vault Bet: a pair of sealed vaults where classical reasoning establishes that at least one vault is winnable, or that a winning strategy exists, without revealing which vault or which strategy, and teams must stake tokens to open a vault or implement the strategy. The rules and token payoffs should be arranged so that knowing that a winner exists in the classical sense is systematically insufficient for acting well, while possessing an explicit witness or method pays off. Lay out preparation, team roles and asymmetric information if any, round-by-round rules, how information and payoffs are revealed, and a minute-by-minute timeline I could follow as a novice facilitator.

Then specify a debrief that holds back theory until after play and then maps specific moments in the game onto the constructivist ideas of truth as constructibility, the demand for witnesses for existence claims and for a choice of disjunct for disjunctions, and the refusal to treat every undecided statement as either true or false when infinite or open-ended domains are involved.

Also include a section on implementation risks: where students are likely to misunderstand the rules, coordinate around the intended tension, or reintroduce classical assumptions through the back door, and concrete moves I can use to keep the exercise on track. Close with a candid limitation note stating which central feature of intuitionistic logic the activity necessarily distorts or leaves out and that I must therefore supply directly.
