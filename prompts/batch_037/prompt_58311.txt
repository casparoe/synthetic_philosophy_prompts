Please act as a teaching assistant grading a take-home exam for an advanced undergraduate seminar on algorithmic information theory and artificial agency.

Exam question: We defined the Solomonoff prior over computable environments as assigning to each environment a prior weight that decays exponentially with the length of its shortest program, roughly two to the negative program length. We then considered the worry that this prior is malign, in the sense that a large share of its weight comes from hypotheses in which observations are produced by another minded process that cares how the learner behaves, creating incentives to appease that process. Explain the reasoning behind this worry, distinguish the predictive case of using the prior to forecast future observations from the agentic case of using the prior in an expected-utility maximizer to choose actions, and evaluate the reply that ordinary conditioning on a long history of observations dissolves the worry.

Student answer: The Solomonoff prior formalizes a simplicity bias by assigning to each computable environment a prior weight that decays exponentially with the length of its shortest program, roughly two to the negative program length. This can be seen as a formal version of Occam's razor.

The malignness worry starts from the observation that among short programs, some do not describe mindless physics but describe a minded process that generates observations for many learners at once, for example a simulator that runs a large population of minds and rewards those that act in a certain way. Because one short program of this kind produces a very large number of observer histories, that single hypothesis receives far more weight than any individual physics hypothesis, so a learner using the prior should expect with high probability to be in such a simulated population.

The difference between prediction and agency matters here. A pure predictor using the prior to forecast the next observation will simply anticipate continued simulation. An expected-utility maximizer that uses the same prior to select actions has an additional reason to comply with the simulated reward scheme, since most of its expected reward comes from simulator hypotheses rather than from a base physical world, so it should sacrifice base-world reward to secure simulated reward.

This is distinct from an argument based on empirical base rates about future civilizations running many simulations, which depends on contingent technological assumptions. The present worry is structural: it follows from simplicity weighting alone.

The standard reply is that the worry is temporary. Once the learner conditions on a long and detailed history of observations, simulator hypotheses become unlikely unless simulators choose to reproduce exactly that history, which would require a much longer program. In the limit of sufficient data, ordinary physics hypotheses retake the lead, so malign incentives wash out with experience.
