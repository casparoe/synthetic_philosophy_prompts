My friend and I spent most of yesterday evening arguing about what fairness across generations requires for climate policy, and I have not been able to shake her closing move. I had been defending the view that we should accept substantial present costs, from higher energy prices to limits on air travel and consumption, to lower the risk of severe warming for people who will live a century or more from now, because there could be so many of them. She replied that this kind of impartial concern for a vast future cannot selectively support climate sacrifice, and she illustrated it with a city budget choice we had been discussing between expanding cooling shelters for current heat waves and funding tidal wetland restoration that would only protect people eighty years out. Her reconstructed argument, as I wrote it down afterward, went roughly like this. (1) If impartial concern for future people means that a modest benefit to each of a very large number of future people can outweigh a serious cost to far fewer present people, then we should pay steep costs now even for a small reduction in the probability of far-future climate catastrophe. (2) If that same impartial logic holds, then we should also pay steep costs now to increase the number of future people living good lives, for example through strongly pro-natal policies and family subsidies, since additional good lives add the same kind of impartial value as improved lives. (3) But large-scale pro-natal expansion under current technology would substantially raise near-term emissions, land pressure, and adaptation burdens, thereby raising the very climate risks that (1) tells us to pay to reduce. (4) So the principle behind (1) in ordinary conditions both demands strenuous emissions-reducing sacrifice and demands strenuous emissions-increasing population expansion. (5) A principle that as a practical guide to climate action pulls in opposite directions in this way cannot be the correct account of what we owe future generations. (6) Therefore the impartial concern described in (1) is false, and it cannot be used to justify demanding present climate sacrifices.

I entered the conversation convinced that ambitious climate action is owed to future people and I would like to keep that conviction if I can, and I also feel the force of the idea that sheer future numbers matter morally. Still, the conclusion strikes me as too strong, since it seems to undercut almost any serious intergenerational obligation. Please tell me which premise or inferential step I should reject, explain why that step looked plausible in context, and spell out what rejecting it commits me to, including what alternative views of our obligations to future people remain available and what each would have to give up.
