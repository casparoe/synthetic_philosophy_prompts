You are serving as a grader for an undergraduate course on paradoxes of rational choice. Evaluate the student response below to the homework problem that follows it. Provide a numerical score out of 20 points and a point-by-point assessment that identifies what the response gets right, what it gets wrong or leaves incomplete, and why, with concrete suggestions for feedback to the student.

Homework problem, worth 20 points total:

Two envelopes are filled with cash by an organizer, with one containing exactly twice as much as the other. You are handed one envelope at random.

Part A, 6 points: Suppose you do not open the envelope. A friend argues that you should switch to the other envelope because, letting A be the amount in your envelope, the other envelope contains either 2A or A/2 with equal probability, for an expected value of 1.25A. Explain whether this reasoning is correct and whether you have any reason to switch.

Part B, 7 points: Suppose instead you open your envelope and find 100 dollars inside. The same friend now argues that the other envelope contains either 50 dollars or 200 dollars with equal probability, so its expected value is 125 dollars and you should switch. Identify the flaw in this reasoning, if any, and explain what additional information you would need to determine the expected value of switching.

Part C, 7 points: Suppose you know the organizer acts as follows. With probability 0.8 the organizer fills the envelopes with 40 and 80 dollars, and with probability 0.2 the organizer fills them with 80 and 160 dollars, then shuffles them and hands you one at random. You open your envelope and find 80 dollars. What is the probability that you hold the smaller envelope, what is the expected dollar value of the other envelope, and should you switch?

Student response:

Part A: The reasoning is incorrect. If I have not looked, switching cannot help or hurt on average. Whichever envelope I start with, switching just swaps a 50-50 chance at the larger amount for a 50-50 chance at the larger amount. The calculation with A goes wrong because A is not a fixed number. In one case A refers to the smaller amount and in the other case A refers to the larger amount, so the two branches of the expectation calculation are talking about different underlying amounts. There is no expected gain from switching.

Part B: The basic problem is the same as in Part A. Seeing 100 dollars does not tell me whether 100 is the smaller amount from a 50 and 100 pair or the larger amount from a 100 and 200 pair. To compute the expected value of the other envelope I would need a prior distribution over what pairs the organizer might have used. The equal probability assumption is justified by the randomness of the initial deal, since I was equally likely to get either envelope. So the 50-50 step is fine. The real issue is that expected dollar value is the wrong decision criterion for a one-time choice, since expected value reasoning only applies to long-run frequencies. With diminishing marginal utility of money, a risk-averse person could rationally stay even if the expected dollar value of switching is 125 dollars.

Part C: Given that I see 80 dollars, I could be in the 40 and 80 case holding the larger envelope or in the 80 and 160 case holding the smaller envelope. Since I was equally likely to be dealt either envelope within whichever pair was chosen, those two possibilities are equally likely. So the probability I hold the smaller envelope is 0.5 and the expected value of the other envelope is 0.5 times 40 plus 0.5 times 160, which is 100 dollars. Since 100 is greater than 80, I should switch.

Grading task: Assess each part separately, awarding partial credit as appropriate, and then give a total score out of 20. For each part, quote the relevant element of the reasoning in your own paraphrase rather than repeating it verbatim, explain whether the key conceptual moves are correct, call out any conflation, missing step, or invalid inference, and state how many points you would deduct and why. Conclude with overall feedback on the students understanding of conditional expectation, the role of prior information, and the difference between probabilistic and decision-theoretic issues.
