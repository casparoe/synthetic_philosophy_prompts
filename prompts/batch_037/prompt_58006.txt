As a professor of philosophy, I am trying to bring my judgments about higher-order evidence into reflective equilibrium and would appreciate assistance in identifying the principle behind them. My current verdicts on four cases are as follows. First, after adding a column of grades late at night I learn that my error rate for such calculations at that hour is very high; I take it to be rational to suspend judgment until I recheck. Second, after extended discussion a well-informed colleague with the same evidence continues to disagree with me about compatibilism; I take it to be rational for me to retain my view with at most a slight reduction in confidence. Third, I believe a historical thesis on the basis of archival material while also believing, on good grounds about my own partisan bias in this area, that my evidence does not support that thesis; I take this akratic combination to be irrational. Fourth, I hold the same historical belief with the same judgment that my own unaided assessment is biased, but in this variant a well-calibrated automated research assistant available in my department has independently rated the inference as strongly supported, and I have good reason to trust its calibration even when I distrust myself; I take retaining the historical belief in this variant to be rational. Is there a precise principle that captures this pattern, or comes close? Please state the principle or the most promising candidates, indicate which of my cases motivates each qualification, apply it to one further case of your own construction, and state plainly which of my four verdicts, if any, must be revised.
