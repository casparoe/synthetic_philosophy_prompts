I serve as chair of a district secondary-school history curriculum review panel that meets twice yearly to decide which episodes from our town's twentieth-century past should retain a dedicated classroom unit, which should be folded into broader thematic teaching, and which should be retired from required instruction to make room for other material.

Please provide a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the philosophical debate between a duty to remember past wrongs and the case for collective forgetting, that panel members without philosophical training can apply in about thirty minutes per episode. The procedure should be presented as an ordered sequence of questions or tests, with guidance on what would count as an adequate answer to each at our level of evidence, clear stopping points where the answer is to retain or to retire, and explicit marking of points where factual inquiry ends and a value judgment by the panel or the superintendent, who gives final sign-off, must take over. In designing the procedure, preserve the distinctions you judge essential from the philosophical debate, such as between mere factual knowledge and active commemoration, between obligations to victims and broader civic uses of memory, and between living memory and settled history, and state briefly which further nuances you are deliberately setting aside for usability and why. The procedure and its rationale must be explainable to parents and defensible at a public board meeting.

Then apply the procedure step by step to three stylized local cases I have constructed for this exercise: A, a 1970s factory strike that ended in street violence and two deaths, still keenly remembered by some families but experienced by current students primarily as a source of division; B, a wartime civilian effort that sheltered refugees, long celebrated in assemblies and memorials and a source of civic pride; and C, a 1960s urban renewal program that displaced a small neighborhood, well documented in the municipal archive but rarely taught and distressing for some students to encounter. Include one application where a faithful use of your procedure recommends an outcome many in the community will dislike, and explain why the procedure so recommends.

Conclude by stating what the procedure cannot settle and where it is most likely to misfire.
