I am preparing a short essay for an interdisciplinary undergraduate seminar on limits of formalization, and I would appreciate critical comments on a draft before I revise it for presentation. The draft is below, followed by the specific kinds of feedback I am seeking.

Draft: Why There Cannot Be a Perfect Handbook

Consider a large institution that attempts to write a perfect employee handbook. By perfect I mean three things at once. First, the handbook is comprehensive: for any workplace situation that falls under its scope, it determines what is to be done. Second, it is coherent: it never issues incompatible directives for the same situation. Third, it is self-certifying: using only reasoning that the handbook itself authorizes, one can show that it has the second property, so staff need not appeal to outside judgment to trust it.

The temptation is to invoke Gödel's incompleteness results here, and my aim is to show both what that invocation gets right and where it goes wrong. Exposited informally, the relevant results concern formal systems that are precisely specified, consistent, and expressive enough to represent basic arithmetic. For any such system, there will be a well-formed claim in its language that is true but not decidable within the system, in the sense that neither it nor its negation can be derived from the system's principles. Moreover, such a system cannot establish its own consistency by means restricted to the system itself.

The handbook analogy is imperfect but instructive if we disambiguate it. Comprehensiveness in the everyday sense of covering many cases is not the same as completeness in the logical sense of deciding every sentence of a formal language. A handbook can be usefully comprehensive while leaving many questions undecided, because most undecided sentences of an arithmetically expressive system correspond to no practical dilemma. Similarly, coherence in the everyday sense of avoiding clashing orders is weaker than formal consistency, since a handbook can tolerate vagueness, discretionary clauses, and priority rules — follow safety rules over efficiency rules, refer novel cases upward — that have no direct analogue in a purely deductive system.

My central claim is therefore conditional. To the extent that the institution insists on removing all discretion and turning the handbook into a fully mechanical decision procedure whose coherence must be demonstrated without appealing to anything outside the handbook, it recreates the conditions that make the incompleteness phenomena bite: sufficient expressive power to talk about its own rules and their application, plus a demand for internal closure. At that point, the pursuit of the third desideratum, self-certification, becomes the vulnerable one. Either the handbook will leave some question about its own directives undecided, or any internal certificate of its coherence will have to rely on principles whose own standing has not been certified. The lesson is not that coherent institutions are impossible, but that coherence has to be secured in part from the outside, through judgment, amendment, and practices of interpretation that are not themselves fully codified.

I would welcome comments on four points. First, accuracy: does the informal exposition of the two incompleteness results misstate any essential condition, especially regarding consistency, expressive strength, and the gap between truth and derivability? Second, the disambiguation: does the distinction among comprehensiveness, logical completeness, and self-certification clarify the argument, or does it introduce confusions of its own? Third, scope: does the conditional claim about mechanical decision procedures overreach in extending a result about formal systems to institutional rules, and if so, where exactly does the analogy break down? Fourth, revision: which step in the draft is weakest, what counterexample or sharper formulation would most improve it, and is the overall thesis interesting enough to sustain a twenty-minute seminar discussion?
