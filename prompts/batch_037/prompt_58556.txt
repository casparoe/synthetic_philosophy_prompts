After four decades as an experimental physical chemist working chiefly with optical spectroscopy, I retired five years ago and have since begun to study philosophy of science systematically for the first time, mainly through a local adult education seminar.

In our recent meeting on the underdetermination of theory by evidence, the seminar leader introduced the topic by comparing theories to mathematical curves fitted to a finite set of data points. Since infinitely many distinct curves can be drawn through any finite collection of points, the suggestion was that infinitely many distinct theories can always be made to accommodate any finite body of observational evidence. The moral drawn was a strong one, namely that empirical evidence alone can never single out a unique theory, so that choice among theories must always rest on non-empirical considerations such as simplicity or convention, with no expectation that further evidence will remove the tie in principle.

This analogy puzzles me because it both resonates with and seems to misdescribe my laboratory experience. When calibrating a spectrometer, for example to determine lead concentration from atomic absorption measurements on six standard solutions, it is mathematically true that infinitely many functions pass exactly through the calibration points. In practice, however, no competent chemist would entertain the wildly oscillating higher-order polynomials that do so. Only smooth, nearly linear, monotonically increasing functions motivated by the physics of absorption are taken seriously, and gross deviations are treated as grounds to repeat the measurement rather than to adopt a gyrating curve. Background theory thus seems to do decisive work that the bare curve-fitting picture leaves out.

I would be grateful for a careful audit of how far the curve-fitting analogy for underdetermination actually holds. Please set out explicitly what corresponds to what, with data points, curves, exact fit, and the infinity of alternatives on one side and observations, theories, empirical adequacy, and rival theories on the other. A table of correspondences is welcome. Then identify the principal points at which the mapping breaks down, distinguishing those disanalogies that matter for the strong moral described above from those that are incidental. In particular, address whether constraints from background theory, the revisability of the data points themselves, and the capacity of theories to make novel predictions undermine the analogy or merely relocate the underdetermination to a higher level. Finally, assess whether the strong conclusion about evidence never singling out a theory survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and suggest whether a revised or entirely different analogy would capture the situation more accurately.
