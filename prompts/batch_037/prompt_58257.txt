As part of my master's work in public health, I sit on a student committee that advises a local charity on how to distribute limited funds for chronic pain management, and our informal discussions keep circling back to a distinction I do not have good language for.

Suppose Program Equal leaves ten patients at a stable functioning level of, say, 50 on our internal scale, while Program Unequal leaves five patients at 50 and five at 70, with no one below 50 in either case. Almost everyone in the room judges Unequal to be better, and judges that moving from Unequal to Equal by withholding treatment from the better-off group, without improving anyone, would be a loss rather than a gain, even though it would make things fully equal. Yet the same people also judge that a third option, Program Priority, which moves four patients from 30 up to 45 while moving three already comfortable patients from 80 down to 75 to pay for it, is an improvement over the status quo, even though total gains and losses are close and inequality remains. So equality by itself does not seem to be what they value, but neither is sheer total benefit, since they give extra importance to improvements at the lower end.

Is there an established concept or literature for this particular combination of judgments, and how should it be distinguished from nearby positions that simply favor equality as such or simply add up benefits regardless of who receives them. I am not looking for a one-word label alone; I want an account of what exactly the view underlying these judgments amounts to, what it excludes, and how to handle edge cases such as when a tiny benefit to many badly off patients is weighed against a large benefit to a few well-off ones, or when equalizing downward would relieve resentment without changing material outcomes.
