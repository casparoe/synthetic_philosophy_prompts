After thirty-one years as an experimental biologist, I have used my retirement to study philosophy of science and technology, and I am trying to see whether my instincts about openness, formed at the bench, survive contact with arguments about frontier artificial intelligence.

I seem to have arrived at five commitments that I find individually plausible but am unsure can be held jointly, and I would welcome a careful examination of how they bear on one another.

(i) Openness improves both progress and safety. In laboratory science, sharing of full methods, data and materials allows others to reproduce, probe and correct work, while concealment tends to hide error and concentrate unaccountable power. I am inclined to extend this lesson to artificial intelligence, holding that broad access to systems for independent study is generally safer than confinement to a few private laboratories, even when the systems in question could be misused.

(ii) Restricted development entrenches inequality without preventing diffusion. When highly capable models are available only through controlled interfaces, universities, public laboratories and workers in low-resource settings remain dependent on a small number of firms and state actors. Since similar capabilities are often arrived at independently by several groups, I suspect that withholding access mainly determines who benefits first rather than whether the capability appears at all.

(iii) Some material should nevertheless be shared only under oversight. In biology I defended not publishing certain detailed pathogen-related protocols openly, but making them available through vetted channels, accepting a cost to reproducibility, on the grounds that unrestricted circulation would materially reduce the effort needed to cause large-scale harm.

(iv) A particular case now tests this outlook. Consider a broadly capable biological foundation model that would be valuable for designing diagnostics and tracking crop and human pathogens in clinics without reliable internet, and for allowing independent researchers to probe its failures, where the developer proposes to make the underlying adjustable parameters openly downloadable. I find myself in favour of such open release even while recognizing that those parameters can be further trained by third parties to disable built-in refusals concerning hazardous biological assistance.

(v) My thinking about such cases is guided by two further ideas. First, that if a developer has tested a system with reasonable care for its intended applications, deliberate harmful uses made of it later by informed parties are the responsibility of those parties rather than the developer. Second, that whether to permit open release should be decided by considering the full range of foreseeable effects, counting both the harms that misuse would bring and the health and epistemic costs of delaying or limiting beneficial and safety-relevant uses.

Can these positions be maintained together, or do some of them undermine others? I would be grateful for an audit that separates merely apparent conflicts from genuine ones, arranges any genuine conflicts by how serious they are, explains for each what would have to be revised or qualified to restore coherence and what that revision would cost, and closes with a judgment about which single change would do the most to make the overall view tenable.
