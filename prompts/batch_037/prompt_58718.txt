I'm TAing for an intro stats class this semester and keep arguing with my roommates about our grading rules, so curious what you think. Our rubric basically gives you almost no credit if you circle the right number but show no work, but almost full credit if your reasoning is right and you just mess up 2+2 at the end. And this term the dept added these little five minute oral check-ins where even if your problem set was all correct you have to explain why you did what you did, or they dock you. My roommate says its all just anti-cheating paranoia, especially with everyone using AI solvers, but I feel like theres something deeper, like we realy think the student who understands is doing something better than the student who just ends up with true answers, even when both would make the same decision in real life. Like imagine two nursing students, one who memorized that drug X helps condition Y because the flashcard said so and it happens to be true, versus one who actually gets the mechanism and could adapt if things go weird — we treat those so differently.

So what view of knowledge versus just being right is baked into that kind of practice? Does grading like that presuppose that knowing is actually more valuable than merely having true beleifs, and if so in what way, like is it supposed to be more useful long term, or good in itself, or what? Or could you explain the whole thing away without that, just by saying its about catching cheaters or encouraging reliability? And how do you square it with the opposite practice in a lot of licensing exams or multiple choice finals where nobody cares how you got the true answer as long as you bubbled correctly — are those two systems presupposing inconsistent things, or can they both make sense together? If we decided as a department that true belief is good enough, what would we have to change beyond just the rubric?
