Next semester I am defending the view that Parfit's hitchhiker is rationally required to pay after being rescued to an audience of orthodox economists and causal decision theorists who understand rationality as maximizing expected utility at the time of action. In the scenario as I will present it, a driver will rescue a stranded hitchhiker only if she predicts he will later pay a large sum, though once rescued it is against his immediate interest to pay. My central line will be that rationality assesses prior policy choice, so that if the hitchhiker could beforehand take a costless habit-forming treatment that makes him reliably disposed to pay and that the driver can detect with high but imperfect accuracy, it would be rational to take it, and paying afterward is simply carrying through that rational policy. Help me anticipate the discussion period by setting out the six objections this particular audience is most likely to press, ranked by a combination of likelihood and seriousness and formulated in their own terms, each paired with a concise response I could give in about two minutes and an honest assessment of where that response remains vulnerable. Close with the further objection I should most hope does not arise and why it would be especially difficult.
