My friend and I just spent a long evening arguing about moral responsibility, and we have agreed to submit the exchange to a neutral judge. The topic was Galen Strawson's Basic Argument for the conclusion that no one can be morally responsible in the deep sense involving genuine desert of praise and blame.

I began by laying out the argument as I understand it. People act as they do because of the way they are, their character, motives, and psychological dispositions. To be truly responsible for an action, one would have to be truly responsible for the way one is. But to be responsible for the way one is, one would have had to choose it on the basis of some prior principles or reasons, and those principles would themselves belong to a prior self for which one would also have to be responsible, generating a regress. Since no one can create themselves from nothing, true responsibility is impossible.

My friend replied that the regress can be stopped by piecemeal self-revision. He offered the case of a former roommate of ours who was habitually hot-tempered, came to recognize this as a flaw, and undertook a deliberate program of reform involving therapy, meditation, and asking others to hold him accountable, eventually becoming reliably patient. My friend argued that the roommate chose the principles of his own change and is therefore responsible for his later character and for actions flowing from it, even if he did not create his initial self.

I answered that this merely pushes the problem back one step. The decision to reform itself depended on features the roommate already had and did not choose, such as valuing patience, having a capacity for self-reflection, or being lucky enough to encounter the right mentor and have the time and resources for therapy. If he had lacked the desire or capacity to change, he would not have changed. So his reform traces back to unchosen luck.

My friend responded that this imposes an impossible demand, namely that to be responsible one must be responsible for every causal influence on oneself, a kind of total self-creation outside of causation. He argued that responsibility only requires a capacity to reflect on, evaluate, and revise one's motives over time, and to identify with the motives on which one acts. On that standard, there is a morally important difference between our reformed roommate and someone who acts impulsively without reflection, and my requirement erases that difference by definitional fiat.

I replied that reflective endorsement does not solve the problem but restates it. An act of endorsement or identification is itself a psychological event with its own causal history. If whether one identifies with patience rather than anger is itself a matter of luck, then coherence among one's attitudes cannot ground desert. Compatibilist accounts of this sort change the subject from the demanding notion of responsibility Strawson is concerned with to something weaker, such as predictability or educability.

He closed by arguing that the demanding notion is itself incoherent because it requires a self standing outside natural causation, a self-caused cause. If that is what true responsibility requires, then its impossibility is trivial and uninteresting. The substantive question, he said, is whether reflective self-revision of the roommate's kind grounds distinctions in responsibility worth caring about, and it plainly does.

We found ourselves unable to settle this. Please adjudicate our disagreement move by move, explaining who is right about each specific step, who is more right overall, and what, if anything, we are both missing.
