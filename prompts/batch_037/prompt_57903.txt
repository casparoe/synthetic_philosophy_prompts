I've been going to this beginner buddhism night at the community center and the teacher leans really hard on this chariot example to explain no-self and I kinda get it but also kinda don't.

The way he puts it is a chariot isn't some extra thing floating above the wheels and axle and pole and all the peices put together the right way, it's just those parts working together, and a person is the same, just a body plus feelings plus perceptions plus habits plus awareness stuck together, no extra solid me inside. And he's using that to say clinging to a solid self is a confusion, and if we see through it we'll be less freaked out about aging and death and take insults and praise less personally.

I want to buy it but the comparison feels wobbly when I poke at it. Like a chariot has a builder and a driver who decide what counts as a chariot and what it's for, so who is doing that job for a person? And all the chariot bits are there at the same time, while I'm stretched over time, I remember being ten and I'm worried about being eighty, a chariot doesn't remember being a tree or dread getting rusty. Mostly a chariot doesn't care if you take it apart for the winter, but I care a lot, and isn't that caring part exactly what makes the self puzzling in the first place?

Plus my uncle restored this old mustang over like ten years until literally every part had been swapped and he still calls it the same car and pays classic insurance for it. Is he just confused according to this view?

Can you lay out what is supposed to map onto what here, where the mapping breaks, what features of chariots and cars get smuggled in that persons don't actually have and vice versa, and whether that whole let go of the solid self conclusion still holds up once the cracks show? And if chariot is a misleading go-to, is there a better everyday comparison that gets closer to what he's after?
