I understand Wittgenstein's private language argument to establish that a word introduced to name a purely private sensation cannot retain a stable meaning because re-identification on later occasions depends on the speaker's fallible memory, with no independent check to distinguish applying the word correctly from merely seeming to apply it correctly. I have been testing this understanding against a modernized diary case: a person living alone introduces the mark S for a distinctive tingling, logs each occurrence with a timestamp without telling anyone, and months later discovers the log aligns almost perfectly with archived data from a wearable nerve monitor that was recording throughout but never consulted, and she can then use S to anticipate episodes slightly before the device's alert becomes visible. If the original problem was the absence of an independent check on memory, this retrospective correlation and predictive success appear to supply it. Does this case refute the argument, or does it show the argument holds only until such external corroboration becomes available?
