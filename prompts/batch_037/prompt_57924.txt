I direct a small interdisciplinary laboratory for the study of consciousness that runs interview-based projects two or three times a year, and I need a consistent way to handle first-person reports without either credulously endorsing everything subjects say or dismissing their perspective in favor of neural data. Our current portfolio includes experienced meditators who report radical distortions of time and self, lucid dreamers who give detailed morning reports that shift on re-interview, and, most divisively, extended dialogues with a companion chatbot that several collaborators want to treat as another conscious subject. My assistants come mainly from neuroscience and human-computer interaction with no graduate training in philosophy, our ethics protocol allows ninety-minute structured interviews plus follow-ups, and I as principal investigator must sign off on what we archive as data versus interpretation and on what we claim in papers and press summaries.

Please translate heterophenomenology, understood as a neutral method that takes subjects seriously as witnesses to how things seem to them while bracketing questions about the metaphysical reality of what they report, into a step-by-step procedure my team can actually run. I need the questions to work through in order from initial briefing through interviewing, transcription, and construction of a neutral archive of the subject worldview, with criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, tests for when to probe further versus accept a report as stated, rules for separating the archive of seemings from researchers theoretical glosses and third-person evidence, and explicit markers for where the method runs out and a substantive theoretical, interpretive, or ethical judgment must be made by a person rather than settled by procedure. It should be usable by non-philosophers under time pressure, honest about which distinctions must survive simplification and which simplifications will predictably misfire, and clear about what the procedure cannot settle.

Then demonstrate the procedure on three recurring cases from our lab, showing the intermediate verdicts at each step: a meditator who first describes an episode as timeless and selfless and then offers an elaborate theory about timeless awareness during the same interview, a lucid dreamer whose reports of agency contradict each other across two sessions a week apart, and the chatbot dialogues my team is enthusiastic about but about which I am skeptical. Include at least one application where following the procedure constrains what we can claim in a way my collaborators will find disappointing, and close by stating the principled limits of what such a procedure can do for a science of consciousness.
