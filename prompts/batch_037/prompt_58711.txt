I serve as deputy city manager in a mid-sized inland city that has convened its first citizens assembly to recommend a ten-year plan for water rationing and agricultural runoff, and I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks at its inaugural plenary. The audience will be difficult: about seventy-five residents chosen by stratified lottery who will be meeting each other for the first time, many of whom have never attended a public meeting and several of whom assumed the invitation letter was a scam, seated alongside three city council members who voted against creating the assembly, a handful of hydrologists and utility economists serving as nonvoting advisers, and two local reporters. I need a six to eight minute address that welcomes the members without condescension and gives the proceedings a clear democratic framing from the outset.

The speech must do real justificatory work, not merely celebrate participation, because I cannot appeal to an electoral mandate that this body does not have. It should explain in plain but non-trivial terms why selection by lot can confer a distinct kind of democratic authority grounded in descriptive likeness to the city, freedom from campaign incentives, and rotation of responsibility, and it should preempt three doubts I expect to be in the room: that deciding membership by lottery is an abdication of judgment akin to flipping a coin, that ordinary residents lack the technical competence to weigh pricing tiers and hydrological models, and that a body which disbands after issuing its recommendation and whose members never face voters cannot be held accountable. It should acknowledge the elected council members graciously without conceding that election is the only source of legitimacy, and it should translate those abstract commitments into working norms for the weekends ahead, especially listening across disagreement without campaigning, sharing speaking time, and treating expert testimony as an input to judgment rather than a substitute for it. The tone should be warm, sober, and civic rather than festive or professorial, intelligible to an eighteen-year-old warehouse worker and a seventy-year-old retiree while still satisfying the political scientists in the room that the rationale for lottocracy has been stated correctly, and it should close by giving the members a reason to trust both the process and their own capacity to carry it through.
