For the past twelve years I have made my living as a mobile DJ for weddings, school dances, and karaoke nights. I did not attend university and I have no training in philosophy, but in my spare time I have kept a notebook about enjoyment, because my work leaves me with puzzles I cannot set aside.

What troubles me is how different my own pleasant moments feel from the inside. Consider three from last month. There was the dull relief in my feet and lower back when I finally sat down and pulled off steel-toe boots after carrying speakers up two flights of stairs. There was the tense, absorbed satisfaction of finally holding a difficult beat-match together for a full minute after two tracks kept drifting apart. And there was the warm, open amusement when my young daughter confidently sang the wrong words to a chorus and looked to me for approval. I want to say all three are pleasures, yet I cannot find one shared sensation in them. The relief is an unwinding, the mixing is a narrowing of attention, the amusement is a kind of swelling outward.

Talking this over with a friend, I was told philosophers have considered roughly three answers to what makes such different states all count as pleasure. The first is that despite appearances they do share a single distinctive feel, perhaps very abstract, that my three cases all contain. The second is that they share no common feel at all, and what unites them is an attitude I take toward them, something like welcoming or wanting each experience to continue while it lasts. The third is that they share a job in my mental economy, disposing me in similar ways to keep doing what I am doing or to seek it again, even if the feels and the likings differ.

I know live sound very well. Every day I work with faders and gain staging, three-band EQ and filters, timbre and texture, tempo and phrasing, headroom and clipping, monitoring a room and reading a crowd, blending one source into another without a jolt. Could you recast each of the three answers I described into that working vocabulary, as carefully as possible, using my three examples to test each recasting as you go. For each one, explain what comes across cleanly, what you have to stretch or bend to make it fit, and what cannot be expressed in mixing terms at all. Then close by giving your judgment on the leftovers: do they reveal a limitation of the mixing vocabulary, or do they suggest that the corresponding philosophical answer was less clear than it sounded.
