As a junior in high school serving as treasurer of our community service club, I have been trying to make sense of a debate we had during advisory about effective altruism and whether our semester project is thinking about doing good in the right way. Some classmates argued that focusing on individual donations overlooks the need to fix larger rules and institutions, others said that taking a higher-paying job mainly so you can give away more money is less admirable than doing helpful work directly, and a third group worried that we naturally prefer activities where we can count lives saved or points improved over slower work whose effects are harder to pin down.

To decide what to actually do, we narrowed our choices to three concrete plans: spending weekends providing one-on-one reading tutoring for younger students where progress can be tracked test by test, running a petition and letter-writing campaign asking our district to change how it funds after-school meals, and working part-time retail shifts to pool our wages for a donation to an anti-malaria charity. From the standpoint of someone who takes those three worries about institutions, earning to give, and measurability seriously, how would you rank these three plans from most to least promising, what score out of ten would you give each for its overall expected contribution to the good, and what reasoning about each worry supports that ordering?
