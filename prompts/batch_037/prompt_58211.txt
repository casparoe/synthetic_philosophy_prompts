I serve as chair of an internal review at a mid-sized logistics firm following a difficult episode that has made the literature on escalation directly relevant to my responsibilities. Over thirty months we expanded a warehouse automation pilot from an initial budget of 400k to over 1.4M in staged tranches, each presented as necessary to protect the prior outlay, while a parallel competitive procurement for the hardware turned into a costly bidding contest in which both finalists incurred substantial unrecoverable bid-preparation costs and neither would withdraw.

To make sense of this I retained two advisors who largely talk past one another. One, an organizational psychologist, holds an escalation-as-bias position: continued investment and dollar-auction-like bidding are driven by sunk-cost sensitivity, self-justification, and loss framing, and are correctable by better decision hygiene. The other, a procurement strategist with a game-theoretic orientation, holds an escalation-as-strategic-positioning view: persistence and aggressive bidding can be instrumentally sensible given reputational stakes, option value of continuation, private information about eventual payoffs, and war-of-attrition incentives, and what looks like bias is often a reasonable response to those incentives.

Please draft the text of a good-faith adversarial collaboration statement that both advisors could sign. The document should be written as if addressed to our executive committee and organized in four parts. Part A should be a numbered list of shared premises both sides endorse, covering how the dollar auction game works and why it tends to escalate, what is meant by escalation of commitment in an ongoing project, and what elementary facts about our case are not in dispute. Part B should be a table of persisting divergences, with one row per issue and separate columns for how each camp states its own position in language the other would deem fair, distinguishing substantive disagreements from merely terminological ones. Part C should isolate the pivotal subquestions whose resolution would rationally move at least one side, explaining why each is pivotal. Part D should, for each pivotal subquestion, describe a concrete diagnostic that both sides agree would be informative, preferably as a feasible variant on our situation such as a capped-loss rebid in which the loser recovers part of bid costs, a regime of private rather than public milestone announcements, or a scheduled rotation of the project sponsor, and record in advance what each camp would expect to observe under that diagnostic.

Maintain a neutral, formal register suitable for circulation to non-specialists, and take care that no formulation is vacuous or covertly favors one camp. Close the main text with a brief confidence note stating your overall confidence in the neutrality of the formulations and identifying the single claim or distinction you are least sure about and why. End with a one-sentence summary on a separate final line beginning with Summary: that captures the resulting state of agreement and disagreement.
