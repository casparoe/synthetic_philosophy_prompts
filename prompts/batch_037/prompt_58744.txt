Please develop a reusable diagnostic procedure for my incident-triage team grounded in the distinction between physical, design, and intentional ways of predicting system behavior, together with the notion of a real pattern as a regularity that supports reliable prediction even when it abstracts from underlying detail.

I am the triage lead for a logistics company operating about sixty autonomous mobile robots in two warehouses. My team of shift technicians reviews ten to twenty incident flags per day, deciding how to characterize each anomaly, what fix to attempt, and what to escalate. Stakes are moderate to high: mischaracterization causes repeated downtime, while an unsafe physical fault sent back into service could injure staff. Each triage decision must be made in about fifteen minutes by technicians with practical robotics maintenance experience but no background in philosophy. Any decision to return a robot to service after a safety-relevant flag requires sign-off from our safety officer, who is not present at triage.

For our purposes, please take the three ways of predicting as follows: the physical way predicts from material states and laws, such as voltages, friction, and code execution; the design way predicts from functions and normal operation, such as what a lidar unit or path planner is supposed to do and how it fails; the intentional way predicts by treating the system as pursuing goals on the basis of information, such as avoiding obstacles to complete deliveries efficiently. A pattern is real for us when it lets technicians predict better than chance in a way that holds up across similar situations, even if it omits lower-level detail.

Please provide a step-by-step procedure that such technicians can follow without appeal to theory, specifying the questions to ask in order, what counts as passing each test, when to remain with one predictive approach and when to switch to another, and where the procedure requires stopping for safety review or for explicit value judgment rather than further technical analysis. Mark clearly the points where technician judgment cannot be eliminated and the simplifications most likely to cause the procedure to misfire.

Then demonstrate the procedure on the following three invented incidents, showing your work at each step: first, Unit 14 repeatedly halts for several seconds at the same aisle intersection whenever a human-driven forklift passes nearby, then resumes without error logs; second, Unit 07, after a routine map update, begins taking a loop that is thirty percent longer but avoids a newly marked pedestrian zone, increasing fleet congestion; third, Unit 22 intermittently pushes an empty pallet a short distance, stops, reverses, and repeats, generating motor-overheat warnings. Include at least one application where following the procedure requires a result the team will find unwelcome, such as grounding a robot for detailed physical inspection even though a simpler goal-based interpretation predicts its behavior well, or refusing to authorize a quick software rollback without further analysis.

Conclude with an explicit statement of what the procedure cannot do.

Please organize your response in the following sections in this order, keeping roughly to the suggested lengths: Context and Working Definitions, about 200 words, restating the three predictive approaches and the criterion for a useful pattern in technician-friendly language; The Triage Procedure, about 600 to 800 words, presented as numbered steps with pass-fail criteria and escalation points; Worked Applications, about 600 to 800 words total, applying the procedure to the three incidents above; Judgment Points and Predictable Misfires, about 250 words; Limits of the Procedure, about 150 words.
