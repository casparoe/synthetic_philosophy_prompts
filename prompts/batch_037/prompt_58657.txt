A tutor in an undergraduate seminar on time and truth has taken a series of positions on future-directed claims, all evaluated from a present standpoint prior to the events described. The pattern is as follows.

First, concerning the present utterance that there will be a sea battle tomorrow, where it is stipulated that battle occurs if and only if the admiral freely decides tonight to attack and that decision remains genuinely open, the tutor will endorse neither the utterance as true now nor its negation as true now, and holds that neither may properly be asserted.

Second, concerning the present utterance that either there will be a sea battle tomorrow or there will not be one, the tutor holds it true now.

Third, concerning the present utterance that if the admiral decides tonight to attack, there will be a sea battle tomorrow, the tutor holds it true now, while continuing to withhold endorsement from the categorical prediction that there will be a battle.

Fourth, concerning the present utterance that the tide will be high at noon tomorrow, where the outcome is taken to be fully determined by current conditions together with regularities of nature, the tutor holds it true now, and explicitly contrasts this case with the sea-battle prediction.

Fifth, concerning the present utterances that it is now settled or inevitable that there will be a sea battle tomorrow and that it is now settled or inevitable that there will not be one, the tutor rejects both as false now.

Sixth, concerning an astronomer's utterance last week that there would be a solar eclipse today, the eclipse having duly occurred today, the tutor maintains that the astronomer's utterance was already true when it was made.

A new dispute has now arisen that tests this pattern. Yesterday, while the admiral's decision was still open, a sailor asserted that there would be a sea battle today. Today a sea battle is in fact taking place. The captain had promised a reward for a prediction that was true when made, as distinct from one that merely turned out to be vindicated by how events subsequently unfolded, and it is disputed whether the sailor's utterance of yesterday qualifies.

Articulate at least two general principles or rules that each fit the tutor's six prior positions equally well but diverge on this new dispute. For each candidate rule, state what it predicts about whether the sailor spoke truly yesterday and whether the reward is due on the captain's terms. Offer a reasoned conjecture about which rule the tutor is more likely to be implicitly employing, explaining what features of the prior positions favor that interpretation. Finally, describe a further hypothetical case, distinct from the sailor case, that would most decisively discriminate between the candidate rules.
