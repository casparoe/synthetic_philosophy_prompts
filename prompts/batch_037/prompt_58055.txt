I am currently taking a seminar that pairs early modern theories of induction with recent results in machine learning, and I am puzzled about how Hume would have reacted to the no-free-lunch theorems. These results are usually glossed as showing that no learner outperforms any other when performance is averaged over all possible regularities in the data, so successful generalization must rely on substantive biases that fit the actual task. Would Hume have taken this as essentially a formal restatement of his own point that past observations cannot by themselves justify expectations about the unobserved, or would he have regarded it as a different and narrower claim about algorithm comparison? In particular, if a contemporary engineer argued that scaling up data and compute could eventually dispense with such biases, how do you think Hume would have assessed that optimism?
