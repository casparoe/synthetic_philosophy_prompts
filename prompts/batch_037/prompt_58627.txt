I am trying to bring my reactions to reliance on other people's moral judgments into reflective equilibrium and would welcome help in identifying the principle that best captures them. Please respond in American English.

The cases that puzzle me all involve accepting a moral verdict without grasping the reasons behind it, yet my reactions vary. Consider the following:

A visitor in an unfamiliar city asks a host whether it is disrespectful to enter a particular shrine wearing shoes, accepts the host's affirmative answer without asking for further explanation, and removes the shoes. This seems entirely unobjectionable.

A busy shopper asks a trusted friend who follows food ethics closely whether buying inexpensive farmed shrimp is morally wrong, accepts the friend's affirmative answer without asking for reasons, and changes purchasing habits on that basis alone. This leaves me uneasy, as if something important to acting well has been missed, even on the assumption that the friend is highly reliable.

If the same shopper instead asks for the friend's reasons concerning labor conditions, environmental damage, and animal suffering, listens to them, and is then convinced, the unease disappears.

A voter who lacks time to study a complicated municipal referendum on housing levies accepts the judgment of a long-trusted neighborhood association that the measure is unfair and votes accordingly without working through the policy details independently. This strikes me as permissible and perhaps even responsible.

A subscriber signs up for a highly accurate automated messaging service that sends brief moral verdicts without explanations, such as where to donate, which job offer to accept when both seem permissible, or what to do in a difficult family dispute, and routinely follows those verdicts. Even supposing the service is more reliable than the subscriber's own judgment, this pattern of deference strikes me as deeply troubling in a way the voter case does not.

A junior clinician serving on a time-pressed ward asks an experienced palliative care nurse whether it would be wrong to withhold a grim prognosis to spare a patient distress, accepts the nurse's answer without discussion, and acts on it. Here I am torn: the deference feels professionally appropriate, yet it also feels like an abdication of the very responsibility the clinician is supposed to exercise.

Is there a unified principle that can explain this pattern, or something close to it? I am especially puzzled by the contrast between the shrine case and the shrimp case, and between the voter case and the messaging-service case, since each pair involves deference without reasons to a presumably reliable source but elicits different verdicts from me.

Please state one principle, or at most two competing candidates, with sufficient precision that each clause is motivated by one of the cases above. For each candidate, explain which of my verdicts it preserves and which, if any, it requires me to revise, what it predicts for two further cases I have not listed, and what it implies for a case on which I remain undecided, namely an adult child who must decide whether to move an aging parent into residential care and is inclined simply to defer to a sibling's judgment that placing the parent would be wrong. Conclude with an assessment of whether reflective equilibrium favors a more complex principle that saves all of my firmer verdicts or a simpler principle that asks me to abandon at least one of them.
