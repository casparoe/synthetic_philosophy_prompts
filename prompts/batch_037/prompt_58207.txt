I am a second-year PhD student in philosophy working on mind and moral psychology, and I am TAing our department's introductory philosophy of mind module this term. After a lively discussion with friends in my cohort about why undergraduates tend to pick theory labels they like rather than views they actually hold, I decided to try a different approach for my section next week on emotions. I want students to discover their commitments through cases before they learn the standard names, since the lecture already covered the textbook contrast between emotions as evaluative judgments, emotions as perceptions of value, and emotions as feelings of bodily change, but left them unsure how those differences show up in practice.

Could you design for me a which-view-do-you-actually-hold sorting quiz for this purpose, written in American English, with eight to ten vignette-based questions each offering two or three response options and a scoring key at the end? The quiz should be pitched to curious nonmajors with no jargon in the questions themselves, and it must sort response patterns onto the judgment view, the perceptual view, and the feeling view, with guidance on how to read mixed or hybrid-looking profiles. Please build the diagnostics around concrete contrasts I can use in discussion, including recalcitrant fear during air turbulence by someone who sincerely judges flying safe, lingering anger after a sincere apology and retraction of the insult, grief that returns full-force years later on hearing a particular song, and a case where dampened bodily arousal seems to flatten the emotion itself, plus a few more of your own invention along similar lines. Phrase every vignette so a student can answer without knowing which theory it favors, make the tree of answers cover the logical space rather than just listing names, and add a brief note on any two positions your quiz cannot cleanly separate and on any answer combination that yields no stable named view and why you think that combination is empty.
