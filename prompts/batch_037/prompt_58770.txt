After a career of more than three decades as an experimental physical chemist, now retired for several years, I have taken up the systematic reading of philosophy that I never had time for while running a laboratory, and I find myself puzzled in retrospect by our everyday habits of reliance on the word of others. In the group I led, no single person could follow a result from synthesis through to publication: we accepted without re-examination the calibration records kept by the technicians who maintained our spectrometers, we entered into our papers the compositions reported by the university microanalysis facility without inspecting the raw spectra, we cited lattice constants and thermodynamic data from the literature as established facts for the purpose of designing the next experiment, and co-authors routinely vouched for sections describing procedures they had not personally witnessed. Yet this routine trust coexisted with a very different posture toward certain other reports: when a visiting group announced an unusually high catalytic yield, or when a new graduate student reported an outlier that would, if correct, overturn our working model, we demanded raw notebooks, independent repetition by a second hand, and, in the case of the visitors, declined to build on their finding until another laboratory had reproduced it.

I have recently learned that philosophers distinguish between reductionist accounts, on which reliance on another's word stands in need of positive reasons, and anti-reductionist accounts, on which such reliance is warranted in the absence of defeaters, and I wonder what view of testimony our mixed practice implicitly embodied. Please reconstruct the conception of testimonial warrant that would make sense of the pattern I describe, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, and consider whether our different treatments of technicians, central facilities, collaborators, published literature, and surprising or high-stakes claims presuppose a single coherent position or several inconsistent ones. Finally, explain what would concretely have to change in how such a laboratory records, checks, and cites the work of others if it were to replace its implicit picture with its principal rival.
