During a supervision on scientific realism, my supervisor appealed to the slogan 'truth is just disquotation' to set aside two questions I had raised: whether claiming that our best-confirmed physical theories are true says anything more than endorsing those theories, and whether their truth might explain their predictive success. The suggestion was that both questions inflate a mere logical convenience into a substantive property.

I suspect a single catchphrase cannot bear that argumentative burden. Could you separate out the different precise theses it might be standing in for? I have in mind, amongst others, theses about logical equivalence, about whether 'true' ascribes a robust property with an underlying nature, about the expressive role of the truth predicate for finite speakers who need to endorse sentences they cannot enumerate, and about explanatory impotence. For each thesis, clarify its content, assess whether it is uncontroversial, defensible but substantive, or mistaken, and state whether it would actually vindicate my supervisor's move with respect to my two questions. To keep the analysis concrete, please apply each thesis to two test cases: a technician who endorses a vast calibration log she has never read by saying everything in it is true, and a realist who says kinetic theory is true. Please organise the answer in numbered sections, finishing with a judgement about which interpretation, if any, licences the dismissal.
