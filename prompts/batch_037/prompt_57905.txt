I serve on the advisory committee of a large philanthropic foundation that divides its giving into two ring-fenced streams. Stream One supports near-term global health interventions assessed strictly by cost per life saved, while Stream Two supports work on low-probability catastrophes such as engineered pandemics and loss of control of advanced AI systems, where estimates are far more speculative. Under our charter, trustees are forbidden to drain Stream One to top up Stream Two even when a back-of-the-envelope expected-value calculation makes the latter look hundreds of times more productive per dollar, a situation that arises almost every year. Yet within Stream Two itself the trustees are not thoroughgoing expected-value maximizers either: last cycle they set aside a proposal to back an unproven deflection architecture on the grounds that its one-in-eighty-million chance of averting a civilizational collapse, however enormous in expectation, should not outweigh a far more probable but modest improvement in pandemic surveillance, and more generally they treat probabilities below a certain level of credibility as insufficient to carry a funding decision no matter how large the putative payoff. I was discussing this double pattern with fellow trustees and found myself puzzled, since the ring-fencing seems to restrain fanatical reasoning across streams while the internal caution seems to restrain it again inside one stream, yet the foundation still justifies Stream Two in broadly expectational language.

Could you articulate the outlook on value under uncertainty that would render this pair of policies intelligible, and probe how tightly the policies depend on it? I would like an account that separates what the ring-fence and the internal probability floor strictly require from what they merely sit comfortably with, that considers rival pictures — for instance, unrestricted maximization of mathematical expectation, views that discount or disregard sufficiently tiny probabilities, and views that give extra weight to avoiding certain kinds of worst cases or to maintaining a division of moral labor — and that assesses whether the two policies hang together or tacitly invoke different ideas about when tiny chances of vast goods may dominate. Finally, please sketch how grantmaking and evaluation would have to be reorganized were the foundation to abandon whatever anti-fanatical commitments you identify and embrace a single expectational calculus across both streams.
