As a novelist preparing my fourth book, I find that much of my plot turns not on what my characters describe but on what they do with words: betting, promising, ordering, bequeathing, apologising, naming, and marrying. I need a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the philosophical understanding of speech acts and performative utterances, that my assistant and I can apply during revision to determine whether a line of dialogue, in its fictional context, should count for readers as having successfully performed such an act.

Please translate the central distinctions — between saying something and doing something in saying it, between an act succeeding, misfiring, or being insincere but still effective, between what requires institutional authority or uptake by the addressee and what does not, and between the act itself and its later consequences — into a plain-language checklist or flowchart in words. It should give the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each for a fiction draft, where the procedure stops with a clear failure, and where it must hand the matter back to authorial judgment. It must be usable in ten to fifteen minutes per scene by people without philosophical training, with final sign-off resting with me, and it must be honest about which simplifications risk misleading us and where the procedure will misfire. The stakes are not legal advice but narrative coherence: several scenes per week must feel binding, authoritative, or hollow in exactly the way the subsequent chapters assume.

Please then run the procedure on the following three invented scenes from my drafts, showing your working at each step:

1. Two estranged brothers in a Leeds pub. The elder says, Fifty quid says Dad walks out before midnight, and the younger laughs and nods while ordering another pint. No stakes are named beyond that sentence and no explicit acceptance is uttered. Two chapters later they dispute whether a bet was ever made.

2. Aboard a coastal freighter, an exhausted captain says to a new deckhand, It would be nice if that hatch were secured before dark. The deckhand hears this as a tired observation and does nothing, and a night accident follows. The inquiry chapter turns on whether an order was given and disobeyed or never given at all.

3. A cliffside elopement conducted by a friend whose online ordination lapsed the previous month. He pronounces the couple married, and they reply only with, We will love you always. For the inheritance dispute that drives the final act, I wish readers to accept them as married in every sense that matters, though the ceremony is institutionally void.

Include in the worked applications one verdict I will not like, explain why the procedure requires it despite my dramatic preference, and conclude with a brief statement of what the procedure cannot settle. Please write your entire response in British English, including spelling, punctuation, and idiom.
