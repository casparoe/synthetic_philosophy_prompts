I am a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy of science preparing for a department colloquium where I will defend the claim that Humeanism about laws of nature is adequate for scientific explanation and that positing governing laws adds no explanatory value in central cases from physics.

My audience will be about fifteen people: five faculty in analytic metaphysics who work on dispositions, powers, and necessity and who generally favor a governing conception of laws, two philosophers of physics who focus on symmetry principles and on how laws function in model building, and several epistemologically oriented faculty and graduate students who are skeptical of metaphysics but care about explanation and prediction. They will press me in their own vocabularies, not in the vocabulary of Humean insiders.

My argument has three steps that I want the objections to target. First, I argue that what scientists need from laws in explanation and counterfactual reasoning is stable generalization under a relevant range of interventions, not metaphysical push. Second, I introduce a stipulated toy case to make this concrete: Twin Grids A and B, two finite cellular grids that I define to have exactly the same local frequencies of particle positions over their full histories, but where I as the modeler stipulate different outcomes after the same localized nudge to a single cell at the same time step, with Grid A returning to its prior pattern and Grid B diverging. I claim the Humean can account for our different counterfactual judgments about A and B without governing laws by appealing to differences in the wider global pattern together with standards of strength and simplicity for systematizing that pattern. Third, I argue that once that Humean resource is in place, appeals to primitive governing necessity are explanatorily idle because they do no additional predictive or unificatory work.

Give me the seven or eight objections I am actually likely to get from these people, not generic textbook objections, ordered by combined likelihood and potential damage. Phrase each objection the way the relevant audience member would state it. For each, provide a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, guidance on what to concede up front and what to hold firm on, and a candid note on where my reply is softest.

Present the prep sheet as a markdown table with exactly the following columns, with one row per objection in rank order: Rank by likelihood times damage; Objection stated in audience voice; Likely source in the room, such as powers metaphysician or symmetry-focused philosopher of physics; Two-minute reply in my Humean voice; Concede versus hold guidance; Soft spot where the reply remains vulnerable.
