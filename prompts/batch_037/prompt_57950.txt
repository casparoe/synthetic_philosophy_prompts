I have been attending a continuing education seminar on teamwork and social ontology intended for managers and engineers, and I have become stuck on the topic of collective intentionality and joint action. The textbook examples all involve explicit agreement or highly scripted cooperation, which makes the philosophical disagreement feel abstract and easy to resolve in favor of either side.

To make the issue concrete, I would like the discussion to be anchored in a particular invented case I keep returning to from my own experience supervising a night cleaning crew in an office building. Two custodians, Mara and Luis, work overlapping shifts but have never discussed how to divide the large atrium. Over several weeks they fall into a smooth routine where Mara moves the lightweight furniture to one side just before Luis arrives with the floor scrubber, each adjusting pace when the other is delayed and covering the central area twice when they notice the other has missed a spot, all without explicit communication and without either of them ever having formed a plan together in words. Consider also a variant of the same routine in which Mara privately believes they are engaged in a friendly competition to finish first while Luis believes they are cooperating, though their outward behavior remains exactly the same.

Please write an extended dialogue between two philosophers who disagree about what makes such coordination a genuine joint action. Let one defend the idea that genuine joint action requires a distinctive shared attitude that is not reducible to individual goals plus mutual expectations, and let the other defend the idea that it is enough to have ordinary individual intentions directed toward the shared pattern, suitably interlocked with true beliefs about what the other intends and will do. Both interlocutors should be equally knowledgeable and charitable, develop their positions through direct responses to the points just raised rather than prepared speeches, test both versions of the custodians case as well as a case where coordination breaks down because one person stops adjusting, and conclude without either conceding that the disagreement matters for when a supervisor may praise or blame the pair as a team rather than as individuals.
