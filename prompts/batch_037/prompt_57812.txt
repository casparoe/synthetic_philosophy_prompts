For our faculty-student forum on democratic innovation, I will interview an invited speaker who advocates lottocracy in a specific institutional form: replacing the elected upper house of our state legislature with a 201-member Citizens Senate selected by stratified random lottery from adult residents, serving single three-year staggered terms with full salary and security against removal except for proven misconduct, working through facilitated deliberation supported by balanced briefing materials and hearings with competing experts, and empowered to enact statutes subject only to override by popular referendum.

Please develop a set of nine substantive questions for this conversation that examine the internal logic of that proposal rather than general ideals of participation. The set should be organized around three central pressure points: the basis on which an unelected body claims authority to make binding law, the capacity of randomly selected members to adjudicate sharply conflicting expert testimony without falling into either uncritical deference or arbitrary choice, and the meaning of accountability for members who neither face voters nor seek another term. Devote two principal questions to each pressure point, and append to each principal question a brief follow-up that anticipates the advocate's probable response, such as invocation of descriptive representativeness, of deliberative transformation, or of rotation in office as itself a form of control, and identifies the vulnerable step in that response. Use the three remaining questions to probe who sets the legislative agenda for such a body, whether informal hierarchies and facilitator influence are likely to reproduce the inequalities sortition is meant to avoid, and how legislative output should be assessed after, say, two full rotation cycles. The tone throughout should be rigorous but courteous, with no questions concerning personal biography or career motivation.
