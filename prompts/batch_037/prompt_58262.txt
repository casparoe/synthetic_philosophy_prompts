After a career as a field ecologist specializing in coastal invertebrates, I retired three years ago and have begun to educate myself in philosophy, joining a small discussion group at our public library. We recently considered the proposal that humanity should take on the deliberate biotechnological abolition of suffering itself, not merely the treatment of particular diseases, but the re-engineering of sentient life so that pain and misery are replaced by stable well-being in both humans and wild animals. As a natural scientist I am accustomed to thinking of pain and stress as evolved signals with ecological functions, and I find I grasp the stakes of such a proposal less through abstract principles than through imagined particulars.

I would therefore be grateful for a short story, perhaps a thousand words, centered on a protagonist not unlike myself, a retired biologist volunteering in a tidal marsh restoration project. In the story, a local conservation trust is offered two pilot interventions by the same foundation: a hatchery strain of oysters edited to develop without the stress responses that normally accompany crowding and harvesting, and a transmissible intervention for wild salmon intended to blunt the chronic fear response during migration without impairing navigation or predator avoidance. At the same time the protagonist, who lives with persistent nerve pain from an old field injury, is herself offered a neural implant that would not merely block pain episodes but sustain a background sense of contentment. Please follow her over a single season as she moves between the hatchery tanks, the town hall debate over releasing the salmon intervention, and her own medical decision.

What I hope the narrative will illuminate, without turning into an essay or a polemic, is the conceptual difficulty that most interests me: whether removing suffering in these three cases counts as the same kind of act, and what, if anything, is lost when a useful aversive signal is replaced by engineered ease. Let the ending remain open rather than resolving in favor of acceptance or refusal, and let the characters articulate both the attraction of a world with less misery and the unease that such control inspires in those who have spent their lives observing adaptation.
