I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, currently preparing a long-form essay on whether communities can deliberately reshape which moral emotion does most of their moral work.

For the essay I am working with a rough contrast that my readers can grasp without specialist background: guilt as a negative response centered on a specific act and what might repair it, and shame as a negative response centered on the self as seen and diminished in the eyes of others. I want to test a strong thesis about this contrast, and I would like you to make the case for it as forcefully as the reasons allow.

Argue for the following combined thesis: in large, mobile, and relatively anonymous societies, guilt is the morally preferable central emotion to shame, yet a culture cannot directly will such a shift into being and can only alter it indirectly by redesigning the practices that scaffold each emotion.

Develop the argument through three institutional settings I have been reporting on, rather than in the abstract: a middle school deciding whether misbehavior should trigger private repair tasks or public exposure to peers; a local newsroom deciding whether coverage of low-level offenses should emphasize the nature and consequences of the act or the character and standing of the offender; and an online community deciding whether moderation should rely on visible pile-ons or on private prompts that invite correction. Show how considerations about reparability, about dependence on a stable audience, and about what happens to bystanders support the comparative judgment, and why direct injunctions to feel more guilt and less shame tend to misfire while changes to routines, roles, and publicity conditions do not.

Conclude with a short reflexive section that identifies where your own case is weakest and specifies which argument or consideration would most plausibly lead you to a different verdict.

Your full response must not exceed 700 words.
