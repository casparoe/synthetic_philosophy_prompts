I am a third-year undergraduate majoring in philosophy with a minor in cognitive science, currently enrolled in a seminar on the science of consciousness. Alongside my coursework, I work six hours per week as a paid research assistant in a perception and timing laboratory, where I conduct short structured interviews after simple visual and reaction-time tasks and prepare cleaned interview notes for the postdoctoral researchers.

I am trying to apply heterophenomenology as developed by Daniel Dennett in this practical setting. As I understand it from the course, the core idea is to treat subjects as authoritative about the content of their reports but not incorrigible about the underlying conscious events themselves, to construct from those reports a neutral model of how things seem to the subject, while bracketing questions about whether those seemings are accurate, and only afterwards to ask how that model relates to neural and functional explanations.

Please translate that idea into a step-by-step procedure that an assistant with my level of training could actually run repeatedly. The interviews last about twenty minutes, participants are nonphilosophers with no background in the theory, I have basic training in active listening but no clinical expertise, the stakes are data quality and replicability rather than medical care, and our principal investigator must sign off on any decision to exclude or heavily reinterpret data. I need an ordered checklist: what to ask first, what counts as a sufficient answer to move on, what to record as observed speech behavior versus what to record as interpreted content, how to bracket ontological commitments without leading the participant, how to probe metaphors, uncertainty, contradictions, and post hoc corrections, and where the procedure should stop and hand the decision to a person because the issue is one of judgment or values rather than further questioning. Please be explicit about which distinctions from the theory must survive the simplification for the procedure to remain faithful, which can be dropped for usability, and where the procedure is most likely to misfire.

Then demonstrate the procedure on the following three invented cases from our lab, showing for each what the interview transcript entry should contain, what should be included in the neutral model of the subjects seemings, what should be flagged for the principal investigator, and why:

A participant stares at a bright ring for thirty seconds and then, in a dark room, reports a grey donut-shaped patch that seems to be floating a few feet ahead and to move when the eyes move. The participant initially insists it is out there in the room, but on re-questioning says it might be in the eyes or in the seeing itself, and seems frustrated by the question.

A participant in a button-press task with an auditory cue reports a clear feeling of having decided to press a fraction of a second before hearing the tone, and repeats this confidently on probing, even though the timing logs make that order implausible for that trial.

A participant in a monotonous sustained-attention task reports that time froze and that a few seconds felt like a full minute, but on follow-up says that is just a way of talking about being bored, not a description of how the interval itself seemed while it was passing.

Include in the demonstration at least one outcome I will not like, for example a vivid and theoretically interesting report that the procedure requires me to bracket, downgrade, or exclude, or a report that seems obviously mistaken that the procedure requires me to retain. Conclude by stating plainly what the procedure cannot do, especially where it cannot distinguish confabulation, misremembering, metaphor, and deliberate uncooperativeness, and where philosophical judgment cannot be eliminated.
