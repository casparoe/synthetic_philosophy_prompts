So I got roped into leading our neighborhood library philosophy club next Thursday, its like 12 regulars mostly retirees plus a couple of high schoolers, none of them have done philosophy, and I picked that old puzzle about a person blind from birth who learns to tell a cube from a sphere just by touch and then suddenly gets sight and has to tell which is which by looking alone. My cousin who works in an eye clinic was telling me about people after cataract surgery and it got me thinking it would be a fun session, but I am realy nervous about explaning it without losing them. Dont explain the puzzle to me, I get the basics, I need a plan for how to teach it in like 45 minutes.

What I need is the order I should introduce things in and why that order matters, like what has to be in place before the yes-or-no question even makes sense, and the one concrete everyday example or demo I should keep coming back to and what it has to get right and what traps it has to avoid, plus the misconceptions this crowd will definitely bring in, like thinking its just about everything being blurry at first or thinking touch and sight obviously share the same idea of shape, and exactly when in my run-through to head each one off. Also give me a quick check I can use at the end to tell if they actually got the deeper bit about matching across senses versus just seeing clearly, and tell me what to deliberately leave out at this level and be upfront about skipping. I also had this half-baked twist I wanted to try where instead of touch someone learns the shapes through sound, like a device that beeps differently for corners versus curves, and then they get sight – I want to know where, if anywhere, to drop that in without derailing the main point.
