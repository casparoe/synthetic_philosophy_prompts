I live in a housing cooperative of thirty flats in a converted mill, and last autumn I was elected to the five-person committee that manages repairs and shared services. We have to settle a dispute about German cockroaches that keeps coming back in the ground-floor flats and the bin room, and I have agreed to circulate a short paper before our vote next month.

A local contractor has given us two workable plans for the same annual cost. The first is a whole-building residual spray every three months, including skirting boards, pipe runs and the bin room, which kills on contact and then stays active for weeks. The second is a slower programme of gel baits in sealed cracks, improved proofing around pipes and doors, weekly trap monitoring, and help for residents with food storage and clearing clutter, with sprays used only in individual flats where numbers stay high after two months. The first plan would almost certainly kill more insects overall, including spiders and other non-target insects, and some residue would wash into our courtyard drains. The second leaves sticky monitoring traps out continuously, where insects sometimes remain alive for a day or more, and it asks more of elderly residents and families with very small children.

I have no university background in philosophy and I work as a joiner, but I have been turning this over on my own and I find the usual positions on the committee unsatisfying. Some neighbours say insects simply do not count and we should just pick whatever protects health and comfort most thoroughly. Others say we should avoid killing at all if we can learn to live alongside them with better hygiene. I am somewhere between those views. I keep wondering whether it matters that we would be killing thousands to prevent stomach upsets and distress rather than serious disease, whether a quick death is less bad than a slow one from bait or a sticky trap, and whether there is a moral difference between killing the insects already here and laying down a poison that keeps killing newcomers for months. I also wonder whether keeping the poison inside our walls is different from letting it spread to insects outside that never bothered us.

Could you write the paper I can send round to neighbours, in plain language they will actually read, no longer than about two pages. It should come down clearly on one plan, set out what we would do in the first three months and when we would review it, and explain in ordinary terms why that balance of human interests, numbers killed, suffering caused, and effects beyond our building is the right one. It should also state fairly the strongest reason for choosing the other plan, and why that reason does not win out in the end.
