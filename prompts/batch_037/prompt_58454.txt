I am preparing a ninety-minute discussion section for second-year undergraduates in an introductory philosophy of language course, and I have noticed that they tend to run together two questions that our lectures have tried to keep apart, namely who or what a speaker has managed to talk about on an occasion and whether what the speaker said about that thing is true. Conversations with friends in the department have convinced me that a fresh, concrete case would help more than further abstract exposition, since the familiar textbook illustrations now prompt rehearsed responses rather than careful analysis.

I would be grateful if you could devise for this pedagogical purpose an original, realistic scenario, told in a single paragraph and set in an ordinary workplace, residential building, or small business, in which a speaker uses a definite description to pick out exactly one contextually salient person, where one element of the descriptive material is plainly mistaken yet competent hearers have no difficulty settling on the intended individual, and where no science-fictional devices, empty or fictional individuals, or hidden stipulations about private intentions beyond what is publicly available in the scene are invoked. The case should be engineered so that a Russellian quantificational account of descriptions and a Strawson-inspired presuppositional or referential account agree about the identity of the person spoken about but diverge on the status of the utterance, with the former counting it as strictly false and the latter treating it as successfully referring and saying something true of that person, and it should avoid the well-worn illustrations involving royalty and similar empty cases while remaining fully accessible to students without training in formal logic.

Please follow the scenario with an analysis that demonstrates that it satisfies each part of the brief, spelling out in plain English without formal symbolism how each of the two approaches arrives at its verdict and why, identifying which structural feature of the case is responsible for pulling successful identification of the referent apart from fit of the descriptive content, explaining what happens to both verdicts when that feature is removed or corrected, and sketching how a committed defender of each side would attempt to explain away the opposing intuition in a way that derives the response from the theory rather than merely stipulating it, since the point of the exercise is to make the theoretical difference vivid rather than to declare a winner.
