My friend and I spent most of yesterday evening disputing whether it can be irrational merely to change what one wants over time, even when one learns no new facts. She pressed the following argument on me, and although I am convinced the conclusion cannot be correct, I found each step difficult to resist in the moment.

1. If an agent willingly accepts each trade in a sequence, yet finishes with strictly less money and with nothing else that she herself values more to compensate for the loss, then she has been irrational at some point in the sequence.

2. Consider a foreseeable reversal: the agent now prefers A to B, foresees that tomorrow she will prefer B to A, and expects no new factual information about A or B in the meantime, only a shift in taste, outlook, or values.

3. A trader who anticipates such a reversal can exploit it. The trader can offer today to exchange the agent's B for A plus a small fee, which the present self will accept given her present preference, and then offer tomorrow to exchange A back for B plus a small fee, which the future self will accept given her reversed preference. The agent ends poorer while holding exactly what she started with.

4. Foresight does not supply a rational escape. If the agent refuses the first offer precisely in order to avoid the eventual loss, she knowingly acts against her own present preference for A over B, which is itself a failure to respond to what she wants.

5. It follows that every foreseeable reversal is irrational, so a rational agent must either never undergo one or pay in advance to bind her future self whenever one threatens.

6. Yet foreseeable reversals of exactly this form are often paradigmatically rational. A doctoral student may now prefer urban intensity while fully expecting to prefer suburban quiet once she starts a family; a patient may now prefer maximizing longevity while foreseeing that lived experience with a chronic condition will lead him to prioritize comfort and independence; more generally, expecting one's tastes to mature with age or experience does not look like a defect of rationality.

If the reasoning is sound, rationality would require us either never to mature in foreseeable ways or to constrain our future selves whenever maturation threatens, which seems absurd.

I am reluctant to abandon either the idea that systematic exploitability of this kind marks irrationality or the idea that ordinary maturation and revaluation can be fully rational. Diagnose the argument: identify the premise or inference that should be rejected, explain why it initially seemed compelling, and lay out the theoretical costs of the main available repairs.
