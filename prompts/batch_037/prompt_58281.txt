I work as a building maintenance supervisor and have no university education in philosophy, law, or politics, but I have been thinking on my own for a couple of years about whether a small country that wants to stay out of other people's wars should keep compulsory military service or move to a fully professional force.

At a recent community discussion about this, a neighbor made an argument that has stuck with me. He said compulsory service is essentially the same institution as jury duty, and that this settles the fairness question. His idea was that just as we do not leave criminal verdicts to hired professional jurors but require ordinary citizens to take turns serving, we should not leave national defense to hired soldiers but require ordinary citizens to take turns serving. On his view, both practices spread an unavoidable burden evenly, keep an important state power under democratic control, and express the principle that certain collective responsibilities should not be outsourced for pay. He applied this to neutrality as well, saying a citizen army made up of short-term conscripts who train for home defense and then return to civilian life is structurally incapable of being sent on foreign adventures, in the same way a jury of ordinary people is structurally resistant to becoming a tool of the government.

The concrete choice we were discussing was between keeping an eight month universal service for home defense only, with no deployment abroad and with a longer civilian alternative in hospitals and elder care, versus replacing it with a smaller full time volunteer force that would be paid competitive wages and could be deployed more flexibly.

I find the comparison powerful but I suspect it hides important differences. Could you examine it carefully for me? I would like an explicit account of which element on the jury side is supposed to correspond to which element on the army side, including the kind of person called up, the kind of task they are asked to do, the skill and preparation required, the personal risk and moral burden involved, and the purpose the compulsion is meant to serve. Then explain where the parallel fails, and distinguish the failures that actually undermine my neighbor's conclusion about fairness and democratic control and neutrality from those that are merely incidental. I am especially interested in what the comparison quietly takes for granted about obedience versus independent judgment, about whether the burden is boredom and lost time or injury and killing, and about whether amateur status really makes an institution more trustworthy. Finally, please say whether his conclusion still stands once those differences are made explicit, and if the jury comparison is misleading, what everyday institution would be a more instructive point of comparison for a conscript defense force in a country trying to remain neutral.
