I would like a case analysis that uses impossibility results in algorithmic fairness to illuminate the well-known pretrial controversy in the United States over statistical recidivism risk scores.

For the purposes of the analysis, please take the following broad facts as stipulated, without adjudicating exact numbers: a widely used risk tool assigned defendants to risk levels intended to predict a measured outcome such as rearrest within a fixed follow-up period; outside critics found a disparity in error rates, in that among defendants who did not experience the measured outcome, Black defendants were classified as high risk at a substantially higher rate than white defendants; the developer responded that the tool was similarly predictive across groups, in that among defendants given the same risk level, the subsequent rate of the measured outcome was approximately similar. Assume underlying measured base rates differed across groups.

Please work with the following working definitions, rather than assuming a particular formalization from the literature: calibration within groups means that a given score or level implies roughly the same observed outcome rate in each group; balance in false positive rates means that among people who do not go on to experience the measured outcome, the share designated high risk is roughly equal across groups. Take as given the core impossibility claim that, when measured base rates differ and prediction is imperfect but nontrivial, these two desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied by the same scores and single threshold, except in degenerate cases.

I would like you to do four things. First, provide a disciplined mapping: identify what counts as the predictor, the decision, the groups, and the outcome variable, explain where the base-rate difference enters, and show concretely what each fairness desideratum would demand of scores and of any high-risk threshold. Second, evaluate a reform proposal I have not seen fully analyzed: an oversight board proposes to keep a common underlying score while requiring both desiderata at once, by using group-specific decision thresholds together with post hoc adjustments to the reported scores to preserve the appearance of calibration, and claims this maneuver escapes the impossibility. Does it escape the tradeoff or merely relocate it, what exactly must give, and what residual normative choice about risk tolerance and error distribution remains. Third, give an honest account in both directions: what the dispute looks like once the mathematics is clear, which aspects of the controversy the impossibility framework explains well and which it misdescribes or leaves out, such as the choice and validity of the outcome measure, the role of human discretion in using the scores, and questions about the legitimacy of the data-generating process itself; and then what the case in turn reveals about the limits of the theory, including where the theory is silent and should remain so. Fourth, throughout your response, clearly separate what is mathematically settled given the stated definitions from what is genuinely contested among researchers and practitioners about which criterion, if any, should govern pretrial decisions, and do not present a contested normative view as the consensus position.
