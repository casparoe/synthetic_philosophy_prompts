I am a novelist, and during a recent residency I was the silent auditor to a protracted disagreement between two colleagues over the depiction of peoples in fiction, a question on which my own work continually depends.

The dispute began when one of them circulated a draft chapter in which an invented port town is presented as marked by a resigned, seafaring fatalism attributed to its national history. (1) The first writer objected that such national-character talk is incoherent: nations have shifting borders and no essence, and variation within any nation dwarfs average variation between nations, so the attribution says nothing determinate. (2) The second replied that coherence does not require essences: such claims can concern prevalent norms, institutions, and habits that shape dispositions only probabilistically, much as we speak of a family manner or a professional ethos without implying uniformity. (3) The first conceded that pattern-talk of that sort might be coherent but held it to be impermissible from the outside: even statistically grounded generalizations constrain how individuals are read and lend cover to condescension, and should therefore be left to self-description. (4) The second answered that abstention does not solve the novelist's problem: we must then rely on tacit generalizations while disavowing them, retreat to a thin universalism in which place explains nothing, or treat every culturally shaped trait as mere individual quirk; defeasible and evidence-sensitive generalization is preferable to silence.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move, indicating who is right on each point and what, if anything, both overlook. Structure your response under the following headings in this order: Summary, approximately 80 words; Exchange-by-Exchange Assessment, approximately 300 words, with a rating of each numbered move from mistaken to sound; What Neither Saw, approximately 100 words; Final Judgment, approximately 50 words stating who is more nearly right overall.
