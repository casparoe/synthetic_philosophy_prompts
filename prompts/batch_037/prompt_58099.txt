As a novelist drafting a multi-generational narrative in which one central figure devotes her later life to the solitary preservation of a remote coastal landscape she believes to be beautiful, while another devotes his to sustaining a small circle of intimate friendships through long illness and exile, I have found myself returning to G. E. Moore's contention that aesthetic appreciation and personal affection constitute the highest intrinsic goods, and I wish to give that contrast a more disciplined philosophical shape than my drafts currently possess.

Could you compare Moore's conception of beauty and his conception of friendship as sources of value, clarifying where they converge and where they diverge in his moral vision? I am especially interested in whether he treats one as more dependent on truthful consciousness of its object than the other, how his idea that the value of a whole may differ from the sum of its parts operates differently in the case of contemplation of a beautiful thing versus mutual love between persons, and how his method of considering what would retain value in isolation would handle two variations I am considering for the plot: a beautiful place maintained at great cost though it will never again be seen, and a deeply sustaining friendship in which each party cherishes a somewhat idealized image of the other.
