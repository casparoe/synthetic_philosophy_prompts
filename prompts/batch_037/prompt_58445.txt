I am a third-year PhD student in moral philosophy specializing in population ethics, and I am preparing for a departmental colloquium where I will defend a hard person-affecting response to the nonidentity problem. My thesis is that in same-number, different-people choices where all lives would be worth living, the fact that one outcome contains people who are worse off than different people in the other outcome gives us no moral reason to prefer the better-off outcome, and that our contrary intuitions in depletion-type cases rest on illicitly importing same-people reasoning. I bite the bullet: if conservation and depletion would produce non-overlapping populations with lives worth living, it is permissible to choose depletion even to spare present people modest costs.

To anchor discussion I introduce a variant I call the Reservoir case: two water-management policies for a valley. Early Conservation imposes moderate rationing now and leads to a later population P1 with average lifetime welfare at level 80. Delayed Depletion avoids rationing now and leads 150 years later to a distinct, non-overlapping population P2 with average lifetime welfare at level 65, still clearly worth living, slightly larger in size but with more inequality and less leisure. No one alive now will be alive then under either policy, and no particular future person exists under both. I argue we have no outcome-based reason to prefer Early Conservation.

My audience will be about twenty people: two faculty population ethicists sympathetic to impersonal principles and to the idea that one outcome can be worse even if worse for no one, one contractualist who thinks wrongness requires a personal complaint, two climate ethicists who work on intergenerational justice and will hear my view as licensing inaction, plus several theoretically literate graduate students.
