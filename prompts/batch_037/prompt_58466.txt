I am a junior majoring in philosophy currently taking an upper-division seminar on social epistemology, and I will be leading a ten-minute discussion on conciliationism versus steadfastness in cases of peer disagreement.

Our seminar has already worked through the familiar restaurant bill, mental arithmetic, and jury examples at length, so I need a fresh case that will feel immediate to other undergraduates and that does not turn on mathematics, philosophical doctrine, or legal verdicts. Could you construct a realistic thought experiment for this purpose that meets the following specifications:

1. Set the scenario in ordinary campus life, for instance two undergraduate research assistants independently coding the same ambiguous survey response with the same codebook, or two lab partners independently reading the same result, or a similarly everyday joint task. The disputed question should be factual, non-evaluative, and in principle checkable later but not yet checked at the moment of disagreement.
2. Present the scenario itself in a single paragraph of about 150 to 200 words. Establish peerhood through a concrete semester-long track record of equal accuracy on similar tasks with shared training, rather than by stipulating ideal rationality.
3. Hold fixed that both agents have the same first-order evidence at the time they learn of the disagreement, that the disagreement is disclosed symmetrically and at the same time, and that there is no antecedent reason to discount either agent for fatigue, intoxication, bias, or carelessness.
4. Engineer the case so that a conciliationist treatment requires noticeable doxastic revision in light of higher-order evidence of disagreement, while a steadfast treatment permits either party to rationally retain substantial confidence on the basis of their own first-order grasp of the evidence. Do not achieve this divergence by giving one party extra private evidence or by stipulating who is actually correct.

After the scenario, please add an analysis suitable for classmates who already understand the basic positions. Explain what the conciliationist view recommends here and why, with reference to higher-order evidence, and what the steadfast view recommends here and why. Identify clearly which structural feature of the case is doing the work in driving the two verdicts apart, explain what would happen to each verdict if that feature were removed, and anticipate how a defender of the steadfast view would try to resist the case. The verdicts should be shown to follow from the theories rather than stipulated, since a case that merely asserts what each side would say would not serve my presentation purpose.
