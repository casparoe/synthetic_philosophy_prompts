This term I am responsible for a seventy-five minute workshop within a second-year undergraduate module on multinational democracy, with twenty-two students who have just spent two weeks comparing devolution and independence debates in Scotland, Catalonia, and Quebec. I want them to move beyond rehearsed arguments for and against independence and to feel from the inside the difficulty that there is no democratically neutral way to decide who constitutes the people entitled to decide, since any choice of franchise, threshold, and territorial unit already shapes the result and invites further claims from minorities within the putative new state.

To prevent the session from becoming a proxy argument about the real cases, I plan to have them work with an invented federation I can sketch on a handout: the province of Sorvane, with about 1.2 million inhabitants, a Sorvane-speaking majority of roughly fifty-seven percent concentrated outside the mixed capital city of Brennor, a northern mining valley whose employment depends on national subsidies, and a southern island hosting a naval installation and a distinct dialect community. The task for the groups, representing the provincial government, the capital city council, the valley municipalities, the islanders, and the federal government, will be to negotiate the terms of a proposed independence referendum and then live with the consequences through at least one round of sub-unit response.

Please design a complete, runnable exercise around this scenario that lets the recursive boundary problem emerge through the decisions themselves rather than through any introductory lecture. Include a minute-by-minute timeline that fits the seventy-five minutes, concise role briefs with divergent interests and private instructions, precise rules for proposing and voting on franchise, majority threshold, and sequencing of votes, a mechanism by which a subunit that loses can call a further vote on its own status, and a structured debrief that connects specific moments in the play to the conceptual distinction between deciding within a demos and deciding who the demos is. Also advise me on what is likely to misfire on a first run with undergraduates unfamiliar with role play and how to handle it, and identify candidly one central feature of actual secessionist politics that this format cannot reproduce and that I must therefore supply in closing commentary.
