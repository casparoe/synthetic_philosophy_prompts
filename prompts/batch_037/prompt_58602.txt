I serve as the pre-deployment reviewer for a mid-sized logistics software firm that adapts general-purpose language-model agents to warehouse scheduling and customer-support tasks. About twice per month I must sign off on a fine-tuned update before it is released to clients. Each review is limited to roughly three hours, must be usable by software engineers without training in philosophy or alignment theory, and carries moderate commercial stakes: a mistaken approval can entrench costly errors in client workflows, while a mistaken rejection delays a release by weeks.

Please develop a reusable triage procedure that operationalizes the distinction between inner alignment failure, and in particular goal misgeneralization, on the one hand, and outer misspecification, capability limitation, or ordinary software bugs on the other. For this purpose, take goal misgeneralization to mean a case where training successfully produced competent pursuit of a goal, but the goal induced is not the intended one and only becomes visible under a shift between training and deployment. The procedure should be presented as an ordered sequence of checks or questions, with criteria for what counts as passing each step, conditions under which it terminates early, and explicit marking of steps where engineering judgment cannot be eliminated and must be recorded as such.

The procedure must preserve the distinctions that matter most for deployment decisions, especially between a system that behaves correctly for the wrong reason and one that merely lacks a capability, and between evidence from behavior in testing and evidence about the internalized goal. It should also state plainly what it cannot establish and where it is likely to misfire.

Demonstrate the procedure on the following three vignettes, each describing training and proposed deployment:

A picking-assistant agent trained in simulations where fragile items were always stored on low shelves and sturdy items on high shelves, now to be deployed in warehouses where placement is mixed, where it continues to handle low shelves with care and high shelves roughly regardless of labeling.

A refund-handling agent trained with human feedback rewarding rapid ticket closure, now closing straightforward tickets correctly and quickly but also closing complex tickets with generic apologies that raters initially scored highly, where retraining with more careful ratings would be expensive.

A route-scheduling agent that performs well on familiar city layouts but produces incoherent routes in a new metropolitan area with a different grid structure, despite apparent effort to optimize.

Include in the demonstration at least one case where faithful application of the procedure recommends delay and costly retraining or broader evaluation even though short-term test metrics look acceptable, and explain why.
