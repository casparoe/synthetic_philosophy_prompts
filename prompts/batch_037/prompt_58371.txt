Consider the following line of reasoning about rigid designation and the necessary a posteriori.

Take a proper name to be rigid when it designates the same individual in every possible world in which that individual exists. Suppose two survey teams track an outer-system body: one introduces Elda for the object responsible for certain perturbation data, the other introduces Rava for the object responsible for certain occultation data, and later work reveals a single body responsible for both.

(1) Elda and Rava are rigid designators.
(2) If Elda is Rava, then in every world in which that body exists, Elda and Rava designate it, so Elda is Rava holds.
(3) So Elda is Rava, though learned empirically, is necessary.
(4) By the same test, Elda exists holds in every world in which that body exists, since in any such world Elda designates something.
(5) So Elda exists is likewise necessary, though likewise learned empirically.
(6) Generalizing, every true existence claim for a named object turns out necessary a posteriori, and no such object could have failed to exist.

That conclusion seems unacceptable: necessity was meant to capture identities like Elda is Rava, not to make existence itself necessary and contingency collapse. Which premise or transition should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? Explain why the rejected step seemed compelling and what a defender of (1)-(3) must pay to block (4)-(6) without undermining (3). I would prefer to retain rigidity and necessary identities if possible, but I want to know the price.
