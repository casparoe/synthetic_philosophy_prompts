Please describe the most important implications of the equal starting points argument for taxing inherited wealth for the private bequest decision my wife and I now face. We have recently had our second child, and my parents, who own several mortgage-free rental flats in Greater Manchester, have proposed transferring one flat outright to each of our children to be held in trust until adulthood. The argument as I understand it is that it is unfair for some children to begin adult life with very large advantages simply through the good fortune of being born into a wealthy family, even when those advantages arise from hard work and genuine parental love, and that society therefore has reason to limit the intergenerational transmission of substantial wealth in order to preserve fair competition for jobs, education and housing. If we take that concern seriously, without necessarily endorsing a particular tax rate or policy, what follows for us as prospective intermediaries who would both benefit our own children and later decide our own bequests? In particular, does the argument distinguish between helping with university costs, providing a deposit for a first home, and passing on an income-producing asset that yields rent for life; between giving outright at eighteen and holding assets in trust with conditions about education or employment; and between favouring our own children and nieces and nephews or unrelated children with fewer advantages? I am not seeking legal or financial advice, only an ethical orientation that takes the philosophical concern seriously whilst recognising the legitimacy of partiality towards one's family.
