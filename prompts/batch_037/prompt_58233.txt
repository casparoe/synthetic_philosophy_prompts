After spending the better part of a decade reading LessWrong, the EA Forum, and adjacent blogs, I have converged on a strong suffering-focused priority for the far future, and I want to stress-test it.

The position I now hold is this: that preventing risks of severe, large-scale future suffering should take precedence over increasing expected future flourishing, even in cases where the flourishing at stake involves much larger numbers of lives or much larger gains per life than the suffering at stake. On this view, a trajectory with slightly lower expected well-being but substantially lower worst-case suffering is to be preferred, and interventions that reduce the chance of lock-in of totalitarian control, indefinite continuation of industrialized animal exploitation, spread of Darwinian competition to other planets, or creation of vast populations of sentient simulations under punitive incentives should be prioritized over interventions that mainly raise the ceiling on civilization.

This commits me in practice to several concrete judgments. I take myself to be committed to opposing a space settlement architecture that would very likely create enormous flourishing but carry a small chance of exporting wild-animal suffering dynamics indefinitely, to opposing AI training paradigms that rely on large populations of reinforcement learners shaped by pain-like signals even if they promise large alignment benefits, and to favoring constraints on detailed simulation of sentient beings even at significant cost to scientific and economic progress.

Lay out for me the full map of what would tell against this stance. I want the empirical discoveries, moral arguments, and decision-theoretic considerations that should move someone in my position, ordered from those that would require abandoning the view to those that would merely create tension. Include both considerations that are already available and ones that could in principle emerge from future work on consciousness, population ethics, or AI development. For each, say whether a committed defender of the suffering-focused priority should acknowledge it as genuine counterevidence or can reasonably set it aside, and say what the view leads us to expect that rival longtermist views do not. Conclude with an assessment of whether the suffering-focused priority as I have stated it can be progressively qualified to avoid refutation, and whether such maneuvering preserves a substantive position or empties it.
