I serve as the compliance lead for a university office that reviews undergraduate-led field experiments in behavioral economics and public health outreach, roughly fifteen to twenty proposals per month during term, most low-risk but involving adult members of the public approached in markets, clinics, and transit hubs. Reviews must be completed within forty-five minutes by staff with basic research-ethics training but no background in philosophy, with only flagged cases escalated to me for full review, and our concern is less legal liability than whether participants have genuinely given valid consent.

Please translate the philosophical work on what makes consent valid into a stepwise procedure my reviewers can actually follow, ordered as questions to ask with criteria for what counts as a passing answer, clear stop points that yield a requirement to revise and resubmit, and explicit markers for points where factual checking ends and a value judgment by a person is required. The procedure must handle three recurring difficulties separately rather than collapsing them into a general sense of voluntariness: deception understood to include outright false statements, technically true but misleading framing, and material omissions about purpose, risks, or downstream uses of data; pressure understood to include explicit threats, offers of payment or services that may be difficult for poorer participants to refuse, and reliance on authority or institutional trust; and competence understood to include age-related thresholds, temporary impairment or low literacy, and fluctuating understanding during extended interactions.

Illustrate the procedure by applying it to three invented proposals currently before us: a door-to-door survey on household budgeting that withholds that responses will be used to train a credit-scoring model; a paid forty-minute interview on vaccine hesitancy offering a grocery voucher worth roughly a day's wage in the neighborhood where recruitment occurs, conducted by staff wearing local clinic uniforms; and a longitudinal text-message study of medication adherence enrolling sixteen- to nineteen-year-olds with mixed literacy levels. Include in the illustration at least one verdict my student investigators are likely to find frustratingly strict, explain why the procedure requires it, and conclude with a brief account of what the procedure cannot settle and where reviewers should expect it to misfire if applied mechanically.
