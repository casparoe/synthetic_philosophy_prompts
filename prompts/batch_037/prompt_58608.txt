Could you translate the central claim of Good's theorem on the value of information into a reusable procedure for repeated, time-pressured professional decisions?

My context is as follows. I serve as a senior planning case officer for a district council in England. Several times each month I must decide whether to request additional evidence before determining an application — a traffic count, a flood model update, an ecological survey, a noise assessment. The stakes are moderate but recurrent: unnecessary requests delay housing, impose costs on applicants and invite appeals for non-determination, while failure to request genuinely decision-changing evidence risks unlawful decisions and judicial review. I have roughly half an hour to screen each such question, no formal training in decision theory, and I must be able to defend the reasoning briefly to a head of service who signs off.

Please provide a step-by-step procedure, expressed as a flowchart in words, that preserves what the theorem actually establishes whilst remaining usable by a non-specialist. It should move in order through identification of the pending decision and live options, isolation of the uncertainties that could alter the choice, estimation of the ex ante benefit of resolving them, accounting for the full cost of the information including delay, applicant burden and loss of flexibility, and explicit checks on whether the idealising assumptions behind the theorem hold in the case at hand. For each step, state what would count as enough to proceed and what triggers a stop with a recommendation not to seek the information. Mark clearly the points at which formal calculation must give way to judgement and cannot be eliminated.

The procedure must be able to handle four difficult situations that recur in my work: information that is free to the council but expensive to the applicant; information that, once obtained, cannot be set aside and must be disclosed on the public file; information whose mere collection is observed by developers, objectors or committee members and changes their behaviour; and situations where I have reason to think that I or the planning committee will overweight a vivid or alarming finding relative to its actual decision relevance.

Then demonstrate the procedure on the following three invented cases, showing your working at each step:

A proposal for 35 dwellings on the edge of a village where the only material uncertainty is peak-hour capacity on a single-track access lane, for which the highways team offers a two-week automatic traffic count at internal cost;

A proposal to convert a vacant warehouse into twelve flats where a consultant quotes a three-week bat presence survey, with a local residents association having publicly stated that any survey mentioning bats, whatever its conclusion, will be used to demand a far lengthier ecological assessment;

A retrospective application for an extended farm shop car park partly within a flood-risk area, where updated surface-water modelling would take six weeks beyond the statutory determination period and the applicant has indicated an appeal on grounds of delay, and where committee practice suggests any ambiguous modelling result would in effect be treated as grounds for refusal.

Include at least one case where faithful application of the procedure recommends a course that a cautious officer would dislike, such as declining apparently reassuring evidence or commissioning evidence despite delay, and explain why. Conclude with a brief statement of what the procedure cannot do and where it is liable to misfire.

Please respond in British English, using British spelling and conventions throughout.
