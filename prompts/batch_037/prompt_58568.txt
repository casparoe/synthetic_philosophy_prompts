After a long and unexpectedly tenacious argument with a close friend about aesthetic testimony, I tried to settle my own view by drafting a short essay, and I would like critical comments on the draft. The argument began when I said that a recent slow historical film must be superb because she, who works as a picture editor and whose eye I trust, told me it was, and she replied that I was misusing aesthetic language by claiming knowledge I could not have without seeing it myself. I defended the idea that I can gain genuine aesthetic belief from her word even if something further, perhaps appreciation or the right to assert with full conviction, is still missing, while she insisted that any such belief without firsthand encounter is either epistemically second-rate or normatively improper.

My draft is as follows. I argue that the acquaintance requirement conflates three different things: using testimony to guide inquiry and planning, forming a thin predictive belief about how I will respond, and undergoing the affectively engaged appreciation that makes aesthetic life valuable. Only the third requires presence, and its absence does not impugn the first two. To make this vivid I introduce two cases. One is intrapersonal testimony from a past self, as when my streaming profile, built entirely from my own prior ratings, predicts that I will find a new album spare and moving, and I book tickets on that basis before hearing a note. The other is what I call placeholder deference, where I accept my friend's verdict that the film's palette is masterful and retain that commitment as a placeholder, fully expecting my later viewing to fill it with feeling rather than to test it as a hypothesis. I claim both are legitimate testimonial achievements, and that the lingering sense of impropriety comes not from an epistemic defect but from mistaking aesthetic life as aimed only at true belief rather than at a temporally extended activity of attending, feeling, and revising. The essay closes by suggesting that testimony is not a shortcut around acquaintance but a way of scheduling and structuring it.

Please comment on whether the central three-way distinction is stable and genuinely moves beyond the familiar debate about acquaintance, whether the two cases actually support my conclusion or can be explained away by my friend's stricter view, and which part of the draft is weakest and most in need of development or counterexample. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
