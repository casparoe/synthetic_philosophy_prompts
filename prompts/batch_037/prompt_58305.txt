I am auditing an undergraduate lecture series on metaethics and wish to assess an overview of expressivism and the Frege–Geach problem that was presented in a preparatory handout for discussion. The handout characterizes the dialectic in approximately the following terms: expressivism holds that a moral utterance such as Tormenting is wrong does not report a moral fact but gives voice to a negative attitude, roughly Boo to tormenting. This story seems to work only for simple, freestanding assertions. In an unasserted context, such as the antecedent of If tormenting is wrong, then paying others to torment is wrong, the speaker is not expressing disapproval of tormenting, so the same words must change their meaning between asserted and unasserted occurrences. Moral modus ponens arguments therefore turn on an equivocation and cannot be valid on expressivist assumptions, despite being obviously valid. Hence expressivism cannot vindicate ordinary moral reasoning unless it abandons the idea that the meaning of a moral sentence is fixed by the attitude it serves to voice.

I have some reservations about whether this compresses distinct issues, but I lack the background to locate them precisely. Could you provide a careful assessment of the extent to which this characterization is fair? Please distinguish what a contemporary defender of expressivism would accept as accurate, what is a helpful but potentially misleading simplification, and what rests on a conflation the view seeks to avoid. In particular, clarify the relation between assertoric force and meaning, whether the problem is best understood as the invalidity of moral modus ponens or as an explanatory demand upon the expressivist, and whether the handout's final conclusion follows once the summary is corrected.
