I am preparing an essay on Ursula K. Le Guin's The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas and seek a philosophical reading of the story through the debate over incommensurable values and hard choices. For purposes of the reading, take incommensurability to mean that the value of the flourishing city and the disvalue of the suffering child cannot be placed on a single scale where more of one straightforwardly outweighs the other, and take a hard choice to mean a situation where reason does not dictate one option as better or equal yet the agent must still decide and stand behind the decision. With those characterizations in view, I would like you to consider whether the story dramatizes staying in Omelas versus walking away as such a hard choice, or whether it presents something different, such as a commensurable utilitarian calculation that the price is simply too high, or a deontological prohibition that refuses calculation altogether. Please show how the philosophical distinction illuminates particular moments that any reader will know, including the narrator's invitation to imagine the city, the revelation of the child in the basement, the visits by citizens who go to see the child, and the eventual departures of those who walk away, and discuss whether different citizens might walk or stay for philosophically different reasons. Where the story resists being reduced to an illustration of the theory, say so plainly, and do not force the fit. There is no need for direct quotation, as I know the plot well and am interested in interpretation rather than textual detail. Conclude by reflecting on what the story contributes to the philosophical discussion that abstract argument alone does not capture, especially about regret, remainder, and what it means to live with a decision after it is made. Please write your response in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a thoughtful email or letter.
