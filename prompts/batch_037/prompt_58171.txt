My seminar treats Harsanyi's aggregation result as more than a representation theorem: under expected utility axioms for each person and for society, plus Pareto indifference, social utility must be a weighted sum of individual utilities, and with extended preferences and impartial observer reasoning this is taken to vindicate utilitarian aggregation. I am inclined to accept that vindicatory reading, granting interpersonal comparability for the sake of argument, but I am unsure what it actually risks. Set out its points of exposure, separating formal or philosophical developments that would defeat the vindicatory claim from those that would only complicate or narrow it, including any distinctive commitment that separates it from rival defenses of aggregation, and indicate which challenges its defenders would themselves regard as damaging and whether reliance on extended preferences shields it from counterexample in a way that should worry us. Give particular attention to cases where individuals differ sharply in risk attitudes, since that seems to strain the move from impartial preference to moral impartiality. If the distinction between the formal result and its philosophical gloss is muddled in my setup, or the question cannot be answered as posed, say so directly instead of working around it.
