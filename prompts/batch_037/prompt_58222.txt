So I'm a PhD student in human factors and I'm spending a couple days a week in a regional hospital network for my dissertation, and I keep getting stuck on this.

They just deployed basically the same AI assistant in two places but with totally different accountability rules and I can't tell what view of responsibility they're actually using.

In the ER, the tool suggests a triage score for each incoming patient and the triage nurse has to hit approve, usually in like 60-90 seconds while juggling other stuff. Everyone knows she can't really double-check the model in that window. But the incident policy says the approving nurse is the decision-maker of record and if the triage is wrong she is the one who gets written up.

In radiology, the same vendor model flags chest scans for follow-up. Radiologists look at the flags in a batch at the end of shift, sometimes just skimming. There if something gets missed it is logged as a system performance issue, it goes to a quality committee, no one person gets blamed, and the vendor even covers part of the cost of recalls.

So what gives? What view of responsiblity does the ER approve-and-blame setup presuppose to even make sense? Is it that having the final click is enough even without real control or a real chance to know better, or could you still defend it on control-based grounds some other way? And does radiology presuppose a totally different, more distributed view? Are the two policies even consistent with each other, or do they presuppose clashing things about control, knowledge, and blame?

I want the implicit theory reconstructed, plus a clear line between what each practice strictly needs to be justified versus what it just happens to fit with, and where the practice is underdetermined so multiple theories could license it. And if the hospital decided the ER view was wrong and wanted to replace it with the radiology view, what would actually have to change in the workflow, not just the blame form?

Tell me if I'm missing something obvious, this has been bugging me for weeks.
