Over the past month, an old thought experiment involving an animal placed at equal distance from two identical piles of feed has become unexpectedly practical for me. I must soon sign a lease and I face two units in the same building that have remained exactly balanced after weeks of comparison: same rent, same square footage, mirrored layouts, equivalent light, noise, and commute, differing only in arbitrary features such as east side of the courtyard versus west side, with no consideration I can defend as favoring one over the other, and with continued deliberation now carrying real costs in time, fees, and risk of losing both.

To avoid paralysis I have adopted a private tie-breaking policy: when I judge further inquiry to be very unlikely to reveal a relevant difference, I select the unit that was presented to me first and I record in a notebook that I did so without taking myself to have a reason favoring it, only a reason to settle the matter. A close friend insists this is not rational choice at all, but mere reflex, and that rationality requires continued suspension until a favoring reason appears or, at minimum, use of an explicit random device.

Please act as a professional philosopher specializing in practical rationality and action theory. Please think carefully through the issue, beginning with brief brainstorming about the distinctions you take to be relevant before developing a systematic analysis.

I would like a numerical credence between zero and one in the following permissibility claim: when an agent judges two options to be exactly equally good and judges further inquiry to be very unlikely to uncover a relevant difference while imposing non-trivial costs of delay, it can be rationally permissible to settle the matter by bare picking of one option without a further reason favoring it over the other, rather than suspending judgment or outsourcing the outcome to an explicit random device.

Please decompose the reasoning that produces your number into three or four subquestions, for example whether rationality requires a favoring reason for the option chosen, whether bare picking without such a reason still counts as an exercise of agency, whether costs of delay and the need for decisiveness can themselves make picking permissible, and whether the case involves genuine indifference rather than incomparability or parity in a way that matters. Assign a rough probability to each subclaim with one sentence of justification for each, explain how they combine to yield your overall credence, and identify which subquestion your final number is most sensitive to and what sort of argument or experience would move it most.

Please clearly separate what is settled among specialists working on indifference, incommensurability, and picking versus choosing from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
