You are a professional philosopher specializing in ethics and moral psychology. Please reason carefully and step by step, beginning with a short brainstorming section that maps the conceptual space before you give your settled explanation.

I want to understand the difference between an action merely turning out to be morally right and its having moral worth in virtue of being done for the right reasons. Please develop a systematic explanation of that difference suitable for an advanced undergraduate who already knows basic ideas about duty, consequences, and virtue, but is confused about the more fine-grained distinctions in this area.

In your brainstorming, lay out at least these three contrasts and how they interact: motivating reasons versus the features that make an act right, being moved by moral rightness as such versus being moved by the underlying right-making features like need, fairness, or kindness without thinking in moral terms, and non-accidentally doing the right thing versus merely coinciding with it.

Then use the following three invented cases to anchor the explanation:

A night-shift nurse who stays past her shift to comfort a frightened patient because she takes patient vulnerability to demand care, although she also knows staying late will earn praise from her supervisor and she enjoys that praise.

A shopkeeper who charges fair prices and refuses to exploit a temporary shortage, not from concern for customers but because he has internalized a strict rule about honest dealing from his upbringing and follows it rigidly even when he does not see the point.

A bystander who rescues a drowning child through a mix of motives, where concern for the child is present and would have been sufficient on its own, but a desire to appear heroic is also present and operative.

For each case, explain whether philosophers would typically count the action as having moral worth, and why the answer turns on more than whether the outward behavior was correct. Discuss how overdetermination, the presence of self-interested motives alongside good ones, and luck in hitting on the right act complicate the assessment, and clarify what work the distinction between being motivated by rightness itself and being motivated by what makes an act right is supposed to do here.

Conclude by explaining what hangs on these distinctions for ideas about praiseworthiness and moral credit, as opposed to mere permissibility or requiredness. Do not provide a bibliography or list of further readings; focus entirely on the conceptual analysis illustrated through the cases.
