I am currently taking a seminar on nonconsequentialist moral theory, and an extended discussion with classmates after our meeting on agent-centered constraints has left me unsettled. Most of us were inclined to accept a familiar deontological verdict about killing, yet one of my peers pressed a diachronic variant of the case that seems to turn that verdict against itself, and I cannot see where the pressure lets up.

The argument as he put it runs as follows. (1) It is impermissible for you to kill one innocent person even in order to prevent five other agents from each killing a different innocent person in comparable circumstances. (2) If (1) holds, the justification must appeal to a special responsibility each agent bears for her own actions that she does not bear in the same way for the actions of others. (3) That special responsibility distinguishes your present action from other actions, including the future actions of the very person you will later be, so it should likewise forbid you from committing one such killing now in order to prevent your own future self from committing five comparable killings later. (4) Yet it seems clearly permissible, and perhaps required, to accept a lesser present involvement in such wrongdoing in order to prevent your future self from doing much worse when that future wrongdoing is foreseeable and outside your later control, as when you know you will tomorrow be drugged, hypnotized, or otherwise manipulated into killing five unless you now take an antidote that itself can only be obtained by killing one. (5) Moreover, if special responsibility means you should care most about keeping your own record clean over a complete life, you should minimize your own violations across time and therefore you should kill the one now to prevent the five later by your own hand, which directly contradicts (3). (6) Therefore the standard constraint in (1) cannot be sustained without either collapsing into a kind of self-focused consequentialism about minimizing your own violations or else fetishizing the present moment over your future self, both of which seem incompatible with the original deontological idea.

I find the conclusion unacceptable, since I want to hold on both to (1) and to the permissibility judgment in (4), but I do not see which step I am entitled to reject. Please identify which premise or inference should be given up, explain why that step initially seemed forced by the preceding ones, and spell out what a defender of constraints must pay to abandon it as opposed to abandoning one of the other plausible claims.
