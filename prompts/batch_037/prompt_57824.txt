I am preparing an interdisciplinary seminar on literature and moral responsibility and plan to assign Anthony Burgess's novel A Clockwork Orange, in the version familiar from Stanley Kubrick's film together with the British novel's final chapter in which Alex matures out of violence on his own, and I would like an essay-length reading of the work through the debate between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso on just deserts and punishment.

For purposes of the exercise, take Dennett to hold that moral responsibility and a defensible sense of just deserts are compatible with determinism, so that punishment can be deserved in a way that also serves forward-looking purposes like deterrence and moral formation, and take Caruso to hold a free will skeptic position on which no one ever deserves suffering for its own sake, so that retributive punishment should be replaced by a public-health oriented system of prevention, rehabilitation, and, where necessary, humane incapacitation without retributive intent.

Trace how central episodes look from each side: the prison chaplain's insistence that goodness produced by conditioning is not moral goodness at all, the state officials' defense of the Ludovico technique as enlightened social defense, Alex's post-treatment encounters in which former victims retaliate against a man unable to resist, and the final turn toward unaided reform. For each episode, say what Dennett and Caruso would each plausibly emphasize, where the episode seems to support one view over the other, and where the work complicates or resists both.

The reading should be faithful in both directions. Do not reduce the novel and film to an illustration of either theory, and do not distort either theory to fit the plot. Where the work trades on a confusion about determinism, agency, or desert, identify it, and where it dramatizes something the philosophical arguments miss — for example about the experience of being reformed from the outside, the moral significance of outgrowing violence from the inside, or the political uses of claims about desert and dangerousness — bring that out. Work from the plot and characters as commonly known, paraphrasing rather than providing direct quotation, and conclude by stating what, if anything, this literary treatment adds to the philosophical debate that argument alone does not capture.
