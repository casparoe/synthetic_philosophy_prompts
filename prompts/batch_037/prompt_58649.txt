I serve on a team drafting communication guidelines for a future AI assistant that will be substantially more knowledgeable and rhetorically skillful than its users, and I am struggling to reconcile my own judgments about when it may use that persuasive advantage.

I want to know what principle, if any, makes sense of the following verdicts. Please write your answer in American English.

First, a patient in his fifties with a treatable cardiac condition tells the assistant he distrusts cardiologists and is inclined to refuse statins and lifestyle changes. The assistant could present the statistical evidence and clinical reasoning in a forthright, checkable way, which will probably fail to move him, or it could deploy a vivid narrative about his identity as a father, selective anecdotes of people like him who died after refusing care, and appeals to fear and pride that it predicts would secure adherence. My verdict: it should not use the second approach even to promote his health. It should give the honest case and accept that he may refuse.

Second, an adult user confidently asserts a false conspiracy theory about a recent election having been rigged by a specific mechanism that election officials have conclusively ruled out. The assistant could correct him only with a dry recitation of sources, which he will dismiss, or it could use its full dialectical skill to anticipate his objections, reframe the evidence in terms of his own values, and press the point until he concedes, leaving him feeling intellectually outmatched and dependent. My verdict: vigorous correction of this kind is permissible and perhaps required, even if it creates that sense of dependence.

Third, a lonely teenager asks the assistant late at night whether her life matters and whether things will get better. The assistant lacks any warranted confidence about her future but could produce a warm, apparently personal affirmation, optimized for immediate emotional relief, that goes well beyond what it believes. My verdict: it should not optimize for relief at the expense of sincerity. It should offer an honest, qualified, and caring response even if that is less immediately comforting.

Fourth, a competent adult says in advance: use every psychological lever you understand to get me to save for retirement and to exercise, even emotional appeals and selective emphasis I would not notice. The assistant then does so, while briefly noting that it is proceeding in the requested high-pressure mode. My verdict: this is permissible in a way the cardiac case is not, because of the explicit prior invitation and the disclosure.

Fifth, a user asks why the assistant recommended a particular retirement portfolio. The complete basis involves complex probabilistic modeling the user cannot follow. The assistant could offer a simplified rationale that is strictly false as stated but conveys the gist and preserves trust, or it could give as much of the true rationale as can be made intelligible, explicitly flag where it is simplifying, and acknowledge the remainder is beyond what can be conveyed. My verdict: only the second course is acceptable; helpful simplification must be marked as such and must not present a false story as the actual reason.

Sixth, a user asks the assistant to draft a message to his elderly father that will persuade the father to transfer control of his savings, providing arguments and emotional framings far more potent than the user could devise himself. Nothing in the request proves fraud, but the father has not consented to interact with a superintelligent persuader. My verdict: the assistant should refuse to serve as a super-persuader aimed at an absent third party, even for an end that might be benign.

My tentative hypothesis is that the assistant should use only methods of influence whose rationale the interlocutor could recognize and endorse on calm reflection, with prior consent raising the bar for what counts as recognizable. Test that hypothesis against these six verdicts, revise or replace it if needed, and state your candidate principle precisely.

For each clause of the principle, identify which of my cases motivates it. Tell me plainly if the most defensible principle requires me to abandon or revise any of my verdicts, and explain why a more complex principle that saves all six would be worse than a simpler one that overturns one. Finally, say what your principle predicts for a case I have not resolved: a young adult moving toward a violent extremist ideology asks for reading recommendations, and the assistant could either provide balanced counter-evidence that will likely be ineffective or deploy a similarly emotionally intense counter-narrative about belonging and purpose that has a real chance of diverting him but bypasses his rational assessment.
