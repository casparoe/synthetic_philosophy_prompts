I am a warehouse supervisor in Sheffield with no university education, but for some years I have been thinking in my spare time about whether a piece of art can still be good when there is something morally wrong with it or with the person who made it. I have some settled reactions that do not seem to fit together, and I would like help finding the rule behind them, if there is one.

These are the cases I keep returning to. First, there is a long novel about two brothers where the revenge killing at the end is presented as honourable and satisfying. I believe that outlook is morally mistaken, yet I still think it is a powerful and well made novel. The moral mistake does not seem to ruin it for me.

Second, there is a catchy pop song whose chorus turns a boy's stammer into a joke. Once a friend explained the words to me, I stopped enjoying it and I stopped thinking it was a good song. That feels different from the novel, though I cannot quite say why.

Third, there are the early series of a sitcom I grew up with. The central performance is still, to my mind, brilliantly timed and the episodes are carefully built. Since learning that the lead actor seriously abused his partner in private life, I still think the episodes are good as comedy, but I feel uneasy about buying the box set because part of the money would go to him.

Fourth, there was a much praised news photograph of an exhausted homeless man asleep on a night bus, which turned out to have been taken after the photographer paid the driver to keep the man awake for two previous nights so the picture would look more desperate. The image is striking to look at, but I find myself thinking it is a worse piece of work because of how it was obtained. I am puzzled why the way of making matters here but the actor's private behaviour did not seem to spoil the sitcom.

Fifth, there is a stand-up routine I saw that got large laughs by doing an exaggerated voice to portray newly arrived asylum seekers as stupid and dishonest. I did not laugh, and my view was not only that it was cruel but that it failed as comedy, because the cruelty did the work that observation should have done.

The case I go back and forth on concerns a set of gentle adventure stories I read to my daughter. There is nothing cruel in the stories themselves, but I have recently learned that their author has spent years campaigning for harsh measures against homeless people, which I strongly oppose. I do not know whether to keep telling her they are good stories, or whether continuing to admire them and recommend them is already a kind of approval of him.

Can you set out, as precisely as you can, what principle or small set of principles would make best sense of most of these reactions, explaining which of my cases motivates each part of the principle? Please also say what your proposed principle would imply for two further situations I have not described, one where the moral fault lies only in what the work says and one where it lies only in what the maker did apart from the work, and give an honest account of which of my present verdicts you think I would have to give up to hold the principle consistently. Please answer in British English.
