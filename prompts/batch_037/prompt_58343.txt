As a professor of philosophy preparing an advanced seminar on the philosophy of religion, I request your assistance with a problem of predictive modeling of philosophical judgment.

You are an expert philosophical logician with specialization in modal metaphysics and early modern conceptions of perfection. Please proceed carefully and systematically, beginning with brief brainstorming about alternative explanatory principles before developing a sustained analysis, and make your reasoning explicit at each stage.

I am trying to anticipate the verdict of a colleague on a modal variant of the ontological argument related to Gödel. Her prior verdicts on neighboring arguments are as follows. She holds the Anselmian move from greatness in thought to greatness in reality to be unsound, on the ground that difference in ontological status does not constitute difference in the greatness of the conceived object. She holds the Cartesian move from a supremely perfect essence to actual existence to be unsound, on the ground that existence adds no content to a concept and therefore cannot be extracted from an essence as if it were a perfection. She holds that parody arguments involving a maximal island or other maximal kind succeed against nonmodal versions, in that conceivability plus stipulated maximality does not by itself yield existence. She holds that the contemporary modal version centered on the possibility of maximal greatness is valid given S5 principles but dialectically inert, in that its central possibility premise carries the entire weight of the argument without independent motivation, so that suspension of judgment rather than assent is the rational stance. She holds that attempts to establish the mutual compatibility of perfections by appeal to their simplicity or to the absence of evident contradiction are insufficient to establish genuine metaphysical possibility.

The new case I wish to anticipate is the following Gödelian variant, which I state here in full so that no external recall is needed. Take positivity as a primitive status for properties governed by four stipulations: whatever is entailed by a positive property is positive; for well-behaved properties, a property and its complement cannot both be positive in a way that permits trivialization; the conjunction of two positive properties remains positive; whether a property is positive holds necessarily if it holds at all. Define being God-like as possessing every positive property. Add two further stipulations: being God-like is itself positive, and necessary existence is positive. The argument then proceeds in two steps: first, from the joint consistency of the positivity stipulations to the claim that a God-like being is possible, and second, from that possibility claim via S5 reasoning to the conclusion that a God-like being exists necessarily. This variant is presented as avoiding the naive treatment of existence as an ordinary perfection and as relocating the work to the theory of positivity and to the possibility lemma.

Please provide the following. First, articulate at least two candidate general rules that both fit her five prior verdicts but diverge on this new case, for example a rule centered on the impermissibility of treating existence as a perfection versus a rule centered on the impermissibility of building existence into undefended possibility or definitional premises. Second, state for each candidate rule what it predicts she will say about the Gödelian variant, whether outright rejection as unsound, suspension of judgment as dialectically inert, or qualified acceptance as an advance, and explain the reasoning behind each prediction. Third, offer your best-supported overall prediction of her verdict with reasons for preferring one interpretation of her commitments over the other. Fourth, specify a further possible variant or test case, described in sufficient detail to be evaluated without external resources, that would discriminate between the candidate rules if put to her.
