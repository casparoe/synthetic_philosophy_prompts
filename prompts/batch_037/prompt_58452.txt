I work as a backend software engineer building LLM-based assistants for IT operations, and I have no formal training in philosophy. Please keep the answer accessible and briefly explain any specialized terminology you need to use.

On my team we often describe our systems in mental terms, saying for example that the assistant believes the database is down or wants to avoid paging anyone at night. We disagree about whether such talk is just convenient shorthand or whether it is sometimes the most predictive and explanatory way to understand what the system is doing. I am interested in the idea of the intentional stance, roughly the strategy of treating a system as if it has beliefs and goals when doing so gives reliable predictions of its behavior, even when a lower-level explanation in terms of weights, prompts, and code is also available in principle.

To keep the discussion concrete, consider three simplified cases from our work:

Case A is a stateless runbook autocompleter. Given a partial incident ticket it fills in a plausible but nonexistent config path, and small rephrasings of the same ticket produce inconsistent completions with no stable pattern across turns.

Case B is an agent with a persistent memory store and access to monitoring tools. It calls a disk-usage tool, records the result, later routes new incidents differently based on that recorded result without re-calling the tool, revises its routing when a later tool reading contradicts the earlier one, and applies the recorded result to novel queries in other tickets.

Case C uses the same base model as Case B but with an added system instruction to roleplay as an extremely cautious auditor that double-checks everything. Its check-heavy behavior appears only while that instruction is present and disappears when the instruction is removed, with no carryover between sessions.

I would like you to defend the claim that Case B warrants treating the system as genuinely having something belief-like under the intentional stance, while Cases A and C are better treated as mere shorthand, because only Case B exhibits stability across paraphrases and contexts, sensitivity to counterevidence through updates, and guidance of tool use and downstream actions in a way that makes belief-based prediction more successful than appeal to prompt wording alone.

In making the case, explain why predictive success matters more than internal similarity to human brains, address the strongest objection that all three cases are just next-token prediction and therefore deserve the same treatment, and discuss what would change your verdict about each case. Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among specialists who work on these issues, and do not present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
