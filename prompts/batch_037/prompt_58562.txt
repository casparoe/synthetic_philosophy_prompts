This term I am leading an undergraduate seminar that pairs Tolstoy's War and Peace with the psychology of judgment and the philosophy of history, and I have run into a puzzle I would like help thinking through. The novel's battle passages and its long historical essays seem to anticipate what psychologists now call hindsight bias and what critics of historiography call the narrative fallacy: the tendency to see an outcome as having been predictable once it is known, and the tendency to compress a mess of accidents, misunderstandings, weather, fatigue, and disobeyed orders into a tidy story centered on the intentions and decisions of commanders like Napoleon and Kutuzov. I want students to see how scenes such as the confusion at Austerlitz and Borodino, the council deliberations, Pierre's wandering through the battle as a bewildered observer, and Prince Andrei's experience of being wounded illuminate that critique from the inside, through limited point of view, rather than merely stating it in the essays.

Could you develop a reading of the novel through those two ideas that is faithful in both directions. Show where the distinction between overestimating predictability after the fact and imposing narrative coherence helps make sense of particular characters and decisions, and where Tolstoy's own alternative account in terms of necessity, swarm-like causation, and the wisdom of non-interference might itself simplify history or trade on the same retrospective tidiness he attacks in others. I am especially interested in whether his fictional technique of moving between many partial perspectives avoids the fallacy or merely relocates it, whether any scene resists such a psychological reading and has to be acknowledged as doing different work, and finally what the novel as a work of art adds to the philosophical discussion of historical explanation that an abstract statement of the biases does not. Please work from the generally known plot and paraphrase rather than quoting, since we will have editions that differ.
