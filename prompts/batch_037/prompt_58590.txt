I am a novelist invited to lead a ninety minute craft session for eighteen adult fiction writers with no formal training in philosophy. My purpose is practical: I want participants to leave with a more precise repertoire for rendering characters whose emotional lives diverge from what those characters avowedly believe.

Please design a complete participatory exercise that allows the group to feel from the inside the force of three competing accounts of what emotions are: emotions as evaluative judgments, emotions as perceptions of value on the model of sensory perception, and emotions as patterns of bodily feeling and readiness to act.

Build the exercise around the following three invented cases, presented in plain language suitable for nonphilosophers. First, a cellist who sincerely judges that her late husband did nothing wrong in his final year and takes herself to have forgiven him, yet each evening is overtaken by grief tinged with anger as if she had been wronged. Second, a hiker who judges with full confidence that a glass walkway over a gorge is safe and can explain the engineering behind that confidence, yet freezes in terror when stepping onto it. Third, a juror who judges that a defendant has done something truly contemptible and can defend that evaluation at length, yet reports feeling entirely flat and unmoved when facing the defendant in person.

Provide a detailed facilitation plan with timing for each phase, grouping, materials needed, and wording of instructions that can be read aloud without prior lecture and without introducing technical terminology until the end. The central task should involve writing or revision decisions and bodily or interactive elements, so that the philosophical difference emerges from what participants do and from the difficulty of revising the emotion by revising the judgment.

Conclude with a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the differences among the three accounts, with particular attention to what each account predicts about whether and how the emotion should change if the underlying evaluation changes. Add a candid note on what is likely to go wrong on a first run with writers resistant to abstraction and how to handle it, and a clear statement of which aspect of the disagreement the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by discussion.
