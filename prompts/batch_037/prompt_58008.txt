For fifteen years I have worked as a writer of literary fiction, and the vocabulary in which I think most precisely is therefore the vocabulary of craft: point of view and psychic distance, scene as against summary, free indirect discourse, the management of interiority, prolepsis and retrospective narration, character arc, and the discipline of withholding from the reader whatever the viewpoint character cannot yet know.

I have recently been preoccupied by a philosophical discussion of transformative experience, by which I mean roughly the following puzzle. Some decisions involve outcomes that are epistemically inaccessible beforehand and that also alter the preferences and self-understanding of the person who undergoes them, so that standard models of rational choice, which ask the agent to weigh expected subjective value on the basis of antecedent preferences, appear to break down. Candidate responses I have encountered include the proposal that one should cultivate a general disposition of resilience in the face of such unknowability, the proposal that the felt revelatory character of the experience itself provides reason, and the proposal that imaginative simulation, empathy, or testimony from others who have undergone the experience can supply an adequate surrogate for firsthand knowledge.

I am now drafting a novel centered on a figure I have invented for this purpose: a forty-one-year-old simultaneous interpreter, raised bilingual, who must decide whether to abandon her first language entirely and live, work, and eventually write only in her second language after accepting a long post abroad. I have stipulated in my notes that she cannot now know what sustained thought and affection in the second language would feel like from the inside, that the choice would revise her memories, her humor, and her relations to her parents, and that she has access to the memoirs of others who have made comparable linguistic migrations, access she both trusts and distrusts.

I would like you to render the central philosophical positions sketched above into the terms of narrative craft with which I am fluent, treating each position in turn and showing how a novelist would have to handle point of view, temporal stance, and the distribution of knowledge between character, narrator, and reader in order to embody it without cheating. Then provide a reckoning of remainder: identify which philosophical claims find a clean equivalent in craft decisions, which can be rendered only by distorting either the philosophy or the craft, and which have no expression at all in how novels manage consciousness and time. In closing, assess whether that remainder exposes a limitation of the novelist toolkit or suggests that the original philosophical distinction was less clear than it appeared.
