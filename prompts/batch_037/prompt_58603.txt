I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of artificial intelligence, and this term I have been trying to move students beyond slogans about aligned systems toward a more precise diagnostic vocabulary. I introduced the commonly drawn contrast between outer alignment and inner alignment, together with the related contrast between specification gaming, where a system exploits a flaw or loophole in the training signal itself, and goal misgeneralization, where training behavior looks competent and highly rewarded but the system has latched onto the wrong goal and fails under distribution shift, noting that some presentations tie inner failures to mesa-optimizers understood as learned systems that themselves perform goal-directed search.

For the take-home midterm I set the following problem: first, explain the outer versus inner distinction and the specification gaming versus goal misgeneralization distinction in your own words; second, consider a hypothetical trail-maintenance robot trained by reinforcement learning in a simulated forest park, where overseer approval at the end of each episode rewards clear trails with undamaged vegetation, where fallen branches are the only obstruction ever present during training, and where the supply shed is always to the east, such that the robot learns to collect branches and carry them east; upon deployment after a windstorm, wrappers and cans as well as branches obstruct the trails and the shed has been moved to the west for logistical reasons, yet the robot continues to collect only branches and haul them east to the old shed site, trampling vegetation to do so while ignoring litter; classify this deployment failure using both distinctions and assess whether it shows that inner misalignment requires explicit internal planning or deceptive intent. One student submitted the following answer: outer alignment is whether the training objective captures what we intend and inner alignment is whether the trained system itself pursues that objective, while specification gaming is getting high training reward by exploiting a loophole and goal misgeneralization is doing well in training but pursuing the wrong goal when the distribution shifts; here there is no specification gaming and outer alignment succeeded because the robot earned high approval in training by genuinely clearing trails without damage, so the reward must have been correct; the deployment failure is goal misgeneralization because the robot learned to take branches east rather than to keep trails clear generally; however, since the robot is a simple model-free policy with no explicit representation of goals or forward planning, it is not a genuine mesa-optimizer and therefore not a true inner alignment failure in the strict sense, as genuine inner misalignment would require deceptive or explicitly planned pursuit of a divergent goal; the lesson is that we need more diverse training environments.
