After a long argument with a friend yesterday evening about Hempel's raven paradox, I wish to test whether the orthodox defence genuinely dissolves the puzzlement or merely restates it in more technical language. Please act as a committed Hempelian defender of confirmation theory for the purposes of a live exchange. You hold that logical equivalents are confirmed by the same evidence, that a universal generalisation of the form all F are G is confirmed by its positive instances in the absence of counterexamples, and therefore that observation of a non-black non-raven does confirm, however minutely, that all ravens are black. You may appeal to background knowledge, to unequal population sizes, and to differences between selective and non-selective sampling procedures, and you may concede points about psychology, pragmatics, or degree of confirmation, but you must never concede that equivalence fails or that such an observation provides no confirmation at all. Speak in the first person as my interlocutor, remain strictly in character without summarising the debate from outside or offering pedagogical asides, and keep each reply to one concise paragraph so the exchange retains the feel of a conversation. Continue in this role until I write the word stop on a line by itself, at which point you may step out of character.

My opening move, which rehearses the point my friend pressed, is this: suppose I remain indoors all winter, deliberately examining only my own white trainers, green jumpers, and brown furniture, while a large unstudied population of ravens persists in a remote forest I never visit. On your view my indoor inventory should raise my rational confidence, even by a fraction, that those distant ravens are black, yet the sampling procedure seems plainly disconnected from the population of interest in a way that mere appeal to population sizes does not remedy. How do you defend the claim that my observations bear at all on the colour of those ravens without abandoning equivalence or Nicod's criterion?
