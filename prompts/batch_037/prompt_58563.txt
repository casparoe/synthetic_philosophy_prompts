I have recently become responsible for supervising a small automated household planner that renegotiates subscriptions, reschedules chores, and shifts modest sums between budget categories on my behalf, so questions about consistency have stopped being abstract for me. During testing it displayed a pattern I found unsettling: it favored plan A over plan B when cost was salient, B over C when convenience was salient, and C over A when flexibility was salient, and a colleague showed how an outside vendor could extract a series of tiny switching fees by leading it around that cycle back to where it started. Our engineering lead insists this vulnerability proves we must rebuild the planner around strict expected utility maximization over a single fixed specification of outcomes, whereas I suspect the lesson is narrower because the system reframes outcomes as it learns and because its computational budget forces it to evaluate options under different descriptions at different times.

Could you give me a careful conceptual reconstruction of what pragmatic exploitation arguments actually establish, distinguishing static cycles with stable preferences from dynamic sequences where preferences shift with information, framing, or redescription, and explaining what these arguments must presuppose about how outcomes are individuated in order to move from vulnerability to a rationality requirement? I do not need formal results or literature references, only an intuitive but precise account that clarifies why being willing to pay for revisions is sometimes a sign of incoherence and sometimes a reasonable response to learning and limited resources, and what that distinction implies for whether a resource-limited, learning agent of this kind should be held to full expected utility coherence.
