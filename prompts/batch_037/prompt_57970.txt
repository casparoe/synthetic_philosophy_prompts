I cover science, law, and ideas for a national magazine, and I am developing a feature on the dispute over desert and punishment associated with Daniel Dennett on one side and Gregg Caruso on the other.

As I understand them, the Dennett camp holds that responsibility can be naturalized: even without libertarian free will, treating people as answerable for their behavior helps sustain norms, deter wrongdoing, and cultivate self-control, so a revised, forward-looking notion of desert is both defensible and worth keeping. The Caruso camp holds that skepticism about free will undercuts desert in the basic sense that would be needed to justify retribution, and therefore punishment as payback is unjust; the response to dangerous behavior should be modeled on public health and quarantine, centered on prevention, rehabilitation, and safe containment rather than moral payback.

What puzzles me as a reporter is how little movement there has been despite substantial convergence. The people I interview now tend to agree on the psychology and neuroscience, agree that deterrence and incapacitation have limited and context-dependent effects, and often endorse similar near-term reforms in practice. Last month I watched a county task force consider replacing a mandatory minimum for a nonviolent drug offense with supervised treatment and job support. An advisor sympathetic to Dennett and an advisor sympathetic to Caruso both supported the change, one on the ground that answerability of that kind preserves agency and the other on the ground that support of that kind is justified even though no one ultimately deserves suffering. The commissioners asked what the philosophical disagreement was actually changing, and I had no crisp answer. My working hypothesis is that the core is not a factual disagreement about human agency at all, but a disagreement about language and moral strategy — whether to retain desert talk for its social utility versus to revise it for honesty — combined with a deeper split over which reactions count as data, entrenched retributive intuitions or revisable judgments about fairness and safety.

I need help diagnosing the structure of the impasse rather than deciding who is right about punishment. Is the dispute mainly verbal, mainly substantive, or two different projects carried on under one heading, one about the meaning and function of responsibility practices and another about their moral justification? Are the sides divided by facts, by standards of evidence, by which cases or intuitions are treated as authoritative, or by starting moral commitments that argument cannot move? And given your diagnosis, explain what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, an empirical finding about folk psychology or about the behavioral effects of retaining versus dropping desert language, a moral argument of a particular form, or a reason to think no progress should be expected because the question is ill-posed or intractable.
