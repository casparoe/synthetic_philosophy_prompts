I am writing as an organizer for our university sustainability group, where we have been debating for several weeks whether individual decisions to forgo meat or to skip a short-haul flight can be defended as making a causal difference to large collective harms rather than as merely expressive or character-building. One member leans heavily on the comparison between ethical consumption and voting in a large election, arguing that just as a single vote is almost never decisive but still has a small chance of breaking a tie that justifies turning out, a single decision not to buy a chicken breast or not to book a seat can have a small chance of preventing a restocking order or a capacity adjustment that prevents a much larger harm, so that expected efficacy preserves individual obligation. Another member replies that the comparison is doing all the argumentative work and obscures how markets actually behave.

To make the discussion concrete we looked at the small cooperative grocery we help run, which orders chicken thighs from a regional supplier in cases of twelve, keeps a buffer of two to three cases in the back, places orders on Tuesdays when recorded sales plus waste cross a reorder point set by the manager, and sometimes lets the manager round orders up or down based on expectations about weather, holidays, or spoilage. I would like an audit of how far the voting comparison actually holds in this kind of setting and by extension for climate-relevant consumption more generally. Please lay out the mapping explicitly in a table of correspondences, indicating what in the market setting plays the roles of the voter and the ballot, the candidates or options, the electoral threshold or tie, the fixed election day and the public count, and wasted or surplus votes. Then identify where the mapping breaks, including differences such as continuous versus periodic aggregation, opaque versus public thresholds, buffering and perishability, supplier discretion and nonlinear supply chains, and the fact that abstention from buying is not the same act as casting a positive vote for an alternative. Distinguish the disanalogies that merely complicate the expected-value calculation from those that undermine it, explain which features of democratic voting the analogy quietly imports that grocery and airline markets lack, assess whether the conclusion that individual abstention is expectably efficacious and therefore morally required survives once those disanalogies are on the table, and suggest whether a different comparison, such as buying a lottery ticket with a lumpy payoff or exerting pressure on a tripwire buried in sand, would carry the argument more honestly.
