For the past twenty-two years I have run a family fruit and vegetable stall in a market town, after leaving school at sixteen. I am fluent in the practical language of that life: regular customers, keeping accounts by notebook, giving a little extra to keep goodwill, extending credit until harvest, exchanging New Year food parcels with suppliers and with the local inspector, finding work for nephews and neighbors, and asking a favor in return when I need a permit stamped quickly.

Lately I have been thinking on my own about what outsiders call corruption. As I understand it, philosophers and social scientists disagree. Some treat any use of a public position for private or family benefit as corruption no matter where it happens. Others argue that gift giving and helping kin are obligations in many places, not bribes, and should not be judged by outside standards. Still others try to draw a line between a gift that builds a relationship and a payment that buys a specific decision.

Could you restate each of those three positions using only the kinds of situations and words I know from the stall, applying them to three cases from my own experience that I will describe: setting aside a crate of best oranges each New Year for the health inspector who inspects my stall; keeping a paid helper job open for the son of my cousin even though a stranger applied earlier and has more experience; and letting a neighbor who works in the town hall take goods on credit indefinitely and move to the front of the queue during the busy hour, because I will later need his help to renew my license without delay.

After the restatement, I would like a clear report on what did not survive the move into my language. Please go through point by point and say which ideas came across cleanly, which you could only express by stretching or distorting my shop terms, and which had no expression at all in that way of speaking. For each loss or distortion, give your judgment on whether the gap shows a limit in my shopkeeper vocabulary or whether it shows that the original philosophical distinction was less clear or less practical than it looked. Please keep the translation honest and do not quietly bring in outside legal or economic assumptions and present them as if they were part of my way of speaking.
