After a long and inconclusive argument last night with a close friend, I am trying to understand why discussion of the moral equality of combatants keeps going in circles. We were focusing on a pair of cases: a conscripted gunner in an army carrying out a clearly aggressive invasion who fires only at opposing military units and takes pains to avoid civilians, and a volunteer defender from the invaded country who does exactly the same things under the same constraints. My friend maintained that, at the level of conduct in war, the two are in the same moral position and may permissibly try to kill each other, because soldiering is a collective role governed by shared expectations, because enlistment whether voluntary or coerced involves a kind of acceptance of reciprocal risk, and because ordinary fighters cannot reliably judge the justice of their own cause in the fog of propaganda and fear. I maintained the opposite, that the justice of the cause transforms what the killing is: the defender is engaged in something like individual self-defense extended to others, while the invader, however restrained and sincere, is participating in wrongful lethal aggression, so restraint can mitigate blame but cannot make the two permissions symmetrical.

What frustrates me is that neither of us moved, and the wider philosophical exchange seems similarly frozen, with each side rehearsing familiar moves about domestic analogies on one side and institutional roles, consent, and epistemic limitations on the other, without any shared test for who has won. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not primarily a factual disagreement about what soldiers believe or experience, but a clash over what kind of theory is being offered, with one side describing the deep morality of killing and the other describing the fair terms for imposing rules on people who must fight under uncertainty, yet both presenting their claims as answers to the same question about permissibility. Alternatively, perhaps the sides are anchored in different paradigm cases that each treats as data, one taking a mugger and a victim as the model for war and the other taking uniformed armies facing each other as the model, so intuitions about liability and fairness never engage.

I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict on who is right. Is this disagreement verbal, substantive, or a case of two different projects carried on under a single heading, and does it turn on disputed facts, on standards for when a domestic analogy carries over, on which cases are allowed to count as evidence, or on bedrock intuitions about collective action that argument cannot dislodge. Please support the diagnosis by engaging what each side actually says in response to cases like the restrained invader and the volunteer defender, and explain what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis, whether a conceptual distinction, a revised test case, a shift in the question asked, or an argument for why no movement should be expected.
