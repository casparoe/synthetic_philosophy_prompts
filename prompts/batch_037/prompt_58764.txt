After almost ten years of lurking on LessWrong, Overcoming Bias, and Astral Codex Ten, and endless late-night arguments with friends about Bayesianism and bounded rationality, I am now a second-year philosophy PhD student about to give my first departmental colloquium on the problem of logical omniscience, and I need help anticipating pushback. My thesis is that standard Bayesianism goes wrong not merely by idealizing away computational limits but by mischaracterizing what good deductive inquiry is, so instead of treating a rational agent as having a single probabilistically coherent credence function that assigns probability one to all logical truths, we should model her as managing a fragmented set of local credence assignments indexed to proof-length and attention budgets, where rationality consists in well-chosen trade-offs between checking, trusting black-boxed lemmas, and acting on unchecked consequences. My running illustration, which I invented to keep the discussion concrete, involves a junior clinician I call Maya who fully accepts the axioms governing a drug-dosage calculation and would on reflection endorse two formulations as equivalent, yet in the clinic confidently assigns high credence to a safe outcome under the transparent formulation while remaining agnostic under a notationally shuffled but logically equivalent formulation that would take forty minutes to untangle, and I argue that no Dutch book constructed by an idealized bookie with unlimited proof-checking power shows Maya to be irrational in any action-guiding sense. My audience will be about eight faculty and advanced graduate students, mostly mainstream formal epistemologists who teach standard conditionalization and regard omniscience as a harmless idealization like frictionless planes, plus two philosophers of mathematics who tend to treat proof as transparent once available, and one theoretical computer scientist who works on complexity and is impatient with philosophical uses of computability talk, all of whom care about preserving the normative authority of Bayesian norms and dislike multiplying credence functions.

Acting as a professional philosopher with expertise in formal epistemology and resource-bounded reasoning, please think through this exchange carefully, beginning with private brainstorming about how each subgroup will hear my Maya case and where my proposal is most vulnerable, and then prepare me a practical Q and A prep sheet for the question period. I want the six or seven challenges I am actually likely to hear from this particular crowd, ordered by a mix of how probable they are and how much trouble they would cause if handled badly, each stated in that audience's own idiom and assumptions rather than in rationalist-blog jargon, each paired with a concise reply I could deliver in about two minutes and a candid assessment of where that reply is thinnest and what I should concede versus hold firm on, closing with the nightmare challenge I should most hope no one thinks to raise. Please write the whole prep sheet in American English with a direct, collegial tone I can rehearse from.
