As a journalist who covers science and ideas, with a current assignment on how mid-sized cities renegotiate public memory, I am preparing a long-form feature on the commemoration review panel of the fictional industrial city of Aldenford, and I have been testing my understanding of its reasoning in conversations with friends outside work who are skeptical that such decisions are ever principled rather than ad hoc.

The panel's track record over the past three years is as follows: it voted to remove the 1934 bronze of Governor Halden Rourke from Station Square, a work of recognized sculptural merit erected during the centenary celebrations to affirm imperial loyalty, and to place it in museum storage with extended interpretation; it voted unanimously to retain the 1891 statue of mill owner Silas Wentworth in Library Square, whose mills relied extensively on child labor, on the grounds that his endowment created the free library and adjoining public park, adding a substantial ground-level plaque addressing the labor history in detail; it approved renaming Crane Lane, named in 1927 for visiting philanthropist Edgar Crane who publicly advocated eugenic sterilization and had no other connection to Aldenford beyond a one-time hospital donation, following a neighborhood petition; and it declined to rename Admiral Tovey Street, named for eighteenth-century naval commander Matthias Tovey who was born in Aldenford and led its coastal defense but also profited directly from enslaved labor on his estates, opting instead for sidewalk markers and school curriculum materials about both aspects of his life. The new case now before the panel is a proposal to remove from Civic Plaza the 1911 bronze of founding mayor Josiah Bell, erected two years after his death by former colleagues, who secured the city charter and built the municipal water system but personally ordered the forcible clearance of the Riverside settlement in 1878 that left several residents dead and dozens displaced, with supporters emphasizing his foundational civic role and the memorial rather than propagandistic origin of the statue and proponents of removal emphasizing the gravity and directness of his responsibility and the message sent by honoring him at the symbolic center of civic life.

If the panel aims to remain consistent with its own precedents, what should it decide on the Bell bronze, and why? Please identify at least two plausible rules that each fit all four prior decisions, explain what each rule predicts for Bell, offer a best guess about which rule captures the panel's implicit logic with reasons for preferring it, name which prior case is the closest analog and where the analogy breaks down, state candidly what broader principle the panel would entrench by deciding either way, and describe one further hypothetical commemoration case that would most clearly separate the competing rules.
