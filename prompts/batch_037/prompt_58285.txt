I coordinate a monthly interdisciplinary workshop that brings together public-health researchers and municipal staff, most of whom have no training in law or political theory, and I have agreed to lead the next forty-five minute session on subsidiarity as a principle for allocating authority within federal and quasi-federal systems, with comparative reference to the United States, Germany, Switzerland, and the European Union. The participants have recently been divided over a proposal to site several large data centers supporting regional hospitals, a controversy that simultaneously implicates street-level noise and traffic, municipal water use, regional grid reliability, and national and supranational climate obligations, and many already arrive assuming that subsidiarity simply means deciding everything as locally as possible or that it is equivalent to a general presumption in favor of constituent-unit autonomy.

I am looking not for an exposition of subsidiarity itself but for a detailed plan for how to present it to this particular audience in the time available, built around that data-center controversy as the single running illustration. Please lay out the sequence in which the conceptual components must be introduced and explain for each step what prior understanding it presupposes, specify what work the data-center case must do at each stage and what features a carrying case of this kind must possess and must avoid in order to keep federalism, decentralization, and subsidiarity distinct, identify the three or four misunderstandings this group is most likely to form or retain and indicate precisely where in the sequence each should be anticipated and neutralized, propose one scenario-based question I can pose in the final minutes that would reveal whether participants have grasped the conditional and comparative character of the principle rather than merely repeating a formula, and note what important but distracting complications should be explicitly set aside for now with a brief rationale for deferring each.
