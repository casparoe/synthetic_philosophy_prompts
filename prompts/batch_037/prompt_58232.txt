I am a high school junior preparing a discussion handout for our philosophy club on what distinguishes acting together from merely acting in parallel. A senior in the club defended a simple analysis of joint action, and his defense seems to prove too much. I would appreciate help locating the error and understanding what a better analysis would have to add.

We considered four cases from school life. Maya and I carrying a stage flat for the spring musical, adjusting our grip and pace to each other, seems a clear case of acting together. Two players contesting a singles tennis match also seem to be doing something together, namely playing a match, even though each is trying to win. By contrast, two rival violinists, Lena and Priya, preparing for first-chair auditions do not seem to be acting together when each practices extra because she predicts the other will practice extra and would ease off if the other eased off. Similarly, drivers in rush-hour traffic who slow down because they predict others will slow down do not seem to be acting together to produce congestion.

The senior's argument runs as follows:

(1) When Maya and I carry the flat together, what makes our activity joint is that each of us intends to perform her own part, each predicts the other will perform hers, and each makes her own effort conditional on that prediction.

(2) That three-part pattern fully captures what jointness consists in: individual intentions plus predictive interdependence.

(3) Lena and Priya exhibit exactly that pattern: each intends to practice intensely, predicts the other will do so, and conditions her own practicing on that prediction. The rush-hour drivers exhibit it as well.

(4) Therefore, on the analysis in (2), Lena and Priya are acting together to raise the audition standard, and the drivers are acting together to create congestion, in the same sense in which Maya and I act together to carry the flat.

(5) But Lena and Priya are competing rather than cooperating, and the drivers are merely coping with one another. Neither pair is doing anything together in the sense illustrated by the carriers and, in a different way, by the tennis players.

I am inclined to preserve the judgments that the carriers and the tennis players act together while the violinists and the drivers do not, and to preserve the observation that carrying need not involve any explicit agreement beforehand. Please indicate which premise or inference should be rejected, explain why it initially appeared plausible, and describe what a corrected account would need to include in order to include the tennis match without also including the audition rivalry and the traffic jam.
