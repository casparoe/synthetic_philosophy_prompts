As a novelist revising my third manuscript, I have become preoccupied by a question that now affects line-level decisions: whether a passage's literary meaning is fixed by what I intended on writing it, or by what a competent contemporary reader can reasonably recover from the words on the page. The worry was forced on me by three situations from my own drafts. First, a husband's parting line, Take care of the garden, which I meant as a coded apology for an affair documented only in my private notebooks, with no clue in the text itself. Second, a copyeditor's mistranscription that turned a flat description into a striking metaphor I never intended but which my early readers found illuminating and well supported by surrounding scenes. Third, a description of a character as evasive and distant that I wrote five years ago, which younger readers now naturally hear as depicting a specific, newly conventionalized social behavior I knew nothing of at the time.

Please write a sustained dialogue between two equally well-prepared interlocutors who disagree about this: an intentionalist who holds that a text means what its author meant by it, with textual evidence serving only as evidence of that intention, and a public-meaning theorist who holds that a text means what competent readers operating with shared linguistic and literary conventions are entitled to take it to mean, with authorial intention serving only as fallible evidence of that. Have them work through all three of my cases in turn, with each genuinely answering the other's last argument rather than delivering parallel lectures, and neither reduced to caricature. Close in your own voice with a committed verdict about which account handles the three cases better overall, giving your reasons rather than splitting the difference.
