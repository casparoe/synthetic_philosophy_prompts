After a long argument last evening with a friend about who should count as part of the people in a democracy, I am still turning over our disagreement. My friend insisted the boundary problem has a straightforward procedural fix, either simply let all current citizens decide on future membership by majority vote or simply extend the franchise to anyone whose interests are touched, while I kept pressing that both moves beg the question, since the first lets a pre-existing demos entrench itself and the second still requires some prior body to decide what counts as sufficient affectedness and whether mere affectedness is enough compared to subjection to binding rules. We parted without resolution, and I would like to turn that stalemate into something productive for my undergraduate seminar on democracy and citizenship.

I lead a seminar of about 24 second- and third-year students meeting for a single 75-minute session, and I want them to feel the force of that circularity rather than merely hear it described. Please design around an invented case I can present without any background reading, the case of Maple Court, a 60-resident student-managed housing cooperative that sets its own budget, quiet hours, and required labor shifts and must now rewrite the charter that will govern membership for the next ten years, with three claimant groups pressing for inclusion in the decision itself, the nonresident kitchen and cleaning staff who must obey house work rules while on shift, the families on the adjacent street who endure late-night noise and share the alley the house controls, and graduated alumni who continue to donate a substantial share of the operating funds but no longer live or work there. The activity should put students in roles with structured choices or small stakes so that they themselves run into the regress of who gets to decide who gets to decide before that label is introduced, and should help them distinguish in practice between being causally affected and being subjected to collectively binding coercion.

Act as an expert in democratic theory and experiential pedagogy with experience running constrained deliberation exercises. Think carefully and work through the design tradeoffs before settling, and begin your response with a brief brainstorming section where you weigh two or three possible mechanisms and explain why you reject or combine them. Then present a complete runnable plan with roles or teams, materials needed, step-by-step rules and timeline, and a debrief that explicitly maps key moments in the exercise onto the conceptual distinctions between affectedness, subjection, and the regress problem, along with an honest note on what will predictably go wrong the first time and how to handle it and a frank admission of which feature of the boundary problem the simulation cannot reproduce and must be supplied by discussion.
