Please help me construct a rigorous examination item for an advanced undergraduate seminar in ethics and decision theory focused on the far future.

The students have already studied, in separate weeks, two ideas. The first is simulation reasoning, understood as the idea that if technologically mature civilizations run many detailed simulations of earlier history, then a typical observer with experiences like ours might well be simulated rather than in base reality. The second is fanatical reasoning about the far future, understood as the pattern in which an action with an extremely small probability of securing an astronomically large amount of future value is recommended by expected-value maximization despite the tiny probability.

I need a single take-home essay prompt, to be answered in 48 hours in no more than 1,500 words, that forces students to think about the interaction of those two ideas, rather than reproducing summaries of each. To keep the exercise self-contained, the examination item itself should stipulate a concrete decision case for analysis: a funder can incur a certain moderate present cost to reduce by one part in ten million a hypothesized extinction risk that, if realized, would forfeit a future containing on the order of ten to the twentieth power future lives of comparable moral importance to present lives. Students should be asked whether the possibility that we are simulated dampens the case for taking that gamble, and why or why not.

Please provide the examination question as students would see it, followed by a model answer of approximately 700 words and a marking scheme out of 25 marks. The marking scheme should identify the specific analytical moves required for first-class work and the specific errors or omissions that should lose marks, with enough detail to distinguish understanding of the interaction from mere recall of the two ideas. In particular, the scheme should reward discussion of at least two distinct mechanisms by which simulation might matter, for example effects on the probability that present action brings about the envisaged far-future payoff and effects on how simulated versus unsimulated value should be counted, and should penalize answers that treat simulation as generic skepticism or that apply expected-value reasoning without addressing whether that reasoning itself is undermined or amplified by simulation considerations.
