I am convening a small reading group on rationality and memory that includes several members without training in decision theory, and our next meeting concerns the absent-minded driver scenario, where a driver travelling at night must take the second of two indistinguishable exits yet cannot tell upon arriving at an exit whether it is the first or the second and cannot recall how many she has passed, so the puzzle turns on whether she should fix in advance a general tendency to continue or to leave, a tendency that can be optimal beforehand while feeling wrong in the moment. I was discussing with friends how humour clarifies this better than diagrams, and I would like to open the session with a satirical piece that gets the underlying logic right rather than treating forgetfulness as simple carelessness.

Please write in British English a deadpan supplement to an official motorway driving manual, of about 500 to 600 words and formatted as numbered advisory clauses with a brief troubleshooting FAQ at the end, addressed to the absent-minded motorist and her well-meaning satnav that advises a pre-set chance of exiting at any exit she encounters. The target of the satire should be the dispute over whether a rational driver sticks with the beforehand-best rule or reconsiders afresh at each exit, together with the common tendency to mock deliberate randomisation as dithering, and the jokes should turn on genuine features of the puzzle such as the clash between ex ante planning and on-the-spot reconsideration, the oddness of intending to toss a mental coin while knowing one will be tempted to ignore the result, and the question of what counts as the same decision repeated. Keep the irony legible throughout so a reader unfamiliar with the literature can tell that forgetfulness, not recklessness, is being ironised, and ensure a specialist would recognise the treatment as fair.
