Consider a regional nonprofit health system, governed by a single board, that simultaneously maintains the following four arrangements.

First, its emergency department examines and stabilizes every person who presents with a potentially life-threatening condition, without prior inquiry into insurance coverage, immigration status, or ability to pay. Second, its financial assistance office writes off hospital bills in full for households below 200 percent of the federal poverty line, conditional on extensive documentation of income, assets, and prior denial from public programs, while households just above that threshold are billed at full charges and may be referred to outside collection agencies. Third, its pharmacy and therapeutics committee declines to stock two high-cost specialty drugs for use in its general clinics on the ground that the gain in healthy years of life is disproportionate to cost, while physicians in its affiliated membership-based practice in the same building may prescribe those same drugs when patients pay out of pocket. Fourth, its general primary care panels are closed to new publicly insured patients on grounds of limited capacity, while its recently opened membership-based primary care arm, staffed in part by the same physicians, offers same-day access and longer visits for an annual membership fee in addition to usual insurance billing.

What understanding of entitlement to health care, of medical need, and of fairness does this composite pattern presuppose, if any? Please reconstruct the implicit view in a way that could make sense of both the universal element in emergency care and the market and administrative elements elsewhere, distinguishing what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with.

In doing so, address whether the four components presuppose a consistent view or quietly rely on incompatible ones, for example a right to a basic minimum alongside treatment of care above that minimum as an ordinary commodity, or a needs-based criterion alongside a willingness-to-pay criterion. Identify which rival understandings are ruled out by the pattern as described, and where the pattern is philosophically underdetermined so that several different theories of distributive justice or of the special moral importance of health could license it. Finally, specify what would have to change in organizational policy and in day-to-day clinical decision making if the implicit view were replaced, on one side, by a thoroughgoing egalitarian view on which allocation follows clinical urgency alone, and, on the other side, by a thoroughgoing market view on which allocation follows price and contract throughout.
