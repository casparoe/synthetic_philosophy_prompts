As a professor of philosophy who has recently assumed the chairmanship of my department, I am seeking a clearer understanding of how formal results in social choice bear on a concrete collective decision we now face. We must select one candidate from three finalists for a tenure-track position, using a ranked ballot among our fourteen voting faculty followed by a deterministic rule that elects the Borda winner with ties broken by seniority, and the process includes a nonbinding straw poll a week before the binding vote, open discussion in between, and the known possibility of abstention. Preferences are not purely private, since judgments of scholarly merit are partly shared, interaction is repeated over years, and junior colleagues may vote with an eye to how senior colleagues will interpret their ballots.

Please describe the most important implications of the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem and the idea of strategy-proofness for a small electorate of this kind. I am especially interested in what the result does and does not entail about the vulnerability of our Borda-plus-tiebreaker procedure to strategic misrepresentation, whether features such as deliberation, the straw poll, transparency of ballots, or the correlation of preferences across subfields mitigate or exacerbate the concern, and what sorts of procedural adjustments a department that values both honesty and decisiveness could coherently consider once full strategy-proofness without dictatorship is understood to be unavailable.
