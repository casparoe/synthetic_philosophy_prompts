After thirty-two years as a physical oceanographer working on instrument calibration, I retired and began auditing an introductory moral philosophy seminar at the nearby university, and I find myself stuck on a question my former scientific colleagues treat as merely technical. In our seminar discussions and in long coffee conversations with friends from the lab, we keep confronting allocation choices where we must weigh gains and losses across very different people — for instance, whether a municipal heat plan should prioritize preventing several weeks of low-grade heat discomfort and sleep loss for many healthy young shift workers or preventing a smaller number of intense heat-triggered migraine episodes for older residents with chronic conditions who have partly adapted their expectations downward, and, closer to home, how I should compare my own present enjoyment of early-morning birdwatching after age-related hearing loss diminished my former pleasure in chamber music with my wife's undiminished pleasure in that same music.

I am not looking for an answer about which option wins in such cases, but for a clear specification of what any adequate account of interpersonal utility comparisons would have to deliver if it is to be usable for such public resource decisions under scarcity rather than as a metaphysical theory of well-being. Could you develop the desiderata list — the kinds of cases it must handle, the distinctions it must preserve such as between intensity and endurance and between a person's scale and our uncertainty about it, the empirical findings about adaptation and misremembered experience it must accommodate, and the invariance or comparability conditions it needs — with the reasoning behind each requirement, an indication of which requirements are non-negotiable for decision use and which reflect the preferences of particular economic or philosophical camps, and a judgment about whether any subset appears jointly unsatisfiable and which single requirement eliminates the most familiar approaches such as reliance on choice behavior alone or on unadjusted self-reports. Please present the core of your answer as a table with four columns where Column 1 states the desideratum in one sentence, Column 2 gives the motivating case or consideration in two to three sentences, Column 3 marks the status as non-negotiable or camp-relative with a brief reason, and Column 4 states what a failure to meet it would look like in practice, followed by a short prose discussion of trade-offs and joint satisfiability; there is no need to provide citations or a reading list, as I will fill those in later.
