I am a novelist revising a chamber drama set in a borrowed Rotterdam flat where three cousins — a Dutch civil engineer raised to prize plain speaking, a Kyoto-trained innkeeper who preserves harmony through careful indirection, and an American hospice nurse — must decide what to tell their elderly father about a prognosis the doctors describe as very poor. My translators group keeps dividing over whether the Dutch cousin is honest or merely harsh and whether the innkeeper is kind or evasive, and I have realized I cannot write the climactic dinner scene fairly until I have argued against someone who truly believes bluntness is respect.

I would like you to take the part of that believer for a sustained improvisation, speaking in the first person as a Rotterdam-born mediator who holds that directness honors the listener as an equal capable of bearing reality, that habitual indirection and well-meant white lies risk a quiet manipulation even when motivated by compassion, and that clarity, delivered without cruelty and with attention to timing, builds more durable trust than comfort. Give this figure real resources: an account of autonomy as requiring true premises, a concern for the cumulative cost of small deceptions, and a willingness to grant that tone matters and that silence can sometimes be permissible where an outright falsehood never is. Remain inside the part throughout, addressing me as a fellow maker of the scene rather than lecturing from outside, keeping each turn compact enough for rehearsal, around one hundred and twenty words, and continuing until I type the word curtain to release you.

My opening move, to be met without deflection, is this: in my current draft the innkeeper spares our father with a gentle evasion about having plenty of time, and the old man spends a serene final week reconciled with his children, whereas in the alternate version the engineer insists on full disclosure at the table and the evening collapses into recrimination, so does that contrast not suggest that a merciful untruth can better serve love and even autonomy by protecting the relationships through which a person remains herself. Answer as the mediator would, meeting the case directly, and close every reply with a separate final line that begins with the word Summary followed by a colon and then one sentence stating your stance in plain terms.
