I am enrolled in an interdisciplinary seminar on philosophy of medicine and have found myself returning to a disagreement I had with friends after a session on end-of-life care. We were trying to disentangle whether death is fundamentally the irreversible loss of integrated bodily functioning anchored in the brain or the irreversible loss of circulation and respiration, and why modern medicine needs both a neurological and a circulatory standard rather than a single unified understanding. What puzzles me is that each standard seems to track something intuitively important, yet they appear to rest on different ideas of what makes a human body a living organism as opposed to a merely perfused collection of organs.

To sharpen the issue, our tutor introduced two stylized cases that we could not resolve. In the first, a young patient after severe head trauma meets all bedside tests for total and irreversible loss of brain function, but with ventilatory and hormonal support maintains circulation, digests nutrition, heals wounds, mounts a fever, and, were she pregnant, could in principle gestate for some time. In the second, an older patient suffers a prolonged out-of-hospital cardiac arrest with no circulation for an extended interval, is then placed on an extracorporeal circuit that restores oxygenated circulation to the body and preserves isolated brainstem reflexes, but with no realistic prospect of regaining awareness or spontaneous breathing or heartbeat. New perfusion technologies that can sustain circulation without brain activity, or preserve fragments of brain activity without a heartbeat, made us wonder whether either standard alone picks out death itself or only a reliable sign of it.

Could you compare the whole-brain understanding of death and the irreversible-circulation understanding of death as competing accounts of what death consists in, rather than as mere diagnostic tests. I would especially like to understand what conception of the organism or person each presupposes, how each would classify the two cases above and why, where they come apart in principle and in practice, and which you find more philosophically coherent when circulation and brain function can be technologically dissociated in these ways.
