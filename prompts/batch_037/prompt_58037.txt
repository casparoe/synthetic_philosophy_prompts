I am a second-year PhD student in epistemology working on social epistemology, and my departmental reading group has spent most of this term circling the reductionism versus anti-reductionism debate about testimony without anyone changing their mind. As we understand it, reductionists hold that testimonial belief is justified only when the hearer possesses positive reasons, typically inductive evidence from observed correlations between reports and facts, that link testimony in general or the particular speaker to truth, while anti-reductionists hold that hearers start with a default entitlement to accept what is said, with justification defeated only when there are signs of untrustworthiness or reasons for doubt. Both camps claim to accommodate the same central data, namely that children learn extensively from elders before they could possibly have gathered inductive evidence, that adults reasonably rely on strangers for directions and on colleagues for findings outside their expertise, and that both gullibility and excessive scepticism seem epistemically faulty. To sharpen our discussion I introduced two variations that split the group further, one involving unthinkingly following an overheard platform announcement at a busy station and another involving accepting a postdoc colleague's word about a calibration check that I could in principle redo but have no time to redo, and we found that reductionists and anti-reductionists each redescribed both cases in their own favour without conceding ground.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse persists because the two sides are not actually answering the same question, with one side theorising the psychology of rational trust and the conditions under which reliance is blameless, and the other theorising the epistemic status that testimony confers even when the hearer does no extra work, and that talk of justification obscures this split. Is that diagnosis correct, or is the disagreement substantive but stalled for another reason, such as rival background commitments about internalism and externalism, different rules about which cases count as data, or unargued intuitions about childhood learning and the threat of scepticism? I would like a diagnosis grounded in what each side actually claims, an assessment of whether the dispute is verbal, methodological, or substantive, and a clear account of what, if anything, would count as progress, whether a conceptual distinction, a developmental or psychological finding, or a reframing of the question, or else a reasoned case that no such progress should be expected. Please respond in British English with a carefully structured analysis rather than a general survey.
