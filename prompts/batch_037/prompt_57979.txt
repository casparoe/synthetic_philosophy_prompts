I am enrolled in an interdisciplinary seminar on welfare economics and distributive ethics, and a late discussion with classmates about Harsanyi's aggregation theorem has left me with a variant argument that feels both compelling and impossible to accept, so I would like help locating where it goes wrong.

The reasoning runs as follows. (1) Each individual has preferences over lotteries over social outcomes that satisfy the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms, and so can be represented by an expected-utility function unique up to a separate positive affine transformation for each person. (2) There is also a social or impartial ranking over the same lotteries that satisfies the same axioms, represented by a social expected-utility function. (3) A Pareto indifference condition holds: if every individual is indifferent between two lotteries, the social ranking is indifferent between them, with a stronger version adding that if someone strictly prefers one lottery and no one disprefers it, society strictly prefers it. (4) It follows that social utility must be a weighted sum of the individual utilities plus a constant, with nonnegative weights, which is the core aggregation result. (5) Nothing in (1)-(4) appears to depend on any metaphysics of personhood beyond the possession of such preferences, so the same derivation should go through under an arbitrary redescription of who counts as an individual. In particular, take a three-person society with Cara, Dan, and Eli, and redescribe Cara as two perfectly coincident subagents, Cara-morning and Cara-evening, who by stipulation have exactly the same preferences over social lotteries as Cara herself. (6) Applying (1)-(4) to the four-member roster of Cara-morning, Cara-evening, Dan, and Eli yields a social utility that counts Cara's utility twice, and by iterating the subdivision we can make Cara's effective weight arbitrarily large without changing any real preference, choice, or lottery at stake. (7) Hence the weights that were supposed to encode impartial tradeoffs turn out to depend on how we carve up individuals, which would make the theorem empty or show that any distribution of weights can be recovered by suitable gerrymandering.

I find the conclusion unacceptable, since it would trivialize the move from Pareto and expected-utility coherence to additive aggregation, yet each step seemed licensed by the previous ones. Tell me which premise or transition to reject, explain why it looked innocent, and spell out what rejecting it costs for someone who would like to keep both individual expected-utility coherence and a Pareto condition if possible.
