I have been trying to formulate the gradual-replacement argument for mind uploading precisely, and I worry that when stated carefully it proves too much.

Suppose a clinic offers a procedure in which a patient's neurons are replaced one by one over many sessions, while the patient remains awake and conversing. Each biological neuron is removed and replaced in the same location by an artificial device that exactly duplicates its input-output relations, so that overall brain dynamics, behavior, and self-reports remain unchanged throughout. Call a completed run of this in-skull procedure R. Consider also a variant procedure S, which is step-for-step the same inside the skull, except that each removed biological neuron is kept alive in nutrient solution and given simulated inputs mimicking those it would have received in situ. At the end of S there is a fully artificial system in the skull that behaves like the patient, and the preserved biological neurons are then reconnected into a whole working brain sustained in a vat. Assume for purposes of the argument that the devices and the preservation methods work as described; the question concerns what follows if they do.

The argument that troubles me runs as follows:

(1) A single in-place substitution of one neuron by a device that exactly duplicates its causal role, leaving everything else unchanged, does not by itself extinguish consciousness or bring a numerically different person into existence. The difference made by one neuron is too small and too local to settle such a global matter.

(2) If each single step of that kind preserves consciousness and numerical identity when performed in sequence, then the endpoint of the fully iterated in-skull procedure is conscious and is the original patient. So R preserves the person.

(3) What happens inside the skull over the course of S is step-for-step the same as in R: the same removals, the same insertions, the same ongoing dynamics, conversation, and reports. If consciousness and identity depend only on the continuity of the functioning system in the skull, then if the skull system at the end of R is the patient, the skull system at the end of S is the patient as well.

(4) The vat brain at the end of S is also conscious and is the patient, since it is composed of the patient's original living neurons reconnected after temporary separation, and temporary disconnection followed by reconnection of the same living material does not by itself end a person.

(5) After S is complete, the skull system and the vat brain are two distinct systems that can be given different inputs and will go on to diverge in experience and behavior.

(6) One and the same earlier person cannot be numerically identical to two later systems that are distinct from one another and go on to diverge.

Therefore the reasoning that secures the uploading verdict in R also entails that after S a single person is identical to two diverging successors, which by (6) is impossible. Either uploading fails even in R, or a person can branch in this way.

This conclusion seems unacceptable, yet each step seemed plausible when I accepted the case for R. Identify which premise or inferential move should be rejected, explain why that step initially seemed compelling, and spell out what rejecting it costs for someone who wishes to preserve the verdict that R preserves the person. If more than one repair is available, compare their costs and indicate which revision leaves the overall position most coherent.
