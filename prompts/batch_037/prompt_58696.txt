Consider a now common arrangement in residential care for advanced dementia. On admission, staff work with families to assemble a detailed life history — photographs, occupations, relationships, formative events and turning points — and caregivers are trained to engage each resident as the continuing protagonist of that history, reweaving confused or contradictory remarks into familiar plotlines where possible and treating sustained disruption less as the arrival of a different person than as a gap to be bridged so that daily interaction can proceed. The same legal order, however, gives binding authority to advance directives and guardianship appointments, requiring physicians and caregivers to follow instructions recorded by the earlier competent person even in cases where the present resident seems content, voices contrary preferences, and no longer recognizes the author of the directive as herself.

What conception of the self and of persistence over time do these paired practices presuppose, if any? In particular, do they require the view that a person is constituted by a sufficiently coherent unfolding story, with the self functioning as an abstract center around which biography coheres rather than as a separately existing inner entity, or do they merely employ narrative coherence as a practical heuristic while remaining neutral on what a self is? Reconstruct the implicit account as precisely as you can, distinguish what the practices strictly presuppose from what they are merely compatible with, and identify rival accounts — for example strictly bodily continuity or moment-to-moment experiential views — that they would quietly rule out if taken literally. Assess whether the care practice and the legal practice presuppose the same thing or work at cross-purposes, and explain what would have to change in how life-story work is conducted and in how advance directives are interpreted if the presupposed view were replaced by each of those rivals.
