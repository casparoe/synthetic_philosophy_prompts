After a long and inconclusive argument with a friend last night about Arrow's impossibility theorem, I would like a clear statement of my side of the dispute. We both accept the basic logic for three or more alternatives with ranked ballots: no procedure can simultaneously satisfy universal domain, the Pareto principle, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship. His view is that the result is a technical curiosity with no practical bite, because independence of irrelevant alternatives is an unnatural condition imposed by theorists, and because allowing indifference and a chair tie-break gets us around the theorem in small committees. My view is that independence captures a central democratic idea about spoilers and manipulability, and that the usual small-committee fixes do not escape the theorem in any normatively interesting sense.

Please argue for my view, using the following invented case as a central illustration. A cycling club with nine members must choose one of three weekend tours, coastal, mountain, and city, by ranked ballot. Three members rank mountain above coastal above city, two rank coastal above mountain above city, and four rank city above coastal above mountain. Walk through what pairwise majorities favour here, what happens under instant runoff with elimination of the candidate with fewest first places, and why the divergence matters for assessing independence. Then generalise: explain why indifference and tie-breaking do not provide an escape from the impossibility, and why dropping independence in favour of scoring or runoff procedures has a genuine democratic cost rather than merely sacrificing mathematical elegance.

Please write in British English in a formal register, address the strongest version of my friend's objection rather than a weak one, and close with a brief account of where your reasoning is most likely to fail or what consideration would lead you to the opposite verdict.
