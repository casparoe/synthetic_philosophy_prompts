I teach a second-year undergraduate seminar in moral philosophy, 24 students meeting for a 75-minute session as part of a unit on consequentialism and distributive fairness. They have already worked through the basic idea of maximizing total well-being and the familiar worries about demandingness, and several of them were discussing after class why it still seems sensible to give resources to whoever will gain the most from them. For the next session I want them to encounter from the inside the pressure created by a case in which one individual converts resources into well-being at a vastly higher rate than anyone else, so that consistent application of the maximizing principle appears to require transferring nearly everything to that individual even as the rest lose almost all. The term for this case and any associated jargon should be withheld until the debrief.

Please design a runnable in-class allocation game that produces that collision without an opening lecture. It should work with only paper tokens, envelopes, and a whiteboard, require minimal preparation, use points toward a symbolic class prize rather than money or grades, and fit the time available with a clear timeline. Build in secret conversion rates revealed through play, at least one round of voluntary trade followed by a collective decision under majority rule about redistribution, and a structure in which the group itself articulates a maximizing rationale before discovering its full implication when the outlier rate is revealed. Include full rules, roles if any, materials, and how to keep score visible as the rounds progress.

Also provide a debrief plan that maps specific moments in the game onto the philosophical pressure points, namely interpersonal comparison of well-being, aggregation versus fairness, and the separateness of persons, along with guidance on what I should do when the group either refuses to redistribute to the high-yield participant out of fairness or does so too eagerly and feels no tension. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run with undergraduates and how to handle it, and close with an honest note on which feature of the underlying idea the game necessarily distorts or cannot reproduce and that I will therefore need to supply verbally, such as the difference between unbounded capacity for well-being and a bounded classroom payoff or between points earned in a game and well-being itself.
