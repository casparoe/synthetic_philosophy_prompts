For a 75-minute workshop on electoral reform attended by 24 adult participants with no prior training in electoral systems, please design an in-class simulation that lets the group experience directly how first-past-the-post and proportional representation can turn identical underlying preferences into different winners and different incentives for voters.

Please build the exercise around the following fixed fictional electorate so that the results do not depend on outside facts. Divide the 24 participants into six districts of four voters each, with each participant assigned a sincere preference for one of three invented parties, Harbor, Meadow, or Ridge. Assign preferences district by district as follows: in the first district three voters prefer Harbor and one prefers Meadow; in the second district three prefer Harbor and one prefers Ridge; in the third district one prefers Harbor, two prefer Meadow, and one prefers Ridge; in the fourth district the same pattern of one for Harbor, two for Meadow, and one for Ridge; in the fifth district one for Harbor, one for Meadow, and two for Ridge; in the sixth district one for Harbor, two for Meadow, and one for Ridge. For the first-past-the-post version, each district awards one seat to the party with the most votes in that district, with ties broken by a coin toss. For the proportional version, pool all 24 votes nationally and award six seats by largest remainder, meaning each party first receives as many whole seats as fit into its proportional share and remaining seats go to the largest fractional remainders. Participants should encounter the central puzzles through their own choices before any technical vocabulary is introduced, in particular how geographic concentration can let the popular vote winner lose the seat count, how supporters of a smaller party feel pressure to abandon their first choice to block a less liked winner, and how proportionality removes that particular pressure but replaces single-party victory with a need to bargain over a coalition in which voting weight and bargaining power diverge.

Describe in narrative form the materials to prepare in advance, the step by step flow of voting rounds under the two rules, a small nonmonetary stake such as control over how the final ten minutes are used that gives the outcome meaning without distorting participation, and a closing discussion that connects specific moments from the simulation to the broader contrasts between the systems. Also explain what is likely to go awry on a first run, such as confusion about strategic options, reluctance to vote insincerely, or disputes over ties and coalition offers, and how the facilitator should handle each difficulty, and close with a candid account of one important feature of real elections that this small-room exercise cannot reproduce and that the facilitator will therefore need to supply by explanation. Please present the entire response in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a letter or email, describing any vote totals and seat allocations in sentences rather than tabular displays.
