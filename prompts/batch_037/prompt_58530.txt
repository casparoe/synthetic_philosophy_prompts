For a graduate seminar on decision under uncertainty, I am preparing a session on the familiar two-urn Ellsberg pattern and a defense of it that appeals to an adversarial game.

The pattern is this: Urn K contains 50 red and 50 black balls. Urn U contains 100 balls, each either red or black, in an unknown proportion. A ball will be drawn from the urn chosen by the decision maker, who is offered a fixed prize for correctly predicting its color. Many subjects strictly prefer betting on red from K over betting on red from U, and also strictly prefer betting on black from K over betting on black from U, in tension with the idea that beliefs about U can be represented by a single precise probability.

In discussion, a participant defends this pair of preferences as rationally prudent by invoking an analogy: betting on the unknown urn is like playing against a cunning adversary who observes which color you bet on and then stocks the urn so as to make you lose. On that picture, preferring the known urn for either color is simply sensible pessimism in the face of an opponent, naturally modeled by a worst-case or maxmin decision rule.

Please audit how far this adversary analogy holds and whether the rationalizing work it is asked to do survives once its limits are made explicit. Your analysis should include an explicit mapping between elements of the adversarial game and elements of the Ellsberg choice, such as the adversary, the adversary's motives and information, the timing of the urn being filled relative to the bet, and the decision maker's beliefs about all of these; an account of where the mapping breaks down, including what the analogy quietly imports from strategic interaction that mere ignorance about a natural process lacks; and a distinction between breakdowns that are incidental and breakdowns that matter for the claim that the Ellsberg preferences are rational.

To test the analogy, apply it to two stipulated variants and compare its predictions with the intuitively reasonable response. Variant A: subjects are told that Urn U was filled last week by a public fair coin toss, all red if heads and all black if tails, with the outcome still concealed, and must now choose an urn and a color. Variant B: contrast three fill procedures for Urn U, where the filler chooses the composition after seeing the bettor's chosen color and aims to make the bettor lose, after seeing the chosen color and aims to make the bettor win but cannot otherwise communicate, and before the bet is chosen with no observation of it. Say what the adversary picture predicts in each case, whether ambiguity aversion should persist, attenuate, or reverse, and what that implies for the analogy.

Conclude with a verdict on whether the claim that Ellsberg preferences are rationally defensible as precautionary reasoning can stand without the adversary picture, and if a different analogy, such as robustness, insurance, or precaution against model misspecification, carries that idea with less misleading baggage.
