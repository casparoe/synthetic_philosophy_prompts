I have worked for over two decades maintaining apartment buildings and have no university training in philosophy, but in the evenings I have been turning over an idea I found in a library book about four different ways that clever behavior can arise, described as Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian and Gregorian minds. As I understood it, some creatures are wired by evolution to do one thing well without learning, others learn by blind trial and error and keep what works, a third sort can run through possibilities internally and discard bad moves before acting, and a fourth sort borrows whole toolkits of thinking from others through language, instructions and shared gadgets and habits. I have been discussing this with my brother-in-law on long walks and we disagree about whether it is just a neat labeling system or something that should change how we deal with the not-quite-smart things around us.

The reason I keep coming back to it is very practical. At home I am trying to house-train a stubborn rescue terrier that mostly learns when it gets a treat or a scolding, I spend two afternoons a week helping my eight-year-old granddaughter who is learning multiplication and keeps making up little stories to test her answers before writing them down, and in my basement workshop I tinker with a small plant-watering rig I built from a kit that adjusts its schedule after each wilted leaf and that I recently fitted with a borrowed temperature forecasting routine. I would like to understand what you consider the two or three most important practical upshots of that four-way distinction for how I should interpret errors, decide when to step in, and decide what kind of credit or patience to extend in these three very different cases. Please commit yourself to a definite ranking and explain your reasoning for it, rather than laying out several possible readings and leaving me to choose.
