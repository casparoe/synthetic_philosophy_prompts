Defend the thesis that within a broadly just and democratic multinational state, a procedurally fair majority vote for independence does not by itself establish a moral right to secede, even when the seceding region has a distinct identity and a history of autonomous self-government.

Work primarily with the following two stipulated hypotheticals, rather than resting your case on contested details of real history:

Aldervale is a coastal province of the democratic federation of Federia, with 4 million of the federation's 30 million citizens. It has its own dialect, universities, and media, and for forty years has had an elected regional parliament with substantial tax and education powers. Its citizens enjoy full voting rights, representation in the federal legislature and courts, and protection of basic liberties. The province is about 20 percent wealthier per capita than the federation as a whole. After a campaign in which the central government offers further devolution short of independence, Aldervale holds an impeccably administered referendum with 78 percent turnout, resulting in 54 percent for independence. The leading public rationale is that continued union imposes unfair redistributive burdens and dilutes Aldervale's distinctiveness.

Kestria is an interior region of the same federation, demographically and institutionally similar to Aldervale, except that its distinct language is in measurable decline among younger speakers. The federal government, without violating basic rights or withdrawing existing autonomy, has repeatedly refused to fund immersion schools or to require bilingual federal administration in the region. Kestria holds an identically clean referendum with the same turnout and the same 54 percent majority, with language preservation as the central public rationale.

Make the case that neither majority establishes a right to secede, and explain why the additional cultural vulnerability in Kestria does not alter that verdict, while specifying what kind of injustice or breakdown would alter it. Your argument should address the strongest opposing consideration, namely that respect for democratic self-determination means a distinct territorial majority that has deliberated and voted to leave must be allowed to do so, and should explain the limits of that principle without denying the value of democracy. You may briefly note what your reasoning implies for how to think about Scotland, Catalonia, and Quebec in broad outline, but do not make your argument depend on fine-grained facts about those cases.

Conclude with an explicit statement of what would change your mind: identify the empirical variation in the hypotheticals, moral premise, or counterargument that would most plausibly lead you to abandon or substantially revise the thesis, and where you take your own reasoning to be most vulnerable to failure.
