Act as a professional philosopher specializing in philosophy of science and algorithmic information theory. Please think carefully and explicitly, starting with a brief brainstorm of the distinct senses of simplicity that might be in play before you settle on your final comparison.

I want a comparison between two ideas that I tend to run together. The first is the formal simplicity bias associated with Solomonoff induction and Kolmogorov complexity, as I roughly understand it: hypotheses corresponding to shorter programs in some fixed universal language receive substantially higher prior weight, and the complexity of a data string is measured by the length of the shortest program that generates it. The second is the informal working rule I use as an engineer: when two accounts both fit the logs so far, prefer the one with fewer moving parts, fewer special cases, and lower maintenance cost.

To make this concrete, consider a case I invented for my own system. A service records a binary outcome each minute, 1 for timeout and 0 otherwise. Hypothesis A is a compact rule, say a 30-line program using time of day and queue length to predict the next bit, which fits 9,850 of the past 10,000 minutes and makes a definite prediction for the next 1,000. Hypothesis B is a long lookup table that simply lists the past 10,000 outcomes verbatim, fits all 10,000 perfectly by construction, and predicts by repeating a gerrymandered pattern that happens to coincide with the past data but diverges from A going forward. Both can be made to fit if I allow enough patching of A with exceptions.

Please compare the two notions of simplicity along at least these dimensions: what each counts as simple, how sensitive each is to the choice of programming language or vocabulary, whether each is meant to guide belief about future observations or only practical choice, and how each handles the fact that any data can be fit perfectly by a sufficiently long enumeration. Explain in which respects the Solomonoff-Kolmogorov idea is a precisification of ordinary Occam-style preference and in which respects it is something different in kind, especially given uncomputability and language-dependent constant overheads.

Conclude with a clear verdict on which notion, if either, provides a reason to expect simpler hypotheses to predict better rather than merely to be easier to work with, with justification.
