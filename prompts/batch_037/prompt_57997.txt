I serve on a university committee tasked with revising guidance on employment reference letters. A proposal under consideration would permit faculty to employ a generative model to produce first drafts of routine letters, provided the assistance is acknowledged in a footnote. In discussion, a colleague rejected the proposal on the ground that What if everyone did that? If letters of this kind became universal, she argued, they would no longer signal individualized judgment, recipients would learn to discount them, and the practice would undermine itself; hence, she concluded, each of us ought to refrain from the practice even now, while most letters remain traditionally authored.

The appeal is commonly taken to express a Kantian form of universalization reasoning, but its exact force in this context is unclear. I would be grateful for a careful disambiguation of the slogan as it functions in this argument.

Please present the competing interpretations as a structured taxonomy of at least four distinct, precise theses the slogan might be advancing. In developing the taxonomy, please attend to variations in how the relevant act is described, who everyone is taken to comprise, whether universal performance is imagined as simultaneous or gradual, and whether the defect alleged in universal performance is causal, conceptual, or volitional.

For each interpretation, state whether it is trivially true but morally inert, substantively plausible, or untenable, and whether it is sufficient to sustain the inference from facts about universal uptake to an individual obligation to refrain under present, non-universal conditions. Finally, indicate which interpretation or interpretations best capture what those who invoke the slogan ordinarily intend, and whether any of them vindicates my colleague's conclusion.
