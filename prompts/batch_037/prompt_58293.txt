I defend a naturalist account of health and disease according to which being healthy is a matter of statistically typical, efficiently functioning bodily and psychological capacities making their standard contributions to survival and reproduction within an appropriate reference group defined by species, sex, and age, with evaluative judgments entering only at the stage of deciding whether and how to intervene, not at the stage of determining what counts as pathological.

I find this view attractive because it promises to separate biological dysfunction from social disapproval and to ground medical classification in facts about how organisms work. I want to understand its vulnerability. Please provide a map of what would count against this position: which observations, arguments, hypothetical or actual cases, and theoretical results would undermine it, what it predicts that normativist rivals do not, and whether it has been protected by qualifications that reduce its exposure.

To keep the discussion concrete and to prevent reliance on familiar textbook examples, please apply the account to the following two stipulated vignettes that I have constructed for this purpose:

Vignette A concerns a stable chronotype variant present in about eight percent of adults in a described population, involving a five-hour shift in sleep onset with no measurable loss in cognitive performance, longevity, or fertility when persons sleep on their preferred schedule, but with substantial daytime impairment under conventional work schedules.

Vignette B concerns an asymptomatic muscle protein variant that scores as subefficient on standard laboratory measures of cardiac output at rest, yet is associated with superior stamina at altitude and with no reduction in survival or reproduction in the current environment of the population.

For each vignette and for the debate more generally, explain what a defender would have to say, where evaluative considerations would have to be smuggled in to make the verdict plausible, and what a normativist would say differently.

I am most likely to be moved by considerations drawn from clinical classification practice and from variation within and across environments, rather than by appeal to linguistic intuition alone. Please distinguish challenges that would be decisive if established from those that would be merely awkward, indicate which challenges defenders of naturalism would themselves recognize as counting against the view as opposed to those they would dismiss as misunderstandings, and offer an assessment of whether the view as currently formulated risks enough to be informative or has been qualified into insulation, with a judgment about whether any such insulation is a defect in this domain.

Conclude with a table with exactly the following columns, with one row per distinct challenge and with the specified content in each cell: Challenge, in which you name the objection in five words or fewer; Category, in which you classify it as conceptual, empirical, classificatory, or methodological; What Must Be Shown, in which you state the fact, case verdict, or result that would have to obtain; Threat, in which you explain why that outcome would undermine naturalism in particular rather than medicine in general; Rival Prediction, in which you state what normativism predicts about the same matter; Defender Concession, in which you state Yes or No for whether a consistent naturalist should concede that the outcome counts against the view; and Force, in which you rate the challenge as Fatal, Serious, or Awkward with a one-sentence justification.
