Consider the following diachronic extension of the classic money pump argument for the rationality of transitive preferences.

(1) If an agent who correctly foresees the full sequence of offers she will receive would find each trade acceptable by the lights of the preferences she holds at the time it is offered, yet the sequence as a whole returns her to her initial holdings minus a sure loss of money, then her pattern of preferences is irrational.

(2) The familiar pump against an agent with cyclic preferences at a single time is a special case of (1), which is why exploitability of this kind is widely taken to reveal irrationality rather than mere bad luck.

(3) Rationality permits an agent to undergo foreseeable preference change in response to information she rationally expects to receive. An agent who now values an outcome highly, while correctly predicting that legitimate learning will later lead her to value it less, need not be irrational either now or later.

(4) Consider Maya. On Monday she owns paperback P and is offered the chance to pay $10 to exchange it for hardcover H. By Monday's tastes, H is worth well more than $10 extra to her. She also correctly predicts that on Tuesday she will read a trustworthy scholarly review revealing that H is abridged in a way she cares about, and that on Wednesday, with that information in hand, she will prefer P to H by well more than $10. A bookie who knows all this offers her the Monday exchange, then on Wednesday offers to exchange H back for P for an additional $10. Judged by the preferences she holds when each offer is made, Maya finds each offer acceptable, and she foresees that she will.

(5) Maya therefore satisfies the antecedent of (1): she foreseeably accepts a sequence of trades that leaves her with exactly what she started with, minus $20.

(6) So Maya's pattern of preferences is irrational, and by the same reasoning any agent who foreseeably revises her preferences in light of expected information in this way is irrational.

The conclusion in (6) seems unacceptable, since learning of the sort described in (3) and (4) looks like a paradigm of rational responsiveness to evidence. Yet (1) looks like the very principle that gives the standard money pump its force, and (2)-(5) each look difficult to deny.

Which premise or inferential step should be rejected, and why did it seem plausible? In answering, explain what a defender of pragmatic arguments for standard decision theory must pay to give up that step, and whether the repair can preserve the force of the synchronic pump described in (2). I am reluctant to abandon either the exploitability test in (1) or the permissibility of learning in (3), but I want to understand the cost of retaining each.
