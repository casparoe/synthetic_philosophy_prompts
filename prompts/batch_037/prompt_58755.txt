After a protracted and spirited disagreement with a close friend last evening, I find myself uncertain about how to extend a familiar philosophical ideal to present circumstances. We were discussing the Kantian vision of a lasting peace secured through republican government, a voluntary federation of free states, expanding commerce and hospitality, and the gradual elimination of practices that make war likely, such as large professional standing armies, interference in the internal affairs of other states, and the financing of conflicts through debt. My friend contends that this vision has essentially been realized among contemporary liberal democracies, which rarely fight one another and are bound together by trade and alliances, and that Kant, were he to observe the present, would therefore regard the current order as a vindication of his hopes.

I am less convinced, and our argument turned on a specific hypothetical that blends old instruments with new ones. Consider a bloc of constitutional democracies that maintain peace among themselves but preserve massive peacetime armies, fund overseas operations through borrowing, regularly intervene covertly in the politics of authoritarian regimes through cyber sabotage of infrastructure and targeted strikes by remotely operated weapons, and increasingly delegate both fighting and logistics to private firms rather than to citizen forces. My friend insists Kant would applaud this as a pragmatic step toward perpetual peace, since war among the federated members has disappeared and commerce flourishes. I suspect he would instead see it as a corruption of the ideal, perpetuating precisely the maxims that keep humanity in a state of war, only displaced outward and rendered less visible by technology and outsourcing. Can you adjudicate this dispute by reconstructing, in some detail, how Kant would likely assess such an arrangement in light of his underlying moral and political reasoning, and explain which of our interpretations is closer to his position?
