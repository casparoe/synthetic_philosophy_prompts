Please undertake a disciplined translation exercise concerning Otto Neurath's conception of a picture language for public communication.

For the purposes of this exercise, take the position to be translated as defined by the following four commitments, and work only from this stipulation rather than from further biographical or textual detail. First, pictorial signs can communicate quantitative social facts to non-specialist audiences without requiring advanced literacy or technical training, and thereby support democratic deliberation. Second, the intelligibility of such signs derives not from natural resemblance but from learned conventions, systematic combination of repeatable elements, and repeated use in context. Third, standardization is compatible with adaptability: a shared core repertoire can be localized for distinct cultural settings and purposes while preserving comparability across cases. Fourth, such a language is to be assessed as an instrument for social orientation that selects, simplifies, and makes patterns memorable for action, rather than as a complete or metaphysically neutral representation of reality.

Assume the addressee is a senior practitioner of public information design and data visualization, fully conversant with visual encoding channels, pre-attentive processing, design systems, accessibility and localization requirements, and usability evaluation, but with no training in philosophy of science or interwar social theory. Render each of the four commitments in that professional vocabulary as faithfully as possible, preserving its intended force.

Conclude with a critical account of the limits of the exercise. Identify which elements were rendered without loss, which could be rendered only by altering their meaning, and which resist formulation in the target vocabulary altogether. For each point of resistance, offer a considered judgment whether the difficulty reflects a limitation of the design vocabulary or an unclarity in the original commitment, with reasons. The translation should not import substantive assumptions of contemporary design theory and present them as equivalent to Neurath's claims.
