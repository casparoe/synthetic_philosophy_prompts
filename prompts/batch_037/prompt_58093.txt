I am a high school junior working on an extended essay for my social studies class on fairness in how well-being is distributed, and I would appreciate your help in clarifying my own judgments.

As I understand it, one view holds that what matters is giving extra weight to improving the lives of those who are worse off, but that inequality itself is not bad when no one is harmed. A different concern is that equality might matter in itself, so that making everyone equal even by making some people worse off without helping anyone could be an improvement. I find the second idea unappealing in the abstract, but some of my reactions to particular cases seem to point in different directions.

I have been testing myself with simple comparisons in which numbers stand for how well life is going overall, with higher numbers meaning a better life, and I would like to know what principle, if any, makes sense of my reactions. In a first comparison between a world where Maya and Leo are both at level 5 and a world where Maya remains at 5 while Leo rises to 9, I judge the second world better. Inequality alone does not seem to make a situation worse if no one loses. In a second comparison between a world where both are at 8 and a world where one falls to 7 while the other rises to 20, I lean toward judging the second world better, since the gain seems vastly larger than the small loss, even though the loss falls on the person who ends up worse off. In a third comparison with the same structure but at a much lower level, between a world where both are at 2, struggling to meet basic needs, and a world where one falls to 1 while the other rises to 20, I judge the first world better. When people are doing very badly, I do not want to accept a further loss for the worse off even for a very large gain elsewhere. In a fourth comparison between a world where the two are at 10 and 11 and a world where they are at 10 and 100, I judge the second world better, but only slightly better. Once someone is already doing well, additional gains for that person seem to matter less and less.

These four judgments seem to fit the idea that benefits matter more the worse off the recipient is. Yet there is a fifth case, drawn from my school, that troubles me. Suppose our school can fund a weekend robotics workshop with limited seats, so that twenty students would learn a great deal while everyone else would be unaffected, neither helped nor harmed. My reaction is that we should not run the workshop unless a way can be found for everyone interested to attend, even if that means no one gets the benefit. On reflection this looks like preferring an equal outcome in which everyone stays lower over an unequal outcome in which some rise and no one falls, which contradicts my verdict in the first comparison.

My tentative principle is that we should give priority to the worse off without treating equality as valuable in itself, but I am not sure it can handle the workshop case or explain why the second and third comparisons come out differently. Could you state precisely the principle or small set of competing principles that best captures these verdicts, explaining which part of the principle is motivated by which of my cases, what each candidate principle would imply for new cases I have not considered, such as whether adding a very happy person to a struggling population improves matters or whether a risk of falling to a very low level should be treated differently from a certain small loss, and which of my verdicts, if any, must be abandoned under each candidate. Please write your answer in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of an email or letter. Please also include an account of what argument or consideration would lead you to favor a different principle than the one you recommend, and where in your reasoning you think you are most likely to go wrong.
