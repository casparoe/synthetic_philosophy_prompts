A general science monthly directed toward educated non specialists publishes several features each year that explain striking biological traits as adaptations for particular functions.

I serve as features editor with responsibility for vetting freelance pitches for such features. Each month I must assess approximately six pitches. The stakes are recurrent and asymmetric: publication of an unsupported adaptive story misleads readers and damages long term credibility, while excessive caution delays coverage of well supported work and strains relations with writers. Members of the screening staff hold undergraduate level biology and can devote about forty minutes to each pitch. Final authority for acceptance, revision, or rejection rests with me, with consultation of an external academic referee only when the procedure calls for escalation.

I request a reusable screening procedure, presented as a flowchart in words, grounded in the Gould-Lewontin critique of adaptationism and its call for pluralist explanation. The procedure should translate that critique into sequential tests suitable for use by non specialists, preserving the distinctions among adaptation in the strict sense, exaptation or coopted function, byproduct or spandrel, developmental and architectural constraint, and drift, while indicating which refinements may be set aside for practical use.

For each step, specify the question to be addressed, the kind of evidence that would count as adequate for a magazine level claim, the conditions that require revision or rejection without further review, and the conditions that require escalation for judgment. Mark explicitly those points where editorial judgment cannot be eliminated and where the procedure is most likely to misfire.

The procedure must be equipped to handle three recurring hard cases: claims about human cognition or behavior where experimental manipulation is limited, claims about visually compelling animal structures where reader appeal creates pressure to publish, and claims where adaptive and non adaptive factors plausibly interact.

Demonstrate the procedure by applying it to the following three stylized submissions, taking the stated evidential claims as given without additional fact checking. First, a pitch asserting that throat fan color in a desert lizard evolved to deter a particular avian predator, supported by a within population correlation and a single clay model experiment, without comparative data, heritability estimates, or fitness measures in the wild, and favored by senior staff as a potential cover story. Second, a pitch asserting that rhythmic humming during concentrated individual work evolved to promote social cohesion in ancestral groups, supported by a cross cultural survey and a laboratory study of mood, without developmental, comparative, or fitness evidence. Third, a pitch asserting that reduction of eyes in a cave fish population is best explained largely by neutral accumulation and developmental tradeoffs rather than direct selection for eyelessness, supported by comparative data from neighboring populations, breeding experiments, and quantitative estimates of metabolic cost.

Include full step by step application in each case, including at least one case where correct application yields a result contrary to editorial or commercial preference. Conclude with a concise statement of what the procedure cannot accomplish.
