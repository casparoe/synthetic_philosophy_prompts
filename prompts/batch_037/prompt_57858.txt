I have been asked to chair a committee at a selective public university that has one semester to revise its merit scholarships and honors admissions criteria. The administration wants the new scheme to be defensibly meritocratic while responding to concerns that current awards track parental income, test preparation, and uneven schooling more than anything candidates themselves control. On the table are several concrete proposals, including setting a threshold of academic preparedness and then allocating some awards by lottery, giving explicit weight to sustained caregiving and community maintenance work alongside entrepreneurial or artistic distinction, and discounting achievements that depend heavily on expensive coaching. In preliminary discussion with colleagues, we keep circling a handful of conceptual tangles, such as whether deserving a reward requires being responsible for the talent or diligence behind it, whether merit only makes sense relative to the purpose of the institution offering the opportunity, and whether a fair selection procedure can redeem outcomes that still reflect brute luck.

Given that our time is limited and our recommendations must be actionable for admissions officers rather than theoretically complete, which two or three conceptual questions or practical implications should we treat as most decisive, and in what order? I am less interested in a survey of positions than in a defended agenda: explain which issues are upstream of the others in the sense that settling them constrains the rest, which are tractable enough for a committee to make progress on in a semester, and which initially striking concerns turn out to be overrated or to not follow from a critique of meritocracy at all. Please make explicit the criteria you are using to rank the issues, the trade-offs between them, and what sort of argument or evidence would lead you to move an issue up or down the list.
