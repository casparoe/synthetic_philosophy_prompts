I have been auditing an interdisciplinary university seminar on language and mind, and last week's session concerned whether the recent success of large language models trained only to predict the next word bears on the classic poverty-of-the-stimulus argument for some form of linguistic innateness. Two doctoral students continued the discussion afterward while I listened in. Lena, who works in cognitive science and leans toward empiricism, and Marcus, who works in theoretical linguistics and leans toward nativism, developed their disagreement over several rounds without either conceding.

First, Lena argued that systems with no built-in language-specific principles can acquire a great deal of English syntax from text alone, which suggests the input available to a learner was never as poor as nativists claimed and that a domain-general learner can suffice. Marcus replied that the comparison ignores scale and developmental plausibility, since such models are exposed to orders of magnitude more words than any five-year-old hears, require enormous computation, still need many more examples than children to master certain generalizations involving questions, anaphora, and structure dependence, and make non-childlike errors, so their data hunger supports rather than undermines the idea that children bring strong prior constraints to the task. Lena countered that this response moves the goalposts, because if innateness now means only that children have helpful general biases for attending to objects, intentions, and statistical regularities, every empiricist already accepts that, whereas the distinctive nativist claim concerned innate specifically linguistic content such as principles or parameters, which the new results suggest are unnecessary. Marcus answered that nativists never made the in-principle claim that no possible learner could extract grammar from unlimited text given unlimited data and compute, but rather an explanatory claim about how actual children succeed under actual conditions involving limited, noisy, largely positive evidence without systematic correction, conditions the models neither face nor illuminate, and he added that even richly structured social and multimodal input still underdetermines the particular generalizations children in fact converge on, which is the real poverty point. Lena closed by pressing the other side of that concession, saying that if the child's effective input does include joint attention, communicative intent, prosody, and interaction in the world, then the stimulus was never poor in the relevant sense and the inference to innate grammar was premature, with the credit instead going to richer input plus general cognition.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move, explaining who is right about which specific point and where each goes wrong, including whether the large-model results refute, support, or are largely orthogonal to the poverty-of-the-stimulus argument once quantity of data, kind of architecture, kind of innateness, and in-principle versus explanatory readings of the argument are distinguished. Conclude with an assessment of who is more right overall and identify anything important that both parties are missing about what would actually settle the dispute.
