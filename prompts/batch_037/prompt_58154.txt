the question I set was this:

Consider a 4-move centipede. P1 moves at nodes 1 and 3, P2 moves at nodes 2 and 4. If P1 Stops at 1, payoffs are 3 to P1, 1 to P2. If we get to 2 and P2 Stops, payoffs are 2 to P1, 4 to P2. If we get to 3 and P1 Stops, payoffs are 5 to P1, 3 to P2. If we get to 4 and P2 Stops, payoffs are 4 to P1, 6 to P2. If P2 Passes at 4, payoffs are 7 to P1, 5 to P2. Assume selfish preferences, full information, and common knowledge of rationality.

a) Walk through what backward induction predicts here and why, step by step.
b) Explain why the result looks paradoxical, both becuase of what P2 should infer if P1 unexpectedly Passes at node 1, and because of how real people actually play games like this.
c) Assess these two replies: (i) Passing early can be rational as a way to signal you are not a defector and thereby induce the other player to Pass back later; (ii) the paradox shows there is something wrong with backward induction reasoning itself when it has to evaluate nodes that should never be reached if everyone were rational.

one student wrote this:

Backward induction predicts P1 Stops at node 1. At node 4, P2 chooses between Stop giving him 6 and Pass giving him 5, so he Stops. So at node 3, P1 chooses between Stop giving him 5 and Pass leading to 4 at node 4, so he Stops. At node 2, P2 chooses between Stop giving him 4 and Pass leading to 3 at node 3, so he Stops. At node 1, P1 chooses between Stop giving him 3 and Pass leading to 2 at node 2, so he Stops. This is just subgame perfect equilibrium found by rollback. The outcome 3,1 is Pareto dominated by continued passing, especially the all-Pass outcome 7,5, which is why it seems self-defeating, and in lab experiments people usually Pass for at least the first couple moves, so theory and behavior diverge.

Reply (i) works even under the stated assumptions. Even with common knowledge that both are rational and selfish and the game ends at node 4, P1 can rationally Pass at node 1 to signal willingness to cooperate, and if P2 reads the signal, both can get to much higher payoffs. So signaling explains cooperation without dropping any assumptions.

For (ii), the reasoning is still valid after a surprise. If P1 Passes at node 1 when theory says he should Stop, P2 should still assume P1 will be rational from now on, so P2 expects P1 to Stop at node 3, which means P2 should Stop at node 2. The deviation is in the past and does not change what is rational going forward.
