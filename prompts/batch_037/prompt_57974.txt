For my upper-level seminar on practical rationality and the philosophy of action, I am designing the final assessment and need an essay question on money pumps and diachronic rationality that rewards genuine analysis rather than rehearsed summaries. After discussing dynamic choice with colleagues last week, I became convinced that students too often recite that intransitive preferences lead to exploitation without understanding whether the irrationality lies in the preferences at a time, in the pattern of choices over time, or in the failure to anticipate one's own future shifts. I want a question that forces them to confront that crux directly.

Please center the entire exercise on the following original vignette I drafted for classroom use, so that no student can answer from memory alone: Nadia, a doctoral student, is allocating her summer between three archival placements in different cities, call them Alpha, Beta, and Gamma. On Monday she ranks Gamma above Beta above Alpha because she values quiet writing time most. She knows with certainty that by Wednesday, after her advisor meeting, she will rank Alpha above Gamma above Beta because she will then value networking most, and she knows this shift is predictable and, by her own lights, entirely reasonable given the different information and salience she will have then. A departmental coordinator, who knows her current and predicted rankings, offers her a sequence of voluntary, small-fee trades across the week: on Monday pay a tiny administrative fee to swap an assigned Alpha slot for Gamma, then on Wednesday pay another tiny fee to swap Gamma for Alpha, leaving her where she started minus two fees. The question for students is whether Nadia is diachronically irrational if she accepts both trades, whether refusing either trade would itself be irrational by her lights at that time, and what difference it makes if she foresees the whole sequence and can adopt a sophisticated strategy of refusing an early advantageous trade, a resolute strategy of sticking to a prior plan, or simply treats her Monday self and Wednesday self as distinct agents.

Act as a professional philosopher specializing in decision theory and the philosophy of temporal agency, and please think carefully step by step, starting with brief brainstorming about the competing diagnoses of the pump and where students are most likely to conflate synchronic consistency with intertemporal coherence before you compose the final materials. I need a single essay question suitable for a 75-minute exam answer, a model answer of about 600 words that works through the Nadia case to show when a money pump reveals irrationality and when it merely reveals predictable preference change plus naivete about it, and a marking scheme out of 25 marks that sharply separates A-range from B-range work by naming the specific analytical moves a top answer must make and the specific errors that must lose marks, such as treating any willingness to pay for a preferred option as a pump, ignoring the role of foresight and backward induction, or assuming without argument that rationality requires preferences to remain stable over time.
