I am taking an undergraduate seminar in bioethics where we have been discussing the distinction between doing and allowing, and more narrowly between killing and letting die, and a pattern at our affiliated teaching hospital has left my friends and me puzzled. The hospital forbids physicians from administering a lethal injection even at the informed request of a competent terminally ill patient, yet it permits them, with similar consent, to withdraw ventilator support knowing the patient will die within minutes, and it also permits them to escalate analgesia to control severe pain even when they foresee that respiratory depression will hasten death, so long as the recorded aim is pain relief. Nurses on the unit report that withdrawing the ventilator feels morally weightier and more like an act than initially declining to start it, even though the official ethics guidance says withholding and withdrawing are equivalent. The puzzle has sharpened because the hospital is piloting a default-expiry ventilator order under which support automatically tapers unless a physician actively reauthorizes it each morning, so that continuing treatment now requires a daily intervention and discontinuation can happen through deliberately letting the order lapse.

What view of action, causation, and responsibility does this combined pattern presuppose, if any? Does it presuppose that killing versus letting die marks a fundamental moral boundary, or a broader doing versus allowing boundary, or something about intending death versus merely foreseeing it, and do the three rules plus the official equivalence claim and the bedside reluctance and the new expiry default all presuppose the same thing? Please reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with, identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined or internally inconsistent, and explain what would have to change in the rules or in the design of the default if the hospital instead adopted the rival view that only outcomes, consent, and intentions matter and how an act is causally produced does not. Please give a committed answer that takes a definite position on which interpretation is best and defends it with reasons, rather than merely surveying possible views and leaving me to choose.
