I would like help bringing my judgments about distributive justice into reflective equilibrium, using the Rawlsian ideas of the veil of ignorance and the difference principle as a starting point.

As a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, I naturally model the veil as a decision problem under uncertainty: suppose I must choose a basic structure for society without knowing which position I will occupy, with some credence over being anyone, and with a utility function that exhibits ordinary risk aversion. I am drawn to something like maximin or strong priority for the worst off, but I am unsure whether that attraction survives when I make the stakes and probabilities explicit. The following are my current verdicts, which I hold with varying confidence.

1. Incentivized inequality. Consider Equal, where everyone is at roughly 50 units of income and associated primary goods, and Incentive, where the worst off are at 55, the median at 80, and the top at 500, with the spread arising because differential rewards induce training, effort, and entrepreneurship that raise productivity. I judge Incentive to be just, or at least more just than Equal, and I would choose it from behind the veil. Mere equality does not seem to have independent weight when everyone can be made better off in absolute terms.

2. Harmless top-end inequality. Consider Modest, where the worst off are at 55 and the top at 80, and Stratospheric, where the worst off are also at 55 but the top are at 5,000 because a small group develops goods that circulate mainly among themselves, such as a lucrative market in positional art or a very expensive life-extension treatment that cannot yet be generalized. No one in Stratospheric is harmed relative to Modest and the worst-off position is stipulated to be identical. Strictly applied, a material version of the difference principle should be indifferent, yet I find Stratospheric less just, or at least I would pay some cost behind the veil to live in Modest rather than Stratospheric. This sits uneasily with my verdict in 1.

3. Material gain versus social standing. Consider Hierarchy, where the worst-off index is 60 but work life involves pervasive deference, arbitrary managerial authority with little exit, and merely formal political voice, and Standing, where the worst-off index is 58 but workers have robust protections, real exit options, and effective political agency. I judge Standing to be more just and would choose it behind the veil despite the lower material index. Material primary goods alone therefore do not seem to define the relevant worst-off position, but I do not know how to expand the index without making it vacuous.

4. Catastrophic gamble. Consider Secure, which guarantees everyone between 55 and 65 with near certainty, and Gamble, which offers a 99 percent chance of 100 and a 1 percent chance of 5, a life of severe deprivation and forced labor, with much higher expected value. I would choose Secure behind the veil and I regard it as unjust to impose Gamble, even though an expected-utility maximizer with mild risk aversion would take Gamble. This suggests something like maximin, or at least extreme risk aversion for very bad outcomes.

5. Dispersed health risk. Consider Precaution, which keeps everyone at 60 by devoting enormous resources to eliminating a rare painful congenital condition affecting 1 percent of births, and Tolerance, which accepts the condition while providing good care and accommodation, so that 99 percent are at 90 and the affected 1 percent are at 30 with significant but not catastrophic suffering. I find Tolerance permissible and I would choose it behind the veil, even though it knowingly creates a 1 percent risk of a bad outcome in exchange for expected gains. I cannot see how to reconcile this with my verdict in 4 except by appealing to a threshold, to a distinction between kinds of badness, or to much more complex risk attitudes than I can state.

My tentative principle is that justice requires maximizing expected utility behind the veil with strong but not lexical priority against falling below a sufficiency threshold, where the currency includes both material goods and relational standing. I suspect this is either incoherent or ad hoc.

Can you formulate one principle, or at most two competing precisifications, that best captures these five verdicts, state each clause precisely and identify which case motivates it, explain what the principle predicts for new cases I have not considered such as a policy that slightly lowers the lifetime prospects of the present worst off to preserve resources for future worst-off generations and a case where the worst off report high satisfaction due to adaptation to deprivation, and give an honest assessment of which of my verdicts cannot be jointly accommodated without unacceptable complexity and therefore should be revised?
