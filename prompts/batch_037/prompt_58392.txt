I am developing a lecture on the philosophy of historical explanation and would like a philosophically serious reading of Isaac Asimov's Foundation premise through the lens of quantitative macrohistory centered on secular cycles theory.

For purposes of the reading, take the familiar premise as given: a galactic empire in decline, a mathematician who claims a statistical science of large populations can forecast the fall and shorten the ensuing interregnum, a plan that works only so long as the predicted population remains ignorant of the details and no single individual exerts outsized influence, and the later disruption of that plan by an anomalous individual with exceptional powers. Take as the philosophical counterpart a structural-demographic account on which agrarian and modern polities tend to move through long integrative and disintegrative phases driven by the interaction of popular well-being, elite numbers and intra-elite competition, state fiscal health and legitimacy, and levels of sociopolitical instability. On this account, regularities such as elite overproduction, popular immiseration, fiscal crisis, and heightened internal conflict make certain periods systematically more fragile than others, but prediction is conditional, probabilistic, and model-dependent rather than lawlike in the manner of celestial mechanics.

I would like an analysis that takes both the fiction and the theory of history seriously and that is usable without consulting either text or datasets. Please address three pressure points in detail. First, whether the fictional distinction between predictable aggregates and unpredictable individuals clarifies or caricatures the macrohistorical claim about scale, aggregation, and contingency. Second, how a proponent of secular cycles would diagnose the Seldon Crises: as genuine forecasts, as self-fulfilling interventions that succeed because they coordinate expectations, or as retrodictive accommodations dressed as predictions. Third, what the disruption by the anomalous individual reveals about reflexivity, endogeneity, and the problem of agency for any science that claims to predict its own knowers.

The reading should be faithful in both directions. Show where secular cycles theory illuminates choices, institutions, and turning points in the narrative, and where the narrative resists that theory and an honest critic must say so, particularly regarding physics envy, measurability, the role of knowledge of the prediction itself, and the difference between a closed fictional system and an open historical one. Conclude by stating what, if anything, the fiction contributes to the philosophical debate about whether history can be quantified and predicted that abstract argument alone does not make vivid. Base the discussion on widely known plot elements rendered in paraphrase, without verbatim quotation and without appeal to external sources.
