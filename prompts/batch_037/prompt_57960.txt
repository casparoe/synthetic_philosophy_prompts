I am preparing a short position statement for a postgraduate seminar on moral reasoning and would welcome a check on whether my commitments hang together. After discussion with friends about everyday duties around our shared allotments, I have settled on the following package: (1) holism about reasons, in that features such as causing pleasure or using another person's tools without consent can count in favour of an act in one context, against it in another, or be morally inert in a third; (2) a claim about deceit, namely that deceit always counts against an act even when it is outweighed, and never counts in its favour nor is ever neutral; (3) explanatory generalism, in that whenever an act is right it is right because it falls under a true general principle that explains its rightness, however complex that principle might be; (4) verdicts on two cases, where borrowing a neighbour's strimmer without asking to clear a blocked communal drain during a flood is permissible, whereas borrowing the same strimmer without asking to tidy one's own patch before an inspection is wrong, with the difference turning only on how the same consideration of avoiding inconvenience behaves in different contexts and with no deeper principle needed to account for it; (5) a view of competence, in that becoming a good judge is more like acquiring a perceptual skill through supervised practice than like memorising a code, so that fully competent judges will often be unable to state without loss the principles that explain their own correct verdicts. I do not see where the pressure points might be and I have no prior view about what I would abandon first.

Please audit the set for joint tenability, distinguishing genuine tensions from merely apparent ones and identifying the cheapest revisions and what each would cost. Please present your answer as exactly five numbered points, each of one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion, ordered from the most severe difficulty to the least severe.
