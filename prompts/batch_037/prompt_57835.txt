I am a novelist currently drafting a work centered on a gallerist, and in developing the character I have been studying the aesthetic judgments of a close friend, Mara, who is a painter and occasional curator. I would be grateful for a careful analysis of the principle underlying her verdicts and its application to a new project of my own.

Mara is firm in the following verdicts on whether something counts as art:

1. A mass-produced ceramic mug with a pleasing glaze, made to be used in kitchens and sold only in a museum shop, is not art, however attractive it may be.

2. That same type of mug, when chosen, signed, and placed on a plinth by an established conceptual artist as part of an exhibition on domestic labor, is art.

3. A lively and inventive doodle made by a child for her own amusement, never shown to anyone and thrown away the same day, is not art.

4. Skillfully built, historically accurate pieces of furniture constructed by carpenters to appear briefly and out of focus in the background of a film are not art, despite the craft involved.

5. A typed card in a gallery instructing visitors to close their eyes and recall a winter evening from childhood in as much sensory detail as possible, with no object made or displayed beyond the card, is art.

6. An image-model output produced from a brief prompt for a pleasant landscape, printed without comparison, selection, or adjustment and hung to cover a bare wall, is not art.

My new manuscript, provisionally called Platform Hours, consists of nearly three hundred pages of transcribed conversations overheard on commuter trains, edited only for punctuation and the removal of names. I intend readers to linger over the cadence, humor, and pathos of the speech, and I plan to submit it to a trade publisher as a novel. I do not, however, intend it as an intervention in or commentary upon literary history; I present it simply as a document of city life that repays slow reading.

Would Mara judge Platform Hours to be art?

Please act as a professional philosopher specializing in aesthetics and the definition of art. Think carefully and methodically, beginning with brainstorming several candidate principles before settling on an answer. I am less interested in a simple yes or no than in the rule Mara seems to be applying, the other rules that fit her six verdicts just as well, what each of those rules predicts about Platform Hours, your best guess about her likely verdict with reasons for preferring one interpretation, and a description of one further hypothetical case that would best distinguish between the leading candidate rules.
