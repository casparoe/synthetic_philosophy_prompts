I seek a principled account of what matters in survival that can accommodate the following judgments about Parfitian teleportation and neighboring scenarios, or else show which judgments must be revised.

Consider six scenarios involving full-brain scanning, with perfect preservation of memories, character traits, intentions, and recognitional capacities unless otherwise noted.

First, one-to-one replacement: my body is scanned and immediately destroyed, and a psychologically continuous replica awakens elsewhere within minutes. I judge that I survive this, and that it would be rational to anticipate the replica's experiences beforehand.

Second, non-destructive duplication: the same scan is taken but my original body is left intact and conscious, while the replica awakens elsewhere. I judge that I remain the original, that the replica is a distinct person, and that I do not survive as the replica.

Third, one-to-two branching with destruction: my body is scanned and destroyed, and two psychologically continuous replicas awaken in different locations and then begin to diverge. I am strongly inclined to deny that I survive as both, yet I am also reluctant to say that I survive as one rather than the other, and reluctant to say that I survive as neither when each replica taken alone would have counted as survival under the first scenario.

Fourth, gradual in-place replacement: over several months, while I remain conscious and psychologically continuous throughout, my neurons are progressively replaced by functionally identical artificial components, with no scanning, transmission, or external replica. I judge that I survive this, and indeed more securely than in the first scenario.

Fifth, archival delay: my psychology is scanned at age thirty and stored; my original body then lives normally for twenty years, forming new relationships and projects, before dying; only then is a replica constructed from the thirty-year-old scan. I judge that this replica is not me in the sense relevant to survival or anticipation, or at least is a substantially worse candidate for survival than the replica in the first scenario, despite originating from an equally accurate scan.

Sixth, edited replica: my body is scanned and destroyed as in the first scenario, but before activation the replica's central autobiographical memories and long-standing commitments are deliberately removed or replaced, while general skills, language, and temperament are preserved. I judge that survival here is at best diminished or indeterminate, though I am unsure whether this is because identity requires those particular contents or because something else has gone wrong.

Is there a single principle concerning psychological continuity, causal continuity, branching, temporal gap, or degree of connectedness that best systematizes these verdicts? Please state the candidate principle or principles precisely, explain which feature of each scenario does the explanatory work, and identify where my verdicts cannot all be jointly satisfied without ad hoc qualification. If a simpler principle requires abandoning or revising one of my verdicts, specify which verdict should go and why. Finally, state what the recommended principle predicts for a further case I have not listed: fusion, in which two distinct living persons are simultaneously scanned and destroyed, and a single replica awakens with substantial and mutually coherent memories inherited from both.
