I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am preparing a feature on Everettian quantum mechanics and the status of probability within it. Over recent months I have interviewed physicists and philosophers who agree on the formalism — unitary evolution without collapse, decoherence selecting quasi-classical branches, all outcomes realised — yet who remain deadlocked over why observed frequencies match squared amplitudes.

The deadlock takes a familiar form. One camp maintains that probability can be recovered from constraints on rational betting behaviour for an agent who expects to branch, such that any coherent set of preferences must track branch weights. A second camp contends that such arguments presuppose the very notion they claim to derive, by building weight-sensitivity into the conception of rationality or by treating branch weights as already probability-like. A third camp holds that talk of uncertainty is misplaced altogether, since nothing is uncertain when every outcome occurs, and that what needs justification is instead why a pre-branch agent should care about successors in proportion to their weights.

To make this concrete for readers I have been using a toy scenario and would like you to reason through it rather than treat it as settled by authority. Suppose a laboratory procedure reliably creates three subjectively indistinguishable successors for a single observer, two of whom register spin up and one of whom registers spin down, while the total quantum weight is divided roughly 90 per cent on the lone down successor and 10 per cent shared by the pair of up successors. Branch-counting intuition favours up by two to one, weight-tracking intuition favours down by nine to one. I want to know which intuition each camp is committed to here and why, and what that reveals about their disagreement.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not at bottom a dispute about physics but about whether probability is something to be derived from rationality plus the wavefunction or a primitive that any physical theory must simply help itself to, with the mathematics of decoherence doing little real work. Test that hypothesis against what each side actually says.

Give me a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey: is this primarily a verbal dispute about the meaning of probability, a substantive dispute about principles of rationality, a hidden physical dispute about how to individuate and count branches, or a case of rival camps answering different questions — one about betting, another about confirmation by observed frequencies, another about how to care about future selves — under a shared label. Then tell me, given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a conceptual distinction, a formal result, a new kind of experiment or observation, a reformulation of the question — or argue that no progress should be expected and why.
