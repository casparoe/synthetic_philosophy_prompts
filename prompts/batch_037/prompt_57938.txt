I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for close to a decade, and one dispute I have watched cycle without any visible movement concerns what intelligence is.

As I see it there are roughly three camps that keep talking past one another. The first treats intelligence as whatever explains individual differences in cognitive test performance in humans, centered on general cognitive ability and its predictive validity for schooling, job performance, and life outcomes. The second, common among machine learning practitioners, treats intelligence as breadth of task competence per unit of training data and compute, with emphasis on few-shot generalization to novel tasks. The third, most familiar from the rationalist forums themselves, treats intelligence as efficient cross-domain optimization: the ability of an agent to steer disparate environments toward its goals, even when the tasks are not pre-specified.

Each camp can absorb the others apparent counterexamples. The first says a system that masters many benchmarks but needs orders of magnitude more experience than a child is doing something different from what varies among humans. The second says a construct validated on human test variance tells us little about crows, octopuses, or artificial systems. The third says both of the others mistake measurement proxies for the underlying capacity to achieve goals under uncertainty.

My working hypothesis is that this is not primarily a factual disagreement about brains or machines, but a disagreement about which cases get to count as data for a theory of intelligence, combined with different explanatory aims being pursued under a single term. To test this on myself I constructed three imaginary systems and found my own intuitions unstable:

A warehouse-routing optimizer that is superhuman within logistics, invents novel routing strategies no engineer anticipated, but cannot do anything outside that domain and has no way to acquire new goals.

A generalist predictive system trained on an enormous corpus that can answer questions, write code, and summarize papers across many fields, but only because it has effectively memorized large parts of the solution space and degrades sharply when the distribution shifts.

A socially adept human of strictly average test scores who is unusually good at noticing which problems are worth solving, reframing ill-posed situations, and enlisting other people and tools to solve them.

Depending on which camp I am channeling, a different system looks most intelligent, and I cannot find a neutral fact that settles it.

I do not want a verdict on which definition is correct or a survey of theories of intelligence. I want a diagnosis of the structure of this impasse: is it largely verbal, is it a substantive disagreement disguised by a shared word, are the sides answering different questions about prediction, engineering, and agency, or is there a hidden empirical claim doing the real work? Ground the diagnosis in what proponents of each view actually appeal to, and then tell me, given that diagnosis, what would count as progress. Would it be a conceptual distinction, a new measurement paradigm, a formal result, an experiment, or a frank splitting of the concept, and why should we expect that to move people who have not moved so far?
