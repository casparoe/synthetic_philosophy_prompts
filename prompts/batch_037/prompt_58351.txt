I drive a delivery van for a living and never took a college philosophy or economics class, but I got stuck on a puzzle a friend from our weekend card night brought up. He described a coin-flip game where someone flips a fair coin until the first heads appears, and if that happens on the first flip you get two dollars, on the second flip four dollars, on the third eight dollars, doubling each time, and he claimed the mathematics says the average winnings work out to be unlimited, so a purely logical person should be willing to pay almost anything to play once. That struck me as wrong, since none of us would pay more than about twenty dollars, and my own hunch is that the puzzle disappears once you think about who is offering the game in real life: no friend, casino, or state lottery actually has unlimited money to pay out if the coin keeps landing tails for thirty or forty flips, and a sensible player discounts those giant but practically uncollectable jackpots and also counts the wasted evening waiting through a freak long run.

Please argue for the position that refusing to pay more than a modest amount for one play is fully rational without needing the textbook story that extra dollars automatically become less satisfying as you get richer. I want you to defend the claim that finite bankrolls, the risk the payer defaults or caps the prize, and the time and opportunity cost of playing do all the real work in explaining our reluctance, and that once those are built in there is no remaining mystery about infinite average winnings. Please take a definite stand and give reasons for it rather than laying out several competing theories and leaving me to choose, and please write your answer in American English.
