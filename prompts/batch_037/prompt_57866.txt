For the past three years, I have been writing a debut novel centered on my maternal grandfather, a loom mechanic and shop steward who helped lead a textile-mill walkout in our town in the early 1970s and died in 1989. Much of the public record is thin: newspaper clippings confirm the dates of the strike and the settlement he signed, but almost nothing about his motives or private deliberations.

To make the pressures on him vivid, I invented a pivotal chapter in which he quietly accepts a modest payoff from management to bring the strike to an early close, then spends the following decade concealing his guilt from his coworkers. My editor finds this chapter the moral core of the book, and my publisher hopes to market the novel under his real first and last name as based on a true story.

My mother and uncle, both of whom knew him well, insist he was stubbornly incorruptible and have asked me to cut the payoff episode or at least to rename the character and present the book as fully invented. I am reluctant to lose what the invention accomplishes, yet I am uneasy about attaching a disgraceful falsehood to a real man who cannot answer back, even with an author note stating that some events have been imagined.

How should I think about what I owe him and his surviving family against what I owe the story? Would a disclaimer or a name change resolve the concern, or does the problem remain so long as neighbors will recognize him? Please answer in American English and in no more than 300 words.
