Develop a runnable 75-minute exercise for an introductory university seminar in archaeological methods with about 20 students, aimed at making tangible the gap between durable objects and the past beliefs and institutions inferred from them.

Base the exercise on this invented case from a fictional coastal settlement: a room with twelve intact elaborately decorated vessels buried upright beneath the floor, a nearby midden containing hundreds of plain sherds from vessels of the same forms, and a large open enclosure defined by postholes but containing no hearths or storage pits.

Have small groups work from different incomplete evidence packets that each support an internally coherent but mutually incompatible reconstruction, require them to commit to an interpretation through a concrete decision before the full assemblage is revealed, and withhold theoretical terminology until after that point. Provide step-by-step instructions using only minimal low-cost materials and a debrief outline that connects each stage of what happened to underdetermination and equifinality in archaeological interpretation.

Also identify one predictable facilitation difficulty with a practical remedy, and acknowledge one central feature of real archaeological inference that the exercise necessarily simplifies and that the facilitator must state explicitly.
