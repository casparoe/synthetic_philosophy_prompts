During a long rail-replacement bus ride back from a university open day, I sat across from two doctoral students in environmental planning who were continuing a seminar discussion about rationality and preferences. To keep the issue concrete they kept returning to a hypothetical involving three community garden plots: Plot A has excellent soil but is far away, Plot B is very close but has poor soil, and Plot C is intermediate on both dimensions but has unusually reliable water access. They imagined a gardener who sincerely prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A when each pair is considered on its own, and they disagreed about whether that cyclic pattern alone warrants calling her irrational, even if she never actually trades plots or loses anything in practice.

Their exchange went roughly like this: (1) Lena argued that the cycle is irrational because it exposes the gardener to a money pump, since starting from C she would voluntarily pay a small fee to swap to B, then another fee to swap to A, then another to swap back to C, ending with her original plot but less money, and that this vulnerability reveals a structural defect in her preferences rather than mere bad luck. (2) Tomas replied that being exploitable in principle is not the same as being irrational, because a gardener who sees the whole sequence coming would simply decline one of the swaps, so the pump only works by assuming she chooses myopically at each step without memory or foresight, which means the argument smuggles in a controversial theory of choice instead of deriving irrationality from preferences alone. (3) Lena countered that the refusal defense does not really rescue the cyclic preferences, since to avoid the pump the gardener must either act against her own avowed pairwise preference at some step or adopt a blanket policy of refusing small-fee trades that would also block many genuinely beneficial exchanges, whereas a person with transitive preferences would need no such ad hoc self-binding maneuver. (4) Tomas answered that this still lets pragmatic loss do all the justificatory work, and that in ordinary life no ingenious trader follows the gardener around offering precisely those swaps; if she will never in fact encounter the sequence, or would learn after one loop and stop trading, it seems odd to condemn her present preferences as irrational on the basis of what would happen under distant hypothetical conditions involving repeated trickery.

Please adjudicate their disagreement move by move as a neutral referee. For each of the four numbered moves, explain to what extent it succeeds, where its reasoning goes astray, and how the other side could best answer it. Then give an overall verdict about whether vulnerability to a money pump of this kind shows that intransitive preferences violate a genuine requirement of rationality, and identify any crucial distinction or consideration that both Lena and Tomas have missed, such as the difference between evaluating preferences themselves and evaluating dispositions for making sequential choices, or between actual and merely hypothetical exploitability.
