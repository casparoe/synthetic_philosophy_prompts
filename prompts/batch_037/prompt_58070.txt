I am a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am preparing a feature on when it is reasonable for non-specialists to trust the instruments they use every day.

To keep the philosophy concrete for readers, I want to center the piece on a single invented field case. Maya is an agricultural extension officer who carries a handheld nitrate probe to advise farmers on fertilizer. Over one season she takes about two hundred readings. On each occasion she notes that the probe displays a value and that its own sample-quality indicator says the sample was good, and she treats each such occasion as a positive instance suggesting the probe read correctly that time. From the accumulated list of positive instances she concludes that the probe is reliable, and she has never checked the probe against an independent laboratory assay. Twice she also compares notes with a colleague who uses another probe of the same make and model; the two devices agree, and she takes that agreement as further support, although neither device has been independently calibrated.

My question concerns the epistemological moral often drawn from such cases under the headings of bootstrapping and easy knowledge: that a subject cannot come to know or justifiably believe that a source is reliable by relying on that very source, without independent check, because that would make knowledge of reliability too easy.

Please give me your credence, as a number between 0 and 1, that Maya-style track-record reasoning of this kind is in principle capable of yielding justified belief or knowledge that the instrument is reliable. I am less interested in the bare number than in how you reach it, so please decompose your reasoning. You may find it helpful to separate, in your own terms, subquestions such as whether perceptual or instrumental entitlement requires antecedent warrant for reliability, what philosophers sometimes call the liberal versus conservative contrast, explained here as whether one needs a prior reason to trust a source before its outputs can justify anything; whether warrant can transmit from particular readings to a general reliability claim; and whether agreement between two uncalibrated devices of the same type adds anything beyond single-source bootstrapping.

Please structure your answer in exactly the following sections, in this order, keeping roughly to the suggested lengths:

Summary Restatement, about 100 words: restate the proposition you are assigning a credence to and clarify any ambiguities you need to resolve to make it truth-evaluable.

Decomposition, about 300 to 400 words: lay out three to five subquestions on which your final credence depends, explaining in one or two sentences for each why it matters.

Credences and Combination, about 200 to 250 words: assign a rough probability to each subquestion, explain briefly how they combine into your overall credence, and state that overall number clearly.

Crux and What Would Move It, about 150 words: identify which subquestion your overall number is most sensitive to and describe the argument or evidence, empirical or philosophical, that would most move you up or down.

I need a reading that my editors and scientifically literate but non-philosophical readers can follow without consulting the literature, so please explain any technical distinction you use and do not provide a bibliography or reading list.
