Please help me design a diagnostic decision tree for an interdisciplinary undergraduate seminar on virtual worlds that I am convening next term. The students will have spent two weeks collaborating and socializing in a persistent social VR environment as coursework, but they will have no prior training in metaphysics or moral philosophy.

I want an instrument that sorts participants by their actual judgments about cases rather than by attraction to labels. The tree should contain no more than six yes-or-no branch questions and lead to five or six leaves. Each branch question must be phrased in plain, non-technical language that a student can answer without knowing which view it favors, and at least three of the questions must turn on my stipulated cases rather than on abstract principles.

The stipulated cases are as follows. The Longhouse: a student group spends eight weeks building a communal meeting hall in-world, with screenshots but no grade or money at stake, and a server rollback deletes it overnight. The Lent Axe: in a world with no real-money trading, one student lends another a difficult-to-craft climbing axe for a joint expedition and the borrower then refuses to return it, claiming it is just pixels. The Cornering: one avatar repeatedly corners another avatar in a narrow passage, uses proximity voice to shout, and triggers unwanted haptic buzzing, with no contact or loss outside the headset.

The leaves must include the following four outlooks, described here in my own terms for classroom use: the view that virtual items are genuine computational objects that can be owned, damaged, and stolen, and that harms to them or to avatars can be direct harms to persons; the view that virtual items are real only insofar as a community sustains practices of valuing and exchanging them, so that wrongs count primarily through effects on lives and relationships outside the simulation; the view that talk of virtual objects is a useful pretense for coordinating imagination, so that apparent in-world wrongs matter mainly for what they show about the agent's character or dispositions; and the skeptical view that nothing in-world qualifies as property or injury without accompanying physical, financial, or reputational loss outside. You may add one or two further leaves if the logic requires it, including an explicitly mixed or unnamed option.

Please construct the tree from the logical space first and the labels second: lay out the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the above outlooks within that space. Briefly diagnose any unoccupied combinations as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and explicitly flag any pair of outlooks your questions cannot reliably separate rather than papering over the overlap.

Close with a brief reflexive note identifying the branch or distinction you are least confident in and describing what sort of counterexample, pattern of student responses, or philosophical consideration would lead you to redraw the tree or relocate a leaf, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
