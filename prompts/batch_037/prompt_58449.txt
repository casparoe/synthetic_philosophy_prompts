I am a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general audience magazine, and I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks at a two-day university symposium on time, jointly hosted by the physics and philosophy departments.

The audience will be mixed. Roughly one third will be physicists and philosophers of physics who know the technical literature, while the remaining two thirds will be members of the public, including teachers and older secondary school pupils, who are curious about time travel but have no technical background. The organizers want me to act as a bridge between the two groups and to set a serious but welcoming tone for the weekend.

Please draft a speech of about eight minutes to be delivered in my voice that accomplishes three things. First, it should introduce, in plain but careful language, the central idea I want the weekend to keep returning to: Gödel's discovery that Einstein's theory permits a universe in which all matter rotates in a way that progressively tilts the paths available to light and matter, so that a sufficiently long journey that always proceeds locally toward the future can in principle return to an earlier point in its own history, with the consequence that such a universe cannot be divided into a single worldwide stack of past, present, and future moments. Second, it should use that possibility to explain why both physicists and philosophers are needed in the room, by distinguishing three different questions that public discussion tends to run together, namely whether the equations allow such looping paths, whether that means the past in some sense remains there to be visited, and whether creatures with our kind of memory and agency could make use of such a path. Third, it should establish a norm for the weekend that rules out two easy dismissals without condescending to either side: the physicists may not treat questions about the reality of passage and the now as mere wordplay, and the non-specialists and philosophers may not treat the mathematics of allowed paths as irrelevant to what time is.

The speech should include one concrete everyday analogy that makes the tilting of available paths intelligible to a non-specialist without misleading the specialists, and it should close by giving the audience a reason to stay with the discomfort that our lived sense of time flowing forward may not correspond to anything global in physics, presented as an invitation to inquiry rather than as a counsel of despair.
