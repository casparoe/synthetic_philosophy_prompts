Consider a large public hospital in a religiously and linguistically diverse city that has adopted the following internal policy for staff conduct and patient care:

First, clinical staff who are visible to patients must refrain while on duty from wearing visible religious or political symbols or garments. Second, the hospital kitchen will provide meals that satisfy major religious dietary requirements whenever a patient requests them. Third, patients may not choose physicians on the basis of religion or sex, with the single exception that a request for a same-sex physician for an intimate examination will be accommodated when staffing permits. Fourth, the hospital follows the national calendar of public holidays, and staff who observe minority religious holidays must use ordinary leave to take time off, subject to the same staffing constraints as any other leave request. Fifth, professional interpreters are provided at no charge to patients who need them, but staff are expected to use the majority language when speaking with one another in shared clinical areas.

The administration presents this package as treating all patients and staff equally while remaining neutral among religions and cultures. Some critics argue that it embodies a French-style secularist idea of neutrality as the absence of religion from public roles, while others argue that its accommodations reflect an Anglo-American multiculturalist idea of neutrality as evenhanded support for diverse identities. Both sides claim that parts of the policy contradict the rationale of other parts.

What conceptions of state neutrality, equality, and religious or cultural identity would have to be assumed for this combination of rules to form a coherent whole? Explain whether the staff dress rule and the dietary and language provisions rely on the same idea of neutrality or on different and potentially incompatible ideas, whether the distinction the policy draws between public roles and private needs can sustain that difference, and which rival understandings of secularism, integration, and accommodation are ruled out by the policy as stated. Distinguish between philosophical commitments the policy strictly requires in order to be justified and broader views with which it is merely compatible, identify points at which the policy is philosophically underdetermined or internally strained, and describe what would have to be revised to bring the policy fully into line with a strict secularist model on the one hand or with a thoroughgoing accommodation model on the other.

Please provide a detailed and systematically organized answer in American English.
