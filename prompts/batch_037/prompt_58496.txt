I have been invited to deliver the keynote address at my city's eightieth anniversary commemoration of the wartime deportations that passed through our railway yard in 1943-44, and I would be grateful for a draft that handles a difficult conceptual task with care.

The occasion is solemn and civic rather than academic or religious: some two hundred attendees in the restored station hall, including historians from the local university, descendants of deported families, descendants of municipal railway workers, secondary-school students attending with their teachers, and members of a veterans' association. The city council remains divided over the wording for a new memorial plaque, with some urging a declaration that the city acknowledges its guilt and others insisting that present residents, most born decades later, can bear no guilt for acts they did not commit. As the speaker, a mayor in her late thirties with no personal connection to the events, I must neither endorse blanket exoneration nor perform blanket self-indictment.

I would like the address, of about eight to ten minutes when spoken, to be structured by the familiar postwar distinction associated with Karl Jaspers between four different things that are often run together under the single word guilt: first, criminal liability adjudicated by courts for specific persons and deeds; second, political responsibility borne by citizens for the actions of their polity and for the continuing burdens and benefits those actions create; third, moral answerability of the individual conscience for what one has oneself willed or failed to resist; and fourth, a broader human solidarity in the face of wrongdoing that implicates witnesses and successors without making them perpetrators. The speech should move deliberately through these levels to clarify what a present-day civic we can and cannot truthfully claim: what cannot be inherited, what does persist across generations, and what must now be undertaken.

The challenge is to make those distinctions do the structural work of the address without lecturing and without jargon. Specialists in the audience should recognize that the distinctions are being observed, while students and non-specialists should follow them in plain language through concrete reference to our case: the tracks maintained by municipal employees, the orders carried out and the choices foregone, the archive we have inherited, and the curriculum and maintenance commitments now proposed. The tone should be sober and inclusive, the use of the first-person plural exact rather than rhetorical, and the close forward-looking: a statement of what continued responsibility will consist in, stated as resolve rather than as penance.
