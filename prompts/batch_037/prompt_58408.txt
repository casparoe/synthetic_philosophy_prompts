I have been attempting to formulate the inefficacy defense of buying factory-farmed meat with full precision, and the result troubles me because it appears to prove far more than intended. Consider a simplified but representative supply chain. A supermarket tracks sales and, for every twenty chickens sold at retail, orders twenty additional chickens from a wholesaler, carrying over any remainder. On this rule, exactly one purchase in each block of twenty alters how many animals are subsequently raised, confined, and slaughtered, while the other nineteen alter nothing of consequential significance. Assume for purposes of the argument that the nineteen non-triggering purchases produce no other consequentially relevant difference and that each purchase benefits the buyer.

The argument then runs as follows:

(1) An individual purchase is wrong on consequentialist grounds only if it makes the outcome worse than it would otherwise have been.

(2) Under the restocking rule described, nineteen out of twenty purchases do not change the number of animals raised, confined, or slaughtered, and by stipulation change nothing else of consequential relevance.

(3) Therefore, nineteen out of twenty purchases do not make the outcome worse.

(4) If a particular purchase does not in fact make the outcome worse, the fact that purchases of the same type sometimes do cannot by itself make this token purchase wrong on consequentialist grounds.

(5) Therefore, nineteen out of twenty purchases are not wrong on consequentialist grounds.

(6) Whether a given purchase happens to be the triggering one depends on prior sales totals and stock levels that are opaque to the buyer and reflect nothing about the buyer's reasons, care, or character.

(7) If two acts are alike in all features accessible to and expressive of the agents, a merely positional difference of the kind described in (6) cannot render one permissible and the other impermissible.

(8) Therefore, all such purchases are permissible on consequentialist grounds, including the triggering ones.

(9) The same pattern applies to any individual contribution to a large collective harm mediated by buffers, thresholds, or large numbers, such as a single vote in a large electorate, a single leisure flight against the background of global emissions, or a single household's excess water use where reservoir releases are lumpy. The result is that consequentialism never requires restraint in such cases, however great the aggregate harm.

The conclusion in (9) seems unacceptable, since it would imply that practices that are collectively catastrophic are wrong for no individual participant on consequentialist grounds. I am inclined to retain a consequentialist framework if possible, and I am reluctant to accept that permissibility can turn on facts that are both unknowable in advance and arbitrary from the agent's point of view, but I cannot accept (9).

Which premise or inferential step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? Explain why the rejected claim initially seemed plausible, what alternative understanding of consequentialist wrongness, difference-making, or moral luck must replace it, and what that replacement commits one to in the meat, climate, and voting cases.
