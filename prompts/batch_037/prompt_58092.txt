I am preparing a handout for an advanced undergraduate seminar in political philosophy on contested statues, street names, and other public commemorations, and I would appreciate a decision tree that sorts respondents into coherent positions on what ought to be done.

Please structure the instrument as a sequence of no more than six yes-or-no questions that begins from the shared premise that a city must decide whether to retain, reinterpret, relocate, or remove an object that some residents experience as honoring a person or cause they judge to be seriously wrongful. Each question should concern a substantive philosophical commitment rather than a policy preference, and it should be formulated in ordinary language so that a student can answer it without anticipating which named position it favors.

The tree should be tested against, and capable of distinguishing judgments about, the following three stipulated cases, which are invented for this exercise: a bronze statue erected a century ago in a central square to celebrate a city founder who also ordered a massacre of an indigenous community; a residential street named a generation ago after a novelist whose published work advanced emancipation but whose private letters express dehumanizing attitudes; and an unauthorized mural painted last year on a municipal building to honor living dissidents, now widely cherished despite its unlawful origin.

The terminal nodes should include at least preservationism on historical-record grounds, removalism on endorsement grounds, a retention-with-contextualization view, a relocation-to-museum view, and a procedural view that makes democratic authorization decisive, with any further leaves you consider necessary to exhaust the plausible space, for a total of six to eight positions. For each terminal position, provide a concise characterization and one sentence identifying the commitment its defenders will find most difficult to accept.

Finally, please identify one logically possible combination of answers that yields no stable or attractive position and explain briefly why it remains unoccupied, and note one pair of positions that your tree does not cleanly separate and why the residual ambiguity persists.
