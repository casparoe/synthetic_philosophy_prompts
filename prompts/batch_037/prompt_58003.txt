Our medical school and philosophy department jointly run a termly ethics dialogue in which advanced undergraduates interview a guest thinker in front of an audience that includes nursing and medical trainees. Next term our guest will be a retired palliative care physician who defends the view that there is a morally fundamental difference between doing harm and merely allowing harm to occur, such that killing an innocent person is in itself harder to justify than letting that person die even when the outcome, motive, and degree of certainty are held fixed. She grounds this in an account of agency as authorship: to kill is to introduce a new threat into the victim's life, while to let die is to fail to intervene against a threat already unfolding, and she maintains that we bear a special responsibility for threats we author that is not reducible to differences in consequences or character.

In our planning meeting, students were especially puzzled by how this authorship idea handles intermediate cases we devised for discussion. One involves a ward nurse who momentarily pauses an automated infusion pump to check the line and then, distracted, delays restarting it by an hour, compared with a second nurse who arrives after a power failure has stopped the same pump and delays restarting it by the same hour with the same foreseeable deterioration. Another involves withdrawing a treatment already begun versus deciding not to initiate that same treatment when both decisions are made on identical prognostic grounds and with identical intent. Students also wondered whether the view can survive a version of the argument that if the distinction were truly fundamental, it should not disappear when numbers shift dramatically, for instance when killing one patient by misusing medication could free resources that would prevent several foreseeable deaths from neglect on the same ward.

Please compose a set of seven to nine interview questions suitable for philosophically prepared undergraduates to put to our guest in a forty-five minute session. The questions should press the substance of her position rather than invite biography or general reflections on medical practice, and they should be charitable but pointed. Include at least one question probing how the authorship account classifies pausing versus failing to restart and withdrawing versus withholding, at least one question testing whether responsibility for authored threats can explain cases where doing and allowing seem morally on a par, and at least one question exploring whether the distinction can consistently both constrain killing in the one-to-one case and permit tradeoffs when many lives are at stake. For each main question, add a concise follow-up that anticipates the reply she is most likely to give, such as appealing to intention, to what is normal functioning, or to prerogatives of the clinician role, and that shows why that reply would still leave a difficulty.
