As a journalist covering science and ideas, I am preparing an article on how major research funders handle uncertain risks of large-scale harm, and I have encountered a pattern I find difficult to characterize fairly.

A large public funder for biomedical and environmental research now applies two different regimes. For work involving engineered pathogens with pandemic potential and for small outdoor experiments on solar radiation modification, proponents must affirmatively establish that the risk is reasonable before proceeding. Uncertainty counts in favor of restraint, a temporary moratorium remains in place until that showing is made, and the harms of delaying the research are treated as secondary. For the staged release of gene-drive mosquitoes intended to suppress malaria vectors and for the rollout of AI triage systems in hospitals, the same funder permits deployment under monitoring, with predefined checkpoints and sunset provisions. Here uncertainty counts in favor of proceeding carefully while gathering data, opponents must produce concrete evidence of harm to pause expansion, and the costs of delay, including lives not saved, are treated as a central reason not to wait.

Both sets of cases involve uncertain probabilities, potentially severe and partly irreversible effects, and benefits that are themselves uncertain. Reconstruct the implicit understanding of precaution embodied in this dual policy. What conception of harm, uncertainty, burden of proof, reversibility, proportionality, and the moral weight of foregone benefits must the funder be relying on for each track to make sense, and do the two tracks presuppose a single coherent principle applied to different facts or two different principles? Distinguish what the policy strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with, noting where the practice is underdetermined and compatible with several rival accounts, and identify which rival understandings of precaution and of rational action under uncertainty are quietly ruled out. Finally, explain what would have to change in institutional design, evidentiary standards, and monitoring practice if the funder moved to a uniformly restrictive approach on the one hand, or to a uniformly permissive learning-while-doing approach on the other.
