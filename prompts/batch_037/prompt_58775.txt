I'm part of a small evening discussion group on markets and morals that meets at our public library — the regulars include a nursing student who has spent time on a dialysis ward and a caseworker from a housing shelter — and last week we deadlocked over a hypothetical municipal pilot program one member invented to get past the familiar cash-on-the-spot-for-a-kidney examples. The proposal, which we called the Harborview pool, would work like this: healthy adults between 25 and 40 could volunteer for extensive independent medical screening plus separate financial counseling paid for by the city, then enter a mandatory six-month cooling-off period with monthly check-ins and a right to withdraw at any time for any reason; those who still proceed would donate a kidney to the general waiting list with no directed donation and no private top-up payments allowed, and in return would receive not a lump sum but a ten-year annuity totaling about sixty thousand dollars plus lifetime transplant-related follow-up care and vouchers usable only for student loans, mortgage down payments, or vocational training, funded out of projected dialysis savings, with a yearly cap on total donors, public auditing of donor income demographics, and automatic suspension if donors come disproportionately from the poorest zip codes.

My own credence that it would be morally permissible for a reasonably just state to run this pilot as described is around 0.6, while my nursing-student friend puts hers near 0.15, and we suspect we are weighting different underlying worries differently rather than disagreeing about the medical facts. Please give me your own credence in that same proposition, stated precisely enough to have a truth value, but I am less interested in the bare number than in the decomposition that produces it: break the overall question into three to five sub-questions you take to drive it, such as whether consent under this kind of economic inducement can be sufficiently voluntary, whether removing a direct rich buyer and paying in restricted vouchers avoids corrupting the meaning of donation, whether the caps, cooling-off period, and demographic circuit-breaker can actually prevent exploitation and unjust concentration of burden, and whether the foreseeable lives saved and suffering averted outweigh any residual wrongness, assign a rough probability to each with one sentence of justification, show how they combine into your overall credence, and say which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to.

Finally, tell me what would most move your number up or down — what specific empirical finding about how such a program works in practice, or what philosophical argument or counterexample, would shift you the most — and where in your own reasoning you think you are most likely to be going wrong, so that we can see where our 0.6 versus 0.15 disagreement most likely lives.
