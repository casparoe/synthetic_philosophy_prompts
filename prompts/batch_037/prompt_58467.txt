I serve on the planning committee for a new undergraduate concentration joining geology, history, and philosophy around the idea of the Anthropocene as a proposed epoch shaped by human activity, and we have found ourselves stuck on how to frame the opening module. Students arrive having heard incompatible stories about when it begins — with early farming and forest clearance, with the Columbian exchange and colonial depopulation, with industrial fossil fuel use, or with the mid-twentieth-century acceleration in population, energy, plastics, and fallout — and about who properly gets to settle the matter, whether stratigraphers looking for a durable signal in sediments and ice, Earth system scientists modeling planetary thresholds, historians and anthropologists attentive to inequality and agency, or the communities most exposed to extraction and sea-level rise. I was discussing with colleagues whether we should center the technical question of markers and correlation, the moral-political question of how different start dates distribute responsibility between early industrializers and later developers or between humanity as a whole and particular economic systems, or the institutional question of what authorizes a geological declaration to reshape historical periodization and public imagination.

For a twelve-week seminar that can pursue only two or three of these threads in real depth, with the aim of leaving students better able to reason about environmental responsibility rather than merely rehearsing positions, which open questions or implications of the Anthropocene debate do you think most deserve that scarce attention, and in what order? I am less interested in a comprehensive overview than in a defended agenda: explain the criteria you are using, such as which question is conceptually upstream of the others, which is tractable for non-specialists without laboratory work, and which would most change practical or political conclusions if answered one way rather than another, acknowledge the trade-offs between them, identify which commonly emphasized implications you take to be overrated or to not follow from the dating or ratification decision at all, and say what sort of argument or evidence in the next few years would lead you to reorder the list.
