I am a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, and I am preparing an extended discussion piece for a circle of friends with a similar background on binding promises and authoritative declarations in the Harry Potter novels, considered through the lens of speech act theory.

For those of us shaped by that milieu, oaths and public declarations are naturally seen as commitment devices and generators of common knowledge, ways for agents who do not fully trust themselves or each other to coordinate. I want to understand what Rowling is doing when she makes such devices magically self-enforcing, and whether that clarifies or distorts the philosophical picture.

Take as background the familiar Austinian picture, which I will summarize so we are working from a shared statement of it: some utterances do things in the world rather than merely describe it, and they succeed only when certain social conditions are met, such as following an accepted procedure, having the relevant standing, securing uptake from the audience, and meaning the act sincerely and with understanding. Related to this is the distinction between the act accomplished directly in speaking, such as promising or christening or pronouncing, and the further causal effects such speech may bring about, such as persuading or frightening, plus the later idea that many institutional statuses exist only because they are collectively accepted as created by such declarations.

I am puzzled by three cases whose broad mechanics will be familiar, which I describe here so you need not consult the text and should paraphrase rather than rely on exact wording. First, the Unbreakable Vow in the sixth volume, in which Snape pledges to Narcissa Malfoy, with Bellatrix as witness-binder, to protect Draco and complete his task if he fails, with the understanding that breaking the vow causes death. Second, Hermione Granger’s jinxed membership list for Dumbledore’s Army in the fifth volume, in which signing one’s name constitutes agreement not to inform, and informing triggers a disfiguring punishment. Third, the Sorting Hat’s annual declarations at Hogwarts, in which placing the Hat and its announcement assign a new student to a house with lasting social consequences.

I would like a reading that takes both the novels and the philosophy seriously. For each case, how should we classify the utterance, what would count as felicitous or infelicitous about it, and where does magic seem to replace the ordinary social conditions such as authority, procedure, uptake, sincerity, voluntariness, and common knowledge? Does Rowling treat a coerced or half-understood vow as still binding, and if so, what does that imply about her implicit theory of promising? What does the contrast between the lethal Vow and the shaming jinx reveal about proportionality, deterrence, and trust, and what does the Sorting Hat reveal about declarations that depend on an authority no one can contest? Where does the speech act framework illuminate a character choice or institutional response, and where does the fiction resist the framework and require the honest critic to say so?

Please conclude by considering what the books contribute that abstract argument tends to miss about why a society would want commitments to be automatically enforced, and what is lost when enforcement no longer depends on continued collective acceptance.
