I've been reading through the literature on no-trade theorems (Milgrom-Stokey, Aumann's agreement theorem, and the broader "Harsanyi doctrine" debate) for a seminar on formal epistemology, and I keep getting stuck on the same interpretive puzzle. The theorems seem to show that if traders share a common prior and it's common knowledge that everyone is rational and risk-averse, then the mere fact that someone offers to trade with you should make you infer they have private information that erases any expected gain from trading with them — so speculative trade grinds to a halt. Yet real markets are saturated with speculative volume, block trades, and disagreement-driven positioning. Something in the model has to give, and different camps blame different premises: some (following Aumann and the Harsanyi doctrine) insist the common prior assumption is basically a rationality requirement and that observed trade therefore reveals widespread irrationality or failures of common knowledge of rationality; others argue that demanding a common prior in the first place smuggles in an unjustified metaphysical assumption about shared "objective" starting points for belief, and that subjective, non-common priors are perfectly rational, in which case trade volume tells us nothing embarrassing at all.

Write an essay that takes a side on this dispute: does the ubiquity of speculative trading in real markets constitute genuine evidence against the common prior assumption (and thus against the Harsanyi doctrine as a rationality constraint), or is it better explained away by relaxing common knowledge of rationality, appealing to noise traders/liquidity motives, or some other move that leaves the common prior assumption untouched? Lay out the strongest considerations on each side — including what's actually at stake epistemologically in treating priors as either shared starting points or as brute subjective inputs — and then defend a definite verdict rather than a survey of options.
