I'm a backend/ML engineer who's been down a rabbit hole ever since a heated Slack debate with a colleague about whether a trained image classifier can be said to "represent" cats in any philosophically loaded sense, or whether it's just responding to pixel correlations with no real content at all. That argument led me to two rival naturalistic theories of mental content that keep coming up in what I've been reading: Ruth Millikan's teleosemantics, which grounds content in the selected (evolutionary or otherwise reinforced) function of the mechanism that produces a representation, and Fred Dretske's informational/causal-covariation semantics, which grounds content in reliable nomic correlation between an internal state and whatever it tracks. Both are trying to explain how a physical state can be "about" something without smuggling in an interpreter, but they seem to diverge sharply on how they handle misrepresentation and on what kind of history or relation actually fixes content.

Could you compare these two approaches in some depth -- not just the textbook one-liner that "one uses function, the other uses information" -- but really work through how each handles classic problem cases, like a frog's tongue snapping at a BB pellet instead of a fly, or a smoke detector that "misfires" on steam? I'm especially interested in whether teleosemantics' appeal to selection history is doing genuine explanatory work that pure informational/causal semantics can't do, or whether it's just relocating the same indeterminacy problem to "what was selected for." If it's useful for sharpening the comparison, feel free to speculate on whether either framework would let us say that a trained neural network's hidden activations "represent" something in more than a loose, metaphorical sense.
