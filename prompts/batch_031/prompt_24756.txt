I want to define deception in terms of the info flow between the system and the audience, not the system's internal states.

One more thing: I'm specifically pushing back on the "deceptive alignment" framing that's popular in the AI safety literature (the idea that an AI "deceives" when it hides its "true" objectives from its operators). I think that framing is still too mental-state based — it attributes "true objectives" and "hiding" to the system. I want a purely output-based / info-flow account.

The audience is a panel of legal scholars — two securities-fraud lawyers and a criminal law prof — who are drafting regulatory guidance on "AI deception" for a financial services regulator. They're committed to the mental-state account because thats how fraud works in law (intent to deceive, knowledge of falsity, reliance, etc.). They care about enforceability (can you actually prove an AI "intended" to deceive?), liability (who is liable — the dev, the operator, the AI?), and precedent (how does this fit with existing fraud law?).
