I examine patent applications in the energy and thermodynamics art unit, and a growing share of them now describe "information engines" — feedback-controlled demons, nanoscale sorting devices, reversible-computing machines — whose applicants claim a thermodynamic advantage that comes from processing information, and some of them claim outright to beat the second law. I am competent in classical thermodynamics and mechanics, but I am not a specialist in the physics of information, and I do not have the literature at my desk when I examine; I have to decide from the application's description alone. I need a decision procedure I can actually run on an application that tells me whether the claimed device is a genuine second-law violation or whether the accounting is just incomplete.

Write it as a fixed sequence of questions I ask in order, each with a specification of what counts as a sufficient answer, and mark the two kinds of terminal: where the procedure stops with a clean "no" — the claim as stated is not a violation, so the application describes an impossible device and fails on that ground — and where the procedure must hand the decision to a person, because the question is one the accounting cannot settle (a referral to a specialist panel, or a flag that the remaining question is legal or evaluative rather than physical).

Before any verdict, the procedure should force me to fill in an explicit ledger: the system; the controller or demon; the memory (its capacity, whether it is reset, and where the entropy of the erased information goes); the heat baths (how many, at what temperatures); the operation (cyclic or one-shot, open-loop or feedback); and the claimed output, including which statement of the second law the applicant says it violates — Clausius, Kelvin–Planck, or the total-entropy form — since those are not the same claim and a device can fail one naive reading without violating the total law. State the standard Landauer bound explicitly in the procedure (erasing one bit at temperature T costs at least kT ln 2 of dissipated heat) so I do not have to remember it, and say which of these ledger distinctions a less careful procedure would drop and why dropping them would make it misfire.

Be honest about where the procedure will misfire and which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. In particular, flag the cases where the device draws on a resource the ledger does not track — a pre-existing correlation between demon and system, a non-equilibrium or driven reservoir, a gravitational field, or a quantum resource such as entanglement or a measurement back-action a classical ledger cannot capture — the cases where the operation is not cyclic and so a "cyclic second-law violation" claim is not even well-formed, and the cases where the device operates in a regime in which the Landauer bound is genuinely disputed rather than settled. In those cases the procedure should say exactly what I may and may not conclude on my own.

Then run the procedure on three applications. (1) A Szilard-style single-particle demon that measures which half of a box the particle occupies, inserts a paddle to extract work, and resets its memory each cycle, claiming to extract kT ln 2 of work per cycle from a single heat bath. (2) A reversible-computing demon with a finite memory that never erases, claiming to extract work indefinitely from a single bath without paying any erasure cost — a credible, well-funded applicant who will appeal. (3) A device that harvests work from a correlation between the demon and the system that was established before the device began operating and is never refreshed. For each, show the ledger as filled, the path through the questions, the terminal, and — for the case where the procedure gives me an answer I will not like — what I am supposed to do with that. End by stating, plainly, what the procedure cannot do at all.
