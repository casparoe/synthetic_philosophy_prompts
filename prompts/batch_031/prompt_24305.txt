So I want to work through the 2016 UK Brexit referendum with a specific conceptual lens, and not the usual "populism vs. the elites" framing that everyone reaches for. For reference on the facts: it was the June 2016 vote, roughly 52 percent of the people who turned out (turnout was about 72 percent) chose Leave and 48 percent chose Remain, Scotland and London voted Remain, and the result was legally advisory but got treated afterwards like a binding mandate.

The lens I want is the old Rousseau distinction between the "general will" and the "will of all". The idea being that the general will is not just the sum, or the majority, of everyone's particular private wills added up; it's something different, the common interest of the body as a whole, and it's a mistake to read what the aggregated individuals happen to want as if it were the will of the people as a substantive unity. On top of that I want the the related claim (I think of it as Schmitt's) that a "people" doesn't have a "will" at all unless it's first constituted as a qualitative, substantive "we", not merely a headcount of individuals each with their own competing will. A bare plurality of wills is not a will, it's just a bundle of wills, so you need some act of constituting the people as one before your even allowed to ask what "the people" want.

Using that, map the episode carefully. What exactly is the "people" whose will is supposed to be revealed here — the UK electorate? the British demos? — and how does that sit with Scotland and London (and Northern Ireland) voting a different way? Is the 52 percent best read as a "will of all", an aggregate of individual choices, being mistaken for a "general will", a unified substantive will of the nation? Or is the referendum actually the device by which that substantive unity gets constituted in the first place, and the "will" is a product of the vote rather than something the vote reveals? What's the status of the 48 percent who voted Remain and the roughly quarter who stayed home — do they get excluded from "the people", and what does that do to the claim that the will of the people has been revealed? And how does the post-referendum "mandate" talk, the idea that the Leave result expresses the true will of the whole nation even against Parliament and the courts, fit the move of demanding a substantive unity and then letting a leader or party speak for it?

Then be honest about where this lens breaks or just misdescribes things. Does treating the referendum as revealing a pre-existing unified will actually track what happened, or was "the will of the people" mostly constructed by the way the question was framed, the campaigns, and the after-the-fact mandate claim? And does the general will / will of all distinction actually buy us anything, or does it just repackage the ordinary majority-vs-minority problem in fancier language? Keep the mapping disciplined about what counts as the people and what counts as the will, and say where the theory goes silent instead of forcing it to talk.
