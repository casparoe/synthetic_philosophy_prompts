You are preparing to present a paper at a summer school on political philosophy. The paper argues the following: Carnap's political and social thought, taken as a whole, is best read as a coherent position in which (i) the "sociological" or causal concept of the state replaces the "normative" concept, so that the state is analyzed as a system of coercive institutions rather than as a source of legitimate authority; (ii) the logical-empiricist ban on substantive value judgments does not yield quietism, because once values are treated as objects of social and psychological investigation, rational social planning becomes a technical extension of the sciences; and (iii) the distinction between internal and external questions, and the denial that there is a single "reality" to be discovered, are meant to dissolve the metaphysical foundations on which conservative and absolutist politics rest. You are presenting this to an audience of mainstream political theorists and a few analytic political philosophers who are sympathetic to the idea that political philosophy should be in conversation with the social sciences, but who are steeped in the Rawlsian and republican traditions: they think in terms of legitimacy, public justification, non-domination, and the distinction between the right and the good, and they regard the state as an object of normative evaluation before it is an object of explanation.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections this audience will actually raise, in roughly the order they will come and in the order of how much damage each can do, phrased the way a political theorist in that tradition would phrase it rather than the way a philosopher of science would. For each objection, tell me (a) which of the three claims of my paper it targets, (b) what I should concede up front in order to keep credibility, (c) a reply of about two minutes, and (d) where that reply is softest, so I know which objection I am most exposed to. Close with the one question I am hoping nobody asks, and say why it would be dangerous. Do not invent a bibliography or cite works I would not be able to check; argue from the positions and concepts themselves.
