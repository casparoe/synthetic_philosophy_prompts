I hold the traditional doctrine of the moral equality of combatants: in an unjust war, combatants on both sides have equal moral status with respect to what they are permitted to do in combat; the injustice of one side's cause does not make that side's combatants liable to defensive force any more than the other side's are; and the jus ad bellum and the jus in bello are independent. I want a map of what would count against this position, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know which of the defeaters a committed defender of the doctrine would themselves concede as counting against it rather than dismissing.

First, the liability problem. The doctrine implies that a just-side soldier who kills an unjust-side soldier is killing a person who is not liable to defensive force, and that this killing is nonetheless permitted. What would have to be true for that implication to be tolerable? Lay out the "self-defense against the non-liable" move — the car thief, the mentally ill attacker — honestly: what exactly it can do for the doctrine, what it costs, and whether the analogy holds under the conditions of actual warfare (imminence, the absence of lesser means, the scale and duration of the threat). Which version of this move would the doctrine's defenders concede is doing real work, and which would they say is a straw man?

Second, the epistemic qualification. The doctrine is often defended on the ground that in most real wars neither side can know which is just, so the soldier may permissibly fight on the basis of his best judgment. How much of the doctrine's force depends on this epistemic point? In particular: does the doctrine collapse in the case of a clearly aggressive war, where the truth is manifest and knowable? Does it collapse for the volunteer or the mercenary, who chose to fight for a cause they had reason to know was unjust? Does it survive the case of the conscript who, once the war is underway, comes to know that his side is unjust? Which of these cases would the doctrine's defenders treat as genuine defeaters, and which would they treat as cases where the doctrine is simply not the right tool?

Third, the collective-responsibility premise. The doctrine depends on the claim that the wrong of the war is the state's or the collective's, and that the ordinary soldier is a mere instrument of that wrong. What would count against this premise? I am thinking especially of command responsibility and the individual's share in the decision to go to war, the case of the soldier who shares the aggressive intent of his superiors, and the case of the soldier whose contribution to the wrong is such that he could not make it in any other way. Which of these would a committed collectivist concede as eroding the premise, and which would they say are consistent with it?

Fourth, the predictions that separate the doctrine from its individualist rival. I want a list of the places where the doctrine and the individualist position make genuinely different predictions about what is permitted or required, not merely about the moral status of the combatant. I am thinking of the treatment of prisoners of war (can an unjust-side prisoner be punished for the war itself, or only for in-bello violations?), the moral weight of the two sides' killings (is the just-side killing of an unjust-side soldier morally "cleaner" than the reverse, or are they equally tragic?), the permissibility of a soldier's refusing to fight on learning that his side is unjust, and the permissibility of fighting for a state that is not one's own. For each, I want to know whether the doctrine's prediction is one its defenders would defend as a feature or retreat from as a bug.

Finally, an honest assessment. Has the doctrine been qualified into unfalsifiability by the epistemic move and the collective-responsibility premise — that is, is there any empirically or conceptually discoverable state of the world in which the doctrine would be false? If it has been insulated, is that a problem for a doctrine of this kind, or is it a feature of the fact that it is a normative claim about what is permitted rather than an empirical claim about what is the case? And if the doctrine is well insulated, what would a rival have to show in order to make the case for individualism on the merits rather than by pointing to the doctrine's qualifications?

I would personally find most persuasive the epistemic move and the collective-responsibility premise; I am least confident about the self-defense-against-the-non-liable analogy and about the treatment of the volunteer.
