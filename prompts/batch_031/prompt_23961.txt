I run avalanche search-and-rescue for a mountain rescue association, and I'm the one who has to call, in the field, which of two buried groups we commit the dog team and the diggers to when we can only work one site at a time. It comes up more then I'd like in a winter: two beacons triangulate to two different pockets, the snow is bad, the team is a single unit, and I've got maybe a two hour window before the buried people are gone. I need a decision procedure I can actually run on a slope in the cold with a head full of static, that tells me what to do when I can get the group of four out of the east pocket or the single hiker out of the west gully but not both, and I make the call and I sign for it.

I don't want it to just be "save the most people." I've read enough of the debate to know that's contested, and I don't want a procedure that papers over that. So ground it in what the argument actually says: what is the real force of the claim that the number of people saved is, by itself, not a reason to save the many over the few (the Taurek line), what are the strongest replies to it, and where does the procedure have to stop and hand the call back to me as a person because it's a values question and not a facts question? Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a "no, don't commit to that site," and the points where it just has to flag "this one's on you." Be straight about which steps are really judgment calls wearing a procedure costume. Then run it on two real-ish calls from last season: one where the four are a ski patrol team with a radio and the single is a solo backpacker who's already been out longer, and one where the single is a kid. And tell me the cases where the procedure hands me an answer I'm going to hate, and what it simply can't do for me.
