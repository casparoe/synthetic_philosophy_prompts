I've been reading about the problem of logical omniscience for a while now — the idea that an ideal Bayesian who is closed under all logical consequences of their evidence can't really learn anything new, that they already know all the consequences of all the evidence they could possibly receive, and that the experience of being surprised is incompatible with ideal rationality. I've seen the standard arguments and objections, but I'd like a case built to order rather than another survey.

Please construct a thought experiment in which two agents with identical evidence and identical payoffs — differing only in that one is logically omniscient (closed under all logical consequences of what they believe, and knows which consequences they know) and the other is not — make different decisions, and in which the logically non-omniscient agent is strictly better off (in terms of expected payoff or epistemic accuracy) than the logically omniscient agent. The difference must come from the logical omniscience itself, not from information access or from the non-omniscient agent being lazy or uninformed. Constraints: no exotic physics, no mind-reading, no stipulation of the verdict — the verdict must be derived from the two epistemic profiles. The scenario must be short (no more than one or two paragraphs). The case must be such that a defender of logical omniscience would find it genuinely challenging, not just a straw man.

After the scenario, walk through what each agent does and why, and tell me which feature of the case is doing the work. Then say what happens if you remove that feature. Finally, tell me how a defender of logical omniscience (the theory that comes out badly) would attack the case.
