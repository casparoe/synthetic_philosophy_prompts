I retired from experimental physics a few years ago and have only recently begun reading philosophy seriously, so I bring to this the habits of someone who thinks in terms of experimental design, controls, and confounds, and I'd be grateful if you'd meet me there. I'd like a philosophical reading of Christopher Nolan's The Dark Knight — specifically the two-ferry sequence in the third act and the closing resolution in which the city agrees to let a lie stand — through the lens of the doing/allowing distinction.

Let me lay out what I take the film to be doing, so we're working from the same text. The Joker places a bomb on each of two ferries, one carrying prisoners and one carrying civilians, and gives each ferry a detonator that will blow up the other. If neither ferry acts before a set deadline, the bomb on their own ferry detonates. After a heated debate, the prisoners decide to blow up the civilian ferry; the civilians, given the same choice, do nothing. Both bombs are defused at the last moment by the men who planted them, who turn out to be people the Joker underestimated, and the Joker, having lost his bet, laments that his experiment has failed to confirm what he believed about human nature. The film then closes with Batman and Gordon agreeing to let it be believed that Batman killed the Two-Face, so that the city's symbol of hope survives; the final image is a gravestone that encodes the lie the city has agreed to believe.

My questions, in roughly order of how much I care about them:

First, does the ferry sequence actually test the doing/allowing distinction, or does it quietly collapse it? The ordinary force of the distinction is that doing harm is worse than allowing the same harm, all else equal. But here the "allowing" option — doing nothing — means the certain death of everyone on your own ferry, so the distinction is being asked to do work in a case where "allowing" is not a live option in the ordinary sense. The prisoners' choice to "do" is forced by the fact that "allowing" is catastrophic. So does the film stage the boundary of the distinction's applicability — the point at which it stops having moral force — or does it simply fail to notice that it has moved the distinction out of its domain?

Second, and this is where my physicist's eye itches: the moral conclusion of the sequence — that people are, after all, good — is delivered not by the ferry passengers' choices but by the defusers' goodness. The Joker's bet is on the passengers, but the payoff is determined by the character of the men who planted the bombs. That strikes me as a confound in the experimental design: the independent variable the film thinks it is testing (the passengers' character under pressure) is not the variable that actually determines the outcome. Does the film know this? Is the rigging part of the point — that the Joker's "experiment" was never a fair test of anyone's character — or is it a flaw the film doesn't see?

Third, the closing resolution is, on its face, a sustained case of collective "allowing": the city lets a lie stand, and that allowing does the good of protecting the symbol. The film seems to treat this as a noble "allowing," an omission that is morally required. But a doing/allowing theorist might press the point that maintaining a lie is not an omission at all — you have to keep the cover-up going, and so the "noble allowing" is really a "noble doing" that the film mislabels. Does the film get this right, or does it trade on the viewer's sympathy for the symbol rather than on the philosophical point?

I'd like a reading that takes both the film and the doing/allowing debate seriously — the cases where the distinction is doing real work, the cases where it is being stretched past its breaking point, and the honest places where the film resists the reading and I have to say so. I'm not asking for a bibliography or a list of papers; I'm asking for the argument, in prose, that I can sit with. And if the film turns out to add something to the doing/allowing debate that argument alone doesn't — some case or some intuition that the abstract cases don't capture — I'd be grateful if you'd say what that is.
