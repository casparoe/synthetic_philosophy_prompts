I'm preparing to defend a strong version of Good's theorem at a mixed seminar next month, and a colleague keeps telling me the talk is doomed because the result is a one-line consequence of the law of total expectation. The audience is mostly Bayesian statisticians who know the theorem as exactly that one line and are suspicious of decision-theoretic framing, two or three decision theorists who have read the literature on negative value of information and will want to know how I handle strategic settings and commitment problems, and one behavioral economist who thinks of information as a commodity people demonstrably overpay for. My thesis, as I intend to state it: for any agent with a well-defined prior over a fixed state space who will act optimally on what a signal reveals, the expected value of any information source is never negative, the expected value of perfect information dominates that of any partial information, and the familiar claim that information can be bad always involves costs, strategic interaction, or boundedly rational use rather than information itself.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the five or six objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way a statistician, decision theorist, or economist would phrase it, with a reply of at most four sentences and a one-line note on where the reply is soft. I want the objections aimed at the theorem's assumptions — the fixed state space, the commitment to optimal use, the prior itself, and the gap between the ex-ante expectation and the realized signal — rather than at the algebra, since I can defend the algebra in one line. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. Keep the whole sheet under 700 words.
