I overheard a long argument between two friends of mine, Marcus and Dana, about a decision puzzle I'd been mulling over — I believe it's sometimes called "Pascal's mugging" — and I couldn't tell who was more right, so I'd like a second opinion. The setup: someone stops you on the street and says, "Give me your wallet, or I'll press this button, which will kill a billion people." You have no way of verifying the claim, and your best estimate of the chance it's true is around one in a billion. The question they were arguing about is what a rational person should do.

Marcus opened by saying the math is on the mugger's side: one in a billion times a billion lives is an expected value of one life, and you're holding the only thing that prevents it, so paying is the rational move and the discomfort you feel is just an emotion overriding the calculation. Dana pushed back that if the math is really that simple, the mugger can make the stakes as large as he wants — a trillion people, everyone in the galaxy — and the expected value only grows, so by the same logic you should hand over everything you own to everyone who ever makes a claim like this, which is absurd. Marcus conceded you can't pay everyone but said that just means you need a rule for which claims to take seriously, and the rule should be the probability, not your feelings. Dana then tried to draw a line between this and, say, buying insurance against a rare catastrophe: the mugger is trying to manipulate you by exploiting the gap between a tiny probability and a huge stake, and the right response to a manipulator is to refuse. Marcus replied that the distinction collapses if the mugger might actually be telling the truth — there could genuinely be a one-in-a-billion chance the button works, in which case he isn't manipulating you at all, he's just making a low-probability, high-stakes claim, which is exactly the case where the math says pay; so the "don't let manipulators exploit you" principle only helps if you can tell he's lying, and you can't always do that. Dana's last point was that the expected-value framework itself may break down when the stakes can be made arbitrarily large, because then there is no stable threshold — any number you pick, the mugger can outbid it. Marcus said that's a real problem but a problem for the toy scenario, not the framework; in the real world the stakes are bounded, so expected value works fine, and Dana is just being bothered by an artificial case.

Can you go through this exchange and say who's right at each step, and what, if anything, both of them are missing? I'm least sure about two points: whether Dana's "unbounded stakes" objection is a genuine flaw in expected-value reasoning or just a feature of the toy setup, and whether her "manipulation" distinction is doing any real work. And please tell me what would change your mind on the overall verdict — which argument or consideration would have led you to a different conclusion, or where your own reasoning is most likely to break down.
