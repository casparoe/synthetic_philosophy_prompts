I've been arguing with a friend for the best part of an evening about whether it is rational to discount the future, and we've gone in circles, partly because I suspect we keep using "discounting" to mean different things. To get some purchase on what I actually believe, I've been working through a list of concrete cases and recording my gut verdicts, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, captures the pattern, what it implies for cases I haven't considered, and which of my verdicts it forces me to drop.

Here are the cases. (1) I would genuinely rather have a coffee now than an identical, certain cup in twenty minutes, and I do not think this preference is a bug or a failure of reasoning; it just feels like the kind of thing I'm allowed to want. (2) I am much less sure about myself over longer horizons: if a week of vacation were guaranteed in two years or in three, the pure timing of it barely moves me, and I can't bring myself to say the earlier one is meaningfully better. (3) I strongly prefer to undergo a painful but certain medical procedure now rather than an identical one a year from now, but I notice that my preference rests entirely on the fact that I might die, get sicker, or change my mind in the meantime; if all of that risk were removed, I think the timing would stop mattering. (4) I reject the practice, which I keep seeing in cost–benefit discussions, of discounting the welfare of people a century from now at a few percent per year; to me a child's death in 2100 is exactly as bad as one today, and I cannot see how the mere passage of time makes it less so. (5) Finally, the case that embarrasses me: I prefer one hundred dollars today to one hundred and five in a month, but I also prefer one hundred and five in two months to one hundred and five in a month, and when I line the two up I recognize the second preference as inconsistent with the first.

My tentative principle, offered for testing rather than as a commitment, is that you should discount only when there is a real reason — uncertainty, mortality, the possibility of changed tastes, or the fact that an earlier good is enjoyed for a longer stretch — and that "pure" time preference, disliking an identical and certain outcome merely because it is later, is not a reason at all. But I'm not confident that principle explains case (1), where I do seem to have exactly that kind of pure preference and don't feel irrational about it. So I'd be grateful if you could state the principle, or a couple of candidates, precisely; say which of my cases motivates each clause; run it through at least one case I have not listed; and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which one I should probably let go.
