Last night I got into a long, slightly ugly argument with my friend Dana, who works in international trade, about the 1987 Montreal Protocol on ozone-depleting substances, and I want a second opinion on the part where I think she's overreaching. Her claim, which she repeated about six times: the ozone treaty is the canonical example of a pure win-win deal, a genuine Pareto improvement where every country that signed came out ahead, and that's exactly why it succeeded while the Paris Agreement is a mess -- climate has no such deal available, so of course it stalls. I pushed back by saying the whole thing only worked because of the Multilateral Fund, which is basically side payments from rich countries to poor ones to cover the cost of switching away from CFCs, and a deal that needs transfers to make people better off isnt a Pareto improvement in any strict sense. I know just enough to know I might be wrong, and I have this half-remembered definition of a "safe Pareto improvement" from a game-theory bargaining paper: a change to the way the parties negotiate that leaves every participant better off no matter what strategy they were originally going to play, so no party can be made worse off by the change regardless of the others' moves. So here's what I want.

First, tell me in your opening sentence whether the Montreal Protocol is best read as a Pareto improvement, a safe Pareto improvement, or something else entirely, and then do the actual mapping: who the players are in the negotiation game, what the realistic options and payoffs roughly looked like (US industry, developing countries, the CFC producers, the rest), what the Fund's side payments and the 1990 accelerated phaseout schedule do to the game, and where the "everyone is better off" story breaks down -- including the obvious points that the baseline the treaty improved on was already a losing equilibrium for almost everyone, and that "better off" for a CFC manufacturer is a pretty weak claim to make. Then say what the episode shows about the theory itself: is it a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the concept was never built for, and what does a safe-Pareto analysis get wrong, or not have the tools to say, about a one-shot international negotiation with weak enforcement, free-riding, and unequal bargaining power? I dont care about the ozone chemistry and I dont want a history of the treaty -- I want the game-theoretic mapping and an honest account of where the Pareto framing does real work and where it just sounds good.
