I've been chewing on the etiological vs. causal-role debate in philosophy of biology, and I keep landing on phantom limb pain as a case that I think actually does more work for the etiological side than people usually grant, but I'm not sure my reasoning is airtight so I'd love a second pair of eyes.

The setup: someone has had a leg amputated but still reports pain in the missing leg. On the etiological account (Millikan's "proper function"), pain was selected for signaling tissue damage, so that's its proper function. In the phantom case there's no tissue damage, so the pain is a false positive — it's running the same program as normal pain but the input it's supposed to track is absent. On the causal-role account, a trait's function is whatever it currently does that contributes to the fitness of the bearer. Phantom limb pain, plausibly, contributes nothing to fitness (and might even subtract, by causing distress and disrupting sleep). So the causal-role theorist has to say it has no function, or at least not the function that normal pain has.

Here's where I think the etiological account looks better. The two pains are, mechanistically, the same kind of thing — same nociceptive pathways, same affective character, just different upstream triggers. The etiological account can say "both have the function of signaling tissue damage; in the phantom case the signal is spurious." That's a unified story. The causal-role account, by contrast, has to say "normal pain has a function, phantom pain doesn't," and the only thing doing the work in that distinction is the presence or absence of actual tissue damage, which feels like it's tracking the wrong variable. The kind of thing the pain is hasn't changed; only the input has.

The response I keep expecting from a causal-role theorist is something like: "the function of pain isn't narrowly to signal tissue damage, it's to signal that something is wrong and to motivate action, and phantom pain does that — it gets the person to seek medical attention, to check for other problems, etc." But I'm not sure that response survives scrutiny, because if we let the function of pain be that general, then almost any painful experience has a function (it always motivates *some* action), and the account loses its discriminating power.

Am I missing a cleaner response the causal-role side could give? And is the "same kind of thing, different input" move actually available to the etiological theorist, or does it quietly assume the causal-role account's own framework?
