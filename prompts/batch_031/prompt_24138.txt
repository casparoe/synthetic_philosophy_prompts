I've come to accept the deflationary view that most contested metaphysical disputes — over, say, whether there are social entities, whether past events are concrete, or whether there are possible worlds — are merely verbal: the parties already share an underlying, non-ontic picture of how things are, and they disagree only about how to carve it up or what to call it, so there is no fact of the matter the dispute is supposed to track. Tell me what would count against this view. I don't want the stock objections; I want the specific observations, arguments, intuitions, possible cases, and results in adjacent areas — about identity, about the relation between language and reality, about what it is for a question to have a fact of the matter at all — that would genuinely move someone off the merely-verbal position, and what the view predicts that its rivals (the ontic realists, and the view that these are deep, genuine disagreements about how reality is) do not. Sort the defeaters by how decisive they would be, from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about which a committed deflationist would themselves concede as counting against the view and which they'd dismiss as resting on a confused intuition or a gerrymandered paraphrase. I'd personally find most persuasive either a case in which settling the metaphysical question genuinely changes the answer to a substantive question — moral, legal, or predictive — or a demonstration that the deflationist's own non-ontic paraphrase quietly re-imports the ontic claim it was supposed to dissolve. Finally, give me an honest assessment of whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability by the move of deciding case by case which disputes are verbal and which are real, and whether that insulation is a defect for a view of this kind or a feature. Begin your response with your bottom line in the very first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble, and do not restate the question.
