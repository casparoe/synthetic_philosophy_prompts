I am writing up my views on just war theory for a seminar on the moral equality of combatants, and I would like a careful consistency check before I present. I hold the following five commitments:

(1) Dualism: the prohibition on targeting civilians is a deontic constraint that applies regardless of the civilians' culpability or moral worth; it is not grounded in desert, and it does not depend on the civilians being blameless.

(2) The parity thesis: combatants on both sides of a war are equally entitled to use lethal force against each other, even when one side is fighting a clearly unjust war. The unjust side does not lose its entitlement to fight simply because its cause is unjust.

(3) A strong jus ad bellum: aggressive wars of conquest are seriously unjust; the political leaders who order them are morally culpable, and the soldiers who fight them are, at least to some degree, morally responsible for the war.

(4) In individual self-defense, it is permissible to kill a non-culpable person who poses an imminent lethal threat (for example, a mentally ill person who is about to kill you, or a child who has a gun and does not understand what they are doing).

(5) The permissibility of killing in war is, in the end, grounded in the same considerations as the permissibility of killing in individual self-defense — namely, the threat the target poses and the balance of interests at stake — rather than in a special deontic constraint that applies only in the context of war.

Please think very carefully before answering. Go through these commitments and identify which pairs are genuinely in tension and how severely, and which pairs only appear to be in tension but dissolve on inspection. For each genuine tension, specify what would have to give: which commitment is the cheapest to revise, and what it costs to revise it. Then tell me which single revision would resolve the most inconsistencies overall. Respond in the style of an expert in just war theory who is also careful about the logical structure of the positions. Do not assume I already know where the problem lies, and do not signal in advance which commitment I should be prepared to give up.
