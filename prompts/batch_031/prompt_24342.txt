I'm reading Camus's "Reflections on the Guillotine" for a class, and I'm stuck on a passage that sounds impressive but that I don't think I actually understand. Camus writes: "Capital punishment is the most premeditated of murders, to which no criminal's deed, however calculated, can be compared. For there to be an equivalency, the death penalty would have to punish a criminal who had warned his victim of the date on which he would inflict a horrible death on him and who, from that moment onward, had confined him at his mercy for months. Such a monster is not to be encountered in private life."

My question is about the structure of the argument. Camus seems to be claiming that there is an asymmetry between a state execution and a private murder, and that the state's act is in some sense worse than the crime it is supposed to be punishing. But I can't tell which feature of the execution is supposed to be doing the work. It might be the premeditation, since the state plans and carries out the killing over months or years. It might be the duration of the victim's helplessness, since the condemned man lives in prison awaiting a date while the victim of an ordinary murder usually does not. Or it might be the power relation, since the state is the only agent that can carry out the sentence and the condemned is entirely at its mercy, a structural asymmetry no private killing replicates. Can you sort out which of these, if any, is actually load-bearing in the passage, and whether they can be separated?

And then there's a harder question I keep coming back to. The argument seems to assume that premeditation and the prolongation of the victim's terror are what make a killing morally worse, that a planned killing outranks an impulsive one. But I'm not sure that's uncontroversial. Some people would say an impulsive murder shows a more dangerous character, or is at least just as bad. So does the argument quietly depend on a disputed premise about what makes killing worse? Or is the real point not that the state's act is worse in the same moral currency, but something different, like the idea that a punishment is supposed to be a response to a wrong and the state cannot commit a greater wrong in the name of righting a lesser one? And does the argument change if the criminal was a cold-blooded planner rather than an impulsive killer, especially since Camus also argues, on the strength of statistics, that most murders are not premeditated?

I don't want a summary of Camus's views or a list of his arguments. I want you to work through the logic of this one passage: what it assumes, what it depends on, where it is strongest, and where a defender of the death penalty would most plausibly press on it.
