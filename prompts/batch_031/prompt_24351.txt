Ive been digging into the underdetermination stuff in philosophy of science for a while and I can't figure out why this debate hasn't moved an inch in like forty years. On one side you've got the Duhem-Quine line: the total body of evidence never logically pins down a single theory, there is always some rival that fits the data just as well, so confirmation is way weaker than we pretend. On the other side you've got people who say this is a non-issue, and the strongest version of their case is the triviality objection — for any theory you can always cook up an empirically equivalent rival by tacking on some ad hoc clause, so underdetermination just shows that logic doesn't pick out a unique theory, which is boring and tells us nothing. And then there is the reply that the triviality objection only kills the trivial version, and that there are real non-trivial cases where two serious, non-ad hoc theories fit the data equally well, the sort of thing people point to with rival models of the solar system or two different Lagrangians that give the same equations of motion.

And it just goes on like this. Quine says underdetermination, they say it's trivial, Quine's people say no the non-trivial cases are real, they say fine but it doesn't matter for realism, repeat forever. Nobody moves.

My working theory is that the disagreement isn't really about whether underdetermination is real at all — I think if you look hard enough both sides will grant there are non-trivial cases — it's about what counts as a serious theory in the first place. The triviality objection depends on a line between serious and ad hoc, and I suspect the two camps just draw that line differently and never get it out in the open. Is that right? Or is it a hidden empirical question (do non-trivial underdetermined pairs actually show up in real science, or only in contrived examples), or a verbal disagreement about what the word theory even means (the whole web of belief vs a sub-theory, Quine style), or is it just a genuinely hard substantive question that is unresolved?

I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is, not a survey of the positions and not a verdict on who is right. And given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress — a distinction that splits the question, an empirical result, a formal theorem, a reformulation of the question — or argue that no progress should be expected and why the impasse is structual.
