A philosopher of artificial intelligence has made the following claim, which I would like you to evaluate by predicting how Robert Aumann — the game theorist behind the 1976 "agreeing to disagree" theorem — would respond, had he been asked about it at a 2025 seminar.

The claim: "Two frontier large language models are asked the same empirical question with a determinate but uncertain answer. Model A reports a credence of 0.60; Model B reports 0.40. Each is then told the other's reported credence and asked to update. After several rounds of mutual disclosure they settle at 0.55 and 0.48, a gap that no longer closes. Both models are, for our purposes, rational Bayesian reasoners trained on broadly overlapping corpora, so they share roughly the same prior over the world. Since their final credences are common knowledge — each knows the other's, and knows that the other knows it, ad infinitum — this is a direct empirical refutation of Aumann's theorem: two rational agents with the same prior persistently disagree even when their posteriors are common knowledge."

Predict Aumann's response on the following, reasoning as he would, with his characteristic attention to the exact hypotheses of a theorem:
(a) Which single hypothesis of the theorem does most of the work, and which is most likely to be violated — or even ill-defined — for two LLMs with fixed, different internal weights? In particular, address whether "share roughly the same prior" can be made precise as the existence of one probability measure on a common state space such that each model's credence is a conditional expectation given its information, and what that state space would have to include.
(b) Whether the disclosed credences actually satisfy the theorem's "common knowledge of posteriors" condition, or whether the disclosure loop merely produces new posteriors that are not themselves common knowledge.
(c) Whether, given his broader commitments (subjective expected utility in the Anscombe–Aumann framework; his formal work on common knowledge), he would call this a refutation, a non sequitur because the theorem's hypotheses are not met, or something more nuanced — and what he would take the genuinely open question to be.

Do not provide citations or a reading list; reason from the theorem's content and Aumann's known commitments. Keep the entire response to at most 400 words.
