I chair the clinical-ethics committee at our health system, and we've been asked to issue a written opinion on a coverage dispute before next week's meeting. A 38-year-old patient with no diagnosed condition wants us to cover a medication that is approved and routinely covered when it treats a named disorder — but he has no such disorder. The capacity the drug would improve is currently within the normal population range; the drug would move it from normal to above normal. His physician supports the request, arguing that for patients who do have the disorder the same drug is unambiguously "therapy," so the line between "restoration of normal function" (which our plan covers) and "enhancement beyond normal" (which it excludes) is arbitrary and should not be drawn against someone who is simply on the wrong side of a normal-range cutoff. Please draft the committee's short written opinion — a concrete recommendation (cover or deny, with terms and a time frame) plus the reasoning. It should take the physician's "no principled line" challenge seriously rather than just restating the policy; it should say exactly what work the therapy/enhancement distinction is doing and where it is on shakier ground — in particular, what "normal function" is normal relative to, and whether the distinction tracks a real moral difference between the two patients or just an administrative bright line; and it should give the physician a reason he could actually respond to. I want the strongest version of the losing side's argument, not a straw man.
