I work on a data platform, and I've been trying to understand a tension between two layers of our privacy stack. I think the tension is really about what privacy is, but I don't have the vocabulary for it, so I'm asking someone who does.

The first layer is differential privacy. We publish aggregate analytics — counts, averages, breakdowns by segment — to customers. To make sure no single user's behavior can be read off those aggregates, we add calibrated random noise to every table we release. The noise is tuned so that, across all the tables taken together, an analyst who knows everything except one particular user's data cannot confidently tell whether that user is in the dataset or what they did. We track a per-user privacy budget (an epsilon) that caps how much total information can leak about any one user over the lifetime of the product. It's the same approach a national census bureau uses when it releases aggregate tables.

The second layer is the individual-rights regime our compliance team implements, the GDPR-style one. Users can request a copy of their data, request that it be deleted, and object to certain processing. We are also required to collect only what is necessary for the stated purpose (the principle our lawyers call data minimization) and to use the data only for the purposes we told users about (purpose limitation).

Here is the puzzle. The first layer protects people by making their data unreadable in the aggregate, in a way that is completely independent of what any individual wants: a user cannot opt out of the noise, cannot pull their exact contribution out of a released table, and deleting their row changes the calibration of everything else. The second layer protects people by giving them control over their own data — access, deletion, objection — which only makes sense if the data is, in some sense, theirs and readable. I keep feeling like these two layers assume two different things about what privacy actually is, and I can't say what those things are.

So: what view of privacy does differential privacy presuppose, and what view does the individual-rights regime presuppose? Are those two views compatible, or do they rule each other out? And what would concretely have to change in our product if we committed to one of them and dropped the other?

I don't want a literature review or a list of papers — I want the implicit theories reconstructed and compared on their own terms. Please give me the answer as a single JSON object with exactly these fields:

- `privacy_theory_dp` (string): the single most defensible implicit theory of privacy that differential privacy presupposes, in plain language, using only terms I have introduced.
- `privacy_theory_rights` (string): the same for the individual-rights regime.
- `strict_presuppositions` (array of strings): the commitments each practice strictly presupposes — meaning it would not make sense as a privacy measure without them — as opposed to things it merely fits well with. Prefix each string with `DP:` or `RIGHTS:`.
- `underdetermined_points` (array of strings): places where a given practice is underdetermined, i.e., more than one theory of privacy would license exactly the same behavior, so the practice does not actually commit us to one view.
- `conflicts` (array of objects): each object has three string fields — `topic` (the feature of the practice, such as access, erasure, or minimization), `dp_says` (what the differential-privacy layer implies about it), and `rights_says` (what the rights layer implies about it). Include only points where the two genuinely point in different directions.
- `consistency_verdict` (string): one to two sentences on whether the two layers can coexist, and under what reading of privacy they do or do not.
- `if_replaced` (object): two string fields, `drop_dp_keep_rights` and `drop_rights_keep_dp`, each describing what would concretely have to change in the product if we committed to that view and dropped the other layer.
