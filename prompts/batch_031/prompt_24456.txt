Two friends of mine got into a long argument over dinner about whether Occam's razor is a deep fact about the world or just a useful rule of thumb, and one of them kept bringing in an idea he'd been reading about called Solomonoff induction. I'm not an expert on any of this, so I'm going to lay out the argument the way I remember it, and I'd like you to referee it.

Here's the setup as I understood it. Solomonoff induction is supposed to be the perfect way to make predictions. You imagine listing every possible computer program, and each program is a candidate for how the world actually works. Shorter programs get more weight, because a short program is a simple explanation of the data. The claim is that if you could actually run this procedure, it would eventually be at least as good as any other prediction method you could write down, because its prior already spreads a little weight over every one of them. That universality is what makes it interesting.

Marco is the pro-Solomonoff one. He says this is the real foundation of Occam's razor: the reason we should prefer the simpler explanation isn't a vague taste, it's that the simpler explanation is literally more probable, because the perfect predictor assigns it more weight. So Occam's razor is a fact about the world, and any good scientist or AI should be approximating Solomonoff induction.

Lena is the skeptic. She says Solomonoff induction can't actually be run: to do it, you'd have to run every program, including ones that never finish, which is impossible. So it's an unattainable ideal, not a real method. And she says the prefer-the-shorter-program part is shakier than it looks, because how short a program is depends on which programming language you write it in, so the answer isn't even well-defined. On her view, Occam's razor is just a practical heuristic — when two models fit the data equally well, pick the simpler one — and it doesn't need any deep foundation.

Then they went back and forth. Marco said that being uncomputable doesn't make the idea wrong, just out of reach — the same way the ideal gas law is never exactly true for a real gas but is still the right target to aim at. Lena said that's not the same, because the ideal gas law is at least something you can approximate and test in a lab, whereas Solomonoff induction is something you can't even get close to in a principled way, so it's more decoration than tool. Marco conceded that for any fixed amount of data, the choice of programming language could in principle change which explanation wins, but he said that doesn't matter, because what matters is the long run, and in the long run every reasonable language gives the same answer. Lena pushed back that the long run is doing all the work: we never actually get there, and for every real decision made with finite data, the language choice could in principle matter, so the theory is telling us nothing we can use. Marco's last big point was that even if Solomonoff induction is only an ideal, it's the only thing that explains why simpler explanations are more likely rather than just more convenient, and without it, Occam's razor is just a mood. Lena's last word was that the whole construction also quietly assumes the world is itself computable — that it's being generated by some program — and if the world is genuinely random or non-computable, Solomonoff induction doesn't apply at all, so it can't be the foundation of a general rule like Occam's razor.

Can you go through their exchange and say who's right at each step — not just who wins overall? And where, if anywhere, do you think both of them are missing something?
