I've been meaning to sort out for myself when an act actually has moral worth, as opposed to just being the right thing that happened to get done, and I keep going around in circles. I'm a retired chemist, so I think in terms of mechanisms and counterfactuals, and what I want is a clean account of what it is about the agent's reasons that makes an act praiseworthy rather than merely right. I fell into all this when my granddaughter started an intro ethics class and kept hitting me with questions I couldn't answer, and I've since been reading a bit of Kant on my own (the shopkeeper who gives honest change so as not to ruin his reputation is the case I keep coming back to), but I don't want to just inherit his answer. I want to see what my own gut says and whether it hang together.

Here are my verdicts on a few cases. A cashier who gives exact change only because cheating would cost him his reputation: right act, no credit at all, and I'm sure everyone agrees. A person who sees a child drowning, is genuinely scared because they're a bad swimmer, but throws themselves in anyway because they think "I ought to save that child," and saves it: full moral credit, this is the case I'm most sure about. A different person who sees the same drowning child and, without any deliberation, just dives in from a spontaneous good impulse and saves it: I want to say full credit, but honestly I keep feeling the scared-but-duty-driven one has more worth, because they overcame a real inclination whereas the impulsive one would have done it anyway. Now, a person who donates a big sum to a good cause purely out of a cold, dispassonate sense of duty, feeling no warmth or connection to the cause: I want to give this full credit too, to stay consistent with the drowning case. But a different donor who gives to the same cause because it reminds them of their grandmother and they feel a deep affection for the people it helps, and who frankly wouldn't give if the cause didn't move them emotionally: I want to say this one has more worth than the cold-duty one, because there's a real connection to the value. And a person who donates to a cause they believe, mistakenly, to be the most effective one, with pure motives but flawed judgment: I think the motive still gets credit even though the act wasn't optimal.

The thing that's been nagging me is that I seem to rank duty above impulse in the drowning case but impulse above duty in the donating case, and I can't see why that's not just a contradiction. Is there a coherent principle that gets all of these, or close, stated precisely enough that I could test it on new cases myself? And what does it say about a case I haven't listed, like someone who, convinced they're doing a terrible thing, does an act that turns out to be exactly the right one? If no single principle works without getting too baroque, which of my verdicts should I drop, and is the simpler account that forces me to drop one actually better than the complicated one that saves them all?
