I'm putting together a problem set for my graduate Madhyamaka seminar and I keep wanting to test whether students can tell a legitimate use of Nāgārjuna's tetralemma from a bogus one, so I've been going by my own gut on a batch of cases and I'd like to see if there's a principle behind the pattern. Here's where I land.

The self — is it produced, not produced, both, or neither — the tetralemma applies cleanly. You reject all four horns and what's left is that the self is empty of intrinsic nature, not that there's no such thing as a person. That's the paradigm case for me.

Causation — does an effect really arise, not arise, arise both ways, or neither — I think it applies too, but the upshot is dependant origination, not "there's no causation." Rejecting all four is supposed to show that production is empty, not to deny that things happen. So it applies, but the reading has to be non-nihilistic.

Emptiness itself — is emptiness produced, not produced, both, or neither — I say it applies here as well. Emptiness is itself empty; if you let the tetralemma off the hook for emptiness, you've reified it into a new intrinsic nature, which is exactly the mistake the whole project is trying to prevent.

A table — does it exist, not exist, both, or neither — the tetralemma does not apply. At the conventional level the table exists, period, and running the tetralemma on it collapses the two truths into each other. Category error.

The two-truths distinction itself — is the split between conventional and ultimate real, not real, both, or neither — I think I have to apply the tetralemma and reject all four, which means the distinction is also empty. And I'm not sure that's coherent, because I just used that very distinction to justify the table verdict.

The law of excluded middle — does it hold, not hold, both, or neither — I say it does not apply. The tetralemma is a dialectical tool, not a thesis that LEM is false; I can reject all four horns about a thing while still using LEM to structure the rejection. (Though I grant the four-horn shape looks like it's mocking the two-horn shape, so I might be wrong.)

So the question. Is there a principle that gets all of these, or at least close? My working guess is "the tetralemma applies to claims that purport to establish intrinsic nature and is suspended for mere conventional designations" — but I'm not sure that survives the two-truths case, since criterion itself looks like an intrinsic-nature claim, and I'm not sure it survives the LEM case either.

What I want from you: the principle, stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause. Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop, if any? And what does it predict for a case I haven't listed — whether the tetralemma applies to "the Buddha's teachings are true"? Commit to something. Pick the principle you actually think is right, tell me which verdict dies, and defend it. Don't hand me a two-level account and a uniform account and let me pick.
