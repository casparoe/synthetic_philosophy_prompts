I just spent an evening arguing with a friend about whether "the will of the people" is a real thing or a useful fiction, and I'd like a careful audit of the analogy he kept reaching for. His move was this: whenever I objected that there is no single people's will — only a bundle of individual preferences that happen to be tallied, many of them selfish, misinformed, or shaped by whoever controls the information flow — he answered that the people are a body. A body, he said, has one will, not a million cells each wanting different things; you don't get a person's will by adding up what every cell wants, and likewise you don't get the people's will by adding up what every citizen wants. So "the will of the people" is a genuine, higher-level, integrated will of the polity, and any dismissal of it as mere aggregation is a category mistake. From there he concluded that the popular will, once expressed in a vote, is authoritative in a way that a bare aggregate of private interests is not.

I know this organic metaphor — the body politic — is an old one and runs throughout the general-will tradition, so I don't want to treat it as a sloppy figure of speech. But I also can't shake the feeling that it is doing all the work in his argument. Can you audit how far the body analogy actually holds? Specifically: (1) give me the correspondence table — what in the body maps onto what in the polity (the cells, the brain or nervous system, the single will, the good of the organism, and so on); (2) lay out the disanalogies, and separate the ones that matter for the particular conclusion he is drawing (unity, non-aggregation, authority) from the ones that are just noise; (3) flag the features of a real body that the analogy quietly imports but a population does not have — especially anything about how a single will gets produced in the first place, and about the priority of the whole over the parts; and (4) say whether his conclusion — that the will of the people is one integrated, authoritative thing rather than an aggregate — survives once those disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole time. If the organic picture is the wrong vehicle, say what a better analogy would be, or whether there is one.

One more thing: since this is an audit rather than a settled result, please note where your own analysis takes liberties or leans on a contested reading of what the analogy is supposed to do, and give a sense of how confident you are in each part of the verdict, singling out the point you are least sure of.
