The units-of-selection debate -- whether natural selection acts on genes, on organisms, on groups, or on something else -- has been running for more than forty years, and the positions keep getting restated without converging. I want a diagnosis of what this disagreement actually is, not a survey of the positions or the literature.

Here is how the debate looks from where I stand; correct me where I am wrong. One camp holds that the gene is the unit of selection because it is the unit of inheritance -- the entity that is replicated with fidelity across generations -- and that higher-level talk is convenient shorthand. The other camp holds that selection acts on organisms and groups because that is where the causal structure of selection -- variation, heritability, differential reproductive success -- actually sits, and that the gene-level story is a bookkeeping trick. Between the two, there are formal results showing that a model of selection written at one level can often be rewritten, without loss of content, at another level, including the standard multilevel decomposition that splits the covariance between a gene and fitness into within-group and between-group components. If I understand these results right, they make the levels empirically equivalent, which ought to end the debate -- but it does not.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not about facts at all. It is about what the phrase "unit of selection" is supposed to do in an explanation: is it supposed to pick out the entity that is inherited, the entity on which the causal force of selection acts, the level at which the best explanation is written, or the entity whose fitness is the real one? And I suspect the equivalence results do not settle the debate, because they show that the models are intertranslatable, not that the levels are the same things.

Diagnose the debate on that basis. First, distinguish at least four senses in which "unit" could be meant -- unit of inheritance, unit of causal selection, unit of explanation, unit of fitness attribution -- and say which of the camps, as they actually argue, is using which sense, and whether any camp is consistent across its own arguments. Second, say what the intertranslatability results do and do not show: whether they dissolve the debate, merely relocate it, or fail to apply to some cases, for instance where the levels would make different predictions. Third, classify what remains: is the residual disagreement verbal, substantive but not empirical (a clash about what a good evolutionary explanation is), or a hidden empirical question (about, say, whether group-level heritability is large enough in nature to matter)? Fourth, given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress -- a distinction, a formal result, a kind of empirical finding, or a change of question -- or argue that no progress should be expected and why.

Structure the response in four sections, in this order, with these approximate lengths: "The senses of 'unit'" (about 250 words), "What the equivalence results show" (about 250 words), "The kind of disagreement" (about 250 words), "What would count as progress" (about 150 words). No citations or references are needed; argue from the structure of the positions themselves.
