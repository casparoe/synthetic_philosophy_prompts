In discussions of Aumann's agreement theorem, a common way to build intuition is the free-bet argument: if two Bayesian agents with a common prior keep disagreeing about the probability of some event, then one can construct a wager that each of them, given his own information, expects to win — a free bet — but under a shared prior no such bet can exist, so the persistent disagreement is impossible. Someone I'm reading uses this free-bet picture to explain why the theorem holds, and to argue that the slogan that rational agents cannot agree to disagree is really just a no-arbitrage condition in disguise. How far does the free-bet analogy actually hold? Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the trader, the market price, the arbitrage, and the no-arbitrage condition — then the places where it breaks down, especially anything about the role of common knowledge versus mere mutual knowledge of each other's posteriors, about the fact that the bet must be a zero-sum transfer defined on a shared state space indexed by the agents' information partitions rather than an actual contract, and about the gap between the impossibility of agreeing to disagree and the stronger no-free-bet condition that turns out to be necessary and sufficient for the existence of a common prior. And tell me whether the slogan survives once those disanalogies are on the table, or whether the free-bet picture was quietly doing the work.
