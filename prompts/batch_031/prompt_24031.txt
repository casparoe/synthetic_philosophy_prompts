I'm preparing a close-reading seminar on William Golding's *Lord of the Flies* and I want to run it through David Lewis's account of conventions — the one developed in *Convention*, where a convention is a regularity that solves a coordination problem, is arbitrary in the sense that the opposite regularity would have solved the problem just as well, and holds because each participant expects the others to follow it and knows that they expect it too, so it is sustained by mutual expectation and common knowledge rather than by any intrinsic merit of the particular regularity. I want to test that account against the novel and see where it illuminates, where it strains, and where the book resists being read this way.

Start with the conch. When the boys gather and it becomes the token of the right to speak, it looks like a textbook convention: the particular sound is arbitrary (any signal would do), its force comes entirely from the shared expectation that everyone will honor whoever holds it, and it solves a real coordination problem (who gets a turn to talk when everyone is talking). Walk through whether the conch actually satisfies Lewis's conditions — especially the arbitrariness condition and the requirement that it be held by mutual expectation — and whether it is a convention in Lewis's technical sense or something else: a custom, a piece of theater, a claim to authority, or a habit that only looks like a convention.

Then push on the rest of the island's order — the assembly, the signal fire, the early, earnest insistence on rules — and ask whether these form a stable convention or are already failing Lewis's test from the start (held by mutual expectation, or by the charisma of whoever is leading, or by fear?). The central question for me: the conch is eventually smashed, and the order it sustained has broken down into the hunt and the killings of Simon and Piggy. Lewis's account treats conventions as stable equilibria that persist as long as the mutual expectations hold; but the novel is essentially a story about a convention dying. Does that mean the boys' order was never a convention in Lewis's sense to begin with, or does it show something the account underplays — that conventions are fragile, that the underlying coordination problem can re-open, and that a convention can be overthrown rather than merely going out of fashion?

Finally, the ending: the naval officer arrives, the boys weep, and the adult world reasserts its own conventions — uniforms, rank, the rules of civilization — as if they were natural. I want a reading that takes seriously whether the novel is making a genuinely Lewisian point here (that all conventions, including the officer's, are arbitrary and sustained only by mutual expectation, so the island's collapse was no more a catastrophe than the officer's world is a triumph) or whether it is doing something else entirely (diagnosing a darkness in human nature that conventions merely paper over) and forcing it into the convention frame distorts it.

Give me the honest critic's verdict rather than a tidy illustration: where the Lewisian reading genuinely illuminates a scene or a choice, where it has to be strained to fit, and what the novel adds to the question of how conventions arise and hold that the argument in *Convention* alone does not. I'm teaching this to a mixed group of philosophy and literature students, so I'd rather have the places where the reading breaks flagged than papered over.
