I'm taking a course on global risks this term and we've been working through the unilateralist's curse — roughly, the idea that when an action will be taken if any one of many agents judges it worthwhile, it is disproportionately likely to be taken by an agent whose judgment is wrong on the optimistic side, so a rational agent should discount their own estimate of the action's value. I've been trying to pin down when the curse actually bites by going by my gut on concrete cases. Releasing a superintelligent AI when roughly fifty labs could each do it: I think I should discount my estimate heavily. Releasing a gene drive into a wild mosquito population when a handful of labs could do it: I think I should discount, but less so. Publishing a detailed synthesis protocol for a dangerous pathogen when many independent researchers could publish it: I think I should discount. Starting a tech startup in a saturated market when thousands of entrepreneurs are doing the same: I don't think I should discount my estimate of whether my particular startup will succeed. Deciding whether to have a child when millions of couples are making the same decision: I don't think I should discount my estimate of whether it is a good idea for me and my partner. The case I keep going back and forth on is releasing a superintelligent AI from a closed, single-lab effort that only my institution could pull off — the outcome, that a superintelligent AI exists, feels like the same public outcome as the open multi-lab case, yet there is no pool of rival agents whose optimistic errors could drive the action. Is there a principle that captures all of these verdicts, or at least most of them, and what does it say about the single-lab case? And if no single principle works, which of my verdicts should I be willing to give up, and why?
