I'm drafting a short piece on Black's two-sphere example and the identity of indiscernibles and I'd like a second pair of eyes on a couple of moves before I send it out. Here's the core of it:

The standard way the example is introduced is as a counterexample to the identity of indiscernibles, full stop. But I think that overstates it, because the principle only has bite once you restrict which properties count. If you let in properties that contain singular terms — "being identical to a," "being two diameters from b" — then any two distinct objects automatically differ in at least one property (a has "being identical to a" and b doesn't), so the principle is trivially true and the spheres are trivially discernible. The example is only doing work against the "pure" version, the one that quantifies over properties that don't contain singular terms. And I'd argue the pure version is exactly the one that does the philosophical work — in the debates about whether there could be two exactly similar possible worlds, or about the individuation of particles in physics — so the example is a genuine counterexample to the principle as its actually used, not just to a strawman.

The move I'm least sure about is the relational-property objection. Someone will say: but the spheres have relational properties, like "being at a fixed distance from the other sphere," so they're not really indiscernible. I want to argue that allowing relational properties doesn't save the principle, because in a universe with exactly two spheres and nothing else, every relational property either sphere has (expressed without a singular term) is had by both. "Being at distance d from the other sphere" is had by both. "Being the nearer sphere" picks out neither, since there's no fact of the matter. So the relational properties don't break the symmetry. I keep coming back to this and I can't quite shake the feeling I'm missing a relational property that would distinguish them.

I also want to address the haecceitist reply: accept haecceity properties ("being a") and the spheres are discernible, so the example fails. I want to grant this but call it pyrrhic, because once you let haecceities in, the identity of indiscernibles is trivially true and useless for the work it's supposed to do. I'm not sure I'm being fair to the haecceitist here, though — maybe they'd say the principle was never meant to be a substantive constraint and that its real role is something else.

Can you tell me: (1) is there actually a relational property, expressible without singular terms, that one of the spheres has and the other doesn't, that I'm missing? (2) is the "pyrrhic" framing of the haecceitist reply fair, or am I mischaracterizing what the haecceitist is for? (3) does the overall framing — that the example is only a counterexample to the pure version, and that the pure version is the one that matters — track, or is there a cleaner way to state the thesis?
