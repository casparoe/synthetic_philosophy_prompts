I'm a TA for an undergraduate set theory course, and I'm grading the midterm problem on the independence of the continuum hypothesis. The problem I set was: "State the continuum hypothesis. Explain what it means to say that CH is independent of ZFC, and describe the contributions of Gödel (1938–40) and Cohen (1963). In each case, be precise about what the result establishes and what it assumes." One student's answer is below. I'd like a point-by-point assessment — which claims are correct, which are wrong or fail to follow, and what the answer misses — together with a grade out of 10 and the specific comments that justify it. Lead with your bottom line (the grade and a one-sentence justification) in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.

Student's answer:

"CH is the assertion that 2^ℵ0 = ℵ1, i.e. that there is no cardinal strictly between ℵ0 and 2^ℵ0. To say that CH is independent of ZFC is to say that, assuming ZFC is consistent, ZFC proves neither CH nor ¬CH.

Gödel's result comes from the constructible universe L. L is built by starting with the empty set and, at each stage, taking all the sets definable over the previous stage. Since each stage only adds definable sets, every real in L is in fact definable — there are 'not that many' of them. Gödel showed that L is a model of ZFC + V = L, and that V = L implies GCH and hence CH. So, assuming ZFC is consistent, so is ZFC + CH.

Cohen's result used forcing. Starting from a model of ZFC, Cohen constructed a generic extension containing new reals, in which the continuum is larger than ℵ1, so CH fails. Thus, assuming ZFC is consistent, so is ZFC + ¬CH.

One thing follows from all this: since both CH and ¬CH are consistent with ZFC, CH has no determinate truth value — it is not really a mathematical fact, and a mathematician who takes a side is just making an aesthetic choice."
