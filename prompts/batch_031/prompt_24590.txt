I work in constructive mathematics, and there is a disagreement among people who all call themselves "constructive" that has gone on, in my experience, for decades without anyone seeming to move the other side. I'd like a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement rather than a verdict on who is right.

The concrete case is Markov's principle — for a decidable predicate on the natural numbers, if it is not the case that the predicate fails everywhere, then it holds somewhere — together with the wider family of principles that are classically valid but not provable in plain intuitionistic arithmetic, such as the limited principle of omniscience and the fan theorem (the compactness of the space of binary sequences).

The positions, as I understand them, are:

- Brouwerian intuitionists reject these principles. Their reason is not merely that the principles are unprovable in intuitionistic arithmetic, but that they are actively refuted by the intuitionistic conception of the continuum: on the continuum of free choice sequences, every total function is continuous, and that continuity principle contradicts the usual (strong) form of Markov's principle and refutes omniscience principles such as the limited principle of omniscience. For them a "function" is something defined on free choice sequences, and the principles in question license reasoning that no construction on that continuum can deliver.

- Russian constructivists in the Markov school accept Markov's principle, typically with a computability or realizability reading of what a function is: a function is a computable procedure, and under that reading the principle becomes harmless, indeed realizable.

- Bishop-style constructivists take a middle position: their base system does not derive Markov's principle, so it is not part of the common core they want classical and intuitionistic mathematicians to share; but they do not reject it the way Brouwerians do, and they do not adopt continuity principles either. They aim for a minimal core and treat such principles as optional add-ons whose status depends on the base theory.

The frustrating thing is that these three groups have been arguing past each other for a long time, and I do not think it is because the arguments on any side are bad. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not really about Markov's principle at all, but about what the continuum is and what a function on it is: Brouwerians quantify over free choice sequences, the Russian school over computable functions, and Bishop's people over whatever functions their base system can construct — so when each camp writes "for every function" or "for every predicate" they are ranging over different domains, and the "same" sentence has different truth conditions in each camp.

Is that right? Or is the impasse better explained as (a) a verbal disagreement over the label "constructive," (b) a purely proof-theoretic disagreement about which base theory is the standard and which principles count as theorems versus axioms, or (c) a substantive ontological disagreement about mathematical objects that neither side can argue for because it rests on unargued philosophical commitments? I want a diagnosis, supported by what each side actually says and does, not a survey of the history. And given your diagnosis, tell me honestly what, if anything, would count as progress — a separation or independence result, a shared formalization that pins down the base theory, a conceptual distinction, a change of question — or whether this is the kind of impasse where no progress should be expected, and why.
