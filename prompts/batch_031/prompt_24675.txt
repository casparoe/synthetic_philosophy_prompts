I'm a retired physiologist — I spent most of my career on homeostatic control and on the quantitative, signal-estimation side of the field — and I've only recently started reading philosophy seriously, so I'd like you to work in the vocabulary I actually think in rather than the philosophical one. Take the standard three-way split of the debate about what emotions are and restate each of the three, as faithfully as you can, in the language of control and estimation theory. The three positions are: (i) emotions as cognitive judgments or appraisals — the claim that an emotion just is a certain kind of evaluation of the situation; (ii) emotions as perceptions — the claim that an emotion is a kind of perception, of the world or of one's own body and circumstances; and (iii) emotions as raw feelings — the claim that the essence of an emotion is its felt, phenomenal quality, the subjective character of the experience. For each, translate into the terms of sensors and effectors, set points and error signals, feedback loops and gain, state estimation and observers, homeostasis, and the probabilistic machinery of likelihoods, priors, posteriors, and expected loss. For each of the three, say what translates cleanly, what has to be distorted to fit, and what has no expression at all in that vocabulary.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I care about most. First, which claims in the emotions debate carry over into the control-and-estimation vocabulary intact, which survive only with distortion, and which cannot be expressed in it at all. Second, test the translation against what I take to be the real stress test: a person whose appraisal and bodily error signals are fully present — they know the situation is threatening, their set points are being violated, their feedback loops are firing — but who reports no felt emotion at all, and the converse, a strong felt emotion with a flat bodily error signal. Can your translation describe these two dissociations, or do they fall in the gap? Third, judge, as honestly as you can, whether that residue points to something the control-theoretic framework is genuinely missing, or to something the philosophical claims are less clear about than they look — in particular, whether feeling is doing real work that no error signal, no posterior, and no observer can capture, or whether it is just a term the philosophers never got around to defining.

I'll add my own prior so you can tell me how far off it is: I'm inclined to think the felt-quality view is the one that should resist translation hardest, and I'd be surprised if it survives intact — so tell me whether my prior is right and why, and what a physiologist should expect to be left over on the table.
