I've been thinking, mostly on my own and without any formal training in philosophy, about when we're justified in saying that a system's inner states are "about" something — when a thing's outputs genuinely represent the world, as opposed to merely tracking it by wiring. A friend described a small machine to me at dinner that I keep turning over: a box with no screen and no memory of past inputs, that when you feed it a simple sentence like "the cat is on the mat" lights a green lamp if and only if the cat really is on the mat, and a red lamp otherwise, its circuitry running straight from input to output. I find myself willing to say that when the green lamp comes on, that lamp is "about" the cat being on the mat, in some thin and secondhand way — but I would never say the box *believes* the cat is on the mat, or that it thinks anything at all. It is about the world only insofar as we are the ones reading it that way, and I want to understand what exactly that "insofar as" amounts to.

Here are my verdicts on a handful of cases, and I'd like to know what single principle, if any, captures the pattern, and which of my verdicts it forces me to give up. (1) The box above: its green lamp is "about" the cat, but the box has no belief. (2) The same box rebuilt from gears and levers, with no electronics, and with a ten-second delay before the lamp: I give it exactly the same verdict as (1), because I don't think the material or the speed should matter. (3) A thermostat that holds a room at twenty-one degrees: I do *not* say its dial is "about" the temperature; it just tracks it, and I want to reserve "about" for something more. (4) A chess program that, mid-game, sits in a state we'd naturally describe as "thinking the queen is in danger": I say it is "about" the board in the same thin sense as the box, but still not a belief. (5) My friend, who genuinely believes the cat is on the mat and is in fact wrong: this is a full belief, with no thinness about it. (6) A person in a deep coma who, when a nurse reads a simple sentence aloud, squeezes a hand "yes" for true sentences and "no" for false ones, yet shows no other sign of mental life — no recognition of faces, no answer to their own name: here I genuinely waffle, and I can't tell whether to sort them with the box or with my friend.

The pairs that keep biting me are (1) and (5): I ascribe a thin "aboutness" to the box's lamp but a full belief to my friend, and I can't say in one breath what the box lacks that my friend has, except that my friend has a whole web of other beliefs and desires the box doesn't — but I'm not sure that's a principle or just a description of the difference. And (3) is the one I can't reconcile with (1): if the box's lamp counts as "about" the cat because it tracks the cat in a general, repeatable way, why doesn't the thermostat's dial count as "about" the temperature in the same way? I suspect the real difference is that the box's output could in principle be about many different cats and mats — the same lamp, fed different sentences — whereas the thermostat's dial is locked to one temperature, but I'm not confident that's what's doing the work. Could you state the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, precisely; say which of my cases motivate each clause; work out what the principle predicts for a case I haven't listed, such as a weather model that "predicts" rain or a person who has exactly one belief and nothing else; and honestly tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate?
