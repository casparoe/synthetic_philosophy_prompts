i cover science and ideas for a magazine and last week at a conference i got stuck in the middle of a fight between two people i'm both writing about, and now i can't tell who's making the better point and i have a piece due. one was a philosopher of mind, the other a logician, and they were going at it over whether conceivability is a guide to possibility. i've left them anonymous since they didn't know i was taking notes. here's the rough back and forth as i remember it:

the philosopher of mind started by defending the idea that conceivability is a genuine guide to possibility — that if you can coherently conceive of P, then P is possible, and that's exactly what makes the zombie argument do its work. the logician said that's backwards: i can conceive of a married bachelor, so conceivability plainly doesn't track what's possible. the philosopher of mind replied that the married bachelor case is a different beast — that's not a failure to imagine a state of affairs, it's a failure to notice the meaning of the words, a conceptual contradiction like a square circle, and the real test is whether you can conceive of P while holding fixed everything you know a posteriori about the actual world. under that test, water not being H2O is not conceivable, because once you've learned the identity you can't coherently imagine the actual world's water being something else, whereas a married bachelor isn't even conceivable in the loose sense. the logician said that move just re-labels the problem: "holding fixed everything you know a posteriori" is doing all the work, and the moment you stipulate that in, you've got a definition of epistemic possibility doing the work of conceivability, so you've only shown that conceivability tracks epistemic possibility, which is trivial and says nothing about metaphysical possibility. the philosopher of mind said that's fine, because what's interesting in the zombie argument is the epistemic gap itself — the fact that i can't rule out zombies a priori is the interesting thing — and the move from epistemic to metaphysical possibility is a seperate, defensible step that only requires assuming the identity in question, consciousness with the physical, isn't a necessary a posteriori one. the logician shot back that that step is exactly where the argument is doing its real work, and that premise — that the consciousness-physical identity isn't a necessary a posteriori one — is just the denial of physicalism in disguise, so you're assuming what you're trying to prove.

neither would back down, and i honestly can't tell who's making the better point. can you walk through their exchange and say who's right about what — not just who wins overall? i dont need a bibliography or a reading list, just the adjudication so i can get the shape of the disagreement right before i write.
