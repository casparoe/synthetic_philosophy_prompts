I was discussing with a friend who collects art the puzzle of why the market pays enormous premiums for authenticated originals. Consider a hypothetical perfect forgery of a Vermeer: every pigment, brushstroke, and trace of canvas aging is indistinguishable from the original by any test, and even the forger's confession doesn't let the eye tell the two apart. Once the forgery is exposed, its price collapses to a fraction of the original's, even though anyone standing in front of the two side by side has the same visual experience of both. The practice runs in a strange double, too: auction houses and conservators treat the original as irreplaceable — a degraded original is restored rather than replaced by a perfect copy, and a lost original is mourned as a genuine loss even though a perfect replica could be made — while museums and art historians happily study, exhibit, and write about forgeries, treating them as objects of interest in their own right.

What view of value does the market's authenticity premium presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does the practice presuppose that the causal-historical connection between an object and its maker is itself part of the object's value, so that a perfect forgery is genuinely worse, not merely known to be worse; or that value lies purely in the experience an object provides, and the premium is explained by the fact that knowing an object to be an original changes the experience of it; or that "originality" is a status conferred by institutions — the artworld, the market, the museum — and the premium is a social fact that tracks no deeper value? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, i.e. which of these theories would license exactly the same market behavior. Then tell me what would have to change in the practice if the presupposed view were replaced by a rival one: what would auction houses, conservators, and museums do differently if the "knowledge changes the experience" view were the true one?

Finally, tell me where your reasoning is most likely to fail: which argument or consideration would change your verdict? For instance, if the premium persisted even when the buyer knew the forgery was perfect and that no one could ever tell the two apart, or if the premium were driven purely by investment speculation rather than aesthetic value, would either of those change which theory the practice presupposes?
