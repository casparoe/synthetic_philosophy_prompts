I'm putting together the specification for a seminar on the probability of natural-language conditionals, and before I survey the competing theories I want the requirements any adequate theory must meet. Write the desiderata for a theory of the indicative conditional — the ordinary, non-counterfactual "if p, then q" as it is asserted in everyday reasoning — with the purpose fixed this way: the theory has to be good enough to model ordinary conditional belief, that is, to say what it is for a rational agent to assign a degree of belief to an asserted indicative conditional, and to explain why, in ordinary cases, that degree tracks the conditional probability the agent would assign to the consequent on the supposition of the antecedent.

For each desideratum, give the motivating case or consideration behind it — the phenomenon or intuition that forces the requirement — and mark which requirements you take to be genuine (forced by the phenomenon itself) and which are preferences of a particular camp that a rival could legitimately reject without giving up the phenomenon. I expect part of the list to be disputed, and I'd rather you flag the disputes than present a smooth consensus.

The part I care about most: state whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — that is, whether the requirements cannot all be met by a single theory — and if so, give the argument for the inconsistency, name the smallest conflicting subset, and say which member of that subset does the most work in generating the conflict and which is the one a theory is most rationally positioned to drop. I take it that there is a well-known negative result in this area, usually associated with Lewis, showing that a natural conjunction of these requirements is unsatisfiable; reconstruct that result from the desiderata rather than just citing its name, and be precise about exactly which requirements enter the conflict and which are needed only as background. If you conclude that no such subset exists, say so and explain what blocks the conflict.

Then, briefly, score the main existing approaches against the list: the possible-worlds or strict-conditional approaches, the probabilist or Ramsey-style approaches that identify the probability of a conditional with the corresponding conditional probability, the approaches that deny the conditional expresses a proposition at all, and the approaches that keep the conditional a proposition but refuse to equate its probability with the conditional probability. For each, note which desiderata it satisfies and which it sacrifices, and whether it escapes the inconsistency by dropping a genuine desideratum or by dropping only a camp preference.

Finally, say where your own reasoning is most likely to fail: which of your judgments about which requirements are genuine versus prejudicial you'd most expect to be overturned under pressure, which consideration would change your mind about which requirement a theory should sacrifice, and which single background assumption the whole specification quietly depends on, such that if it were dropped the list of requirements would come out very differently.
