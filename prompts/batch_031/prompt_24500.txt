I'm designing a 75-minute session for a 12-student undergraduate seminar on the ethics of AI. The students have read about reward hacking in reinforcement-learning agents, but I haven't yet introduced the Goodhart literature, and a colleague warned me that the standard "count the words and watch the word counts explode" activity is too on-the-nose — the students see through it in round one and stop taking the target seriously. I want a game in which the divergence between the measure and the target emerges in what happens, not in what I say afterwards: teams of three build a small artifact in a blind round, in which I score them on a proxy metric I keep secret; then I reveal the metric, and they get a second, shorter round to improve their score. The target should be something I can judge fairly and quickly — a comprehension test, a physical test, something with a right answer — and the proxy should be objectively countable by hand in under two minutes per team, and cheaply verifiable, so that a team can't simply misreport it (or, if you think unverifiability is a feature of the exercise, say so and handle it). Two further constraints: the proxy must be a genuinely decent proxy in the middle of the distribution — not a strawman — so that the divergence appears only under selection or optimization pressure; and I must not see the proxy score before judging the target, or my judgment will be anchored, so tell me how to keep the two evaluations independent. A readability-style count (say, the number of words of six letters or fewer) as a proxy for comprehension is the target/proxy pair I'm leaning toward, but propose a better one if you have it and say why yours is decent in the middle. By the end of the session, the students should be able to state, in their own words, the difference between "the measure is a bad description" and "the measure is a good description but a bad target," and no one should hear the word Goodhart until the debrief.

In the debrief, I want the game's moments mapped onto the four subtypes of Goodhart effects that I keep seeing in the literature: regressional (the measure is a noisy proxy, so ranking by it is noisy), extremal (the further you select on the measure, the more you are selecting on the noise, so the top scorers are the least representative of what the measure promised), causal (the measure and the target share a common cause, so pushing the measure doesn't move the target), adversarial (the agent actively optimizes the measure, and the measure-target correlation degrades as the optimization pressure increases). I'm not sure the first two are really distinct — regressional and extremal look like the same mechanism at different intensities — so tell me whether the game can actually let participants distinguish them, and which of the four the game cannot reproduce at all and what I have to supply in words. And since the whole point of the session is the bridge to AI, tell me which part of the game is the analogue of the reward function and which is the analogue of the environment, and where the analogy breaks down: the human teams in my game can't optimize the proxy the way a trained agent can, and I want to be honest with the students about how much of the reward-hacking intuition the game actually generates versus how much I have to add. Deliver the minute-by-minute timeline, the materials list (no real money; a trivial prize is fine), the exact scoring rule for the proxy, the debrief script at the level of "at this moment, say this," what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how to handle each failure mode.
