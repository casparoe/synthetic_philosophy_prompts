I played the dollar auction in a decision-making class last spring (prof puts a twenty on the board, highest bidder wins it but the second-highest also has to pay their bid, that kind of thing) and it stuck with me, because somewhere around the middle everyone's hands just kept going up and I could feel the arithmetic dragging me to keep bidding even though I *knew* it was a trap. So I've been trying to figure out what I actual believe about when it's rational to keep going once you've already put something in, and whether there's a real principle behind my gut or if I'm just inconsistent.

Here's where my instincts land. In the dollar auction itself, if I'm at nineteen and the other guy is at eighteen, I should bid twenty — that's just math, my alternative is walking away having lost nineteen, so escalate, no shame. But if I've spent three years on a side project and I can see its not going anywhere and I keep telling myself "I've come this far," I think I'm being an idiot and should kill it. Same with a stock I bought at a hundred that's now at sixty — the hundred shouldn't enter the decision at all, only where it's headed. And a country that's already lost ten thousand soldiers in a campaign shouldn't let that make it more willing to keep fighting, the dead are dead. So far this is all just "ignore the past, look forward." But then: I've been with my partner for fifteen years and it's not good, and I *don't* think "we've invested too much to leave" is simply wrong here — something about the shared history, the kids, the actual fabric of the life together, feels like it has value that isn't just "future payoff," and I can't tell if that's a real thing or me rationalizing. And one more that muddies it: in a house bidding war, once I've offered five hundred K and the sellers counter, I feel sort of locked in, and part of me says that's the sunk-cost fallacy, but another part says my visible commitment has actually changed how the sellers will treat me, so quitting now would be strategically dumb — which is exactly the shape of the dollar-auction move.

So is there one principle that captures all of these, or at least most, and what does it say specifically about the marriage case and the house case? And if the cleanest principle forces me to drop one of my verdicts — I'm guessing it's either the dollar-auction "no shame" one or the marriage one — I'd rather know which and why than get a fudge that saves everything.
