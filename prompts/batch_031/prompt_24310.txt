I am a novelist, and I am writing a novel in which a theoretical physicist and a literary critic argue about whether a fictional character is "real" in the same sense that a rock is real, and I need the philosophy to be able to withstand a careful reader who has read Dennett. The position I am asking you to attack is the one my critic character holds, and it is, as I understand it, the standard Dennettian one: the three stances — the physical, the design, and the intentional — are not three different kinds of fact about an object, but three different interpretive regimes, each of which is legitimate only up to the point at which the predictions it yields cease to be more economical than the raw data; and the "truth" of any ascription made at the design or intentional level is a real pattern in the sense Dennett develops in "Real Patterns," i.e. a pattern that is observer-independent in the sense that any sufficiently sophisticated system with the right inputs can be expected to converge on it, but that is real in virtue of that convergence rather than in virtue of any brute correspondence with mind-independent microstructure.

What I want is a map of what would count against this position, with the following demands:

First, distinguish what would count against it from what would merely be awkward for it, and give me the strongest candidates for each, ranked by how decisive they would be.

Second, include arguments from within the Dennettian tradition itself, or from sympathetic quarters, not only the stock objections from the usual critics; I want to know what the position's own defenders would concede as counting against it, and what they would dismiss, and why.

Third, treat in particular the following points, which I suspect are where the position is most exposed: the asymmetry problem, namely why the physical stance seems to ground the other two if the three are merely regimes of description; the question of which compression ratio to use, and whether the convergence criterion is circular, since what counts as a sufficiently sophisticated observer seems to depend on what patterns are already real; the boundary case of a pattern that is true of no possible observer given finite resources, and whether such a pattern is real or not; the question of whether the intentional stance can be false in a way the design stance cannot, or vice versa, given that design ascriptions track actual design while intentional ascriptions track behavior; and finally what happens to responsibility, meaning, and value if they are all real patterns of this kind, and whether that is a consequence the position is committed to that turns out unacceptable.

Fourth, for each of these, say what a committed defender would concede and what they would dismiss, so that I can write a scene in which each character makes the strongest version of their side and both are right about something.

Close with an honest assessment of whether the position is well insulated, and whether that insulation is a problem for a view of this kind, and tell me what I, as the novelist, should find most persuasive about the strongest objection, since I want the critic character to lose the argument by a point rather than by a landslide.

I do not need a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I need the arguments themselves, stated in a way a novelist can turn into dialogue.
