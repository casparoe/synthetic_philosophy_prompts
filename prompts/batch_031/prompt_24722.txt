I'm in a set theory / foundations course and I've been going in circles over Skolem's paradox, and one of my study partners tried to cash it out into a straight-up argument that ZFC is unsound. I can't find the crack in it and I want to nail it down before the problemset is due. His argument, as he laid it out:

(1) If ZFC is consistent, the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem gives a countable model M of it. (2) M satisfies every theorem of ZFC, including the one saying the reals are uncountable. (3) So M has some set R that it takes to be the real line, and M satisfies "R is uncountable." (4) Unpacked, "M satisfies 'R is uncountable'" means that no element of M is a bijection from M's naturals onto R. (5) But M is countable, so I can list all of its elements in a sequence, and if I keep only the terms that fall in R I get a bijection from the naturals onto R. (6) So there really is a bijection from the naturals onto R. (7) But (2) and (4) together say M insists there is no such bijection, and ZFC itself proves there is no such bijection. (8) So ZFC proves a false statement, since (6) just handed me a counterexample. (9) Therefore ZFC is unsound. (10) And if ZFC is unsound then half of analysis and topology is built on sand, which I'm pretty sure is wrong.

I'm happy to grant (1) is a real theorem and I don't want to throw out the soundness of ZFC, so the mistake has to be hiding somewhere in the middle, but (4) through (8) all read to me like the same sentence rewritten four times and I cant see which one is doing any work. Can you point at the exact step that carries the argument, explain why it's dressed up to look like a harmless restatement, and tell me what I'd actually be giving up if I conceded that step instead of, say, (2) or (5)?
