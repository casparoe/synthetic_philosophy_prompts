I'm a junior taking a philosophy of biology seminar at my school, and our teacher has us working through the question of what, exactly, a biological function is. My group has split into two camps, and we've been arguing for about a month without making progress, so I want to try the format that working philosophers use in adversarial collaborations: drafting a single joint statement that a committed member of each camp could sign after good-faith discussion. One camp endorses the etiological account, the Millikan-style view that a trait's function is what it was selected for, so a trait's function is fixed by its evolutionary history. The other endorses the causal-role account, the Wright-style view that a trait's function is the role it currently plays in a causal chain that contributes to the fitness of its bearers. Please write that joint statement for us. It should open with a numbered list of claims both sides can accept, about what a satisfactory account of biological function must explain — malfunctions, the difference between a trait's function and its mere side effects, exaptation, the fact that we can meaningfully ask why organs fail — and about the phenomena themselves. Then it should give the precise statements of where the two views still differ, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording of its own view, followed by the cruxes, the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one side, and, where possible, for each crux, a concrete case or argument both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table.

To give you a sense of where we're stuck: last week one of us pointed out that if a trait's function just is its contribution to fitness, then a heart that has stopped contributing to its owner's fitness seems to have lost its function, and the other side replied that that is exactly what a malfunction is, and we couldn't get past each other. I suspect one of the real cruxes is whether "function" is a normative notion at all — the etiological side thinks a malfunction is a genuine failure relative to a standard set by selection history, while the causal-role side thinks a trait that fails to contribute simply has no such function — and another is how to handle exaptation, like the standard case of feathers apparently being co-opted for flight after being selected for a different purpose, such as thermoregulation, but I'd like you to find the cruxes yourself rather than just expanding on my guesses. If you conclude that part of the dispute is verbal, about what we mean by "function" rather than about the biology, say which part and why. Please don't end with a verdict on which camp is right; the point is a document both camps could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made as exact as possible.
