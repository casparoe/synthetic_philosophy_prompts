A friend of mine ran a dollar auction at a workshop last month. You know the setup: you bid on a dollar, the highest bidder pays their bid and gets the dollar, and the second-highest bidder pays their bid and gets nothing. It got up to about ninety-nine cents before both bidders dropped out, and two of my colleagues got into a pretty heated argument about who had made the mistake.

Marcus said the person who stopped at ninety-nine cents was the one who erred. His point was that once you're in the auction, the money you've already put in is sunk and shouldn't affect your next decision. The only question at each step is whether the next cent is worth it, and if you think there's a reasonable chance the other person will quit, then yes, the next cent is worth it. So the rational play, once you're in, is to keep going. And he said the "you should never have entered" argument is just ex post rationality — it's like saying the rational move in chess is to not sit down at the board. Once you're at the board, that advice is useless.

Elena said no, that's exactly the confusion. The rational move was never to enter, and the fact that you're in the auction doesn't change that. A strategy is supposed to be a complete plan you'd actually follow, and a plan that says "enter and then keep bidding" isn't a stable plan, because at every step you have a reason to deviate and stop. So the only rational strategy is not to play at all, and anyone who plays is, by definition, not playing rationally. The person who stopped at ninety-nine cents was the one who was acting rationally — they saw that the game has no rational play and cut there losses.

Marcus pushed back and said Elena was moving the goalposts. If "rational" just means "don't enter," then the question "what's the rational move in the game" has no answer, and her whole argument reduces to "the game is a trap, don't fall in it," which is a different claim from "the person who stopped was rational." You can't define rationality as "not playing" and then use it to grade moves that were actually made.

Elena said that was exactly the point. The game is a trap, and the rational response to a trap is to not fall in it, and if you do fall in it, to get out as fast as you can. There is no rational way to play a trap, and pretending there is just lets people rationalize their losses.

I'm especially curious about the ex ante vs. ex post thing — is it even coherent to ask "what was the rational move at ninety-nine cents?" when the only rational thing was not to be in the auction in the first place? And is there a sense in which the person who stopped was both right and wrong? If the whole question is confused in a way I'm not seeing, just say so plainly — I'd rather hear that than a polite split-the-difference verdict.
