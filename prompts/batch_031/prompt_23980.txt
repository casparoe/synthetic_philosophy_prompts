I'm giving a talk at a small workshop on conditionals next month and I want to be ready for the question period. My thesis is that the indicative/counterfactual distinction isn't a semantic one at all. There's just one conditional, and whether we say it in the indicative ('if it rains, the streets will be wet') or the counterfactual ('if it rained, the streets would be wet') is a matter of what the speaker takes the antecedent to be: a live possibility, or something we already know or assume to be false. So there aren't two different truth conditions, just one conditional plus a pragmatic marker of the speaker's expectation, and no separate counterfactual semantics doing any real work.

The room is going to be about six formal semanticists who live inside possible-worlds semantics (the Stalnaker-Lewis kind) and think of counterfactuals as having context-dependent truth conditions, plus two probabilists who are deep in the probability-of-conditionals literature and will immediately grab for the claim that the probability of 'if P then Q' is just P(Q|P). There are no traditional philosophers of language in the room who defend the old 'indicative conditionals express propositions' view, so I dont have to prep for that camp.

Give me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get from this room, phrased the way a possible-worlds semanticist or a probabilist would phrase them, ordered by how much damage they'd do. For each one, tell me what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and a short reply that works in about two minutes. And at the end, the one objection I'm hoping nobody asks but that would be the most embarassing if they did.
