I'm a clinical ethics consultant, and a case that keeps coming to me is a patient facing one of the big, irreversible decisions in their life — whether to have a first child, whether to have a major operation, whether to take a treatment that will change how they feel about themselves — where the usual advice of "imagine how you'll feel afterward and weigh it" breaks down, because they can't honestly picture an experience they've never had. I've been turning this over on my own, and I'd like a decision procedure I can actually run in a one-hour consultation: the questions to ask, in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, how the procedure handles the fact that the patient can't attach a reliable value to the outcome in advance, where it stops, and where it has to hand the decision back to the patient as a matter of their own values rather than something I can settle for them. Please ground it in whatever the philosophy of decision-making actually says about experiences that can only be known from the inside, but write it so someone without that background can follow it, and be honest about which steps are just judgment dressed up as procedure. Then run it on two cases: a woman in her thirties deciding whether to have her first child, and a patient deciding whether to have an operation that will end years of chronic pain but leave them in a wheelchair.
