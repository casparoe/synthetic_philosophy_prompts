So I spent about thirty years as an experimental physicist, and since retiring I've gotten into philosophy on the side, mostly the decision-theory stuff, because thats the part that feels like physics to me. I keep running into a result I think is called Good's theorem: if you're about to make a decision, it's never worse, in expectation, to get more information first. I've read the proof and I believe it, but I can't feel it, and I suspect that's why a little voice in my head keeps objecting. I run a small monthly workshop at the community college -- 14 to 18 people, a mix of retirees and grad students, 75 minutes, one room with tables -- and I want to design an activity that makes the participants feel the theorem themselves instead of hearing me explain it. So: give me a game or a structured exercise with real rules, roles, and payoffs (no real money, chips or points are fine) where the structure of the game makes them run into the phenomenon on their own -- the part where the information looks useless or like it's going to be bad news, and they refuse it, and then discover they should have taken it. I'd like at least one round where the information is provably worthless, so they can feel the contrast with the rounds where it's worth something. And I want the setup to make it possible for a single peek to actually cost someone in that round, so that in the debrief I can show them that the theorem is about the average over all the signals you might get, not about any one signal. What I want them to walk away seeing is that information is never a net liability in expectation, and that its value is exactly zero right where it can't change what you do. Please give me the rules, the payoffs, a timeline for the 75 minutes, and a debrief that maps the moments that actually happened in the room to the pieces of the theorem. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be frank about wich parts of the theorem the activity can't show and I'll have to just tell them -- I'd rather know that in advance than discover it in front of the group.
