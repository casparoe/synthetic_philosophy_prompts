my advisor keeps pushing this on me and i cant get out of it. he thinks moral status is computational — that what makes a system a moral patient is that it's running a computation with the right functional organization, the kind that's the basis of experience. and he thinks the attribution problem is a fatal objection to that. here's his argument:

(1) if a system has moral status, it's because it runs a computation of the right kind (one whose functional organization grounds experience).
(2) a system "runs" a computation just in case there's a mapping from the system's states to the computation's states such that the system's state transitions track the computation's transitions.
(3) take a laptop running a simulation of a pain-reporting agent. the pain computation is run by the cpu, but it's also run by the whole laptop, by the room it's sitting in (the room's state changes every time the cpu does), by the electrons in the transistors, by the building, and by the earth's magnetic field.
(4) so the pain computation is run by the cpu, the laptop, the room, the electrons, the building, and the earth.
(5) by (1), all of those systems have moral status.
(6) but the room and the earth don't have moral status.
(7) so (1) is false — moral status isn't grounded in which computation is running.

i feel like (3) is doing something shady but it looks fine on the surface, because the room's state really does change when the cpu does. but here's the thing that's really bothering me: even if i grant that the room doesn't count, the exact same move threatens the transistor and the electron, and i can't find a princpled line between "the cpu runs the computation" and "the transistor runs the computation." so i'm not sure rejecting (3) actually saves (1), or just kicks the can to a finer scale.

which step do you reject, and what does it cost me? i'd rather keep (1) if i can, because i actually think moral status is computational, but i'm not sure i can afford to.
