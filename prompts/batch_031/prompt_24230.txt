I'm working on a philosophy project about vagueness, and I keep coming back to something I can't quite sort out: the way the law draws age cutoffs. The law sets different age cutoffs for different privileges and responsibilities — voting, buying alcohol, being drafted, claiming retirement benefits — and these are not all the same age. I take it they're all supposed to track the same underlying thing, namely whether a person is mature enough to bear the responsibilities that come with each privilege, but the cutoffs don't line up. A person can be an "adult" for voting purposes and a "minor" for drinking purposes at the exact same time, and I keep finding that puzzling.

What I'm stuck on is what view of vagueness that practice actually presupposes. Does it treat "adulthood" or "maturity" as a genuinely vague concept — one with no sharp boundary — and then simply draw bright lines for the sake of tractability? Or does it presuppose that there really is a sharp line somewhere, and the different cutoffs are just different approximations of it? And if it's the latter, is it coherent to place the line in one spot for voting and a different spot for drinking, or does that quietly commit the law to the idea that "adult" means something different in each context — which seems to make the word do no real work?

I'd also like you to work through the sorites structure of this. If a 17-year-old is a minor and an 18-year-old is an adult, what is the status of someone who is 17 years, 364 days, and 23 hours old? The gap between that person and a full 18-year-old is only a few hours, yet the law treats them as entirely different kinds of persons. Whatever view of vagueness the practice presupposes, does it have a story for why the line falls exactly where it does, or is the precise placement of the line just brute convention?

Two last things. First, please tell me what the practice really requires of a theory of vagueness as opposed to what it's merely compatible with, and point out where the practice leaves the theory open — that is, where more than one view of vagueness could license the same cutoffs. Second, I want to know where your own reasoning is most likely to fail: which argument or consideration would change your verdict about what the practice presupposes, and which assumptions does your answer depend on, so I can see how it would shift if I dropped them?
