I'm preparing a job talk for a philosophy-of-mind position, and the hiring department is an odd mix: a few people who took the explanatory gap seriously back in the nineties, a couple of hard-problem people who think the gap is a genuine metaphysical obstacle, and a handful who believe the gap is largely closed and that consciousness is on its way to being explained the way thermodynamics was. My thesis is that the hard problem is not a refutation of reductionism but a symptom of greedy reductionism. The explanatory gap, I argue, is produced by trying to reduce experience straight to neural or physical facts in a single leap, skipping the intermediate levels at which the explanation would actually have to be built up. Once you distinguish cranes from skyhooks, the "gap" looks less like a metaphysical chasm and more like an artifact of the greedy jump — a methodological failure that has been misdiagnosed as a metaphysical one.

Prepare me for the question period. I want the objections this particular room will actually raise, not the generic objections you'd get from a philosophy-of-mind survey. Order them by how likely and how damaging each is, and phrase each the way the person most likely to raise it would phrase it — the hard-problem person won't put it the same way as the "gap is closing" person, and I need that difference, not a blended version. For each objection, give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, a note on where that reply is soft, and a clear sense of what I should concede up front versus what I should hold. I'm especially worried about two things: that I'm merely relabeling the gap rather than explaining it away, and that my own move smuggles in a skyhook by treating the intermediate levels as if they were already in place and doing explanatory work. Tell me which of these two I should be more afraid of, and then give me the one question I'm hoping nobody asks.
