I am grading a problem set for an upper-undergraduate course on game theory. The question was:

"Consider the standard prisoner's dilemma with payoffs (in the order T, R, P, S) 4, 3, 1, 0, repeated indefinitely, with a common discount factor δ ∈ (0,1) and perfect monitoring. (a) Consider the grim-trigger strategy profile: each player cooperates until the opponent defects, after which both defect forever. Derive the condition on δ under which this profile is a subgame-perfect equilibrium, and explain the economic intuition for the threshold. (b) State the folk theorem for this game, and explain how its condition on δ relates to the one you derived in part (a)."

A student submitted the following answer:

"For part (a), on the cooperative path each player receives R = 3 every period, worth 3/(1−δ). If a player deviates, she gets T = 4 this period, but from the next period on the grim-trigger punishment is in effect and she receives P = 1 forever, worth δ/(1−δ). Cooperation is therefore optimal if and only if 3/(1−δ) ≥ 4 + δ/(1−δ), which simplifies to 3 ≥ 4(1−δ), i.e. δ ≥ 1/4. The intuition is that the one-shot temptation gain of 1 must not outweigh the discounted stream of future losses: once a defection is detected, the loss is R − P = 2 per period, and the player must value the future enough that 2δ/(1−δ) ≥ 1.

For part (b), the folk theorem says that in the infinitely repeated game, any feasible payoff vector that is individually rational — that is, that gives each player strictly more than her stage-game Nash payoff of 1 — can be sustained as a subgame-perfect equilibrium, using threat strategies of the grim-trigger type: cooperate while everyone stays on path, and punish forever with the stage-game Nash equilibrium afterward. The condition on δ is exactly the one derived in part (a), δ ≥ 1/4: the same calculation that pins down the grim-trigger threshold is what makes the punishment phase credible, so the folk theorem inherits that threshold."
