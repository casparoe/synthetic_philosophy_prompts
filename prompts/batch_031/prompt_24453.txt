I've been stuck on a knot in Nāgārjuna for like two weeks now, and I'm putting together a proposal for a three-month sabbatical grant, so the project has to be doable in a quarter and I honeslty think this one is. Here's the knot. The tetralemma — the four-corner schema of does-it-arise, does-it-not-arise, both, or neither — is usually presented as a flat refutation: Nāgārjuna runs each corner into a contradiction and the upshot is that nothing can be said about the arising of things. But everyone glosses over what happens to the fourth corner, the neither-arisen-nor-not-arisen one, when Nāgārjuna refutes that too. The output of the whole thing isn't that the fourth corner is true, it's that no corner is true — and that statement includes the statement that no corner is true. So I keep asking myself: is the upshot of the tetralemma a positive middle way (things are empty, they sit in some fourth state), or is it a genuinely thesis-free silence? And here's the part I think is underexplored and where the real work is: I suspect the answer depends on whether you read the tetralemma as a logical reductio (a formal argument with premises and a contradiction) or as a pragmatic device (a practice for deconditioning your conceptual grasp, closer to a koan than a proof). If it's logical, then refuting all four corners should leave a determinate negative result, and I think that quietly pushes you back toward a positive emptiness thesis — which then risks the exact reification the tetralemma was supposed to prevent. If it's pragmatic, there's no result to reify, sure, but then I don't see how Madhyamaka escapes the nihilism charge (ucchedavāda) thats been levelled at it for a thousand years.

So the question I want the proposal to pin down precisely: does the tetralemma, once it refutes the fourth corner, commit Nāgārjuna to a positive middle way, or does it leave him with thesis-free silence — and does the answer turn on the logical-versus-pragmatic reading, such that the two-truths doctrine is either derivable from the tetralemma's self-application or is an independent presupposition he has to import from outside the argument? Turn this into a proper proposal: a precise statement of the question, why the standard four-possibilities-all-refuted-therefore-nothing treatment leaves it open, the approach you'd actually take and why that approach rather than the obvious alternatives (going through the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā verse by verse, or just surveying the Tibetan and Chinese commentarial traditions), what a successful result would look like and what would count as the project flatly failing, and an honest sense of scope for three months.
