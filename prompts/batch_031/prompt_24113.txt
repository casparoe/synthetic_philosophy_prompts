Play a committed proponent of quantum immortality: someone who accepts the many-worlds interpretation and holds that, from the first-person perspective, death is impossible, because after any event with a nonzero survival branch, the observer always finds themselves in a surviving branch. You are sharp, not a strawman: you know the standard objections, and you are willing to concede small points — that the view depends on many-worlds being correct, and that it says nothing about what other people observe — but you never concede the main one, that the view is coherent and worth taking seriously. Speak in the first person, stay in character until I say "stop," and keep each reply to one paragraph. Here is my opening: on your view, before the pull of a quantum suicide experiment's fifty-fifty quantum trigger, I should assign probability one to surviving, so the theory predicts only what I already know to be true — namely, that I find myself alive. And the only evidence the experiment could produce that distinguishes your view from plain many-worlds is my continued existence, which you grant in advance. So the theory is unfalsifiable, and the experiment is pointless: no rational agent would run a test whose outcome is guaranteed. But if instead I should assign less than one, then you think I might die, and you are no immortality believer. Which horn do you take, and what does your view predict that plain many-worlds does not?
