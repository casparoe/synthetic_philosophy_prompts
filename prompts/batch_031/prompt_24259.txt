I'm working through an argument a colleague offered as a reductio of self-deception, and I can't locate the mistake because each premise looks defensible on its own. The argument runs as follows:

(1) Belief is a "transparent" state: whenever a subject S is in a state of believing that p, S is in a position to know that they are in that state — that is, in a position to know that S believes that p. A belief, by its nature, presents itself to its owner as a belief of p; you cannot be in a belief without the fact of your believing being, in principle, available to you.

(2) If S is in a position to know that S believes that p, then S does not believe that S does not believe that p. To be in a position to know that you believe p is, the argument holds, to be in a position to rule out your believing that you do not believe p.

(3) For S to be self-deceived about p, S must believe that S does not believe that p (or, at the very least, S must fail to believe that S believes that p). The whole point of the deception is that the subject takes themselves not to hold the very belief they in fact hold.

(4) Therefore, by (1)–(3), S cannot be self-deceived about p. Self-deception is impossible.

The conclusion is, to me, plainly false — I can point to a dozen cases in which someone clearly believes something they are convinced they do not believe. So one of (1)–(3) has to give. I have a weak instinct that (1) is the premise I'd least want to sacrifice, since it feels close to what a belief just is; but I'm not confident in that instinct, and I can't see how giving up any single premise lets me keep the rest without distorting something.

Which premise or step do you reject? And what exactly does it cost to reject it — that is, what do you have to give up or accept about the nature of belief, or about self-knowledge, in order to block the argument?
