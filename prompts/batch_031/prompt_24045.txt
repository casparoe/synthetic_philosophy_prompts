Argue that the presumption of independence — the move by which inductive reasoning treats two pieces of confirming evidence as jointly supporting a hypothesis more strongly than either does alone, on the assumption that the evidence is independent given the hypothesis — is not a genuine default that may be invoked in the mere absence of counter-evidence, but a shorthand for the much stronger claim that the hypothesis is the sole common cause of the evidence, so that conditioning on it removes the dependence between the pieces of evidence. Explain why this stronger claim is typically not licensed in precisely the settings where the presumption is deployed (independent testimony, multiple independent confirmations, arguments from coincidence), and what this does to the security of the inductive arguments that rest on the presumption.
