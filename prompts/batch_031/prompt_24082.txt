For a graduate seminar in contemporary philosophy, write an essay on a contested internal problem in Rorty’s neopragmatism: whether his antirepresentationalism can support a noncircular account of moral and political progress when the existing moral vocabulary of a community is itself the source of harm. The essay should take seriously the worry that, if truth is not a matter of correspondence to mind-independent nature, and justification is a matter of solidarity, redescription, and the eventual success of a community’s practices, then Rorty may have no way to say that a new moral language is better than an old one without merely expressing a preference or flattering an audience already disposed to value the change. Weigh the strongest arguments on both sides. Address whether redescription can have normative force without an independent criterion for distinguishing liberating change from rhetorical change; whether appeals to reduced suffering, greater agency, and wider sympathy smuggle back in some naturalistic or representationalist standard; whether Rorty’s irony, hope, and anti-foundationalism can answer the objection that his account is persuasive only within a community already committed to the values it is supposed to justify; and whether a pragmatist can distinguish between a vocabulary that merely alters our feelings about a practice and one that changes what we are entitled to recognize as harm. The essay should not end on a noncommittal note. It should reach and justify a conclusion: either Rorty’s view can be coherently reconstructed so that it gives a nonrepresentationalist account of moral progress, or it cannot, and the best response is to revise or abandon some part of his antirepresentationalism. Argue from the concepts and arguments themselves, without citations, bibliographies, reading lists, or verbatim quotation from Rorty’s texts.
