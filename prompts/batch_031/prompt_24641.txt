I have just come out of a long and rather exhausting argument with a friend about luck egalitarianism, and I have come to suspect that most of what we were actually disagreeing about was not the theory itself but the standard we were each quietly applying to it. He kept defending the pure version — the idea that justice requires us to neutralize the effects of brute luck while leaving the effects of genuine choice intact — and I kept objecting that the version he was defending either collapses into a libertarian desert view or quietly turns into blaming the victim, and I could not pin down which specific requirement was doing the work in each of those failures. So I would like to step back and get the specification straight before we argue about any particular account again.

Could you write out, as a specification, what a luck-egalitarian account of distributive justice actually has to deliver to count as adequate, where adequate means good enough to serve as the foundational principle of a real theory of how a society ought to distribute its goods? I want the list of requirements — the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must respect, the intuitions it must be consistent with — with the motivating case or consideration behind each one. It seems to me, for instance, that any such theory has to draw the line between brute luck and option luck in a principled way rather than merely asserting it, and it has to ground that line in some account of responsibility or choice that is neither trivially true, in which case almost every inequality is the product of a choice and there is nothing left to redistribute, nor trivially false, in which case the theory has silently become a plain egalitarian or sufficientarian view. It also has to handle the cases where the misfortune was never something the agent had a real chance to choose or avoid — a person born with a serious disability, or with a low natural endowment — without the theory collapsing into the implication that such a person simply deserves to be left behind. And it has to say something defensible about voluntary risk, where the agent did choose but the bad outcome was still in a sense a matter of luck. For each requirement, give me the case that forces it, and flag which of the things people demand of these theories are genuine desiderata and which are really the prejudices of one camp — the libertarian, the sufficientarian, the prioritarian — smuggled in under the label of what justice requires.

Then, and this is the part I care about most: tell me which of these requirements are non-negotiable and which are negotiable, and, if you think so, whether some subset of them is jointly unsatisfiable, in which case the list itself is the result and the pure theory my friend was defending is impossible rather than merely imperfect. And identify the single requirement that does the most work excluding candidate theories — the one that, once you take it seriously, knocks out the largest number of the standard positions. I do not need you to score Dworkin, Arneson, Cohen, or anyone else against the list in detail; a brief note on how the main contenders fare is welcome, but please keep the weight on the requirements and their motivating cases, because that is where the actual thinking is, and it is where my friend and I keep talking past each other.
