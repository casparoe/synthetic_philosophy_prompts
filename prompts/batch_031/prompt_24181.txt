I'm in a philosophy of computation seminar and I keep coming back to this one knot that I cant seem to untangle. I've ended up siding with the view that the Church–Turing thesis — at least the strong or "physical" version, the one that says no actual physical machine can compute a function that no Turing machine can — is a substantive empirical claim about the world, more or less on par with a law of nature. Not a conceptual truth, not something we just stipulate by defining "computable" to mean "Turing-computable," and not an a priori truth about the nature of effective procedure. I find it weird that we treat it the way we do, like its settled by the original Church and Turing arguments, when those arguments seem to me to only establish the weak version (the one about abstract algorithms) a priori, and leave the physical version as an open empirical question we've just never actually tested. I keep picturing some analog machine or "hypercomputer" that might compute something no Turing machine can, and I'm not sure we've really ruled that out the way we act like we have.

So: what would count against this? I want the map of where this view is exposed, not just the standard "well, it's really just a definition" deflationary move. Specifically: which arguments or considerations would actually defeat the claim that the strong CTT is empirical rather than a priori or stipulative, and which would just be awkward? I'm especially stuck on the falsification problem — what would it even take to observe a counterexample, and does the fact that I can't seem to specify a clean test cut against the empirical reading, or is that just a practical limitation on us? And I want to know what a defender of my view would themselves concede as counting against them, versus what they'd push back on. And be honest with me: has this view been qualified into a position where it risks nothing, and if so, is that a problem for a view that's supposed to be an empirical claim in the first place?
