I've been thinking a lot lately about the idea that many social systems are stuck in a trap: every participant is doing the rational thing given what everyone else is doing, but the equilibrium no one actually wants, and no one can unilaterally escape. I've seen this described under different labels — "Moloch," multipolar traps, race-to-the-bottom dynamics — and I'm not sure how much these are different ideas or the same idea with different emphasis. One distinction I keep coming back to is between a single-axis race, where everyone is competing on one metric like wages or military capability, and something more multipolar, where the competing parties are optimizing different mixes of goods, so that there is no single "defection" that gets you out. I was discussing this with friends who work in policy, and what came out of that conversation is the question I want to ask.

Suppose you are advising a small research group — maybe four people, about five years of funding — whose mandate is to figure out which of these collective-action traps is most worth trying to break, and how. Please pick a short list of candidate traps to compare — say, among arms-race dynamics between major powers, regulatory and tax competition between jurisdictions, wage suppression driven by capital mobility, the incentive structure of the attention economy, and the competitive dynamics of AI development, or something I'm not listing — and rank them for this group's purposes. I'm less interested in a survey of all the traps than in the reasoning behind the ordering: for each candidate, how bad is the trap (in what sense, and for whom), how hard it is for any one actor to exit unilaterally, whether it is "upstream" of the others in the sense that breaking it would change the payoff structure of the rest, and how much attention is already being paid to it by well-funded institutions.

Please make the criteria explicit and say how you are trading them off — I suspect "how much harm is being done" and "how tractable is it to change" will pull in different directions, and I'd rather see you grapple with that than just list the most urgent-sounding traps. I also want to know, for each trap on your shortlist, the mechanism that makes it a trap rather than merely a bad policy: what would have to change in the incentives for it to stop being a trap, and why that change can't be achieved under the rules as they currently stand. And what would change your ranking if the group had only one year instead of five, or if their audience were voters rather than policymakers? Please don't end with a reading list or a bibliography; I want the argument itself.
