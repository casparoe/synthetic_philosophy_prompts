I work in compliance at a small private fertility clinic. Roughly thirty times a week I take a fifteen-minute call with a patient before a procedure, walk through the consent form, and flag any case that looks shaky to the attending physician, who is the one who actually signs off. I have no legal or philosophy training, but I've been thinking on my own about what actually makes a consent valid. I keep landing on the standard list — the person has to be informed, the choice has to be voluntary, and the person has to be competent — and I can't tell where the lines are. Is a patient who is offered a 50% discount this month being coerced? Is a patient who is told the correct success rates but is told "this is the only option that works for you" properly informed? Does competence work as one switch, or can a person be competent about one part of a decision and not another?

Please turn that standard list into a decision procedure my team could actually run during the call: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Mark clearly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then run it on these three cases from this month: (1) a patient who is given the discount and says "obviously we'd go with your clinic," with nothing else changed; (2) a patient who is told the correct odds but is told the other clinics "cut corners," and when I press her she says she would still want the procedure but "not from them"; (3) a patient with early memory problems who is completely clear about the procedure itself but keeps confusing the five-year embryo storage clause, with her partner present on the call and answering for her whenever she hesitates. If the procedure gives an answer on any of these that clashes with what we have been doing, say so plainly, and end by saying what the procedure cannot do at all. Keep the whole thing to a maximum of 800 words.
