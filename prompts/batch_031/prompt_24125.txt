I'm writing up a section of my dissertation on the knowability paradox, and I keep getting stuck on a meta-question: what actually counts as a good resolution? Everyone — Williamson, DeRose, the restricted-quantification crowd, the paraconsistent people — points at a step of the proof and declares that it is where things break, but they never agree on what a resolution is supposed to accomplish, so I can't tell whether two competing solutions are genuinely in tension or just describing the same maneuver in different vocabularies. Could you write out the desiderata that any adequate resolution of Fitch's paradox has to satisfy? I want the full specification: which ingredients of the proof it must preserve, which it is permitted to drop, which cases or results it must get right, and which distinctions it cannot collapse — with the consideration or motivating case behind each requirement, and a clear mark on which are non-negotiable and which are merely preferences of a particular camp (anti-realist versus realist, classical versus non-classical).

To keep us anchored to the same object, here is the proof as I am tracking it, so each desideratum can be tied to a specific line: begin with the knowability principle, ∀p(p → ◇Kp); take an arbitrary p and suppose p; then suppose for reductio that ¬Kp, so that the self-referential proposition p ∧ ¬Kp is true; by the knowability principle applied to that very proposition, ◇K(p ∧ ¬Kp); but K(p ∧ ¬Kp) would yield Kp and K¬Kp by closure under known conjunction, and K¬Kp yields ¬Kp by factivity, so K(p ∧ ¬Kp) is impossible and hence ¬◇K(p ∧ ¬Kp), contradicting the earlier line — so ¬Kp was wrong, and p → Kp, i.e. every truth is in fact known. The load-bearing resources are therefore the unrestricted knowability principle, factivity, closure under known conjunction, and classical logic, with the quantifier ranging all the way over the self-referential proposition.

The part I most want to see is whether the desiderata are jointly satisfiable at all. If your list demands preservation of the unrestricted knowability principle, full generality of the knowledge predicate, classical logic, factivity, and closure simultaneously, then by Fitch's own theorem no theory can meet them, and the list itself becomes a result — a trilemma or whatever it turns out to be — so please say plainly which subset is jointly unsatisfiable and why, rather than pretending a single theory can tick every box. Then, briefly, map the main contenders onto the list: Williamson's outright rejection of the knowability principle, DeRose's context-sensitivity, the restricted-quantification move, the claim that the predicate simply cannot be applied to its own paradoxical instance, and the non-classical-logic options — which desideratum does each one give up, and which single desideratum knocks out the most of them. A table for that last part is fine.
