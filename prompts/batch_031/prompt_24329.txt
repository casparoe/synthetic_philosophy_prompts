I work in model theory — structures, automorphisms, orbits, types, definability — and I want the debate over the identity of indiscernibles translated into that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. The source material: Leibniz's principle that no two distinct things can share all their properties, in both its monadic and relational versions; Max Black's two-spheres counterexample, with two perfectly identical spheres in a universe arranged so that everything is spherically symmetric about the midpoint between them; and the standard replies, namely the restriction to natural or fundamental properties and the objection that the spheres are still distinguishable as the left and right sphere relative to a coordinate system.

I suspect Black's construction is just a model with a nontrivial automorphism swapping the two spheres, and that the principle amounts to a claim that the structure of the world is rigid, but I don't trust that the whole debate maps that cleanly. So after the translation, give me the residue report: which claims translate cleanly, which only with distortion, and which have no expression in model theory at all. In particular, address the triviality worry — that if properties include self-distinguishing ones like being identical to a given sphere, the principle is vacuous — and whether the principle is intended as a logical truth about all structures or as a contingent metaphysical claim about the actual world. Then tell me whether the residue is a defect of my vocabulary or a sign that the philosophical positions were less clear than they looked.
