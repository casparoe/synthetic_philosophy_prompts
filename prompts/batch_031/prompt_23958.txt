I sit on the grants committee of a global-health foundation, and this cycle we have to divide a fixed $12 million pot among the proposals in front of us. One proposal — a high-throughput screening program for a neglected tropical disease — carries a claimed expected benefit roughly forty times the next-best proposal and about a hundred times the median. The rest of the pool is a portfolio of roughly thirty smaller programs, each individually modest. Our standing guidance says we allocate by expected cost-effectiveness, but it was written for a pool of comparable proposals and says nothing about what to do when a single option dominates the rest by this kind of margin.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate to the committee before Friday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete allocation for this cycle — a specific split of the pot — and state the general principle we should adopt so we can handle a similar case next cycle. Treat it as the decision-theory problem it is: the utility-monster case, in which one option absorbs a disproportionate share of the total expected benefit, so that pure expected-value maximization says to pour essentially the whole budget into it and abandon the rest. Weigh the case for concentrating on the dominant intervention against the case for capping per-proposal allocation and diversifying — the uncertainty in the dominant proposal's impact estimate, the risk that it is an outlier rather than a real forty-fold edge, the option value of keeping smaller programs alive, and the equity argument that a foundation should not defund the populations the smaller programs serve. Engage the strongest version of the losing side honestly, not a strawman.

Please clearly separate what is settled among people who actually work on cost-effectiveness and decision theory from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position — either that pure expected-value maximization is the right rule or that it is the wrong one — as though it were the consensus. Keep it to a couple of pages, no citations, written so a non-specialist commissioner can follow every step of the reasoning.
