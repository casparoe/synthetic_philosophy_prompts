I do data viz for a public health org and we keep getting asked to make "infographics" that are basically just bar charts with clip art, and I want to push us toward something more like Neurath's pictorial language idea — the Isotype thing where you build diagrams of social facts out of standerdized little pictorial elements, like rows of the same person-figure to show a proportion, so the picture is grounded in countable material stuff instead of just a designer's spin. I want a decison procedure my design team can actually run step by step when we get a statistic we're supposed to turn into one of these panels. For each step I want the question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to it, and where the procedure says no and stops. The steps, in order: (1) whether the fact we want to show can even be represented in a pictorial language at all, and what kinds of things resist it — rates, abstractions, negatives, "lack of X," stuff that isn't a countable material thing; (2) how to pick and standardize the elements we build it out of, so the same element means the same thing across all our panels; (3) how to handle scale and proportion, i.e. when the row-of-100-figures trick is the right tool and when it misleads, and what to do when the number is too big or too small to count out by hand; (4) how to check the finished picture for the failure modes Neurath himself was worried about, like a picture that looks more precise than the data actually are, or one that quietly picks a side by what it makes visible and what it leaves out; and (5) where the procedure has to stop and hand the decision back to a person, because choosing which facts to foreground in the first place is an editorial or value call, not something the formalism settles. Mark which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then run the whole thing on two concrete facts: the share of a city's households that don't have access to a car, and the change in a region's infant mortality rate over a decade. I suspect the second one hits a wall on a rate-of-change and I want to see exactly where and why.
