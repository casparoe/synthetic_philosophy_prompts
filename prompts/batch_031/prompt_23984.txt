I've been going back and forth over the rationalist-blog discussions of Solomonoff induction and Kolmogorov complexity, and I think the usual Occam's-razor gloss has become so familiar that it obscures what is actually doing the work. Please argue, in a careful and somewhat technical but self-contained way, that Solomonoff induction does not provide a clean vindication of Occam's razor as a general epistemic norm. I want the argument to focus on the fact that the universal prior is defined relative to a choice of universal machine or programming language, that its probability mass over hypotheses is a length prior over self-delimiting programs rather than a measure of simplicity in any language-independent sense, and that the invariance theorem only gives bounded differences in program lengths across universal encodings, not exact invariance of the posterior over concrete hypotheses. On that basis, make the case that the familiar slogan of preferring simpler hypotheses in the Solomonoff setting is better understood as a machine-relative, encoding-relative feature of an idealized agent's prior, rather than as a direct normative principle that bounded agents should adopt. I'd especially like you to address the objection that bounded machine-dependence is not a serious problem because the invariance theorem makes the prior approximately robust, and to explain why approximate robustness is insufficient for a clean normative vindication when the hypotheses being compared may have nearly equal likelihoods.

You may assume I know the basic setup: a universal prior over programs, a sequence of observations, and posterior updating by likelihood ratios. But please do not simply restate the standard story that shorter programs receive more prior probability. I want the argument to be in the weeds about why the usual Occam's-razor reading is too quick, and why the result is better explained by the enumeration, normalization, and self-delimiting constraints of the universal prior. I'm not asking for references, a reading list, or citations; I just want a strong argument that a reader with a technical background could follow without needing to check a source.
