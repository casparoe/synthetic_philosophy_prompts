I referee for a philosophy of mind journal, and I have just come out of a long, genuinely heated argument with a friend of mine who also works in the area, about the causal exclusion argument — whether it really forces the non-reductive physicalist toward epiphenomenalism, which premise is actually doing the work, and whether the standard replies are genuine replies or merely restatements of the position the argument is meant to pressure. The argument has left me doubting my own refereeing, because I keep receiving submissions that claim to establish mental causation or to defeat the exclusion argument, and I am not confident I am applying a consistent standard. Some papers clearly engage the argument premise by premise; others seem to assume it away, or to answer a slightly different argument; and on a first read I cannot always tell which is which. So I would like you to write me a decision procedure I can actually run on a submission, the way an editor runs a checklist before making a recommendation.

The procedure should be a sequence of questions asked in a fixed order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, a clear point at which it stops and returns a verdict or a flag, and a marked point at which the question is one of metaphysical values rather than logic and I have to register a judgment call. It needs to sort the known replies correctly: the paper that denies causal closure or causal completeness of the physical; the paper that accepts closure but insists that mental properties have non-derivative causal relevance; the paper that simply accepts overdetermination; the paper that goes epiphenomenal; the paper that blocks the inference with a priority view; and the paper that appeals to downward causation or to agent causation. I want it to separate a genuine reply from a restatement or a straw man, and to flag the case where a paper is answering a different argument than the one the exclusion argument states. Please be honest about which steps track real distinctions in the literature and which are just my taste dressed up as a test.

I referee perhaps three or four papers a quarter, each review is due within a month, and I want the procedure to be runnable in about an hour on a paper I have already read once. Then run it on two hypothetical submissions so I can see it in action: one that tries to establish mental causation by arguing that a mental property plays an irreplaceable causal role inside a mechanism, and one that replies to the exclusion argument by arguing that overdetermination is not the defect the argument assumes it to be. Include at least one case where the procedure hands me an answer I will not like — for instance, a well-written paper it nonetheless flags as not actually responding — and say plainly what the procedure cannot do, that is, the cases where it will mislead me and I have to fall back on my own judgment.
