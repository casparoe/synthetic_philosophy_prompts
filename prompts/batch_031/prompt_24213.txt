I'm teaching a 75-minute upper-level seminar in applied ethics — thirteen students, mostly junior and senior philosophy majors with a couple of graduate students — and I want to close our unit on saving lives in medicine with an activity around a thought I find genuinely unsettling: that in a case where you can save either one stranger or five strangers, the five who die are not worse off than the one who dies, so that the number of victims may not by itself give you a reason to save the many. Last term I tried handling this with a simple show of hands and it immediately collapsed into a trolley-problem discussion, with students arguing about the agent's responsibility rather than about whether the five's death is worse than the one's. This time I don't want to lecture them into the view, and I don't want to name its principal defender until the debrief; I want them to run into the force of the "no worse" claim themselves, through choices they make in the room, and to come away able to say where their own "save the many" intuition was actually getting its grip — from the victims' welfare, or from some feature of the total picture.

Please design a staged, in-class activity with rules, roles, a payoff or stake scheme that doesn't require real money, and a minute-by-minute timeline. I'm imagining two rounds: in the first, students make a forced, face-to-face choice between saving one and saving five; in the second, they are put in a format — a card, a form, a structured vote — that forces them to commit, before discussion, to specifying for whom exactly the five's death is worse than the one's death, and to discover that they can only point to an aggregate feature of the outcome rather than to any particular person's life. I want that second-round failure to be the point of the exercise rather than a gotcha, so please build the format so it is a fair test. Then give me a debrief that maps each round onto the part of the argument it was exposing — the comparative claim, the aggregation move, and whether my own intuition survives the diagnosis — plus a frank note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I expect at least one graduate student will try to philosophize it out of in round two) and how to handle it, and a candid statement of which feature of the view the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself. Begin your reply with the activity itself — title, setup, rules, timeline, debrief — with no preface and no restatement of my request.
