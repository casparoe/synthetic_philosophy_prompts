I'm working through a set of arguments against what people call the "measurability bias" in effective altruism, and I've landed on one that I think proves too much, and I can't find the exit. The target is the standard EA decision procedure — choose the action that maximizes expected moral value (EMV) — and the argument is meant to show that this procedure gives us no genuine moral guidance at all. Here it is, the way a friend and I got it:

(1) The core of EA's decision procedure is: act to maximize expected moral value (EMV).
(2) To maximize EMV, you must be able to compare the EMVs of your available options.
(3) To compare EMVs, you must represent each option's moral value as a quantity on some scale.
(4) To represent a moral value as a quantity on a scale, you need a unit of moral value and a way of measuring value in that unit.
(5) There is no fact of the matter about what the unit of moral value is; choosing a unit is a choice, not a discovery.
(6) So any choice of unit is either arbitrary, or else it is itself a substantive moral judgment.
(7) If the choice of unit is a substantive moral judgment, then the EMV computation presupposes the very kind of judgment it was supposed to do for us, and is circular.
(8) If the choice of unit is arbitrary, then the EMV ranking it produces depends on an arbitrary choice, and so is not a fact about which option is genuinely better.
(9) Either way, the EMV computation does not deliver a non-arbitrary, non-circular answer about what we should do.
(10) Therefore, the core of EA's decision procedure — maximize EMV — provides no genuine moral guidance.

The conclusion is one I don't want, and what makes me think the argument proves too much is that the same structure would apply to any procedure that ranks options by a metric: if it worked, it would also refute plain utilitarianism and expected utility theory, neither of which I think is as obviously guidance-free as this makes them. I have a nagging feeling that the argument is only as strong as a claim about what "measuring" a value actually requires, but I can't pin down which step that is. Which premise or step do you reject, why did it look fine on first reading, and what do I pay by rejecting it — in particular, what must the defender of EMV concede, and what does the measurability-bias critic lose?
