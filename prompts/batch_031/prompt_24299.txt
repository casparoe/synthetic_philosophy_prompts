For a graduate decision-theory seminar I'm teaching this term, I want a decision tree that takes a student who begins from the premise "I accept that the Ellsberg urn case is a genuine violation of subjective expected utility" and leads her to a named position on ambiguity and ambiguity aversion. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first the branch questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then locate the actual positions in that space. Each branch node must be a question a student could answer without already knowing which position it leads to — no node may simply ask "do you believe in imprecise probabilities?" The distinctions that matter for my purposes are three: (i) whether the ambiguity is representable probabilistically at all — a unique prior, a set of priors, a higher-order distribution over the red fraction, or no probabilities; (ii) whether the agent's preference is best read as a violation of the sure-thing principle, a violation of dominance, or a preference that is not representable as expected utility in the first place; and (iii) whether the aversion to ambiguity is a feature of the environment or a feature of the agent's knowledge. The leaves must include, at minimum: the "it is just a mistake" position (a unique prior, in the spirit of Smith's critique), the imprecise-probability / multiple-priors maximin position, the Choquet expected utility position, the no-probabilities Wald maximin position, and the higher-order view. Note any logically available combination of answers that no one actually occupies, and say briefly why — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot really separate. Keep the whole thing to at most 600 words.
