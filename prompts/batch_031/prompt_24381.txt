I'm setting the final exam for an upper-level seminar on the ethics of algorithmic decision-making (students have a working command of probability and some philosophy). I want one essay question, answerable in about 50 minutes, on the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness, together with a model answer of roughly 600 words and a marking scheme. The question must not be answerable by reciting that the fairness criteria are mutually incompatible; it should force students to confront the conditions under which the incompatibility actually holds and what the result does and does not show.

The crux to target: the impossibility is usually presented as an inherent, absolute conflict among the criteria (calibration within group, equalized odds, demographic parity), as though no risk score could ever satisfy them all. But it is in fact a conditional statistical incompatibility: it arises specifically when the base rates of the outcome differ across groups (and, for some pairwise results, when the predictor is imperfect), and if the base rates were equal, the criteria would be compatible. So the result is not a deep or absolute impossibility but a consequence of the empirical structure of the problem (unequal base rates plus imperfect prediction), and the choice of which criterion to prioritize is a normative judgment the mathematics does not settle.

In the marking scheme, separate an A from a B by naming the specific moves each must contain (correctly specifying the base-rate condition; distinguishing an absolute impossibility from a conditional statistical incompatibility; noting that the choice among criteria is a value judgment), and give a one-line note on how students typically get each of those points wrong.
