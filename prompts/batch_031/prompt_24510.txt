A friend of mine who does social choice walked me into a corner and I can't find the exit. He's trying to show that utilitarian social welfare is basically the only defensible option, and here's the argument he gave me:

(1) We want a social welfare function W that maps a profile of individual preferences to a social ranking, and we want it to be non-dictatorial, i.e. no single person's preferences just are the social ranking.

(2) We want W to respect the Pareto principle: if everyone prefers x to y, then society should prefer x to y.

(3) We want W to satisfy independence of irrelevant alternatives: the social ranking of any pair {x,y} should depend only on how each person ranks that pair, not on how they rank other options.

(4) By Arrow's impossibility theorem, there is no W satisfying (1), (2), and (3) when preferences are just plain ordinal orderings. So as stated we're stuck.

(5) Harsanyi's aggregation theorem is the escape hatch. It says that if each individual has a von Neumann-Morgenstern expected-utility function (preferences are cardinal, defined over lotteries, and interpersonally comparible), then there is a W satisfying IIA and Pareto, namely the weighted sum W(x) = sum_i w_i u_i(x) for some non-negative weights w_i, not all zero.

(6) So the way out of Arrow's dilemma is to be a utilitarian: once you assume cardinal, interpersonally comparable utilities, the weighted sum is the unique non-dictatorial, Pareto-respecting, IIA-satisfying social welfare function.

(7) Therefore the only defensible social welfare function is a utilitarian weighted sum of individual utilities.

(8) But a utilitarian weighted sum is indefensible: it can require letting one person lose everything to hand another person a trivial gain, it leaves no room for rights or the Rawlsian difference principle, and we have strong reasons to think those matter.

(9) So (1)-(5) can't all be right, which means the assumptions behind the escape hatch are doing something fishy.

He's confident and I cant find the crack. I feel like something slipped around (5)-(6), where the escape hatch is supposed to actually fix Arrow's problem, but I can't name it. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd most like to keep the Pareto principle and the idea that a non-utilitarian, Rawlsian social welfare function is genuinely defensible; I'm least attached to the strict independence condition, but I want to know exactly what I'm giving up and whether the escape hatch in (5) is even doing what he claims.
