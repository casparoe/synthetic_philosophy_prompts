I'm three months out from my dissertation proposal deadline, and I'm trying to decide whether to commit to the "safe Pareto improvement" program in cooperative AI theory. For the uninitiated: imagine two principals who don't play a game themselves but instead design or instruct agents to play it for them, with aligned delegation—telling each agent to maximize the principal's own utility—as the default. A safe Pareto improvement is an intervention on the agent interaction (committing to forgo certain actions, installing surrogate objectives, and so on) that you can show is good for both principals without having to resolve hard equilibrium-selection questions in either the base game or the modified game. The appeal is that the argument works under weak, qualitative assumptions about how agents play different games. I've been reading the literature and I'm increasingly uncertain about which parts of the program are genuinely deep and which are just bookkeeping, so I'd like help thinking about where the real open problems are.

Could you give me a prioritized map of the open questions in this area? In particular, I'm drawn to a cluster of worries: the gap between the binary case (showing that the intervention beats aligned delegation head-to-head) and the realistic case (choosing among many possible interventions, where the binary argument doesn't obviously pick out the right one); the problem of selecting which safe Pareto improvement to propose when several exist and the principals can't coordinate; the question of why the Pareto criterion is the right one rather than some "safe improvement for me" criterion; and the worry that the whole program quietly depends on the principals trusting each other's equilibrium reasoning in ways the theory never justifies. Are these the right questions to care about, or am I missing something more upstream?

For each question you prioritize, I want a defended ranking, not a survey: why it's upstream of the others, how tractable it is for someone with my background (solid game theory and decision theory, weak in formal logic and mechanism design), how much of the rest of the program's credibility depends on its answer, and what evidence or a successful argument over the next year or two would move it up or down the list. I'm deliberately not asking for a reading list or citations—just the reasoning about which problems deserve a researcher's effort first and which are overrated or not really open at all.
