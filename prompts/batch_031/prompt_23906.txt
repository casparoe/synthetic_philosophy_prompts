In the secondary literature on the paradox of tragedy, a familiar move is to read Aristotle's catharsis on the medical model — the purgation theory — and then to use that reading to dissolve the paradox itself. The idea is that tragedy acts on the spectator's pity and fear the way a purgative acts on the body: it evacuates an excess, and the pleasure we take in a good tragedy is really the pleasure of relief, on the model of being glad to be out of a fever. On this account there is no genuine paradox, because the spectator is not enjoying sadness at all; she is enjoying the purging of it, and relief is a positive state, so the negative emotion never needs to be enjoyed as such.

I would like you to audit this analogy carefully. Begin by laying out the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of the body, the toxin or excess, the purgative agent, the act of purging, and the post-purge relief, in the case of tragedy. Then identify the disanalogies, and — this is the part I care most about — separate the disanalogies that bear on the analogy's intended work, namely dissolving the paradox, from the disanalogies that are merely cosmetic. I keep returning to three pressure points, and I want each one assessed on its own merits: first, that the spectator's pity and fear are generated by the play rather than pre-existing in the spectator, which seems to invert the direction of causation the medical case requires; second, the dose-response profile, since on the medical model a stronger purgative should produce a better result, yet the most harrowing tragedies are not the most pleasing; and third, whether pity and fear are genuinely excesses or toxins at all, or whether the analogy simply imports that conclusion from an unexamined assumption about the emotions. Beyond those three, note any further disanalogies you think matter. Finally, say whether the conclusion the analogy was carrying — that the paradox of tragedy dissolves once we see the pleasure as relief rather than as sadness — survives with the disanalogies on the table, and if it does not, offer what you take to be a better analogy for the pleasure of sad art, and say precisely what it gets right that the purgation picture gets wrong.
