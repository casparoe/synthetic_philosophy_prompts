I'm covering the neuroscience of pain for a long piece, and I've been trying to figure out how much weight to put on a claim that keeps coming up in my sources: that what makes pain bad — the feature that makes it a bad thing in itself, rather than just a signal that something is wrong — is a component of the experience that can come apart from its sensory-discriminative qualities (where it is, how intense it is, what it feels like). The standard evidence cited is pain asymbolia: patients with thalamic lesions who, when given a stimulus that would ordinarily be painful, describe it accurately as a burning or aching sensation but report that it is not unpleasant, not distressing, something they don't mind. I don't want a hedge or a bibliography; I want a number and the reasoning in the open. Give me your credence that pain's badness is genuinely dissociable from its sensory-discriminative profile, and break it down: (1) the probability that the asymbolia reports are a real, stable dissociation rather than an artifact — that the patients aren't misreporting or underreporting, that the lesion isn't also flattening their affect in general or disrupting their attention to, or learning about, the stimulus, and that the sensation they report is genuinely the same sensory state as ordinary pain and not something else; (2) the probability that, even taking the reports at face value, the dissociation actually establishes that badness is a distinct component of the experience, as opposed to being consistent with badness's being identical to some sensory feature, or with the unpleasantness's being a learned or attentional overlay that the lesion has stripped away; and (3) whatever third term you think the estimate really rests on — the scope of the effect, the moral stakes, the comparison to other dissociations — with a number and a line of justification for each, then how you combine them. Then tell me which term you are least confident in and what evidence or argument would move your number the most.
