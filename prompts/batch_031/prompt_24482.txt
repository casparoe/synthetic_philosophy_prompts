I'm taking a graduate seminar on practical reason this term, and our professor has been running a recurring exercise: she presents a series of cases and asks whether the person in question has a reason to act, and she gives a verdict for each. The course has been moving through Williams's internalism and the integrity objection, and I've been trying to reverse-engineer the principle our professor is applying in the exercise, because I think the verdicts are jointly consistent with more than one principle and I'd like a second set of eyes. Here are the cases she's covered so far and her verdicts:

1. A person who is completely indifferent to music — no desire to listen, no project involving it, no aesthetic interest, and music plays no role in any relationship or identity of his. Does he have a reason to listen to Bach? Her verdict: no.

2. A person who has a long-running project of restoring an old house and who discovers that the foundation needs urgent repair. Does she have a reason to repair it? Her verdict: yes.

3. A person who is a committed environmentalist. Does he have a reason to reduce his carbon footprint? Her verdict: yes.

4. A person who currently has no desire to be healthy and no project involving his health, and for whom being healthy is not tied to any relationship, commitment, or sense of identity. Does he have a reason to exercise? Her verdict: no.

Next week's case is this: a person who is a parent. Because of some circumstance — the child is a grown adult who has moved far away, and the two have grown emotionally distant — the parent currently feels no particular attachment to the child and no desire to help. But the parental relationship is a constitutive part of the parent's identity. Does the parent have a reason to help the child?

Here's what I'd like you to do. First, identify the candidate principles — about the relationship between a person's reasons and that person's motivations, values, or identity — that are jointly consistent with all four of the earlier verdicts, and say what each of those principles predicts about the new case. I expect at least two of the surviving principles to diverge on the new case; if they don't, tell me why and what I'm missing. Second, give your best guess about which principle the professor is actually running, with your reasons for preferring it. Third, name a further case that would tell the surviving principles apart, and say what verdict on that case would confirm each principle. I'm more interested in the structure of the principles and the diagnostic case than in a definitive answer to the new case itself.
