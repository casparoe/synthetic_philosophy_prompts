I've read the rationalist blogs and forums for most of the last decade, and I think a lot about Bayesian reasoning, so I'd like a careful comparison of two models of how beliefs and decisions spread through a population. The first is the sequential information-cascade model: agents move one at a time, each receives a private signal about the state of the world and observes only the actions, not the signals, of everyone who moved before him, and a cascade is a self-reinforcing run of identical actions that begins once the accumulated public evidence is strong enough that a later agent rationally ignores his own private signal; a key result is that with continuous private signals, cascades never occur, because each new signal carries unbounded additional information. The second is the DeGroot model of opinion dynamics: agents move in parallel, each updates his belief by taking a fixed weighted average of his own current belief and the beliefs of his neighbors, and under mild conditions on the network the population converges to a consensus that is a weighted average of the initial beliefs.

Compare the two models, addressing the following five points: what each model's core mechanism is and what it is really modeling; what the cascade model captures that the DeGroot model cannot; what the DeGroot model captures that the cascade model cannot; where the cascade model breaks down or gives counterintuitive results; and which of the two is a better model of a real phenomenon such as the rapid spread of a financial panic or a viral trend, and why.

Please give your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
