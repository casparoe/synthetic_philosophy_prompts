Two colleagues of mine have been going back and forth in our philosophy reading group about Samuel Scheffler's "afterlife conjecture" — the claim that what makes human extinction bad, over and above the deaths it involves, is not (merely) the loss of future lives or future well-being, but the end of the continuation of the things we value: our relationships, projects, cultural and intellectual achievements, and moral concerns, which we take for granted will persist long after we are gone. They asked me to referee, and I honestly can't see who has the better case. Here is the exchange, compressed:

Dana: Scheffler is right to reject the consequentialist framing. Extinction isn't bad because it reduces net future welfare — it would end future suffering as well as future flourishing. What's bad is that it ends the continuation of value itself, and that's a different kind of thing from a quantity of welfare.

Marcus: That's not as non-consequentialist as it sounds. The "things we value" — the relationships, projects, culture — are all carried by particular future minds. A culture that continues is a culture that some future people are living. So "the continuation of value" is just future welfare, measured in meaning and significance rather than happiness. Scheffler hasn't escaped consequentialism; he's just switched the currency.

Dana: No. Consequentialism is indifferent to which particular minds exist, so long as the total is maximized. What matters on Scheffler's view is continuity with our particular values and projects — a future that maximizes welfare but has no connection to what we care about wouldn't be an afterlife for us. So it's not a different currency of the same quantity; it's a different kind of thing altogether.

Marcus: I'll grant that continuity is part of the picture. But continuity with our particular projects only exists if particular people carry them on, and that's exactly where Scheffler runs into the non-identity problem he thinks he's dodging. If we bring about extinction, the particular future people who would have carried on our projects don't exist, so no one is made worse off. His whole account is built on particular continuations that the non-identity problem shows we can't appeal to as victims. He's just relabeled the problem.

Dana: That misreads the non-identity problem. It only bites if you're trying to say a particular person is harmed. Scheffler never makes that move. He says extinction is bad because it ends the continuation of value — a fact about the loss of value, not about the welfare of any particular victim. So the non-identity problem is a non-sequitur against his account.

Marcus: But is it really? If the only thing lost is "value," and value exists only in and through particular minds, then "the loss of value" is just "the non-existence of particular minds who would have had value." So Scheffler's loss is either (a) a harm to particular people, in which case the non-identity problem applies, or (b) a harm to no one, in which case it's unclear why it's bad at all. He can't have it both ways.

Can you go through their exchange and say who is right about which specific point? In particular, I'd like you to (1) assess whether Marcus is correct that Scheffler's account is really just consequentialism with a different measure of value, or whether the continuity-versus-quantity distinction Dana draws is genuine; (2) adjudicate the non-identity problem exchange — is it a non-sequitur against Scheffler, or does it bite after all; and (3) say whether there is anything both of them are missing. Please be specific about which moves are strong, which are weak, and why, rather than just picking a side.
