I was talking with a friend who works on the pricing side at a sportsbook, and he mentioned something that sounds like a philosophy problem to me. For a three-outcome event — say a soccer match with home win, draw, away win — the bookmaker's posted odds imply probabilities that don't add up to 100%, but to like 105%. So if you bought all three outcomes at the posted prices, staking in proportion to the implied probabilities — say $48, $31, $26 — you'd pay $105 total and recieve exactly $100 back no matter who wins: a guaranteed loss, built into the menu itsself. He called it the overround and said it's just how the house makes money, but it looks to me like a Dutch book in the textbook sense, a set of bets where the person taking all the sides loses for sure.

So what does that pricing practice actually presuppose? Is it only meaningful if there's a fact of the matter about the 'true' fair odds for each outcome, such that the posted prices are just marked-up versions of those — i.e., does the practice quietly assume that probability is objective and that rational credences have to be coherent, with the summing-to-100% thing as a requirement? Or is it compatible with a view on which there is no such fact, and the posted odds are just what the market will bear? And how does the practice square with the same company running a bet exchange, where odds are set by users, sum to 100%, and the house only takes a cut of winnings — there, no one is being Dutch-booked, yet the house is happily taking the other side? If the overround is just a price markup and says nothing about anyone's rationality, what does the Dutch book argument actually buy us, and what would have to change in how we think of the house's edge if we gave up the idea that incoherent credences are a defect?
