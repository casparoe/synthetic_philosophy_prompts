I'm taking a graduate seminar on the history of pragmatism, and one of the assigned readings is a popular history of the movement that tries to set up the Peirce–James split in a single chapter. I've been re-reading the relevant section because a friend of mine has been insisting the two are basically the same person wearing different costumes, and I'm not sure I can tell whether I'm inclined to agree because the book convinced me or because I just haven't done the work. Here is roughly how the book puts it: "Pragmatism began with a single move: stop arguing about the 'essence' of a concept and ask instead what practical difference it makes. Peirce called this the pragmatic maxim — to work out what a concept means, spell out the conceivable practical consequences of the thing it refers to, and the meaning is just that set of consequences. James picked this up and applied it to truth itself, asking what concrete difference it would make in someone's life if an idea were true rather than false; he called this the idea's cash value. On this reading a belief is true just in case it is useful to act on — it works, it pays off in experience, and that is the whole story of truth. Peirce and James disagreed, the book notes, but only about scope: Peirce thought the maxim was a rule for clarifying the meaning of concepts, not a theory of truth, while James was the one who extended it into a full account of what truth is. Their quarrel was therefore a technical one about how far the method reaches, and once you strip away the jargon they are in substantial agreement: truth is whatever works in practice."

I suspect the last two sentences are doing a lot of quiet work, but I can't isolate exactly where the book is being sloppy. Could you go through that passage clause by clause and tell me which parts are accurate, which are common simplifications a sympathetic reader would forgive, and which are outright misrepresentations that Peirce or James themselves — or a careful historian of the dispute — would object to? In particular, I want to know whether the move from "works" to "that is the whole story of truth" is fair, and whether it is right to present the split as a mere difference in how far the method reaches. And then the bottom-line question: does the book's conclusion that the two are in substantial agreement survive once those corrections are made, or is the disagreement substantive in a way the summary papers over? I'm happy to be told my friend is right, but only on the strength of the actual arguments.
