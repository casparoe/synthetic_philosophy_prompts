I'm in a graduate seminar on the philosophy of mathematics, and I'm preparing a response to a classmate who has been pushing a version of the Lucas–Penrose argument that I can't quite place. He states it as follows:

(1) By Gödel's first incompleteness theorem, for any consistent formal system T strong enough to encode arithmetic, there is a Gödel sentence G_T that is true but not provable in T.
(2) I can see that G_T is true: given T, I can check that T is consistent and verify the meta-proof that G_T holds, so I grasp a truth that T cannot establish.
(3) So my mind reaches truths that T cannot.
(4) Hence my mind is not identical with T.
(5) Since T was arbitrary, my mind is not identical with any consistent formal system.
(6) But any digital computer is describable by a consistent formal system.
(7) So my mind is not a machine.

He's confident this shows the mind is non-computable. I'm a computationalist, so I can't accept that conclusion, and I can't find where his argument breaks. The incompleteness theorems are real and do genuine work, and I'm happy to grant them all their legitimate force, but I don't think they license the jump to the mind. The argument has a "proves too much" smell to me — the conclusion seems to outrun what the premises license — but I can't tell whether the problem is in the incompleteness step, the "I can see it's true" step, or the final jump to machines. Can you tell me exactly which step breaks, why that step looked fine, and what I'd have to give up to reject it? And does a defensible version of the argument survive the fix, or does it collapse entirely?
