I'm a quantitative risk analyst at a mid-size firm, and I maintain a standing set of probability forecasts — the chance that a given project will be delayed, that a supplier will default, that a regulatory change will take effect by a set date — and I know in advance exactly which scheduled pieces of evidence will arrive and when: a monthly loss report, the result of a quarterly stress test, the next earnings release, a court ruling on a known docket date. Before each of those scheduled evidence events I want to check that my current forecasts are coherent with what I expect to believe once the evidence lands, using the diachronic constraint on credences that van Fraassen calls the reflection principle.

Please turn that principle into a decision procedure my team can actually run in the thirty minutes before a weekly forecast review: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and flags a reflection violation rather than spitting out a number, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of judgment rather than arithmetic. It has to say explicitly what to do when I can't get a precise number for one of the future credences, and it has to be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure and which are genuine checks. Any forecast the procedure changes has to be signed off by the head of the risk team, so mark clearly which outputs are automatic and which require that sign-off.

Then run the procedure on two concrete forecasts. First, my current 30% forecast that a project will be delayed, where I am certain that if the scheduled stress test comes back clean I will move to 10% and if it comes back dirty I will move to 60%, and where I currently put a 70% chance on the clean outcome — show the full arithmetic and the decision. Second, a forecast about a project I personally champion, where I have a strong incentive to keep the success probability high and where the scheduled evidence is ambiguous enough that I am not actually sure what I will believe after seeing it — I want this one to end in an answer I will not like, and I want the procedure to show me exactly why it is entitled to that answer.

Finally, say plainly what this procedure cannot do: the cases where reflection is silent, where its preconditions fail, or where following it would be a mistake.

Respond in the style of an expert in Bayesian epistemology who also has to be useful to a practitioner, not a philosophy seminar. Think very carefully before answering; the value of this is in the translation — keeping what the principle actually says while making it runnable by someone who has never read it.
