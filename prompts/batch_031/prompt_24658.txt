I am writing a novel in which a character, a quantitative risk analyst, is offered at a dinner party a game of chance with the following rules: a fair coin is tossed repeatedly until it first lands heads, and if the first heads occurs on the nth toss, the player wins 2^n dollars when n is odd and loses 2^n dollars when n is even — that is, +$2, −$4, +$8, −$16, and so on. This is the game Nover and Hájek called the Pasadena game, and I want the scene to be mathematically honest. Please write a self-contained essay of roughly 1,200–1,500 words, pitched at a reader with a first course in probability, that does the following. First, lay out the probability distribution of the outcome exactly, including the fact that the game terminates with probability one, and note that the payoff is unbounded in both directions. Second, show step by step that the expected value of the game does not exist — not that it is infinite, but that the sum of the positive expected-value contributions diverges to positive infinity while the sum of the negative expected-value contributions diverges to negative infinity, so that the expectation is undefined even in the extended real numbers; and contrast this with the St. Petersburg game, where only the positive side diverges and the expectation is the well-defined extended quantity positive infinity. Third, explain the rearrangement fact: the series of expected-value contributions is conditionally convergent, so that summing the outcomes of the very same game in a different order can yield positive infinity, negative infinity, or — by Riemann's rearrangement theorem — any real number at all, and say plainly why this is a defect in the notion of "expected value" for this game rather than a defect in any particular calculation. Fourth, compute the median payoff, show that it is well defined, and contrast its stability with the mean's failure. Fifth, answer the question the character actually faces: the bookie asks what entry fee would make the game fair for a risk-neutral player, and the essay should explain why no finite fee does — subtracting any constant from the payoff leaves the expectation undefined — and then survey the defensible positions a careful decision theorist could take: refuse the game, accept it for free, or replace the mean with some other summary of the distribution such as the median, stating the cost of each, and noting that a minimax criterion offers no verdict either, since the worst outcome is unbounded below. Close with one or two sentences on what the case shows about the scope of expected utility theory as a theory of rational choice. Write in clear, unhurried prose; no citations or reading lists are needed, and the essay should stand on the game's own description rather than on its name in the literature.
