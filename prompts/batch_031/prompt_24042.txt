I've been reading through Penrose's Shadows of the Mind for a reading group and the Lucas-Penrose argument against machine minds has been bugging me. The basic move, as I understand it, is that for any consistent formal system a human can "see" the truth of its Gödel sentence but the system itself can't prove it, so the mind can't be a Turing machine. Everyone I've talked to just lists the standard objections — that humans make mistakes, that the soundness premise is too strong, that the argument only shows the mind isn't a single fixed system but could be some non-uniform hierarchy of them. But I keep thinking Wittgenstein would have had a completely different set of objections, and I can't quite pin down what they would be. He died in 1951, well before Lucas's 1961 paper, but he wrote about Gödel's theorem in the Remarks on the Foundations of Mathematics, and his later work on rule-following and the "picture of the mental" seems to hit exactly the kind of picture the argument relies on.

So: what would Wittgenstein have thought of the Lucas-Penrose argument? I don't want a generic "Wittgenstein would be skeptical of everything" answer — I want you to map his specific resources (the rule-following considerations, the "eye cannot look at itself" point, his actual remarks on Gödel, the critique of the mind-as-symbol-processing-machine picture) onto the specific steps of the argument, and say where the Wittgensteinian critique actually lands and where it might miss. And tell me what would change your mind: which specific argument or consideration would make you think Wittgenstein would actually endorse the core of the Lucas-Penrose argument rather than just dissolve it.
