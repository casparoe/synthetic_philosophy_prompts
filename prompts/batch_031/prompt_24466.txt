I'm in a philosophy of mind seminar that has just finished working through Dennett's The Intentional Stance, and a colleague in the group keeps reaching for the line "it's just predicting the next token" whenever anyone suggests that a large language model might be in the business of representing something — beliefs, commitments, or anything with the shape of a propositional attitude. Last week he used it to shut down a discussion of whether the model's confident-sounding but factually wrong claims count as a kind of false belief, and the week before, to dismiss the question of whether the model has any goal or policy at all, as opposed to merely producing text that looks like goal-directed behavior. I want to bring this back to the group, but I'm not sure I can say precisely what the slogan is doing, because I suspect it is doing several different jobs at once and he is not being consistent about which one.

Could you lay out the distinct precise claims that "it's just predicting the next token" could be making? I have a sense there is a range from the trivially true (the training objective function is next-token prediction) to something much stronger and more contentious (next-token prediction is, in some sense, all there is to the model's cognitive life, so no belief attribution is ever warranted). For each reading, I would like to know whether it is true, and if so, whether it actually supports the dismissal of the belief-attribution question or whether it only supports a weaker conclusion that he is overclaiming. I also want to know which reading he is probably invoking in each of the two contexts I mentioned — the false-belief case and the goal-or-policy case — and whether there is a reading on which the slogan is simply false, or at least not the decisive consideration it is being made to do.
