I've been working through the question of whether qualia are an incoherent posit, and I wrote up an argument for a study group last week that I now think runs away with itself. It was meant to be my own version of the quining line — press on what a quale would have to be and find that nothing qualifies — but the version I actually wrote ends in a conclusion I can't defend, and I can't see how to stop it short of that. Here it is:

(1) A quale is an intrinsic property of an experience: the experience has it in virtue of its own intrinsic nature, not in virtue of how the experience stands to anything else.
(2) For a property to be intrinsic to an experience in that way, the experience must be able to have the property on its own, without the experience's having any other properties at all.
(3) But no experience can have any property without also having other properties: every experience has a duration, a subject, a causal history, and so on.
(4) So no experience has any intrinsic property.
(5) So there are no qualia.
(6) The same reasoning applies to objects of any kind, so nothing is red, nothing is round, and nothing has any intrinsic property at all.

I'd be willing to live with (5), but (6) is where it stops being defensible, and I can't see how to get (6) without also getting a version of it I should reject. A friend who saw it is convinced the flaw is in the first premise, but I don't follow that either. I'd rather keep the idea that intrinsic properties are a coherent notion than give up the quining conclusion, though I'm not sure which I'm actually committed to. Please respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: item 1 names the step I should reject, item 2 says why that step looked plausible on the page, item 3 states the distinction the argument is collapsing, item 4 says what I give up by rejecting it, and item 5 says whether the quining conclusion survives once the argument is repaired and, if not, what it would take to get it back.
