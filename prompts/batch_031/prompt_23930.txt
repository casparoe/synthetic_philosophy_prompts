I'm on my org's model deployment review board, and we have to vote next week on whether to greenlight a large language model for a high-stakes agentic deployment — it'll be drafting and in some cases executing legal and medical triage decisions, with a human in the loop but a tight turnaround. The safety team walked us through their evals: a big battery of alignment and capability tests, and the model passes with a high average score, maybe 97-98% on the suite. But when I pressed them, they admitted they can only give us an average-case number. They can tell us the failure rate on the eval distribution; they can't rule out that there's some class of inputs where the model does something really bad, and they were explicit that no one can give a worst-case guarentee for a learned system. The board is split. Half of us say the average-case bar is high enough and that demanding worst-case guarantees is either incoherant (worst case over what, exactly?) or just a way to block every useful deployment ever. The other half say that for something this high-stakes, average-case is not the right standard, and we should require worst-case-over-a-specified-class guarantees or at least a high-confidence statistical bound on the tail failure rate before we approve.

Can you draft the written opinion I'm going to circulate before the vote? I want it to land on a concrete bottom line — approve, reject, or approve with specific conditions — and to actually reason through the crux, which I think is that "worst-case" is not one thing: a universal worst case over all possible inputs is either vacuous or unattainable for a learned system, so the real decision is which bounded class of inputs or environments we're demanding robustness over, and that's a modeling choice with real costs (narrow the class and the system becomes useless, widen it and you trade away average performance for tail robustness). I also want you to honestly steelman the side that loses, not just wave it away. And at the end, give me a short note on how confident you are in the reasoning and single out the one assumption or framing you're least sure about — like whether a high-confidence statistical bound on the failure rate is close enough to count as a guarantee for deployment purposes, or how you'd draw the line on the input class.
