I'm writing a piece for our ideas desk on how mathematicians actually prove existence results, and I ran into a paragraph in a popular math book (I'm not naming it, but it's the kind that lives on every "math for the curious" shelf) that I want to fact-check before I quote any of it. Here it is, more or less:

"Zorn's lemma is one of the workhorses of abstract algebra and analysis. The idea is simple: suppose you have a partially ordered collection of things — say, all the linearly independent sets in a vector space, ordered by inclusion — and suppose that whenever you have a chain of them (a sub-collection in which any two are comparable) you can always find something in the collection that sits above the whole chain. Then the collection has to contain a maximal element, and in fact a single largest one. This is why you can always extend a linearly independent set to a basis, why every vector space has a basis, and why so many 'build it up piece by piece and you'll reach the top' arguments go through. Under the hood, Zorn's lemma is just the axiom of choice in a different costume: the axiom of choice is the perfectly obvious principle that from any family of nonempty sets you can pick one element from each, and Zorn's lemma is a theorem that says the same thing in the language of partial orders. Since the axiom of choice is obvious, Zorn's lemma is really obvious too — it's just a reformulation that's hard to see at a glance."

A set theory friend of mine read this and kept grimacing at one or two spots, but when I pressed him he just said "you're conflating two things" and "that's not obviously true" and wouldn't elaborate, which isnt helpful when I have a deadline. So: go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what a working set theorist or algebraist would accept as accurate, what's a subtle slip they'd wince at, and what's flat out wrong — and whether the book's punchline, that Zorn's lemma is "really obvious" because the axiom of choice is, actually follows from the parts of the summary that are correct. I'm especialy unsure about the "maximal element, and in fact a single largest one" bit and the whole "the axiom of choice is obvious" framing, but I don't want to assume I'm right about which parts are the real problems.

One more thing, and it matters for how I write the piece: please keep straight what is just settled math (definitions, equivalences, independence results) from what is genuinely contested among people who work in the area. I have a strong suspicion the book is presenting one side of a live disagreement — about whether the axiom of choice is "obvious" or acceptable at all — as if it were the consensus, and I'd rather not do that in print. If "it's all obvious" is actually the majority view and I'm the one being weird, just tell me so.
