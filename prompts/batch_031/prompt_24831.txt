I'm writing up my position on probability for a philosophy-of-science seminar and I'd like a consistency check before I present. Here's roughly where I've landed, in five commitments:

(1) In actual practice I'm a subjectivist. I treat probability as a degree of belief: I start from a prior, I update by conditioning on evidence, and I act by maximizing expected utility over my current credences. When two of us disagree about the probability of a horse race, I don't think one of us is simply wrong in the way we'd be wrong about the mass of the horses.

(2) I think the Dutch book arguments really do show that coherent credences are required of a rational agent. If your credences violate the probability axioms, there is a package of bets you would all accept that guarantees you a net loss, and I take it that no rational agent wants to be guaranteed a loss. So coherence is, for me, a requirement on rational belief, not just a convenience.

(3) But I also hold that what makes a probabilistic model scientifically good — as opposed to merely rationally defensible for the person who built it — is empirical calibration. A model that assigns 90% to a class of events should be right about 90% of them, and I judge competing models by their calibration and coverage on held-out data, not by how closely they track my personal prior. I'd rather trust a calibrated model I don't fully believe than a coherent model that is systematically off.

(4) I've become increasingly suspicious that probabilities of genuinely one-off events — this particular candidate in this particular election, whether this particular patient survives this particular surgery — are not well-defined in the way probabilities of repeatable setups are. There is no sequence of trials for the number to converge to, and I'm not sure what it would even mean for the probability of this election to be 0.6 apart from the degree of belief of whoever is assessing it.

(5) When I want a probability that is objective — that does not depend on who is doing the betting or what they happen to know — I reach for the propensity interpretation: the idea that a physical setup (this particular coin, this particular batch of radium) has a real, mind-independent tendency to produce certain outcomes. Of the objective accounts I've considered, propensity is the only one that can attach a probability to a single setup rather than to a long run.

My question: do these five hang together? I've been operating with all of them at once and assuming they do, but I'm not confident, and I'd rather you discriminate than flag everything as slightly off. Please go through the set and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like they are. For the genuine ones, rank them by severity — I want a sense of which tension is most load-bearing, not a flat list — and for each, say specifically what would have to give and what it costs to give it up. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I want to know where I actually have to move.
