Two of my colleagues got into a long argument over lunch about Hume's essay on miracles, and I've been asked to referee. One works in philosophy of religion; the other is a historian of early modern philosophy. The dispute is about whether Hume's central argument is a priori or a posteriori, and I'm not sure they're genuinely disagreeing or just talking past each other.

The philosopher of religion, Dana, argues that Hume's argument is a priori and structural. On her reading, Hume's famous rule — that testimony for a miracle is only sufficient to establish it if the falsehood of the testimony would be a greater anomaly than the event it reports — is a point about the logic of evidence, not about any particular historical case. Because a law-violating event is, by hypothesis, so improbable, no finite body of testimony can raise its posterior probability above the probability that the testimony is false. So Hume is arguing that miracles can never, in principle, be rationally established by testimony. It's a point about the relative weights of evidence, and it holds no matter who the witnesses happen to be.

The historian, Owen, says this misreads Hume. On his reading, the comparative rule is only a general criterion, and Hume's actual argument is empirical. After stating the rule, Hume goes on to give a list of considerations that weaken miracle-testimony in practice — the character and interest of the witnesses, the consistency of the report, the tendency of miracle-claims to cluster among the ignorant and the superstitious, and the way the same kind of miracle is invoked to prop up mutually contradictory religions. These are inductive observations about the kinds of testimony that actually come with miracle claims. So Hume's conclusion, on Owen's reading, is that the testimony we actually get for miracles will always be weak — not that strong testimony is impossible in principle. If a miracle were attested by a large body of disinterested, sober, mutually independent witnesses, Hume's own criterion would, Owen argues, license belief.

Dana pushes back. She says Hume grounds the improbability of miracles in the uniformity of experience on which our notion of natural laws rests, and he treats those laws as so uniform that we may rationally rely on them. If the prior probability of a law-violation is that low, then even a thousand witnesses can't overcome it, because the probability that all a thousand are mistaken or deceiving is still higher than the probability of the violation itself. So the argument is a priori — it's a point about the relative weights, and it isn't sensitive to the particular witnesses in any case.

Owen replies that Hume is an empiricist and doesn't think we have a priori access to the probability of law-violations. On his reading, the improbability of a miracle is itself an empirical generalization drawn from experience, so the whole argument is a posteriori: it's an empirical claim that, given how experience works and how testimony tends to degrade, miracle-claims will always underperform. The language about the firmness of our experience, he says, is Hume describing our actual epistemic situation, not stipulating an a priori bound.

I couldn't settle it, and I'd rather not guess. Could you go through their exchange and say who's right at each step — not just who wins overall? In particular, I'd like you to say (a) whether the a priori / a posteriori distinction they're drawing is the right frame for the dispute or a false one, (b) what, if anything, both of them are missing about what Hume's argument actually does, and (c) what would change your mind — which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict, or where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
