I'm the instructor for an upper-level philosophy course on the metaphysics of properties, and I'm grading a short essay assignment (worth 20 points) from the unit on dispositions. The relevant lectures covered dispositions, the straightforward counterfactual analysis, masking, finking, and the safe-disposition response. Below is the question I assigned and one student's answer. Question: Explain the distinction between a finked disposition and a masked disposition. Then explain why the fink case poses a problem for the straightforward counterfactual analysis of dispositions (the analysis according to which an object has a disposition just in case it would manifest that disposition under the relevant stimulus conditions), and how the notion of a safe disposition is supposed to resolve the problem. In your answer, be explicit, in each of the fink and mask cases, about whether the object has the disposition and whether it would manifest it.

Student answer:

A disposition is a property of an object that is defined in terms of what the object would do under some triggering conditions. The straightforward analysis says that an object has a disposition just in case, if the relevant stimulus were to occur, the object would manifest that disposition. Fragility is the standard example: a glass is fragile just in case, if it were struck, it would shatter.

Both finked and masked dispositions are cases in which an object that has a disposition fails to manifest it, and both are therefore counterexamples to the straightforward analysis. In the masked case, some factor prevents the disposition from manifesting, but the disposition is genuinely present. A fragile glass wrapped in protective padding is still fragile; the padding simply blocks the shattering, and if the padding were removed the glass would shatter when struck. In the finked case, by contrast, the disposition is not really present in the first place, so there is nothing to manifest. A person who has been given a drug that removes their fear of snakes is no longer disposed to be afraid of snakes, so when a snake appears they are not afraid, and this is not a failure of the disposition to manifest but simply the absence of a disposition.

The difference between the two cases, then, is that in the masked case the blocking factor is external to the object and independent of the stimulus, whereas in the finked case the disposition itself has been eliminated. Because the fink case shows that an object can fail to manifest a disposition even though the stimulus occurs, the straightforward counterfactual analysis is in trouble: the conditional "if struck, would shatter" can come out false for an object that is genuinely fragile.

The notion of a safe disposition is meant to fix this. A disposition is safe just in case it would actually manifest when the stimulus occurs. The idea is that the fink case involves an unsafe disposition, so it no longer counts as a clean counterexample, and the straightforward analysis can be repaired by restricting it to safe dispositions rather than abandoned altogether.
