I sit on the resource-allocation council of Taveta, a small island cooperative. The island runs on a single freshwater aquifer that, at our current technology, can sustain about 1,000 people year-round; we treat that number as a hard ceiling rather than a target. We currently number 990. When newcomers arrive, the council decides how many to admit and how to adjust water rations, and we record a short rationale for each decision.

Our last three recorded decisions:

- Two years ago, a landslide on a neighboring island displaced 24 people who were in immediate danger (no shelter, no water). We were at 950. We admitted all 24, taking us to 974, and, by resident vote, cut everyone's water ration 2% for the year to make room. Recorded rationale: "people in immediate danger of death or destitution."
- Last year, a mainland government offered to send 120 seasonal workers; none were in danger, and admitting them would have taken us to 1,094, past the aquifer line. We declined. Recorded rationale: "admitting them would take us past the aquifer's carrying capacity."
- Six months ago, 8 people fleeing an active war arrived, in immediate danger of being killed. We were at 980. We admitted all 8, taking us to 988, and, by resident vote, cut the ration 1%. Recorded rationale: "people in immediate danger."

Now 40 survivors of an ongoing genocide have reached our harbor. They are in immediate, life-threatening danger, though not all equally: 12 come from a village under active attack that is expected to be destroyed within days, and the other 28 are in a besieged camp facing slow starvation. Admitting all 40 would take us to 1,030, past the aquifer line; at current technology we can sustain at most 1,000, so admitting all 40 would require either building a desalination plant (a large one-time cost plus a sustained ration cut for existing residents) or accepting a slow water shortage.

Working only from this record, please address the following: (1) name the distinct rules or principles our three decisions are jointly consistent with; (2) say what each of those rules predicts we should do in the genocide case; (3) give your best guess for the decision that best coheres with our own precedents, identify which of the three precedents is the closest analog, and explain why that analog does not actually settle the new case; (4) for each of the three plausible decisions — admit all 40 and commit the residents to the desalination cost; admit only up to the 1,000 line and turn away the rest; admit only as many as the residents vote to bear the cost for — state plainly what principle the council would be adopting by choosing it; and (5) name one further case, different from the genocide case, whose outcome would tell us which of the candidate rules we have actually been running.

If you think this framing is confused — for example, if the three precedents do not genuinely underdetermine the rule the way I believe they do, or if there is no real divergence among the candidate rules to work with — say so plainly rather than forcing an answer. Please keep the entire response under 800 words.
