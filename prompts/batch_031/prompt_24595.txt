I've been trying to nail down what I actually think about open-sourcing AI models by going on gut reactions to specific cases, and I want to know if there's a principle behind the pattern. Open-sourcing a small open-weights model, say a 7B general one: fine, I think it's good, transparency, lets people inspect it. Open-sourcing a frontier model that could help a small group do advanced cyber or bioweapons work: not fine. Open-sourcing the training code and data but not the weights: fine, feels like sharing the recipe not the finished dish. Open-sourcing alignment research, the "here's how to stop it doing X" papers: fine, and honestly the most important kind, you want everyone to know the defense. Open-sourcing a genuinely dual-use model, like one that helps with both drug discovery and designing a pathogen: this is the one I keep flip-flopping on. Open-sourcing a frontier model in a world where one state is already five years ahead of everyone else: not fine, it just hands the leader an advantage, doesn't level the field.

The pair that bugs me: I say open-sourcing the frontier model is bad but open-sourcing safety research about that same model is good, and both are "disclosing info about a dangerous capability," so where's the actual line? Is the principle about the capability itself, intrinsic dangerousness, or about who ends up with it, the change in the power distribution, and does the defense-vs-offense thing need its own clause? And does the right answer even depend on the geopolitical landscape, so it's not a property of the model at all? Tell me what principle, if any, gets most of these, what it says about a case I haven't listed, say open-sourcing a model that's dangerous but where the worst actor can already build it on their own, and which of my verdicts it forces me to drop.
