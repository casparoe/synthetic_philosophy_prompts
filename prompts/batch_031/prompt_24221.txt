I'm prepping a seminar on population ethics and animal welfare for next term and I keep circling back to this question of whether conservation policy should even be trying to reduce wild animal suffering, or whether the whole project is self-defeating. I wrote down where I think I land so I can sanity-check it before I put it in the syllabus, but I have a nagging feelng that something's off and I can't pin it down. Here's the package:

(1) I'm a total utilitarian about welfare — what matters is the sum across all sentient beings, not the average, and I weight a unit of suffering the same as a unit of happiness in absolute value. I'm fine biting the repugnant conclusion if that's what comes with it.

(2) I think the empirical case is pretty strong that the average wild animal life is net negative — predation, disease, starvation, parasitism, the total suffering just outweighs the total happiness. So the sum of welfare across a wild population is negative.

(3) I think it follows that reducing the number of wild animals is, all else equal, welfare-improving, and that habitat destruction — by lowering carrying capacity — is net welfare-positive. I've been telling my students this is basicly a direct consequence of (1) and (2).

(4) I think the non-identity problem is a real obstacle to person-affecting claims about population change — you can't say habitat destruction makes the would-be animals worse off, since they don't exist in the counterfactual. But I don't think this matters for my view, since total utilitarianism is impersonal and doesn't need person-affecting claims.

(5) I also think wild ecosystems have value that isn't reducible to the welfare of the animals in them — there's something valuable about biodiversity, about species persistence, about 'wildness' as such, over and above the sum of individual welfare.

(6) I think that if we could reduce the suffering of wild animals, we should do it, even if it turns out their population grows as a result, because reducing suffering is always welfare-improving.

Can you audit whether this actually hangs together? I want you to tell me which commitments genuinely pull against each other, which pairs only look like they're in tension but dissolve on inspection, and for each real tension, what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs. Rank the tensions by severity rather than just listing them all as equally bad, and be specific about which commitment would have to give in each case. I don't want a lecture on the topic — I want the audit.
