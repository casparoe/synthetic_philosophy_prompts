I keep seeing this analogy used to explain the Ellsberg paradox and ambiguity aversion, and I want to stress test it because I think its quietly carrying more weight than it should. The standard picture goes like this: risk is a roulette wheel or a fair die, the odds are posted and you just live with the variance, whereas uncertainty in the Knightian sense is a coin whose bias you dont know, or a casino where the house might have swapped in loaded dice. So of course a rational person treats the ambiguous case differently, and Ellsberg preferring the known fifty-fifty urn over the ambiguous one is just common sense, not a bug in expected utility that needs explaining. I've read a fair amount of the decision theory stuff on the blogs over the years (the maxmin expected utility posts, the multiple priors / Gilboa-Schmeidler line, the whole "is Ellsberg behavior actually rational" argument that never seems to end), so skip the basics and just audit the analogy itself.

What I want is the correspondence table first: what in the urn problem maps onto what in the die/coin picture. Then the disanalogies that actually matter for the conclusion that ambiguity aversion is rational, and I have a specific suspicion I want you to take seriously: I think the unknown-bias-coin picture smuggles in an objective probability assumption, i.e. that there is a real fixed bias out there in the world that you just haven't learned yet, which is exactly the assumption a subjective Bayesian denies, and once you deny it the whole risk/ambiguity distinction starts to collapse into "how much evidence do you have," which changes the story a lot. Also please keep the two-urn version (one urn known fifty-fifty, one urn unknown) separate from the one-urn version (a single unknown urn with no safe alternative to compare it to), because I think the analogy does genuinely different work in each and people conflate them constantly. And end by telling me whether the conclusion the analogy is loading for, that Ellsberg is the sane one and the expected utility maximizer is the weirdo, survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was the whole load-bearing wall all along. If there is a better analogy that doesn't smuggle in the objective probability piece, tell me what it is.
