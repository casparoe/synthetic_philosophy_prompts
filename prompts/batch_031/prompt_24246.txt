I'm a journalist who covers science and the philosophy of mind, and I'm putting together a piece on how we're supposed to study consciousness in the first place. I've been reading about heterophenomenology — the Dennettian move of treating a person's own reports about their experience as just another kind of text to be interpreted from the outside, rather than taking first-person testimony as privileged or authoritative. I find it appealing, honesly, because it's the only version of a science of consciousness I can see that doesn't immediately beg the question by smuggling in the very qualia it's supposed to be explaining.

So I want to push back on my own leaning. What would actually count against heterophenomenology as a method? I don't just want the stock "but what about qualia" objection. I want the specific, in-the-weeds defeaters: the worry that interpreting the reports already requires a theory of mind, so the method is circular; the worry that if reports are systematically confabulated or theory-laden then you've got garbage in and the method has no independent way to check; the worry that denying first-person authority is not a neutral methodological choice but a substantive metaphysical claim the method can't actually establish from the third person; and the worry that because any report can be reinterpreted, the method is just unfalsifable and can absorb any finding.

Can you sort these (and any others I'm missing) by how decisive they'd be? I'd like to know which of these a committed heterophenomenologist would themselves concede as a real cost they're willing to pay, versus which they'd dismiss as just the hard-problem crowd being uncomfortable. And I want an honest read on whether the method has been qualified so much — "well, we interpret the reports charitably within a best-explanation framework" and so on — that it no longer risks anything, and if so, whether that's a genuine problem for a method that's supposed to be rigorous. Personally, the circularity worry is the one that keeps bugging me, but I'd like to see whether it's really fatal or just a cost.
