ok so I know how this sounds, but in my philosophy of religion seminar this semester I've actually come around to thinking al-Ghazālī's occassionalism is the most defensible view of causation on the table — or at least way more coherent than the common-sense view where causes really have powers in them. My working version: created things don't have any genuine causal power, what we call "causes" are just the occasions on which God directly produces each event, and the regularities we see are just Gods customary way of acting, which he can suspend (miracles and all that).

Play it straight: what would count against this? What would have to be true for occasionalism to be wrong? I want the real exposure, not just counterintuitiveness. Like: what does the view actually commit you to about time and change — does every event need a fresh divine act, and what is that act in time, before the effect or simultaneous with it, or does the question even make sense? What happens to the distinction between occasionalism and just being a theist who is also a Humean about regularity — is there anything in principle that could tell the two apart, or has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability? And what does it do to our everyday practice of manipulating things — if the fire doesn't really burn the cotton, what are we actually exploiting when we light a match, and does the view have to account for the success of that, or is it fine to leave it as a mystery of divine custom? Tell me which of these a committed occasionalist would concede as counting against them and which they'd push back on, and rank the defeaters from fatal to just awkward.
