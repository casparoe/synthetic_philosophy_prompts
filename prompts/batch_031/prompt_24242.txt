I teach a small upper-level philosophy seminar on infinity and the foundations of mathematics — about fourteen students, one ninety-minute session. I've been circling the Thomson lamp puzzle (toggle a lamp on and off at successively halving intervals so that every switching is done before a fixed deadline) because I recently got into a long argument with a colleague about whether the lamp is on or off at the deadline, and we both just kept asserting our answers without either of us being able to say precisely why it is hard. I want to design an in-class activity that makes the students feel that difficulty themselves rather than hear me explain it.

The core thing I want them to run into, on their own and before I name anything, is the wedge between two things that coincide for any finite task but come apart for an infinite one: (a) having performed every step, and (b) having a final step that fixes a final state. I want the activity built so that the students do a lot of the steps for real — a card flipped face-up and face-down, a light toggled, some token moved — and feel that every time they think they have settled what the final state will be, one more step overturns it, and that the finite version works only because it has a last step doing the work, whereas there is no last step to do that work in the infinite version. The idea has to show up in what happens during the activity, not in a lecture afterwards. I have a rough shape in mind along those lines, but I am open to a better mechanism if you have one.

Please give me the full runnable design: the setup, the exact rules and sequence, the materials, any roles or commitments the students make, a timeline that fits ninety minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the parts of the idea. In the debrief, tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run it for the first time and how to handle it, and be frank about which feature of the puzzle the activity genuinely cannot reproduce — I suspect the "infinitely many steps packed into a finite time" part, since we cannot physically complete infinitely many steps in the room — and what I have to supply by hand.

One more thing, and it matters to me: keep the settled separate from the contested. That the halving intervals add up to a finite total, and that the on/off sequence has no limit, are settled mathematics, and the response should treat them as such. But the philosophical verdict — whether the task is possible at all or impossible, and whether the final state is genuinely indeterminate or simply not fixed by the setup as stated — is a live disagreement with real people on each side, and I do not want the response to present any one verdict as the consensus. Where the debrief takes a side, say so and flag that it is a side, not the settled answer.
