The dispute over whether deflationism gives a complete theory of truth has been going on for decades without settling. On one side, the deflationist holds that the truth predicate has no substantive nature and that the disquotational schema, T('P') ↔ P, captures everything there is to say about truth. On the other side, the critic argues that the schema cannot be fully general: the word 'truth' is used in ways the schema cannot cover — generalizations that quantify over what one believes, uses of 'truth' as a noun rather than a predicate, and self-referential sentences — so deflationism is at best a partial account. The deflationist replies that these are not genuine uses of the truth predicate, or that they can be rephrased in disquotational terms. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is mostly verbal: the two sides disagree about what counts as a genuine use of the truth predicate rather than about what truth is. Is that right, or is there a real formal disagreement in disguise about whether the schema can be fully general? Diagnose the structure of the disagreement using what each side actually argues, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or whether no progress should be expected and why.
