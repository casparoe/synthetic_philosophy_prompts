I am a novelist, and I am working on a story in which a character's choices are the main way the reader comes to know what she wants and values. I have been reading about revealed preference theory, and I would like you to argue a particular position about what the "utility" in that theory is supposed to mean. Please argue that, in revealed preference theory, the utility function should be understood not as a measure of a real psychological state of the agent — not as her satisfaction, happiness, or the strength of her desire — but as a purely formal device whose only job is to rank her alternatives, and that this formalist reading is forced on us by the fact that any two utility functions representing the same preference relation differ only by a strictly increasing transformation, so that no fact about the amount or intensity of utility is fixed by the theory. In making the argument, please bring out why this reading, while it rescues the theory from the impossibility of measuring utility, does so at the cost of stripping not only "utility" but "preference" itself of all psychological content, so that what the theory ultimately describes is a property of the agent's choice function rather than of her inner life. Please write your answer as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a letter.
