I've been reading Camus's Reflections on the Guillotine, the essay he wrote in 1957, on my own (in an English translation), and I'm stuck on what I take to be a contradiction in the way the argument is put together.

The essay opens with the story of his father, who got up in the dark to watch an execution in Algiers and then "came rushing home, his face distorted, refused to talk, lay down for a moment on the bed, and suddenly began to vomit." Camus's explanation is that his father "had just discovered the reality hidden under the noble phrases with which it was masked." To me, that story is doing the emotional work of the whole piece. The case against the death penalty reads as basically this: the practice is so revolting that even a man who believes the punishment is richly deserved is sickened by it.

But a little further in, Camus writes, "It would be just as unfair to attribute my conviction to mere mawkishness. I am far from indulging in the flabby pity characteristic of humanitarians, in which values and responsibilities fuse, crimes are balanced against one another, and innocence finally loses its rights." And he says he considers the death penalty "not only useless but definitely harmful."

That's where I get confused. I read those lines as Camus explicitly disowning the horror argument. He does not want his position to rest on disgust, so he anchors it instead in a practical case: that executions are useless because they neither deter crime nor restore order, and harmful because of the risk of executing an innocent person and the damage they do to the people who carry them out. On that reading, the vomiting story at the beginning is just a rhetorical hook that he is trying to distance himself from, and the real, defensible argument is the practical one.

If that is the right way to see it, then I have a problem. I have also read that the evidence on whether the death penalty actually deters murder is genuinely mixed, and if so, the "useless" half of the argument seems much weaker than it sounds. Which would mean the whole case collapses back into the very sentiment Camus says he is rejecting, and the essay undermines itself.

So can you walk me through how the practical argument is actually supposed to hold up, especially the deterrence point? And if the deterrence evidence really is as mixed as I have been told, how is the position supposed to survive without falling back on the emotional story he claims to disown?
