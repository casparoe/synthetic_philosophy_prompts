Two of my colleagues got into a long argument at a workshop about whether the relation "better than" is transitive, and whether the class of reasoning sometimes called "spectrum arguments" is sound. I sat in on most of it and honestly could not referee, so I would like you to.

One of them opened by arguing that "better than" is not always transitive. Her example: picture a long sequence of candidate outcomes in which each is a small net improvement on the previous one — a little more of a good, a little less of a harm — but the sequence is long enough that the last outcome ends up no better than, or even worse than, the first. So you can have "A is better than B, B is better than C, …, Y is better than Z" all true, while "A is better than Z" is false. She said this has the same structure as the classic sorites paradox, and it is exactly what makes spectrum arguments work: because the relevant property shifts only gradually, any sharp cutoff between the two endpoints — say, between "clearly good" and "clearly bad," or between two moral categories — is arbitrary, so the categorical distinction is not really there.

The other replied that "better than" is transitive, at least as a rational requirement on anything that is supposed to rank outcomes by their value: if it were not, we could go in circles, preferring A to B, B to C, and C to A, and our value judgments would be incoherent. He said her example establishes no non-transitivity at all. At most it shows that one of the marginal comparisons along the line is wrong, or that she is conflating "slightly better" (a matter of degree) with "better" (a strict ordering). He added that the spectrum argument is simply a fallacy: from "there is no sharp boundary between adjacent cases" it does not follow that "there is no real difference between the endpoints." There is no sharp line between "warm" and "hot," yet there is a real difference in temperature; a difference of degree is still a real difference. So the absence of a sharp cutoff does not dissolve the distinction.

The first came back and said that move was question-begging: he was stipulating transitivity into the very notion he was defending and then declaring the counterexample a mistake, which is circular. She pressed further: even granting that a difference of degree is a real difference, that does not answer her point, because the question was whether a categorical distinction — a difference of kind, not merely of degree — is real, and a purely gradual difference cannot ground a sharp category.

He answered that she was confusing our fallible, context-sensitive judgments of "better than" — which can look non-transitive because of framing, salience, and how a case is presented — with the relation itself, which is what those judgments are trying to track and which is transitive. If our judgments come out non-transitive, that is a reason to revise the judgments, not to conclude that the relation is incoherent. And her claim that degrees cannot ground a category was a separate mistake: many genuine categorical distinctions are realized through degrees, the way legal adulthood, or a ripe fruit, is a real category even though there is no single moment at which it is acquired.

They left it there. Could you go through their exchange and adjudicate it point by point? Specifically: is "better than" transitive or not, and what exactly does the first person's example show? Is the spectrum argument a valid inference or a fallacy, and if a fallacy, which one? What is each of them right about, and what is each of them missing? And do the two of them share a blind spot about the difference between the relation itself and our fallible judgments of it?
