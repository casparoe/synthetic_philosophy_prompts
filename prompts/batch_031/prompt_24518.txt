Okay, so the Lucas-Penrose argument. You know the one: Gödel's incompleteness says that for any consistent formal system F there's a sentence G_F that F can't prove but is true, and if the human mind *is* some formal system F, then we can "see" that G_F is true even though F can't prove it, so the mind isn't F, so the mind isn't a Turing machine, so consciousness is non-computational. It's been "refuted" by logicians for decades and it still comes up every single time someone on a forum asks whether AI can be conscious, and I've been tryng to figure out why the debate just doesn't end.

I grew up on LessWrong and I absorbed the standard response early: the argument is self-undermining, because the premise "we can see that G_F is true" already assumes our own consistency, which by Gödel's second theorem we can't verify if we're a formal system. So the argument assumes exactly what it's trying to prove. And on top of that, the diagonal construction just gives you an infinite regress — for any F you propose, there's a G_F it doesn't prove, so you just keep climbing the ladder, and at most you've shown the mind isn't *any particular* formal system, which is trivial. Plus the whole thing assumes the mind is consistent, and if we're inconsistent the argument falls apart, and we can't check that either. As far as I can tell every logician who's looked at this agrees the argument is dead.

But Penrose keeps bringing it up, and a bunch of philosophers and physicists find it genuinly compelling, and the debate just stalls. The logicians say "the argument is refuted," the Penrose side says "no, it shows consciousness is non-computational," and nobody moves. I've been trying to figure out what kind of disagreement this actually is, because it doesn't feel like a normal empirical or formal disagreement.

My working hypothesis, and I'm presenting it as a hypothesis: I think the two sides are answering different questions under one name. The logicians are answering "is Lucas's argument valid?" and the Penrose side is answering "is there a physical mechanism of consciousness that isn't computational?" The argument is a proxy for a deeper disagreement about whether mathematical understanding is a physical process at all. And the "self-undermining" objection is an epistemic point — it shows you can't *use the argument to know* that the mind is non-computational — not an ontological one. The mind might still be non-computational; the argument just doesn't get you there. So the logicians think they've won (the argument is refuted) and the Penrose side doesn't care (they're not defending the argument, they're defending the conclusion).

Is that the right diagnosis? Or is it actually a substantive factual disagreement (is the brain a Turing machine? which is an empirical question that nobody is actually doing research on), or a verbal one about what "computational" or "mechanism" even means, or a disagreement about what counts as a valid refutation? And given whatever diagnosis you land on, is there anything that would actually move it — a formal result, an empirical finding, a conceptual distinction — or is the impasse a sign that the core question (what does it even mean to "see" that G_F is true?) is an intuition that neither side can argue for, and the debate is just going to keep stalling forever?

I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of the argument and its objections. And an honest answer about whether anything could actually end it.
