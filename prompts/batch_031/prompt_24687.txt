I teach a graduate seminar on the philosophy of machine learning, and two of my students have been arguing about the relation between the no-free-lunch theorem and Hume's problem of induction. I would like a careful adjudication of their exchange, which I reproduce below as faithfully as I can.

Elena: 'The no-free-lunch theorem settles Hume's problem of induction. The theorem shows that, averaged over all possible worlds, no learning method outperforms any other — not even random guessing. So there is no rational, non-circular justification for preferring induction to any arbitrary rule. Hume was right that induction has no justification, and the theorem finally proves it.'

Marcus: 'That is not what the theorem proves. The averaging is over all possible target functions with a uniform measure, and that uniform measure is itself an inductive assumption — the assumption that the world is as likely to be any of those functions as any other. So the theorem uses an inductive premise to conclude that induction is unjustified. That is precisely the circularity Hume identified, not a refutation of it.'

Elena: 'The uniform measure is not an assumption about the world; it is part of the statement of the theorem. The combinatorial fact is real: for any two methods that ever disagree, the worlds in which one beats the other can be paired one-to-one with the worlds in which the other beats the one. The theorem just makes explicit what Hume already knew — that no inductive rule is provably superior to another purely logically. The circularity objection is a red herring, because the theorem does not claim that the world is uniformly distributed, only that no method wins across the board.'

Marcus: 'But that is exactly where the measure choice matters. The pairing argument works for the uniform measure because the uniform measure is the only measure under which the counting is symmetric. If you weight worlds by simplicity — which is what any actual inductive bias amounts to — the symmetry breaks, and the theorem says nothing about your method. So the theorem's conclusion holds only relative to a prior, and the choice of prior is precisely what Hume was asking about. You cannot use a prior to prove that no prior is justified.'

Elena: 'Grant that. But the theorem still does real work: it shows that any free lunch — any justification for preferring one inductive method to another — must be local, that is, must rest on an assumption that restricts the space of possible worlds to a structured subclass. So the theorem relocates Hume's problem from logic to the question of which class of worlds we inhabit, and that is progress: it tells us exactly what a justification would have to look like.'

Marcus: 'Relocating a problem is not solving it. And the structure you appeal to is exactly what the uniform measure erases. The theorem is a tautology about counting, and Hume's problem survives it.'
