I'm drafting a short interpretive essay for a graduate seminar on the history of British utilitarianism, and I'd like feedback on whether my central claim is defensible. The claim is this: Sidgwick's role as a founder and first president of the Society for Psychical Research was not a Victorian eccentricity or a private lapse into credulity, but was driven by a specific structural problem in his own ethics, the so-called dualism of practical reason. In The Methods of Ethics he argues that egoism (maximize your own happiness) and utilitarianism (maximize the general happiness) are both self-evident yet cannot be reconciled by reason alone, and that the only thing that would guarantee doing your duty is always in your long-run self-interest is a God who rewards and punishes together with an immortal soul. But Sidgwick insists that moral philosophy must be scientific in the sense of excluding bare supernatural assumption, and he had already resigned his Cambridge fellowship on the ground that he could no longer in good conscience declare himself a member of the Church of England, dying an agnostic. So he cannot simply import the religious guarantee into his moral theory. My interpretation is that psychical research, as the SPR framed it, offered a way to obtain empirical, non-confessional evidence for the survival of the soul, and that Sidgwick was therefore motivated to support it as a kind of service to moral philosophy: an attempt to supply, on methodological rather than dogmatic grounds, the very premise his dualism shows utilitarianism needs in order to be practically binding on a self-interested agent. I've tried to keep it honest by noting that he died an agnostic and that much of the SPR's early work was devoted to exposing fraudulent mediums, so I present him as an investigator seeking evidence rather than a believer seeking confirmation.

Could you comment on two things specifically. First, accuracy: is there anything in this account of the dualism or of Sidgwick's biographical situation that is wrong or misleading, and does the connection I'm drawing between the dualism of practical reason and his psychical research actually have textual or biographical support, or am I retrofitting a tidy story onto two facts that are merely co-located in one life? Second, the strength of the interpretation itself: what is the most serious objection to the reading that psychical research was, for Sidgwick, a program in the service of his ethics, and what would it take to make it more than a plausible-sounding reconstruction? And please be explicit about what would change your mind on this: which argument or piece of evidence would lead you to conclude my interpretation is right rather than merely clever, and where is your own reasoning most likely to fail in judging it?

Please treat me as someone who has read The Methods of Ethics and the basic secondary literature but is genuinely uncertain about the interpretive leap, so you can assume I know the difference between egoism and utilitarianism and don't need a primer on hedonism; I want the pressure on the specific connection, not a survey of Sidgwick's ethics. I'm also happy to be told that the honest answer is that this is under-determined and we can't really know his motivation, but I'd like you to say what you would need to see before you'd upgrade that to a verdict that the connection is real.
