The pessimistic meta-induction presses on scientific realism at a single point: the no-miracles defense needs the premise that past successful theories were "roughly true," and the pessimist replies that this phrase is doing all the work. I've been collecting my verdicts on whether the unobservable posits of an older successful theory survive in its successor, and I'd like to know whether a principle captures them. My verdicts: Newtonian gravitation to general relativity — survival; masses, the gravitational field, and spacetime geometry persist as limiting structures. Phlogiston theory to the oxygen theory of combustion — no survival; the successor explains the old successes by reference to nothing phlogiston-like. Caloric theory to the kinetic theory of heat — the caloric fluid does not survive, but temperature, heat flow, and thermal equilibrium do; I call this a partial case and am not sure I should. The luminiferous aether to special relativity — the aether does not survive, the electromagnetic field does. Miasma theory to germ theory — the idea of invisible disease-carrying agents survives in some weak sense, and I am not sure this should count as survival at all. The contrast that troubles me most is between Newton-to-GR and caloric-to-kinetic: in both cases the old theory's equations are recovered as limits of the new one, yet I call one survival and the other not. Is there a principle, stated precisely, with a clause for each of my cases that motivates it, that delivers all these verdicts — or a simpler one that delivers most of them and tells me which verdict to give up? Then apply it to a case I have not considered, say whether the electron survives from classical electrodynamics to quantum field theory, and say honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate without extra machinery. And does such a principle make the no-miracles defense defensible, or show that "roughly true" is ineliminably vague?
