I'm taking a philosophy of science seminar this term and I keep running into a pattern that I can't put a name to, and I've been trying to figure out if its already been carved out somewhere. The setup is the standard one: you've got two rival theories, say T1 and T2, and they're underdetermined by the evidence because the same data fits both. So far so good, I get that. But then the discussion shifts, and someone points out that the "evidence" isn't actually a fixed, theory-neutral set of observations. The observations you can make, and even how you record them, depend on which theory you're working with. So T1 and T2 don't actually agree on the same evidence — each one comes bundled with its own set of observations, and there's no view from nowhere to say which set is the "real" one.

To make it concrete: imagine two accounts of some physical system, and the measurements you'd do to tell them apart require instruments and background assumptions that are themselves shaped by the theory you're testing. So the raw data is already cooked by the theory before you even compare the two. I've seen people talk about the theory-ladenness of observation, but that feels like it's about how you interpret a particular observation, not about the fact that the very set of observations available to you is different under each theory. And it's not quite the Duhem-Quine thing either, which is about not being able to test a single hypothesis in isolation from its auxiliaries. I'm after something more radical: the evidence itself is theory-relative, so the underdetermination isn't just "the data underdetermines the theory" but "the data is part of the theory."

Does this have a name? If there's an established concept, what is it, and how does it differ from theory-ladenness and from Duhem-Quine, and which of those is the closest fit? If there isn't a name, can you coin one and define it carefully, including the boundary cases, like where it stops being "the evidence is theory-relative" and starts being just plain underdetermination with a fixed evidence set? I'd rather have a careful account of exactly what this thing is and isn't than a bare label. Think very carefully before answering, and respond in the style of someone who works in the philosophy of science and has thought about this a lot.
