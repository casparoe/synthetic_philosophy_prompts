I'm a retired field ecologist who spent most of my career on experimental design — replication, effect sizes, reliability, and the whole apparatus of deciding when a pattern in the data is worth believing. Only in the last couple of years have I started reading philosophy for its own sake, and I'm currently working through the situationist critique of virtue ethics, the line of argument associated with Mischel, Baumeister, and Haidt, along with the replies the virtue ethicists have offered. I've been going over it with a former postdoc who's now a social psychologist, and we keep talking past each other, which tells me the disagreement is at least partly a clash of vocabularies: the situationists' case reads to me as mostly a statistical case, while the virtue ethicists' reply is partly statistical and partly something else I can't yet name, and I suspect that mismatch is where my confusion lives.

Could you restate the whole exchange — the situationist critique and the virtue ethicist's response — in the vocabulary I actually work in: effect sizes, test-retest reliability, internal consistency, construct validity, base rates, and above all the distinction between a measure's failing and the thing it is supposed to measure's failing to exist? I'd like the translation to be as faithful as you can make it, and I don't want you to paper over the places where the philosophical argument doesn't quite fit a statistical frame. Then give me the residue report, which is the part I care about most: which claims translate cleanly into my vocabulary, which only translate with distortion, and which have no expression in it at all. In particular, I want your honest judgment on two things. First, whether the situationists' move from "the data don't support global character traits" to "global character traits don't exist" is a statistical error — a mistake about what the evidence licenses — or a legitimate philosophical point that statistics simply cannot settle, and if it is the latter, why. Second, whether the normative, teleological heart of virtue ethics — what a virtuous life is for, and what moral development is aimed at — survives the translation at all, or whether it is something my vocabulary was never built to carry, and what that tells us about whether the residue is a defect of my framework or a sign that the philosophical disagreement is less substantive than it looks.
