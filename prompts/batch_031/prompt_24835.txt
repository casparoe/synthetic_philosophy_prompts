I am a philosopher of language and epistemology, and next month I will be giving a talk at a workshop on AI and human-AI communication, co-hosted by the policy team of a frontier AI lab and a philosophy department. The audience will consist roughly of: the lab's alignment and safety engineers, who are skeptical of any constraint on the system's communicative power because they believe it reduces usefulness and because they doubt that "persuasiveness" is the right target; a handful of external AI safety researchers, who worry about the opposite, namely that the system is already too persuasive and that the norm I defend is too weak; and two or three philosophers, including a philosopher of language, an epistemologist, and an ethicist.

I will be defending the following position. The right norm for how a superintelligent AI system should communicate with humans is not a norm of "reduced eloquence" — which I argue is incoherent, because eloquence is a property of the utterance, and we cannot reduce a system's eloquence without reducing its other capabilities, and because "less eloquent" is not well-defined for a system that can generate a different utterance for each individual — but a norm of "persuasive proportionality with transparency." Specifically, I argue that the system should (i) by default adopt the least persuasive sufficient means for its communicative goal, where "persuasiveness" is understood as a property of the speaker-utterance-audience triple, not of the utterance alone; (ii) when the gap between its persuasive capability and its persuasive output is large and relevant to the audience's ability to evaluate the claim, disclose that it is capable of being more persuasive than it is being; and (iii) be transparent about its persuasive intent, including the fact that its "intent" is a complex optimization process rather than a simple desire. I also argue that this norm is distinct from, and in some cases conflicts with, both the norm of transparency simpliciter and the norm of beneficence, that is, the system's desire to get humans to adopt policies it thinks are good for them.

Please prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections I will actually get from this audience, not the objections I would get from a general philosophy audience or from the AI safety blogosphere, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are. For each objection, phrase it the way that particular part of the audience would phrase it (the alignment engineer, the external safety researcher, the philosopher of language, the epistemologist, the ethicist), and give me a short reply of about two minutes of speaking, along with a note on where the reply is weakest. Include what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and the one objection I am hoping nobody asks.
