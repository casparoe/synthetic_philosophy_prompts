I'm prepping a position paper for a graduate seminar on self-deception, and I've been oscillating between the impossibilists and the various possibilist reconstructions, but I've landed on a specific move I want to defend rather than just survey the landscape. Here is the position I want you to argue for, and I want you to commit to it rather than lay out the options: self-deception is genuinely possible, and the reason the standard objections to its possibility fail is that they all quietly assume the self-deceived subject ends up with a genuine belief. Drop that assumption. The mental state the self-deceived person is in is belief-like but is not belief — it guides the person's action, it is reported as a belief, and it feels like one, but it lacks the full doxastic commitment of real belief, specifically the normal responsiveness to counter-evidence and the pressure to revise that genuine belief generates when the evidence turns against it.

Now run that argument against the two strongest objections and show how the belief-like-but-not-belief move defuses each. First, the intention paradox: if I intend to make myself believe not-p, then I must already know that p is true (otherwise, why would I aim to install a falsehood?), but if I already know p, then I don't really believe not-p, so I haven't been deceived at all. Second, the two-people objection: deception seems to require a deceiver and a deceived, and in self-deception those are the same person, so the phenomenon either collapses into a contradiction or requires the subject to be split into two agents. I want you to explain, in each case, why the belief-like-but-not-belief state lets the subject be genuinely mistaken (so it isn't mere pretense or role-playing) while the mistake is actively maintained by a motive (so it isn't mere error or a simple lapse of attention).

Please take a definite line throughout. Don't end by saying it's complicated or that there are tradeoffs on both sides — commit to the position, give the reasons, and say what would have to be true for it to be wrong. And if the view has a real cost — for instance, that it draws a fuzzy boundary between belief and belief-like states, or that it reclassifies a lot of ordinary cases of irrationality as something else — name that cost explicitly and defend it anyway, rather than hiding it.
