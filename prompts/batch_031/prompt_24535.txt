I'm trying to reverse-engineer the implicit rule my colleague, a decision theorist, applies when judging whether it is rational to defer to a group's choice against one's own private lean. Her verdicts on a series of earlier cases:

1. A person who is only mildly hungry sees a long queue forming at a new restaurant and joins it — rational.
2. A junior doctor, whose own reading of the evidence only weakly favors the old protocol, adopts a new treatment protocol because all of her more senior colleagues have — rational.
3. A startup founder, whose own benchmarks show a small performance disadvantage, adopts the industry-standard framework because all of her competitors have — rational.
4. A juror who is fairly confident in her lean toward acquittal switches to a conviction vote after learning that the other eleven jurors voted to convict — not rational.
5. A person with a clear private preference for one brand of coffee switches to a different brand because it is trending on social media — not rational.

The new case: a mid-level engineer has done her own careful analysis and is strongly convinced that technical approach Y is superior, but the senior leadership team, who are on average more experienced and better informed about the business context, has publicly decided on approach X. She must decide whether to go along with X or to push for Y.

What rule does she seem to be running? Which other rules, if any, fit her five verdicts just as well? What does each candidate rule predict for the new case, which prediction is her most likely verdict, and why? Finally, name a further case that would best tell the candidate rules apart.
