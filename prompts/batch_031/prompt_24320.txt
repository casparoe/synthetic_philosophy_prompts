I'm teaching a philosophy of language seminar next semester and I want to do a hands-on activity on Davidson's radical interpretation. I've got about 15 students and 90 minutes, and the usual "here's a list of his ideas" approach doesnt work for me — I want the students to actually feel the problem. The core ideas I want them to run into are the principle of charity and the indeterminacy of radical translation, but I'd also love if triangulation came up naturally without me having to name it. Can you design a classroom activity where the students are the interpreters and they have to build a translation manual for a constructed language, and the language is set up so that they hit the limits of what they can figure out from the data alone? I want the rules and the setup to be such that the philosophical problem shows up in what they do, not in what I say afterwards. Include a timeline, the roles, and a debrief that maps the moments they experience to the specific Davidsonian ideas. And be honest about what the activity can't reproduce — I dont want to oversell it to a room full of students who've read the abstracts but not the papers.

Also, I was talking with a friend about this and she said the whole thing might just collapse into "you can't figure out the ambiguous word so you guess," which is fair but I want the debrief to be more than that. And I don't want the students to be able to just ask the speaker for clarification in English — that would ruin the whole point. So the rules need to be tight there, and I'd appreciate a note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, because I've learned the hard way that my cleanest activity designs are the first to fall apart.
