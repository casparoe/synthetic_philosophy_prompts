I'm taking a seminar on the philosophy of decision-making this term and I need to submit a proposal for my term paper (about 6,000 words, due at the end of the semester). The topic I keep coming back to is the sunk cost fallacy, and more specifically whether the standard verdict — that honoring sunk costs is always irrational — actually holds.

My half-formed hunch is that the "always irrational" verdict quietly assumes a particular picture of the agent: a single, unified, stable decision-maker who could in principle have chosen the whole course of action at once. Once you model the agent the way the behavioral literature actually has to, as a sequence of selfs who decide at different times, with different information, and with different vulnerability to future temptation, the verdict gets much less clear. I suspect there are at least three genuinely distinct conditions under which it can be rational to let a past, unrecoverable investment affect a present choice: when the sunk cost is a reliable signal of your true preferences; when incurring the cost was a deliberate commitment device against your own future weakness of will; and when what gets labeled a "sunk cost" is really a recoverable asset like a skill, so that the cost was never sunk to begin with.
