Draft the joint statement that a committed "binding" theorist and a committed "non-binding" theorist about Pascal's muggings could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The binding theorist holds that when a mugger makes a claim of the form "give me X, or I will bring about a very large harm Y (with some small but nonzero probability, or with certainty)," a rational agent should pay — or at least cannot rationally refuse without giving up a principle it otherwise accepts. The non-binding theorist holds that a rational agent should not pay, and that there is a principled reason, not mere gut, why the mugging's expected-value calculation fails to bind.

Produce, in order: (1) a numbered section of claims both camps accept — about the scenario itself, about what is and is not at issue, and about the fact that neither side is satisfied by the answer "just trust your intuition that the mugger is a scam"; (2) the precise statements of where they still differ, each phrased so its own camp would accept the wording of its own view; (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. I expect the cruxes to include, at minimum: whether the why-me / dilution consideration (the mugger's threat could have been aimed at anyone, and there may be many such muggers) legitimately drives the rational probability of this particular threat down to near zero; whether unbounded or infinite utility is coherent, given that unbounded utility makes expected utility degenerate, since many actions then carry infinite expected value and cannot be ranked against one another; and whether the correct decision criterion is expected-utility maximization or a robustness criterion such as maximin or satisficing, which is insensitive to tiny-probability, huge-payoff claims. (4) For each crux, the concrete case or argument both camps would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction about that case recorded in advance.

Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a cruxes table, with one row per crux giving: the crux; the agreed case or argument that bears on it; the binding theorist's prediction; and the non-binding theorist's prediction. And say explicitly where, if anywhere, part of the dispute is merely verbal — for instance, whether the two camps actually disagree about what a rational agent should do in the concrete scenario, or only about which principle licenses the answer.
