I'm a PhD student in the history and philosophy of science, and for the past year I've been working on accounts of scientific progress — the Lakatosian view that a research programme is progressive so long as its internal heuristic keeps yielding novel predictions, and the Kuhnian view that change is a shift between paradigms, where "progress" is at most a widening of the range of problems the new framework can absorb. I keep getting asked, at talks and on qualifying, whether philosophy makes progress, and I've realized I can't answer it without first pinning down what my own criterion is, because the criteria I use for science don't obviously transfer. So I've been going by my gut on a set of concrete cases, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, captures the pattern.

Here are my verdicts on whether a given development counts as progress in philosophy:

1. The Gettier counterexamples to the justified-true-belief account of knowledge count as progress, even though they produced no new consensus theory and we may never get one. What got us was a better-mapped problem space, not a better answer.

2. Russell's theory of descriptions counts as an advance over the pre-Russell treatment of definite descriptions, even though it has since been superseded and some of its central claims are now rejected.

3. The development of formal logic — Boole, Frege, the logicist programme — counts as progress in philosophy, but I have a persistent sense that this is progress in a tool philosophy uses rather than in philosophy itself: the subject matter (the nature of truth, knowledge, meaning) looks the same before and after Frege.

4. Kant's critical project is an advance over Hume's empiricist account, but I suspect part of my verdict is the drama of the story — a thinker jolted awake by a rival — rather than a genuine improvement in how the problem is handled.

5. The rise of experimental philosophy does not count as progress in philosophy. It imports a method from psychology, and I don't think it improves on the armchair method the way formal logic did.

6. The rise of formal epistemology — probability and decision theory applied to belief, knowledge, and rationality — does count as progress, even though it too is a methodological import from outside philosophy.

The pair I keep going back and forth on is 5 and 6: both are imports of a method from another discipline, yet I judge one as progress and the other as not, and I can't yet articulate the difference. My tentative guess is that formal epistemology changes the standards by which arguments in the subject matter are evaluated — it gives us a precise notion of coherent, rational belief — whereas experimental philosophy merely supplies a new instrument for eliciting the same kind of evidence the armchair method was already after. If that is the real difference, then 3 falls on the "changes the standards" side, and 1 and 2 fall on a different side altogether: they are not methodological imports at all, but refutations that sharpen the problem.

What I'd like is a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely, that captures these verdicts. For each candidate, tell me which of my cases motivates each clause, what it predicts for cases I haven't listed (say, the rise of computational philosophy, the application of decision theory to ethics, or the analytic revival of metaphysics), and — honestly — which of my six verdicts it cannot accommodate without doing violence to some clause. I don't need it to save all six; if the simplest coherent principle forces me to drop one, I want to know which one and why. I'm not after a bibliography or a survey of the literature on progress; I'm after the criterion itself, stated so that I can apply it to a new case on my own.
