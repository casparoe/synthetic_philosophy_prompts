I have spent my career as a field ecologist, and only in retirement have I begun to read Rousseau seriously; I find his 1758 letter to d'Alembert on the theatre far more coherent than the usual summary of it as a simple ban on stage-acting. Next month I am moderating a small colloquium whose guest has just written a book defending a particular reading: that Rousseau's real target was never dramatic art as such but the professional actor and the idle spectator, and that the sketch Rousseau gives of a theatre in which citizens themselves perform for one another proves he never intended to forbid tragedy. I would like you to draft the questions I should put to her. Please respond in the style of a seasoned philosophy interviewer, and think very carefully before answering. Give me exactly six questions, each immediately followed by a one-line follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give. The questions must turn on the internal tensions of that reading rather than on general biography: press the point that if the fault lies in the professional actor, the citizen theatre still retains the idle spectators he diagnosed as corruptible; press the point that he ranks comedy as more dangerous than tragedy precisely because its characters resemble ordinary citizens, which seems to make a citizen comedy the worst case by his own lights; press the point that he concedes Molière's Misanthrope is a masterpiece yet treats its moral order as inverted, so ask what in his alternative theatre would cure that inversion once the staging is no longer professional; and press the point that his Greek exception — ancient tragedy was tolerable because it was embedded in civic ritual — makes his principle a claim about the social embedding of a practice rather than about the practice itself. Do not ask her to restate the book's thesis, and do not ask for a bibliography or a list of further readings. End your response with a single final line beginning "Verdict:" that gives, in a short phrase of no more than six words, the one point of greatest internal tension in the reading that your questions are built to expose.
