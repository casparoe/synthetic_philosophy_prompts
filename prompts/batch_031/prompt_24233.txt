I chair my city's public health budget committee. We have a fixed sum to allocate between two prevention programs, and the whole decision turns on a question we keep dodging: whether a very large number of very small harms can, in the aggregate, outweigh a small number of serious ones — and, if so, what else we'd be committed to accepting. Program A prevents a severe respiratory illness in an estimated 200 residents a year — each case means months of suffering and a real risk of death. Program B prevents a mild, self-limiting skin condition in an estimated 500,000 residents a year — each case means a day or two of minor discomfort. We can't fund both. Please draft the decision memo I'll circulate before Friday's vote. It should recommend a concrete allocation with a time frame, and it should work through the aggregation question on its merits rather than defaulting to a single off-the-shelf metric. Structure it in four sections, in this order: "Bottom line" (the recommendation and time frame, ~80 words); "The case for the many small harms" (the strongest argument for letting the 500,000 tiny cases aggregate, ~150 words); "The case for the few serious harms" (the strongest argument that a per-person threshold should dominate, ~150 words); and "Why I recommend what I do" (which considerations tip the balance, an honest concession of what the losing side gets right, and any review trigger, ~120 words).
