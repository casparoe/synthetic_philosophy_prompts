I've just come out of a long, somewhat heated argument with a friend about whether the modern monetary system is, in effect, an institutionalization of exactly what Aristotle condemned, and I'd like a careful reconstruction of what the system actually presupposes rather than a point-scoring of his position.

Let me fix the practice concretely so we're both talking about the same thing. In the modern system, commercial banks create new money when they extend loans: a loan simultaneously creates a matching deposit, so the money supply expands endogenously as credit expands, constrained only by reserve and capital rules rather than by a fixed stock of something like gold. On top of that, the central bank sets the policy interest rate and can expand the monetary base through open-market operations and quantitative easing, and it is widely held that these levers causally move real output and employment. And the whole structure runs on interest: every loan carries a price, understood as the time value of money.

My friend's claim is that this is chrematistics as Aristotle described it — wealth-getting pursued for its own sake, unbounded, and driven by usury, which Aristotle treated as contrary to nature because money is barren and does not beget more of itself. On his reading, we have converted oikonomia, the bounded management of a household aimed at a good life, into an unbounded engine for generating more money, and the one thing we have kept from Aristotle is his point that money is merely a convention, a token whose value is not intrinsic — which he thinks makes his critique of the rest even more damning.

What I want is a disciplined account of the philosophical commitments the practice actually presupposes. First, what view of what money is does endogenous creation under central-bank control commit us to — a barren convention, a real store of value, a social fact whose quantity is managed rather than fixed, or something else? Second, what does the belief that interest-rate and money-supply policy causally affects real output presuppose about money's causal role, and does that presupposition sit comfortably with the "money is just a token" point, or does it quietly require money to be more than a token? Third, does the practice of interest and the time value of money presuppose that money is generative in a way that directly contradicts the claim that money is barren, or is there a reading on which the two coexist? Fourth, does the system as a whole presuppose the unboundedness of chrematistics, or is it compatible with some bounded, oikonomia-style end?

Then separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, say where it is philosophically underdetermined so that several views of money would license it, and tell me what would actually have to change in the system if we replaced the presupposed view of money with the barren-convention view — or, conversely, what Aristotle's critique would have to give up if the presuppositions are in fact coherent.
