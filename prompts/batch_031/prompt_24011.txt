Question: State the Principal Principle and the condition on the evidence for it to apply. Explain the undermining problem for the Principal Principle, and explain how Lewis's New Principle is supposed to respond to it.

Student's answer:

The Principal Principle says that a rational agent's credence in a proposition p about the outcome of a chance process should equal the objective chance of p, given the agent's admissible evidence. Evidence is admissible if it is historical information about the past, or information about the laws of nature and the general theory of chance — that is, evidence that bears on p only through the chance of p, rather than directly on whether p will in fact occur.

The main problem for the Principal Principle is the undermining problem. On a Humean reductionist view, the chance of p is fixed by the complete mosaic of categorical facts in the world, and that mosaic includes the agent's own credences and observations. So the agent's credence in "the chance of p is x" is itself a mosaic fact. If the agent is a poor judge of chances, then the fact that the agent believes the chance is x is evidence that the chance is not x. The agent's evidence then bears on the outcome independently of the chance, and the Principal Principle is undermined: the agent should not simply set her credence equal to the chance.

Lewis's response is the New Principle. The New Principle makes the agent's credence in the chance admissible evidence, so that the agent can take the undermining into account when updating. Because the agent's credence in the chance is now admissible, the undermining argument no longer goes through, and the agent may set her credence in p equal to the chance of p. The New Principle thus solves the undermining problem, and it is now the consensus view.
