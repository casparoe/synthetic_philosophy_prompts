I'm taking a philosophy of quantum mechanics seminar this term, and we've been asked to post our views on the Everett interpretation and the quantum immortality / quantum suicide debate ahead of a discussion, so that the other students can poke at them. I've tried to be precise about what I actually hold, and I'd like a consistency check before I post. Here is where I've landed:

(1) I accept the Everett interpretation as the correct interpretation of quantum mechanics: the universal wavefunction is a real physical object, unitary evolution never breaks down, and measurements produce genuine branching rather than collapse.

(2) I accept the Deutsch–Wallace decision-theoretic derivation of the Born rule: a rational agent in an Everettian universe should, when making pre-measurement decisions, weight the branches by their Born-rule weights, and this is what justifies treating those weights as the agent's subjective probabilities.

(3) I think that, from the first-person perspective, a quantum suicide experiment looks like nothing happens: the observer always experiences survival, because the version of the observer who dies has no experience to report, and you can only find yourself in a branch in which you exist.

(4) I think personal identity is not preserved across branching: there is no fact of the matter about which post-branching copy is "you," so the question "will I survive the experiment?" is not well-formed.

(5) I think a rational Everettian should never perform a quantum suicide experiment, because the expected disutility of the near-death experience in the branches in which you survive outweighs any epistemic gain about the interpretation.

Do these hang together? I'd like an audit rather than a lecture: which pairs are in genuine tension, which only look like they are, and for each genuine tension, what is the cheapest revision and what does it cost? Please order the tensions by severity and say specifically which commitment(s) have to give in each case.
