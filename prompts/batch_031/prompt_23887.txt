Okay, I've been chewing on this statue-and-clay thing for a couple of weeks now and I want to stress-test where I've landed, because I'm not sure I can actually live with the consequences. I've come around to the idea that when a sculptor shapes a lump of clay into a statue, you end up with two distinct objects sitting in exactly the same place — the statue and the lump of clay — and they're genuinely not the same thing, even though they're made of the exact same bits of clay and occupy the exact same space. My main reason is the modal asymetry: the lump of clay could get squashed into a blob and it'd still be that same lump, but squash the statue and it's destroyed, so they've got different "could-have-been" properties, and if two things are truly identical they should share every property. So they can't be the same object.

But I'm uneasy about what this commits me to. What would actually count against this two-objects-in-one-spot view? I'm not just after the "well, isn't it weird to have two things in the same place" objection, though I'll grant that one has some pull. I want to know which specific arguments, intuitions, or results from adjacent areas — persistence over time, personal identity, the ship of Theseus, whatever's relevant — would really threaten it, and crucially, what a committed defender of the view would themselves concede as counting against them versus what they'd just wave away. And honestly, has this view been qualifed into unfalsifiability at this point? Is there any possible argument or finding that would actually change a defender's mind, or is it just insulated? And if it's insulated, is that a problem for a metaphysical view of this kind?
