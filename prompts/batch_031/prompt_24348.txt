okay so in AP phil we got into this whole debate about vague words, like whether there's a real cutoff for "bald" or "heap" or if it's all just in our heads. There are two main theories floating around: one where the cutoff actually exists and it's just that we can't locate it (epistemicism i think), and one where you consider every precise way you could draw the line and a sentence only counts as true if it's true under all of them (supervaluationism). i kind of like the second one, so i tried to find a flaw in it and i think i came up with something pretty devestating, but i'm not sure it works because the conclusion feels like it's proving too much. here's the argument:

(1) supervaluationism says a vague sentence is true just in case it's true under every possible way of precisifying the vague word.
(2) let B be "this guy is bald," where the guy is a genuine borderline case.
(3) since he's a real borderline case, B comes out true under some precisifications and false under others.
(4) so B is not true, because it's not true under every precisification.
(5) by the exact same reasoning, "B is not the case" is also not true.
(6) so neither B nor its negation is true.
(7) but the law of exluded middle says B or not-B has to be true, full stop.
(8) so now "B or not-B" is true even though neither B nor not-B is.
(9) that's a contradiction, because a disjunction can't be true if neither of its parts is true.
(10) so supervaluationism is incoherent and the whole theory collapses.
