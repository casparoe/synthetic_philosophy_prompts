spent the last three hours arguing with a friend about advance directives and I still feel like I'm the one who's wrong even though I can't say why. Her position is basically that honoring a living will is just respecting the person, full stop — the competent them wrote it down, so the incapacitated them has to follow it, and anyone who hesitates is really just letting a doctor take over. And I get it, I do, it sounds airtight. But the case that keeps bugging me: the person with late-stage Alzheimer's who's actually not suffering, who's calm and even kind of settled, but whose directive from ten years ago says 'do not keep me alive if I can't recognize my own family.' What exactly gives the sharp ten-years-ago version of them the right to veto the content version of them? And here's the part that really throws me — we don't let a person do this anywhere else. A competent adult can't sign a contract now that binds their future self to, say, work for a year at a pittance, or to never quit a job, or to be their kid's guardian no matter what; the future self gets to change their mind about pretty much everything, a promise can be broken, a preference can be revised. So why is the one domain where the past self gets iron-clad, enforceable authority over the present self the one where the present self is least capable of objecting? I think the whole practice of advance directives quietly presupposes some particular theory of what makes a person the the same person over time, and that the theory it needs isn't the one we actually use for contracts or promises or just changing your mind. Can you reconstruct what view of personal identity and self-continuity the advance-directive practice strictly requires, seperate that from what it merely fits well with, point out where it contradicts how we treat the same person's commitments in every other context, and tell me what would have to change if we forced ourselves to use one consistent view of the self across all of it?
