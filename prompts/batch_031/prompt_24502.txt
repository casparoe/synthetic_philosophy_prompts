My father died on Tuesday, at seventy-three, after thirty-one years as a state tax auditor. He was also, to the enduring bewilderment of his colleagues, a lifelong libertarian, and "taxation is theft" was never a joke to him; it was the lens through which he read every statute he enforced. He left a note asking that his eulogy not do either of two things. It must not stand up and declare the slogan a settled truth, because he knew the claim only works if you first accept a theory of property that much of the room would not, and he would have called that sloppy. And it must not soften the slogan into a harmless quirk or a phase, because the conviction was the organizing principle of his life and he would have called that a lie. I'm giving the eulogy, and the room will include his former colleagues from the tax office, his family, and a handful of his libertarian friends. Please write it. It needs to be warm enough to comfort a room of people who are grieving and who do not share his politics, while doing the real work of explaining what the slogan was for him — not as a claim he wanted everyone to accept, but as a discipline: a refusal to let the word "theft" be applied loosely to things that are merely disagreeable, and a way of taking the rights of the individual seriously even when the individual is standing on the wrong side of a tax form. It should land as affection and as an act of understanding, not as a political speech or a lecture, and it should be something he would have considered honest rather than either flattering or evasive.
