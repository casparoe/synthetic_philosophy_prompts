I'm building this for a seminar on epistemology and the conclusion is one I can't live with, so I'm pretty sure there's a gap somewhere and I just can't find it. The setup is the lottery paradox and the preface paradox, which I'll take as granted: in a 1000-ticket lottery it's rational to believe each ticket loses but not to believe they all lose, and the book author rationally believes each of her thousand claims yet also rationally believes the book contains an error. Both are just the same high-probability threshold doing its thing. Now here's the argument, and it's the second half that I think is doing something fishy.

(1) Fallible rational belief = a belief a careful person can hold while conceding they might be wrong. It's licensed by a probablistic threshold: if P(p | evidence) is high enough, you may rationally believe p even though p might be false.
(2) The lottery shows this kind of belief is not closed under conjunction: I may rationally believe each ticket loses, but not rationally believe all of them lose.
(3) The preface paradox shows the same non-closure from the other end: the author rationally believes each claim and rationally believes at least one claim is false.
(4) The author's reason for (3) is statistical, and it's the *lottery* reason: each claim is ~99% likely to be right, so a 1000-claim book is almost certainly wrong somewhere. So "the book contains an error" is itself a fallible rational belief, formed by the same threshold.
(5) So far no contradiction: non-closure means she can hold both "each claim is true" and "some claim is false" without the set being inconsistent.
(6) Now turn the threshold on the belief-forming process itself. The author has made lots of high-probability judgments over her career; most were right, some weren't. So by the threshold it's rational for her to believe at least one of her current beliefs is wrong.
(7) That belief, "at least one of my beliefs is wrong," is itself one of her beliefs. And the same statistical reasoning that produced it applies to it too, because it was itself formed fallibly.
(8) So the reasoning that justifies "the book has an error" also justifies "my belief that the book has an error is wrong," and the same for every belief in the stack. The threshold eats its own tail.
(9) If every fallible belief is thereby itself probably wrong, then no fallible belief is secuity, and the threshold that was supposed to license fallible belief licenses nothing stably.
(10) So fallible rational belief is self-undermining: the probabilistic reasoning that makes it possible guarantees each instance is probably false, so you can't rationally hold any of them.
(11) Conclusion: there is no such thing as fallible rational belief. Belief is either all-or-nothing certainty (which is basically nothing) or it's irrational.

I can't accept (11). I'd much rather keep the idea that careful fallible belief is a real, stable thing — that's basically what most of my life is.
