I run a small podcast where I interview philosophers about topics that sit at the intersection of philosophy and computer science, and next month's guest is a philosopher who defends epiphenomenalism — the view that phenomenal consciousness (qualia) is genuinely real but has no causal powers. I've been reading up on the "paradox of phenomenal judgment," which I understand roughly as this: if qualia really play no causal role, it's hard to see how my judgment that "this experience is red" could be correctly about the red qualia, since nothing about the qualia figures into the causal process that produces the judgment. I want the interview to go well beyond having the guest restate the view or deliver a canned defense. Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that press specifically on the paradox of phenomenal judgment? I'd like at least one question that probes whether the paradox really depends on the assumption that a judgment can be about a qualia only if the qualia causes the judgment, and at least one that asks whether the epiphenomenalist can secure the "aboutness" or correctness of phenomenal judgments through a non-causal relation — for instance, the qualia's being grounded by, or identical to, the underlying physical state — without that quietly counting as the qualia having causal power. For each question, please also give me a follow-up that's keyed to the answer the guest is most likely to give: for example, if they appeal to the identity of qualia with neural states, a follow-up that presses on whether that move reintroduces causal efficacy and so undermines the epiphenomenalism they're trying to defend, and if they insist that qualia are strictly non-physical, a follow-up that asks what non-causal relation is supposed to do the explanatory work and whether that relation is any more intelligible than the causal one they're rejecting.
