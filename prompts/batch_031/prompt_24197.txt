Okay so i'm writing a short essay for a seminar on medieval Jewish philosophy and i want to settle an argument i keep going back and forth on. The setup: Maimonides (Rambam) argues in the Guide that animal sacrifice was never part of God's ideal plan for the world. It was a concession — a way to wean the Israelites, fresh out of Egypt and steeped in pagan temple worship, off of the animal-killing and incense-burning habits they already had, by redirecting that behavior toward the one true God inside the Temple. The specific rules (which animals, etc.) just tracked what the surrounding cultures already venerated, so that redirecting them would actually bite. Nahmanides (Ramban) pushes back hard, basically saying this "concession" reading is offensive — it makes the divine table look polluted, as if the whole sacrificial system was only ever a trick to scrub false beliefs out of the heads of the wicked and fools. Nahmanides holds that sacrifices had real intrinsic value, especially a cosmic-theurgical one: the commands are a way of repairing a breach in the cosmos caused by the Fall, answering a "need" on God's side that only the commandments can meet.

Write an essay weighing these two readings of why the sacrificial law exists, then take a side — don't end on "both have merit." Argue for whichever you think is the stronger interpretation and say what the loser gets wrong. Structure it like this:

- "The concession reading" (~150 words): steelman Maimonides, what the theory actually claims and what it explains.
- "The intrinsic-value reading" (~150 words): steelman Nahmanides, what's at stake if sacrifices are only a weaning device.
- "Where they actually disagree" (~200 words): pin down the real fault line, which i think is less about sacrifice and more about God's nature — a transcendent God with no needs versus a God who has a need the commandments answer.
- "My verdict" (~150 words): pick one and justify it, and name what the other reading gets wrong.
