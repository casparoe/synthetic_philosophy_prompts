Imagine the standard setup: a predictor puts $1,000,000 in an opaque box if it predicts you'll one-box, and $1,000 in a transparent box that's always there. But now add a twist: before you decide whether to one-box or two-box, you can choose to look inside the opaque box (it's actually transparent, but you can choose whether to look). The catch is that the predictor makes its prediction before you decide whether to look, and it's 99% accurate. So if you look, you see the contents of the box, and then decide. If you don't look, you decide without seeing the contents.

I want you to construct this as a proper decision problem and work through it. Specifically: what does causal decision theory say you should do? What does evidential decision theory say? And what does a 'two-stage' analysis say, where you first decide whether to look, and then (conditional on your decision) decide whether to one-box or two-box? I'm particularly interested in which structural feature of the case makes the two-stage analysis give a different answer from the other two, and whether removing that feature (e.g., making the predictor perfectly accurate, or removing the option to look) collapses the case back into a standard Newomb problem.

Also, be honest about which feature of the case is doing the work. If the whole case hinges on the predictor being 99% accurate, or on the option to look being a genuine option, say so. And tell me how a defender of the theory that comes out badly would attack the case.
