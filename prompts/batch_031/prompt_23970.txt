I run the philosophy club at my school, and this term I've been given the session on Newcomb's problem. The complication is that it's one of those puzzles that has already leaked into the culture: a few members have seen it in a video and walk in convinced they know the answer, most of them landing on "just take both boxes, the money is already where it is" or, less often, "obviously one box, the predictor is essentially perfect," and a handful are strong enough in math and statistics that the moment I put numbers on the boxes they reach straight for expected value, which is exactly the move that's supposed to be the source of the trouble. I have one forty-five-minute slot, a whiteboard, and maybe fifteen people in the room with mixed backgrounds.

What I want is not the standard setup-and-payoff-table walkthrough the videos do; I want a plan for how to make the actual puzzle land, which is that two individually compelling ways of deciding — the "it's already decided, so take both" dominance reasoning, and the "one box has the better expected payoff" reasoning — really do point in different directions, and neither is obviously a mistake. So plan it for me: what has to be in place before the question even bites, and in particular how airtight the predictor's reliability has to be, because I suspect that the instant people doubt the predictor the whole thing quietly turns into a different and easier question; the one concrete version of the boxes I should build the entire session around, and what it must contain and, just as important, what it must leave out so that it doesn't get hijacked into a debate about free will, foreknowledge, or whether a perfect predictor is even coherent; the order the two ways of deciding have to be introduced in so that the clash feels like a genuine collision rather than one side being a dumb error; the misconceptions the group will bring in or form along the way — the dominance reflex, the "the predictor can't really be that good" dismissal, and the expected-value people who quietly arrive at what I consider the wrong box and can't say why — and exactly where in the sequence each one should be headed off; one question I can put to the group at the very end that will tell me whether they've actually felt the tension or just picked a box and stopped thinking; and, finally, what I should openly refuse to cover in this session and how I should say that I'm leaving it out.
