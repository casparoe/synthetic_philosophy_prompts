I've been going down a rabbit hole on Nagarjuna's tetralemma — the four alternatives for any claim about the ontological status of a thing: it is, it is not, it both is and is not, it neither is nor is not — and I keep running into the claim that Belnap's four-valued logic (true, false, both, neither) is basicaly the same shape, or that the catuṣkoṭi is some kind of proto-four-valued logic. I've seen this connection waved at on blogs for years but never a straight treatment. I'm a CS person, databases by day, so I know the SQL three-valued world (Kleene, NULL, "unknown" is not the same as false) and I can follow a lattice diagram.

What I want: restate the actual Madhyamaka position — the tetralemma plus what Nagarjuna is doing with it in the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, the emptiness / no-intrinsic-existence (svabhāva) thing and the reductio structure of the arguments — in the vocabulary of Belnap–Dunn four-valued logic and bilattices: the four values, the logical order vs. the information order, negation, entailment. Be as precise as you can about which parts of the philosophical machinery have a counterpart in the lattice and which don't.

Then give me the residue report, in three buckets:
1. What translates cleanly.
2. What only translates with distortion — where the philosophical claim is doing something the bilattice can't express. E.g. is the tetralemma about the ontological status of things rather than the truth-values of sentences? Is Nagarjuna using the fourth corner as a reductio rather than accepting it as a legitimate value? Does the split between the two orders matter to the argument, or is that a red herring for this?
3. What has no expression in the lattice vocabulary at all.

And here's where I need you to be commited, not a survey: is the tetralemma best read as (a) a claim about a four-valued logic the world actually runs on, (b) an exhaustive proof that no claim has an intrinsic truth-value, so all four corners collapse including both and neither, or (c) a purely dialectical tool that isn't a logic in the first place? Pick one, defend it, and say what that choice does to the translation: do the "both" and "neither" values survive or get eaten, and is Madhyamaka emptiness closer to a gluts-and-gaps theory of truth or to a move that dissolves the question? I don't want "some scholars say X, others say Y." Take a line and argue for it.
