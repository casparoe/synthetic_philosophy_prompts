I'm in a small reading group that has been working through Bernard Williams, and last week two members, Marta and Daniel, got genuinely stuck over the relationship between two of his famous moves: the integrity objection to utilitarianism, developed in his 1973 essay "A Critique of Utilitarianism," and the "one thought too many" argument he uses to show that a consideration can be a reason for an agent without the agent's needing a further, theory-level thought confirming that it is. They agreed on the texts and disagreed about what the argument is actually doing, and I couldn't tell which of them was reading Williams more carefully. Here is the exchange, roughly as it went:

(1) Marta: The integrity objection and the "one thought too many" argument are really one argument. The integrity point is that a utilitarian agent is required to evaluate her own projects from an external, theory-level perspective, which alienates her from them. What makes that alienation a genuine cost, rather than a mere preference the theory could weigh, is the "one thought too many" point: your reasons are yours precisely because they don't need to be validated by a further thought about whether they fit the scheme. Without that point, "integrity" is just a desire, and the utilitarian can count it like any other.

(2) Daniel: That conflates two separate problems. The integrity objection is a substantive objection about the *content* of utilitarianism: in the hard cases it tells you to act against your deepest projects, and that is a bad answer to the question of what to do. The "one thought too many" argument belongs to a different debate — the internalism/externalism debate about what makes a consideration a reason for you — and it is a point about the *structure* of reasons. You can reject utilitarianism over integrity without committing to anything about that debate.

(3) Marta: But the connection is exactly that external-evaluation move. Williams's example is a person who has made a promise: the claim is that he doesn't need a further thought — that he is the kind of person who keeps promises — in order for the promise to be a reason. That is precisely what makes integrity possible: your reasons are yours, and yours means they don't depend on being confirmed by an external standard. So the integrity objection depends on the internalist point. Drop it, and integrity becomes an unexplained preference that the utilitarian can happily weigh.

(4) Daniel: The utilitarian's reply to that is that she can weigh the desire to be loyal, and weighing it doesn't destroy loyalty. So the real threat to the objection is this: if the cost of integrity is just "your projects can be sacrificed," that's a welfare claim, and a sophisticated utilitarian can simply weigh the welfare cost of losing a project and sometimes let the project stand. On that reading, the objection collapses into a dispute about weights, not a genuine incompatibility. Only the "one thought too many" point gives integrity a structural, non-eliminable status — so Marta is right that the two points are connected, but she has it backwards: the internalist point is what saves integrity from being reduced to a weight.

(5) Marta: That's the utilitarian's best reply, and Williams saw it. But the "just count the welfare cost" move is precisely what destroys integrity, because a project only survives if its continuation doesn't depend on its utility score. The moment its survival is a matter of weighing, it has already ceased to be the project. So it is not a weight dispute.

(6) Daniel: Fine, but then the objection is doing the work of a claim about the nature of projects and identity — that some things in your life can't be sacrificed without ceasing to be what they are — and that is a claim you can make without any internalism about reasons. The "one thought too many" argument is a separate point about reasons that happens to be in the same neighborhood.

Please adjudicate this move by move: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and — most important to me — what, if anything, both of them are missing about whether the integrity objection genuinely depends on an internalist theory of reasons, or whether it can be stated in a way that leaves the internalism/externalism debate entirely out of play.
