I've come to hold that the Condorcet jury theorem is a substantive epistemic result rather than a vacuous restatement of its assumptions: given only that each voter is more likely than not to be correct and that votes are independent, the majority verdict is more likely than not to be correct, and the margin grows with group size, so the theorem gives genuine, if conditional, support for majority rule. What would count against this? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, including the circularity charge that the competence and independence assumptions already encode the conclusion, what actually happens when votes are correlated — in which direction does the result break, and can it invert so that the majority is less reliable than a random voter? — the problem that many real decisions have no determinate fact of the matter against which voter competence could even be measured, the fact that the theorem's limit result is silent about which voting threshold is optimal when the two kinds of error carry asymmetric costs, and the tension between the theorem's assumption of sincere voting and the incentives the theorem itself creates for voters who know the competence distribution. Which of these would a defender of the theorem concede as a real limitation, which would they treat as misaimed at a different theorem, and is the position as a whole so insulated that it risks nothing — and if so, is that a problem for a result of this kind?
