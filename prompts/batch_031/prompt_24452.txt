I've come to accept that the discursive dilemma in judgment aggregation is a genuine impossibility result rather than a mere artifact of how the problem is set up. In brief: a collective (a jury, a committee) must render a verdict on a conclusion that is a logical consequence of a set of premises, and each member judges each premise and the conclusion. Each member's judgment set is consistent. But no aggregation procedure can guarantee that the collective's judgment set is also consistent — that is, guarantee output consistency given input consistency — while satisfying (a) independence of irrelevant propositions (the collective's judgment on any given proposition depends only on the members' judgments on that proposition) and (b) non-dictatorship (no single member dictates the collective's judgment on every proposition). The standard illustration is the jury syllogism: the defendant acted with intent (P), the act caused the harm (Q), and the verdict is that the defendant is guilty (R), where the law makes guilt a logical consequence of intent plus causation, so that P and Q together entail R. Majority rule on the two premises yields guilty, while majority rule on the verdict directly yields not-guilty, so the collective judgment set {P, Q, ¬R} is inconsistent even though every individual's set is consistent.

Given that position, tell me what would count against it. I want the exposure mapped, not just the standard objections. In particular:

- Which of the two conditions, independence or non-dictatorship, is doing the real work, and is there a defensible weakening — of independence, or by restricting the logical structure of the agenda — that dissolves the impossibility while keeping the resulting procedure nontrivial?
- The structural or agenda-restriction escape: characterize precisely which deductive structures of the agenda are safe for majority rule and which are not, and say whether real decision problems (legal verdicts, policy committees) actually fall on the unsafe side or the safe side.
- Whether the impossibility is genuinely new or is just Arrow's theorem, or a variant of it, in disguise: does judgment aggregation yield anything that social-choice theory does not already give us, or is the "dilemma" a re-description of an old result?
- Whether the inconsistency is even a defect: could a collective rationally hold an inconsistent set of judgments — because the "collective" is not an agent but a set of individuals, or because the inconsistency is only latent and never actually acted on?
- How the three standard remedies (premise-based aggregation, conclusion-based aggregation, and implication-based aggregation) each trade off the conditions, and whether any of them survives the impossibility by quietly dropping a condition the defender thought was essential.

Finally: what would a committed defender of the "genuine impossibility" reading concede as counting against it, versus what they would dismiss? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and give an honest assessment of how exposed the reading really is — including whether it has been qualified into a form that no longer risks anything.
