I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on the political philosophy of competition, and I would like you to write a short essay that reconstructs, as carefully and charitably as you can, what Bernard Mandeville would have made of the modern AI arms race.

Begin by stating the claim you are testing. In the literature on Moloch, multipolar traps, and races to the bottom, the central idea is that when several powerful actors compete, each faces a standing incentive to sacrifice the collective welfare for a relative advantage; as a result, an outcome that everyone would prefer if it could be sustained — for example, a cooperative agreement to slow the race or to share in its gains — cannot be sustained, because no single actor can afford to be the one who holds back while the others do not.

Now set this against Mandeville's own thesis, the one put forward in his Fable of the Bees: that private vices and the selfish pursuit of advantage are, paradoxically, the very engines of public benefit, so that a society animated by self-interest prospers precisely because of the vices that a moralist would condemn.

Your task is to work through the tension between these two claims. Mandeville's thesis and the Moloch claim look almost like exact opposites, yet both are claims about what happens when many self-interested actors interact, and so they must diverge on some specific point. I want you to identify that point of divergence and then adjudicate it. In particular, address the following:

1. The charitable reading. Is the AI arms race simply another instance of Mandeville's thesis, in which the vice of each state or firm racing ahead produces a genuine public benefit — faster, cheaper, more capable systems — even if the path is turbulent? If so, what is the benefit, who captures it, and is it real or merely anticipated?

2. The counterexample reading. Or is the arms race a domain in which the fable of the bees breaks down — a setting in which the same selfish optimization that enriches the hive can destroy it, through existential risk, the collapse of deterrence, or a locked-in equilibrium that leaves everyone worse off than a cooperative baseline? If so, what is it about this domain — the stakes, the irreversibility, the difficulty of verification — that takes it outside the range where Mandeville's mechanism works?

3. The escape question. What, if anything, would Mandeville say about the prospects for escaping the trap — a single dominant power, an international treaty, or some other sovereign-like arrangement — given his deep suspicion of the benevolence and trustworthiness on which such arrangements depend?

Do not end on a non-committal note. Reach a verdict on the central question: does the modern multipolar AI race confirm, refute, or refine Mandeville's thesis, and why? Defend that verdict with the strongest argument available to you.

Write the essay in a polished, self-contained form of roughly 900 to 1,200 words, in a tone suitable for a graduate seminar handout that a student could read without further assistance.
