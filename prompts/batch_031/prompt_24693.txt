I hold the view that the moral status of combatants in a war does not depend on the justice of the war itself (jus ad bellum). On this view — often called the moral equality of combatants, or MEC — combatants on both sides of a war, even a war that is manifestly unjust on one side, are equally permitted to kill each other, and neither side's combatants are more blameless than the other's. I think this is the view that the traditional just war framework supports, and I think it best respects the autonomy of jus in bello from jus ad bellum.

What would count against this view? I want the arguments and considerations that should move me, sorted by how decisive they would be. In particular:

First, which specific cases or scenarios would, if they obtained, show that MEC is false — not just that it is incomplete or needs qualification? Are there cases where it seems clearly wrong that an unjust combatant is permitted to kill a just combatant, and if so, what makes them so?

Second, what does MEC predict about the permissibility of killing that its main rival — the revisionist view, on which the moral status of combatants tracks the justice of the war — does not predict? And where do MEC's predictions go wrong, or become counterintuitive?

Third, which of the standard objections to MEC would a committed defender of MEC concede as counting against the view, and which would they dismiss? I'm thinking of the problem of the unjust aggressor (the fact that unjust combatants are not, in the relevant sense, acting in self-defense); the asymmetry in the permissibility of killing (the fact that it seems more permissible for just combatants to kill unjust combatants than vice versa); and the distinction between the moral equality of soldiers and the moral equality of combatants (the fact that individual soldiers may be blameless even if the war they fight in is unjust). For each of these, I'd like to know whether a MEC defender would concede the point or push back, and on what grounds.

Fourth, is MEC well insulated by qualifications — for example, by restricting it to combatants in a narrow sense (excluding, say, soldiers who knowingly fight in a manifestly unjust war of aggression), or by adding caveats about proportionality, discrimination, and the laws of war? And if MEC has been qualified in this way, is that a problem for a view of this kind, or is it a sign of its maturity?

Personally, I would find most persuasive any argument that shows MEC leads to a conclusion I find unacceptable — for example, that a soldier who knowingly participates in a manifestly unjust war of aggression is nonetheless permitted to kill a soldier who is defending their country against that war.

I'd like an honest assessment of how exposed MEC actually is — not just a catalog of objections, but a map of where the view risks something and where it is safe.
