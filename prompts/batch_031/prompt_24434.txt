I've been trying to figure out what I actually believe about how much extra weight to give to helping the worst-off, by just checking my gut on cases, and I keep landing in a spot I can't explain.

The clear ones: if a dollar can either take a lot of suffering off someone who's really bad off or a little off someone who's just mildly uncomfortable, I give it to the really bad-off person, no contest. And if I'm picking between two aid programs that do the same total good, I care alot about who gets it — the program that lifts up people at the bottom should beat the one that just tops off people who're already doing fine. So I guess I'm some kind of "the worse off count for more" person.

But then I hit cases that bug me. Say we could shave a bit of happiness off a happy person and hand it to a miserable one so they end up closer together — I feel like that's just making someone worse off, and I don't think that's an improvement just because the gap got smaller. And I'd never want to make a well-off person miserable just to make the whole picture look more even; that feels plainly wrong.

So Im stuck. I want the worse off to count for more (first batch) but I don't want to make the well-off worse off to close the gap (second batch). Aren't those in tension — if the worse off really do matter more, shouldn't I be willing to trade the well-off's welfare against theirs? Is there one principle that captures all of this, or do I have to drop some of these verdicts, and if so which? And whatever principle you end up with, tell me what it says about a case I haven't listed: a benefit to a really bad-off person that's small in raw terms, versus a benefit to a moderately bad-off person that's much bigger in raw terms — does the "worse off" weighting always win, or is there a point where the size of the benefit just overwhelms it?
