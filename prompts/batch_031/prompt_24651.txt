I'm taking a course on the philosophy of statistics and I keep getting stuck on the p < 0.05 thing, and I think part of the problem is that nobody in the field can quite say out loud what the practice is actually for. So here's the setup as I understand it: in most of the empirical sciences you run a hypothesis test, you get a p-value, and if it's below 0.05 you get to call the effect real and the paper is publishable, and if it's above that it's not significant and the paper mostly just dies. And on top of that there's the file drawer problem, which is that the studies that didn't clear the bar rarely get published at all, so the published literature ends up being a stack of mostly significant results. I keep reading that this is a big part of the replication crisis, and fine, but what I actually want is for someone to tell me what the practice is quietly assuming.

Two things bug me in particular. First, a p-value is defined as the probability of getting data at least as extreme as what you observed, assuming there is no effect at all — it's a probability about the data, conditioned on the null. But then we flip it around and treat a small p-value as evidence that there IS an effect, i.e. as evidence about the hypothesis itself. Those are two different probabilities, and I don't think the first one licenses the second one. Second, we only publish the studies that cleared the bar, and then in a meta-analysis we pool all the published studies together and treat them as if they were a fair random sample of every study that was ever run. But the publication step already sorted the studies by the very thing we're trying to measure, so the random sample isn't random at all.

So here's my actual question: what view of probability and evidence does the whole setup actually presuppose? Does the 0.05 gate only make sense under a strictly frequentist reading, or does the way we actually use the number — as evidence that the effect is real — secretly require something more like a Bayesian reading, where you need a prior on how likely the null hypothesis is to be true before you look at the data? Is the practice internally coherent, or does it quietly mix two incompatible ways of thinking about what a number like 0.03 is supposed to mean? And if we swapped the bright-line gate for something else — say, reporting effect sizes and uncertainty, or a posterior probability — what would actually have to change in how papers get accepted and how meta-analyses are done?
