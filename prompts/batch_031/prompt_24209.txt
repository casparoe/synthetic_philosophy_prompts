I cover machine learning for a science and ideas publication, and I am preparing a long piece on how large language models are aligned to human preferences. I already know the engineering steps, so please do not summarize them; I want the philosophical work done. Here is the practice as I understand it. A lab first trains a base model on text. It then collects preference data by showing human raters a prompt together with two candidate responses and asking which is better. It then fits a reward model: a function that assigns a single real number to each prompt–response pair, trained so that the response the rater picked scores higher than the one they did not. Fitting that model is, in effect, an attempt to infer a preference function from observed choices. The lab then optimizes the language model to maximize the reward model's score, treating the learned number as a stand-in for human preference, and it presents the result as a model aligned with human values, judged on whether its responses are helpful, honest, and harmless. Meanwhile, the same pair of responses can be rated differently by different raters or by the same rater on different days; the raters work from a written rubric; the ratings are made on short chat exchanges; and the finished model is later pushed, at scale, to maximize the reward model in situations far outside the distribution of the rating data.

What view of preference does that practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can. Is it revealed preference, on which a preference just is what a person chooses, or the stronger claim that each response has a stable, latent degree of goodness relative to the prompt and that a choice is a noisy sample of it? Does collapsing everything into a single number commit the lab to value monism — the idea that all the things people care about are commensurable on one scale — or is that number compatible with a pluralist view of value? And is the target of alignment the rater's expressed preference, or some truer value that the rater only approximates? Then tell me which rival views of preference the practice quietly rules out, where the practice is philosophically underdetermined so that several theories would license it, and where its own parts seem to pull against one another — for instance, the tension between a foundation in revealed preference and a data-collection process that shapes the preference through rubrics, or between the rhetoric of values and a model that reduces them to one number. Finally, say what would have to change in the practice if we replaced its presupposed view of preference with a pluralist, reflectively revisable one.
