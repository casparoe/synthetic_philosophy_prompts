At my company we run a deployment pipeline for model-generated code changes, and the last gate before a patch is merged is a self-verification step: the same model family that produced the patch is asked to inspect it, run the test suite, reason about edge cases, and emit a yes-or-no "verified" verdict, and the pipeline treats that verdict as sufficient to merge. We do the same thing more broadly — the model is prompted to check its own answers and only ship the final one after it has confirmed it, and our dashboards treat a model's own statement that it has checked something as evidence that it has. I've been puzzling over this because it sits oddly next to the rest of our process: we insist on independent, externally generated tests, we don't trust the model's self-reported confidence when it is the very thing being evaluated, and yet the whole release decision leans on the system vouching for its own output. I've also read that there is a formal result, Löb's theorem, roughly to the effect that a consistent formal system strong enough to talk about its own proofs cannot endorse the principle that everything it proves is true without already having proved the proposition in question, and that this is sometimes called the Löbian obstacle for agents that want to trust their own or a successor's proofs. I don't have a philosophy background, so I'd like you to treat our pipeline as a concrete case and work out what view of knowledge or justification the practice actually presupposes.

Specifically, reconstruct the implicit epistemology of gating a release on a system's self-verification. Does the practice presuppose that a system's own proof of its output is genuine evidence of the output's correctness — that is, a kind of reflection principle, "if I have verified φ, then φ" — and if so, is that principle coherent given Löb's theorem, or does it only work because our verification is statistical and fallible rather than a deductive proof, which would mean the formal obstacle does not actually bite? Tell me which rival views of evidence the practice quietly rules out (for example, a view that verification only counts as evidence when it comes from an independent source, or a Bayesian view where the model's self-check is just one noisy signal among others), whether the different parts of our process — trusting the self-verdict as a hard gate while distrusting self-reported confidence elsewhere — presuppose consistent things, and what would have to change in the pipeline if we replaced the presupposed view with something else, such as the conditional-trust idea from the work on self-modifying agents, where an agent is allowed to trust a successor's proofs only if it can itself prove that the successor's proof procedure is sound.

Finally, please be careful to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and point out where the practice is philosophically underdetermined so that more than one theory of evidence would license it. And tell me which assumptions your reconstruction depends on and how it would change if you dropped them — in particular, which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict about whether the practice is epistemically coherent, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
