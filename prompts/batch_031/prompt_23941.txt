Ive been going down a rank-dependent utility rabbit hole for a few weeks and I think I've actually landed on a position I can defend, so before I start repeating it on the forums I want someone to try to knock it down. Specifically: I've come around to the view that the right fix for the Allais paradox is the dual theory / rank-dependent utility line -- keep the vNM utility for outcomes but swap the linear probability weighting for a concave cumulative-prospect weighting function that over-weights small probabilities and the sure thing -- and that this beats both (a) just accepting expected utility and eating the Allais violation as a human bug and (b) going full non-expected-utility. I'm especially sold on the fact that it preserves first-order stochastic dominance and that it gives you a clean story for the certainty effect instead of pretending people just make mistakes.

Here's where I want you to be adversarial, because I've already steelman'd the two objections I keep running into and I want to know if I've actually handled them or just talked myself out of hearing them. First, the starve-a-child problem: I keep telling myself it's just an artifact of using a weighting function that's too concave, and that if I dial the over-weighting down I keep the Allais fix without the reductio where you'd gamble a child's survival on a small chance of a huge payoff. But I'm not sure that's coherent, because the same non-linear weighting that does the work in explaining Allais is what over-weights the tiny probability in the child case, so I suspect there's no clean parameter region where you get the Allais fix without the reductio -- or maybe there is and I just don't know. Second, the preference-dependent utility cost: the utility or weight of an outcome ends up depending on which lottery it's embedded in, so utility isn't a fixed property of outcomes the way vNM wants it to be. I've been telling myself that's a feature not a bug, that it's exactly what lets you capture the common-consequence effect, but I want a straight answer on whether that's a defensible philosophical cost or whether it quietly destroys the idea that utility is measuring anything stable about the person.

So: what would actually count against the position as I've stated it, not against a strawman of "people don't maximize expected utility"?
