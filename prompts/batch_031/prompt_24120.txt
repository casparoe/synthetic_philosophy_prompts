I've been working for a while on whether the combination problem is a genuine, possibly fatal, objection to panpsychism, and I've landed on a position I'd like you to stress-test. Let me state it as precisely as I can, and then I want a map of its exposure rather than a summary of the standard objections.

The position: the combination problem, as it is usually posed, is overstated. The right framework is Russellian monism, or in its weaker form panprotopsychism. The ultimate physical entities possess intrinsic proto-phenomenal, or proto-experiential, properties. These are not themselves conscious experiences, but they are the raw intrinsic qualities that, when the entities are arranged in the right structural organization, give rise to unified macro-level consciousness. The principle of organizational invariance does the work: a system that is organizationally isomorphic to a conscious system is itself conscious, and the combination of micro-level proto-phenomenal properties into macro-level experience is no more mysterious than any other compositional process in physics—roughly the way the arrangement of molecular properties gives rise to bulk thermodynamic properties. On this picture the combination problem is either dissolved, if proto-phenomenal properties are the right kind of stuff to combine into experience, or reduced to a problem of knowledge: we don't yet know the exact combination mechanism, but that is an empirical gap, not a conceptual impossibility. On this telling the view is the most defensible physicalist, or at least neutral-monist, answer to the hard problem.

Now, what would count against it?

1. Which arguments, intuitions, or empirical results would count decisively against the position—forcing me to abandon it, not merely to revise it—and which would be merely awkward, requiring caveats or further explanation but not, on their own, sinking it? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. And for each, say whether defenders of Russellian monism or panprotopsychism would themselves concede that it counts against the view, or whether they would dismiss it.

2. I'm especially interested in the relocation objection: the claim that the combination problem is not dissolved but pushed one level down, so that we now need to explain how proto-phenomenal properties combine into phenomenal properties, and the same problem reappears at the micro-level. Is that objection fatal, or does the Russellian monist have a genuine reply—for instance, by appealing to the fact that proto-phenomenal properties are simpler or more fundamental than phenomenal properties, or by denying that the micro-level combination is a problem because the micro-level entities are not themselves conscious? And is the problem of the many—which overlapping micro-level entities are the ones that combine to form a particular macro-experience—a separate, independent objection, or just another instance of the relocation problem?

3. What does this position predict that its rivals do not? I want the empirical and conceptual differences that would let me tell the views apart, not verbal differences. The rivals I have in mind are illusionism (there are no micro-level experiences at all), the masking hypothesis (micro-level experiences are present but masked at the macro-level), and cosmopsychism (the top-down view that the universe as a whole is conscious and that micro-level experiences are parts of one universal consciousness).

4. Has the position been qualified into unfalsifiability? The claim that the combination mechanism is a matter for future empirical science risks making the view a metaphysical stipulation rather than a scientific hypothesis. Is there any empirical result that could, in principle, count against it? If the view is well insulated, is that a problem for a view that claims to be a physicalist solution to the hard problem, or is it acceptable given that it is, after all, a metaphysical view?

Finally, close with a note on your own confidence. Indicate how confident you are in the claims you make about the position itself—that is, in your characterization of what the Russellian monist or panprotopsychist actually holds—and single out the one point in your answer you are least sure of, whether that is a claim about the position, a claim about one of the rival views, or a judgment about whether a given defeater is fatal or merely awkward. I'd rather you flag your uncertainty than present a false certainty.
