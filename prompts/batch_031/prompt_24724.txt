I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm working on a piece about how philosophers deal with vague words like 'bald' and 'tall.' A philosophy-of-language professor I interviewed summarized the main debate for me like this: 'There are two leading ways to handle the fact that "bald" has no clear cutoff. The epistemicist says the cutoff is really there, it's just impossible for us to pin down, so "this man is bald" is either true or false, we just can't tell which. The supervaluationist says forget the cutoff, there's no single one — instead you line up every way of sharpening "bald" into a precise term, and the statement is true just in case it comes out true on all of them. So really the two are doing the same thing: both are saying the cutoff isn't something you can just point to, and the only live question between them is whether the cutoff is unknowable or nonexistant.' I'm not confident about that last sentence, and I dont want to print it as is. Is this a fair summary? Where does it get each view right, where does it quietly flatten a real disagreement, and does that 'only live question is unknowable vs. nonexistent' framing actually capture what's at stake between the two?
