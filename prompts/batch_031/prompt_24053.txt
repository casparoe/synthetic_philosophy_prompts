I'm a decision theorist who has been building a probabilistic forecasting system that has to incorporate noisy sensor readings, and in the process I keep running into a behavior in the standard recipe for updating your credences when the new information is itself uncertain — that is, when you don't come to be certain of any single proposition but only shift your degrees of belief across a set of mutually exclusive possibilities. The recipe, as I understand it, is to take a weighted average of the answers you would get if you had been certain of each possibility, using your new degrees of belief in those possibilities as the weights. It works fine in isolation, but a colleague and I noticed that the answer the recipe gives us depends on how finely we describe the set of possibilities we are uncertain about, even though we have not changed anything about what the sensor actually reported.

Here is a concrete case. Suppose a thermometer gives us a smeared reading of the temperature of a fluid — we are not sure whether it is 98, 99, or 100 degrees, and we assign ourselves degrees of belief of, say, 0.2, 0.5, and 0.3 across those three readings. If we run the recipe with this three-way description, we get one updated credence in some hypothesis H about the fluid. But if we coarsen the description and instead treat our evidence as "the reading is below 99" versus "the reading is 99 or above," and we assign the corresponding degrees of belief (0.2 and 0.8), and we run the very same recipe, we get a different updated credence in H. We have not observed anything different; we have only described the same smeared observation at a different grain. And the same thing happens if we refine the description further — we can keep obtaining different answers by choosing different ways to slice up the same set of possibilities, while the underlying observation stays fixed.

I keep needing a word for this specific behavior: the dependence of the updated credence on the resolution at which the uncertain evidence is described, holding fixed what was actually observed. I am aware of related issues in this area — for instance, the question of how to choose the weights in the first place, and the broader question of whether this recipe is the right way to update on uncertain evidence at all — but I do not think this particular behavior is the same as either of those, and I would not be satisfied with an answer that simply identifies it with one of them. So: is there an established term or a settled body of discussion for this specific phenomenon? If so, what is it, and how exactly does it differ from the nearest issues I have named — in particular, is it a genuine defect of the recipe, or merely an artifact of describing the evidence at the wrong grain? And if there is no settled name, I would be grateful for a proposed term, a precise definition, and a clear statement of where its edges are: which cases count as genuine instances of it, and which are just ordinary sensitivity to the prior or to the choice of hypothesis.
