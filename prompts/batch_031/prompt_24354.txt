I'd like you to compare two pictures of consciousness and draw the line between them precisely. The Cartesian theater is the picture Dennett rejects: a single place in the mind or brain where all the elements of experience are gathered and unified, and presented to a single "self" or homunculus that does the seeing and deciding. Two influential theories both want to get rid of that picture, but they do so in different ways, and I want the difference drawn sharply. Dennett's multiple drafts model rejects the single place itself: there is no one location where experience is assembled; instead there are several parallel streams of processing, and "consciousness" is a label we apply to whichever stream happens to be available for speech, action, or memory. Baars' Global Workspace Theory, by contrast, keeps a single place but drops the single audience: there is a global workspace, a bottleneck through which information must pass to be broadcast to the rest of the brain, and passage through it is what makes the information "conscious" — but no single homunculus is sitting in the audience receiving it; the information is simply made available to many systems at once.

My question is whether keeping the single place is a fatal concession. Does the global workspace, by requiring that all conscious content pass through one bottleneck, reintroduce the single-stage, single-location picture that the Cartesian-theater objection was supposed to eliminate? Or is a "broadcasting" bottleneck genuinely different from a "theater" with an audience of one, and if so, what is the precise difference — is it that no single agent is doing the receiving, that the workspace is a functional role rather than a physical location, or something else? Please argue which side of this is stronger, and say exactly at what point the two theories stop being variants of each other and become genuinely opposed.
