So I teach epistemology and the philosophy of probability, and I keep getting the same question from my students, a lot of them med students on the clinical rotation: a test comes back and the report says the finding makes disease A twice as likley as disease B, and they have no idea what to do with that number. They just throw Bayes at it and get it wrong, because most of the time A and B don't exhaust the differntial, and honestly I'm not sure the right update rule is what they think it is when the evidence is a ratio rather than a plain observation.

I'd like you to write me a decision procedure a clinician can actually run at the bedside in a few minutes when they get evidence phrased as a relative likelihood or a likelihood ratio -- "the scan makes A twice as likely as B," "this marker makes condition C k times more likely," and so on. Ground it in whatever the philosophy of probability actually says about updating on this kind of evidence (the whole Judy Benjamin / conditioning-versus-cross-entropy line of work, whatever of it is genuinely usable by someone who isn't a philosopher), and be honest about which steps are real math and which are judgment calls dressed up as steps.

The procedure should:
- lay out the questions to ask in order, and what counts as a sufficient answer to each
- tell the clinician how to tell when the options being compared actually partition the space (so a plain ratio does the work) versus when they don't (the harder case), and what to do in each
- say how to bring in the prior, and what to do when the clinician can't get a number for it
- mark clearly where the procedure has to stop and hand the call to the person, because the question is about values or about what the patient actually cares about rather than about the numbers
- be runnable in under ten minutes, with a small set of test findings they can apply
- produce something the clinician can defend to an attending on rounds and explain to a patient in plain language

Then run it on three findings: (1) a positive test where the disease and its absence are the only two options on the table; (2) a scan that makes one tumor type twice as likely as another, but there are at least three other plausible diagnoses in the differential; and (3) a case where the procedure will land on an answer the clinician will not like -- pick one where the math says to keep a scary diagnosis on the table even though it feels intuitively wrong, and say what the procedure can and can't do there.

Think very carefully before answering, and respond in the style of an expert who has actually worked through this rather than someone summarizing a textbook. Don't give me a reading list or citations -- I know the literature, I want the procedure.
