I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm preparing a long-form piece on whether Kierkegaard's critique of the press and the public has been exhausted by its own success or still has work to do in the age of social media. I want to focus on a tension I think is underexplored: Kierkegaard's insistence that the task of the age is individuality and that the public is a theoretical, leveling power that cannot have a task of its own, set against the fact that he himself waged a prolonged press war in the late 1840s and, in his final work on the press, signed with his own name rather than a pseudonym. The deeper question is whether his concept of inwardness — the idea that the individual's relation to the absolute is a matter of inward commitment, not outward appearance — can be reconciled with the fact that his critique of the public was itself a public act, performed in the press.

I'd like you to help me think through which open questions, implications, or lines of work in this area matter most, and why. I'm less interested in a survey of the secondary literature than in a defended agenda: which of the following questions is most worth working on, and why. For each, tell me what the answer depends on, how tractable it is for a philosopher rather than a historian, and what argument or evidence over the next few years would move it up or down the list.

The questions I'm considering:

- Is Kierkegaard's critique of the public a critique of the press as such, or a critique of a specific form of public life — the theoretical public, the leveling public, the circle? What does the answer depend on, and what would it change about how we apply his critique to contemporary media?
- How should we understand the self-reflexive problem: Kierkegaard uses the press to attack the press. Is this a contradiction, or is there a principled distinction between the individual who signs and the public that does not? What does the signing of his own name in his final work do to the argument, and does it change the status of his earlier pseudonymous attacks?
- Is Kierkegaard's concept of the task of the age a secular concept or a theological one? Does the critique of the public depend on the theological framework — the single individual before God — or can it be secularized without losing its force? What would a secularized version look like, and what would it lose?
- How does his critique of leveling relate to modern egalitarianism, and how does his concept of the circle relate to the echo chambers of social media? Is the circle a useful concept for understanding contemporary public life, or is it too specific to nineteenth-century Danish public life? What would it take for the circle to be a genuinely general concept rather than a historical curiosity?
- What is the relationship between his critique of the public and his self-understanding as a sign? Is the sign a role that can be played by anyone, or is it a vocation that is given? What does this imply for the possibility of a Kierkegaardian critique being practiced by a journalist, or by a philosopher who is himself a public figure? Is there a sense in which the critique is self-undermining, because the act of performing it publicly already constitutes the kind of public activity it criticizes?
