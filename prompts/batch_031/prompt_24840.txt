I teach a small reading group in philosophy of physics — about ten graduate students, one ninety-minute session. I want a structured in-class game that makes them feel the force of the Born-rule problem in the Everett (many-worlds) interpretation: if a measurement yields all outcomes, each in its own branch, then every outcome occurs with certainty, so what does it even mean to assign a chance to one of them, and why should that chance be the squared amplitude rather than a count of the branches?

I want the problem to arise from the mechanics of the game, not from my telling them about it. The rules should force each player to make a real bet or allocation decision *before* a "measurement" that — as the game is rigged — copies every player into every branch, so that afterward each copy of a player experiences a different outcome. The players should discover on their own that the obvious strategy, "count the branches and back the majority," is unstable, because the game should let them see, or at least try, that a low-weight outcome can be made to win by branch count simply by splitting it into many tiny sub-branches. Only after they have run into that should the debrief name the Born rule and explain why amplitude-squared weights are the stable, non-gameable weighting.

What I want them to come away seeing is not that Everettians cannot do quantum mechanics, but that the very notion of probability — what it is a probability of, and what it is for — comes under pressure once there is no single uncertain outcome.

Please give me: (1) the full rules and a concrete payoff scheme that uses no real money (points, tokens, or a shared pool divided by a rule); (2) the specific quantum states and branch structures to use, with amplitudes chosen so the branch-counting trap is real but not obvious on the first pass; (3) any roles — for instance a "splitting" player who may subdivide a branch, a bookmaker, a regret auditor; (4) a timeline for the ninety minutes; and (5) a debrief that maps particular moments in the play onto particular parts of the problem: the disorientation of betting when every outcome will in fact happen to you, the failure of branch-counting, and why that failure points toward amplitude weights. You may gesture toward the decision-theoretic line of argument (Deutsch and Wallace) as the serious attempt to make this rigorous, but the activity has to stand on its own without it, and the debrief should not collapse into a lecture.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. My suspicion is that the students will either refuse to take the copying seriously and play it as ordinary probability, or get lost in the arithmetic of the amplitudes and never reach the conceptual point; tell me how to handle each. And be frank about which feature of the Everettian probability problem the game simply cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.
