A colleague in my logic seminar has been arguing that we should be highly confident the axioms of ZFC are consistent, and I can't find the flaw. Here is the argument, as they presented it:

(1) If ZFC is inconsistent, then for some sentence σ, ZFC proves σ and ZFC proves ¬σ.
(2) ZFC is a classical theory, so by ex falso quodlibet, if it proves σ and ¬σ, then it proves every sentence τ.
(3) So if ZFC is inconsistent, ZFC proves both "2 + 2 = 4" and "2 + 2 = 5," and likewise both "PA is consistent" and "PA is inconsistent."
(4) Once a theory proves both a claim and its negation, it can no longer serve as a reason to believe either, so we lose every ZFC-based reason to believe any arithmetical fact we have established.
(5) But we plainly have strong reasons to believe basic arithmetical facts: they underwrite the mathematics that science and engineering actually use, and that mathematics works.
(6) Therefore ZFC is not inconsistent.

I cannot accept the conclusion as a genuine reason to believe ZFC is consistent, yet each premise looks fine in isolation, and I cannot say which step is doing the illicit work. I would most like to keep (5). Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked fine, what rejecting it costs, and what the strongest conclusion that actually does follow is.
