I'm in an intellectual property seminar, and the professor reaches for the same one-liner to close out arguments: "you can't own an idea." He used it last week to dismiss a student's claim that a particular way of matching buyers and sellers online should be patentable. He'd used it a few weeks earlier to tell another student that a novelist has no standing to stop a competitor from publishing a book built around the same plot. And he invoked it, more obliquely, when a student asked whether a company could keep a proprietary recipe from a competitor who had reverse-engineered it. I'm fairly sure the sentence is doing different work in those three cases, and I'm not sure it's doing any work at all in the third. Could you lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims that "you can't own an idea" could actually be asserting — from the most trivial reading to the most radical — and for each, say whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or actually false? And for each reading, say whether it would genuinely support the conclusion the professor was drawing in each of the three cases. I want the readings separated out and verdicts attached, not a single favored reading explained, and I won't be satisfied with a bare label for any of them; I want to know what each reading commits its author to and where it breaks.
