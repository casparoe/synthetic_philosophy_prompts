I've been developing an argument intended to show that pragmatic encroachment proves too much, and I'm no longer sure it's valid. To set the scene: pragmatic encroachment is the view that whether a subject knows that p can depend on the practical stakes the subject faces in acting on p. My argument is meant to show that this view is false because it implies an absurdity, namely that no one can know anything in high-stakes circumstances. Here it is.

(1) If pragmatic encroachment is correct, then whether a subject S knows that p can vary with the practical stakes S faces in acting on p, holding S's evidence and the truth of p fixed.
(2) The stakes S faces in acting on p consist in what S has to lose if p turns out to be false.
(3) For any proposition p that S believes, there are possible circumstances in which S has a great deal to lose if p is false.
(4) Therefore, for any proposition p that S believes, there are possible circumstances in which S does not know that p, no matter how strong S's evidence is.
(5) But there are propositions S believes that S knows in every circumstance: when S is in pain, S knows that she is in pain, and this seems to hold whatever the stakes.
(6) Therefore, pragmatic encroachment is false.

Two requests. First, identify which step to reject and explain why that step appeared to be fine on the surface. Second, if I have to choose, I'd rather give up (5) than (1); I want to understand what that costs, in particular whether a coherent version of encroachment can be saved by restricting it to non-incorrigible beliefs, and what that restriction gives up.
