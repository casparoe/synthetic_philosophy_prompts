I'm writing the eulogy for my old professor, who died last month. He was an evolutionary biologist, and he spent the last thirty years of his life pushing and developing the spandrels argument -- the idea, from that famous paper Gould and Lewontin wrote, that a lot of the features we see in organisms aren't really adaptations selected for the job they appear to do, but byproducts of other processes that later got co-opted. His favorite example was always the original one: the spandrels in the dome of St. Mark's in Venice, those triangular bits of wall that just exist because of how the arches and the dome fit together, which only later got painted with frescoes, so people started assuming they were 'meant' to hold paintings. He used to say the exact same thing is going on in biology all the time, and that we have this bad habit of looking at something that seems designed for a purpose and just assuming it is one.

The problem is that the room at the service is split. Half are his students and people who think he was basically right and that plain adaptationism is overblown; the other half are colleagues who respected him a lot but thought his skepticism went too far and that most features really are adaptations. I don't want the eulogy to take a side on that, because I'd be starting a fight at his funeral, and honestly I dont know how to say 'he was a great scientist' in a way that's true for both camps without it sounding like I'm dodging. And there's a weird tension I keep running into: the whole point of a eulogy is to say that someone's life had a purpose, that they mattered for a reason, that their work left a real mark -- but he spent his career arguing that you can't just look at something and assume it has the purpose it appears to have. So I'm a little stuck on how to eulogize a man whose life work was to deny that things have the purposes they seem to, without either contradicting him or making the speech sound like a lecture.

Can you write it for me? I want it to be something I can actually read out loud to that mixed room, so it has to land for the people who knew his science without losing the family and the people who just knew him as a person. And after the speech, please give me a short separate note -- not part of the eulogy, just for me -- where you sort out what's actually settled in this debate (like, whether spandrels and exaptation are real, established things) from what's genuinely still contested among people who work in the field, and flag the specific spots in the speech where I'm taking a side that his critics would push back on, so I can decide whether to soften them.
