I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a long-form piece about the ethics of a future in which we might be able to create or preserve unlimited numbers of minds — through uploading, through space colonization, through simulation — and a reader recently wrote to me asking how we're supposed to compare possible worlds that contain infinitely many people, and I realized I couldn't answer it cleanly. Almost all of the ethical theories I reached for were built for a world with a finite, countable number of people, and they quietly fall apart the moment the population is infinite, so I'd like you to write me a taxonomy of the positions on how we should compare such worlds. I want it organized not as a list of named theories but as a sequence of diagnostic questions that the positions genuinely come apart on, so that a reader can answer the questions without already knowing the labels and thereby locate themselves in the space.

Please build the taxonomy from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first lay out the questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then locate the actual positions in that space, and note any combination of answers that no one seems to occupy, with a brief diagnosis of whether that combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. I want the diagnostic questions to be concrete cases, not abstract principles, and I have a few in mind that you should use or improve on: a world in which infinitely many people each receive utility proportional to 1 over n, set against one in which they each receive utility proportional to 1 over n squared; a case where one option is better for exactly one person and equal for all the rest, alongside a variant where it is slightly worse for one person and very slightly better for everyone else; the question of whether adding an entire new infinite population of moderately happy people to a world that already contains infinitely many happy people makes the world better; and the question of whether two options that differ only for a single person out of infinitely many should count as different at all. You may add cases of your own, but each one should be a case on which the positions actually diverge, phrased so someone can answer it without knowing which label it leads to, and if two of the positions you end up with cannot really be told apart by any of your questions, say so explicitly rather than pretending the questions separate them.

For the leaves, I want the positions a careful reader would actually find themselves holding: a totalist who still insists on maximizing the sum and has some answer about what a divergent sum means, an average utilitarian who has to say how the average is even defined over an infinite population, a satisficer who cares only that welfare clears some threshold, a discounting view that tapers future utility so the series converges, a measure-based view in which differences that affect only a vanishingly small fraction of the population don't matter, an asymptotic or limit-based view that compares options by the behavior of their difference far out, a lexicographic view that ranks worlds by their worst-off member first, and a finitist or deflationary view that thinks the whole project is ill-posed and we should either avoid infinity or admit there is no fact of the matter. Where a position has a widely used label, use it; where it doesn't, describe it and mark it unnamed, and give each one a sentence on its most awkward commitment — the thing a sympathetic defender would find hardest to live with. I'm not asking for a bibliography, a reading list, or any citations; I want you to reason from first principles, and I'd rather you mark a position unnamed than guess at an attribution. And because this is going into a letter to the editor, please write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you'd write an email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables, just clear prose that walks the reader from one diagnostic question to the next and tells them, in words, which positions fall on which side of each question and where the unoccupied corners of the space are.
