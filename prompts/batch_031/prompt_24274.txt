QUESTION (25 pts):
Benacerraf's dilemma is often described as the most serious challenge to the foundations of mathematics. (a) State the two horns of the dilemma as precisely as you can, making explicit the epistemic principle that drives the first horn. (b) Mathematical structuralism is one proposed response. Evaluate whether structuralism genuinely escapes the dilemma or merely relocates one horn.

STUDENT ANSWER:
(a) Benacerraf's dilemma puts mathematical realists and anti-realists each in a bind. The driving principle is an epistemic one: roughly, if we genuinely know something, there has to be some non-mysterious explanation of how we could come to know it, and Benacerraf cashes this out by requiring that the known thing stand in some causal connection to the knower.

The first horn is the epistemic problem for realism. On Platonism, mathematical objects such as numbers and sets are abstract: they are mind-independent, non-spatiotemporal, and — crucially — causally inert. They don't enter into any causal relations. But if knowledge requires causal contact, and abstract objects can't causally interact with us, then we can't have any knowledge of them. Yet we plainly have knowledge of mathematical truths (e.g., that there are infinitely many primes). So realism can't explain our mathematical knowledge.

The second horn is the semantic problem for anti-realism. If we reject realism and say mathematical statements are not literally true (because there are no abstract objects to make them true), we run into trouble with the meaning of mathematical language. Mathematical sentences have the logical form of quantification over objects — 'there exists a prime greater than 7' is an existential claim. If there are no such objects, the standard truth-conditional semantics for the quantifiers breaks down, and we can't explain what it would be for a mathematical sentence to be true. On top of that, mathematical truths are necessary, and an anti-realist account struggles to explain that necessity. So anti-realism faces a semantic problem.

The dilemma: realism → epistemic problem; anti-realism → semantic problem.

(b) Structuralism says mathematics is not about particular abstract objects but about structures — the structure of the natural numbers, the field of the reals, and so on. A number isn't an object with intrinsic properties; it's just a position in a structure. This is supposed to help with the semantic problem: since mathematics is about structures rather than objects, we don't need a semantics for quantification over mind-independent objects, and the 'existential' sentences are really about the existence of structural positions, which we can understand without positing a realm of abstract objects.

As for the epistemic problem, structuralism helps because structures are 'instantiated' all over the place — physical systems, patterns in our experience, the structure of our own reasoning — so we do have causal contact with instances of mathematical structure, and that's how we come to know it. So structuralism escapes both horns: it dissolves the semantic problem by re-describing mathematics as about structures, and it solves the epistemic problem by grounding our knowledge in the causal contact we have with physical instantiations of structure.

One caveat: a critic might say that the structures themselves are still abstract, so the epistemic problem isn't fully gone. But I think that objection misfires, because what we know is the structure as instantiated in the physical world, not some separate abstract entity.
