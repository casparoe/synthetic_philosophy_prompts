I've been trying to figure out what I actually believe about when I should back down when someone I respect disagrees with me, just by going on my gut on a bunch of cases. Here's where I land, and I'd like to know if there's one principle (or a small family of them) that actually generates all of these, or whether I'm just a mess.

1. My roommate and I both worked out the same tax problem from the same return, and we get different numbers. I think I should basically split the difference — or at least move a fair chunk toward her number. If she's as likely to be right as I am, why do I get to trust my hunch more?

2. But if she's a CPA and I'm a lawyer, and we're looking at the same return, I don't split. I just go with her. Same documents, same "we both did the work," but her answer wins almost completely.

3. My brother is sharp in general, but he's an idiot about wine. If we disagree about which vintage is better, I don't let his disagreement move me at all, even though I'd trust him on a dozen other things. So it's not "how smart is this person," it's "how smart is this person about this."

4. We argued about whether a movie was good. She said it was garbage, I said it was great. I don't think I should split — "it's great and garbage" isn't a view I can hold. I keep my answer. (This one feels different from the tax one, but I'm not sure wether it should be.)

5. If I find out she actually read a study I haven't seen, then even if she's not an expert, I move a lot toward her. Her disagreement now comes with (probably) new evidence, so it's not the same as her just shaking her head.

6. Here's the one that really bugs me. If I know I'm just a better reasoner than her — I've trained more, I'm more careful — then why should her disagreement move me at all? If I'm more likely to be right, I should keep my view and treat her disagreement as what it is: someone less reliable objecting. But that feels too smug, because I can be overconfident, and the fact that a competent person disagrees with me should make me at least a little nervous. I cant get this one to sit right with #1.

7. And there are questions where I genuinely think the evidence could go either way — a borderline case, a call call. On those, I don't think I should "split." I think we can both be rational and disagree, and the right move is to recognize that we're both within the range the evidence allows, not to average.

So: is there a coherent principle behind all this, or a small set of candidates? If you can, state it precisely, and for each piece of it, tell me which of my cases is what forces it in. Then tell me what it predicts for cases I haven't listed — like, what if five peers all disagree with me instead of one, or what if my "peer" is someone I know just never changes their mind no matter what. And be honest about which of my judgments it can't save — if the price of a clean principle is that I have to give up one of these, I'd rather know which one and why.
