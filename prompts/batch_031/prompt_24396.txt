I keep running into this pattern at work and I can't find a word for it.

In git, when you fork a repo the fork is a complete copy, byte for byte. But we still talk about the "original" repo and the "fork." The "original" is just the one the community points to. No physical difference. And if the "original" gets deleted the fork just becomes the "original." Nobody does any metaphysics about it, it just shifts.

Same with reproducible builds. If I can byte-for-byte reproduce a binary, the "original" and my "reproduced" one are physically identical, same hash. But we still call one the "original" because of provenence — it was built from the canonical source at the canonical time. The "original" status is entirely a matter of history, not the object.

And the forgery case is the weirdest one. A forger makes a fake painting. The fake is the "original" of its lineage, first instance. If I copy the fake, my copy is a "copy of a fake" but the fake is still the "original" of its lineage. So "original" and "genuine" are orthogonal. A thing can be original and not genuine, or genuine and not original.

So the pattern I need a word for: "original" is a property of a lineage, not an object. "Original" and "genuine" are orthogonal. And when the "original" is destroyed the copy just becomes the "original" without any cermony.
