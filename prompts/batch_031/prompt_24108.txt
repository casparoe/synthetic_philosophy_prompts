I've spent the last couple of hours arguing with a friend about prioritarianism, and I've ended up defending it more firmly than I expected. My position is that the levelling-down objection is not a genuine threat to the view, because it only works on a reading on which the welfare of the worse-off person is treated as a bad thing in itself when the better-off person is made worse off; the correct reading, I now think, is one on which the moral weight of a benefit increases as the recipient's absolute welfare level decreases, so that there is no reason to prefer an outcome in which both people are worse off. Please take this position seriously and tell me what would count against it: which arguments, intuitions, or formal results would actually defeat it rather than merely being awkward for it, and which of these a committed prioritarianist would concede versus dismiss. I also want you to engage with the related worry that the weighting is a function of absolute rather than relative welfare levels -- the problem where making everyone slightly better off can change who counts as worse off and thereby reverse the ranking of outcomes -- and to say whether my defence risks anything at all or has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that is a problem for a view of this kind. Give me a committed answer: take a clear side on whether the defence survives, and say what would have to be true for it to be false.
