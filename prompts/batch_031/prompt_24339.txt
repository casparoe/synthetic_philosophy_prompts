You are an expert in welfare economics, social choice theory, and decision theory. Treat the following as a technical problem and think very carefully before answering: work only from the axioms I state and from standard mathematics, give no survey of the literature, and do not cite or name any papers, authors, or reading lists.

Setup. A social observer has a preference relation over lotteries on a finite set of social states, and each individual i = 1, …, n has a preference relation over the same lotteries. Both the social preference and each individual preference are assumed to satisfy the full von Neumann–Morgenstern expected-utility axioms, so each is represented by a utility function, unique up to a positive affine transformation, and the social utility is linear in the probability of each state. The observer is assumed to satisfy a Pareto condition: if every individual weakly prefers lottery A to lottery B and at least one individual strictly prefers A, then the social preference ranks A at least as good as B. The Harsanyi-style aggregation result to be examined is: under these conditions, the social utility of any lottery equals a weighted sum of the individuals' utilities, with fixed, state-independent, individual-specific weights.

Now answer the following, in order, with the explicitness of a careful proof.

1. State the result precisely and give the standard proof. In the proof, identify exactly which step requires the linearity of expected utility, and which step would fail if the individual and social preferences merely satisfied weak-order axioms together with the Pareto condition.

2. Interpersonal comparability. The conclusion is a sum of utilities belonging to different individuals. Identify the specific comparability assumption the proof uses — namely, that the social utility of a state is a function of each individual's utility of that state alone, and that this function is affine — and explain exactly what is being compared across persons. Then state precisely what this assumption does and does not require: in particular, whether it requires that individuals' utilities be measured on a common cardinal scale, and whether it requires comparability of utility differences.

3. Identifiability. The weights are fixed across states but are not identified separately from the utilities. Construct an explicit counterexample with two individuals and two states: two different pairs (weights, individual utility functions), where the underlying individual preference relations are identical but at least one individual's utility representation is rescaled by a factor other than 1, that yield the identical social utility function over every lottery. Then explain what this non-identifiability implies for any claim that the result "derives" a particular weighting scheme.

4. The equal-weights question. The result alone does not fix the weights; it only constrains their form. Explain the standard impartiality / veil-of-ignorance argument used to argue for equal weights: an observer who does not know which individual she will be, and who maximizes expected utility, assigns each individual equal probability and thereby chooses equal weights. Then give at least two substantive objections to this argument, one of which must concern the fact that the observer's own preferences are already assumed to satisfy expected utility, and the other of which must be a substantive egalitarian or prior-probability objection.

5. Residue report. For each of the following, state which parts of the result it is compatible with, which parts it contradicts, and which parts it leaves untouched: (a) a theory of value that is not a weighted sum of individual utilities, for example one that assigns direct weight to inequality or to the minimum welfare; (b) a theory in which the weights depend on the state or on an individual's position in the distribution; (c) a theory in which interpersonal utility is not cardinally comparable at all.

Formatting requirements. Use mathematical notation where it makes the argument clearer. Number your sections 1 through 5. In each section, state the answer first and then give the justification. Do not end with references or suggested further reading.
