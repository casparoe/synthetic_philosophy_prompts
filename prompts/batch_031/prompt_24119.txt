I've been stuck on this for a few weeks because I work with probability estimates constantly (risk models, ML confidence scores, uptime forecasts) and it only just hit me that I don't actually know what a probability statement is supposed to be. Is "there's a 30% chance this service fails this week" a fact about the world, or a fact about my uncertainty, or something else entirely? So I've been going through some concrete cases and just writing down my gut verdicts, and I was wondering if there's a principle behind them or if I'm just being inconsistent.

Here's what I've got. A die with a chip on one face that actually lands on 6 about 20% of the time: if someone says "the probability this die shows a 6 is 1/6," I think that's flat out false, it's 0.2 because the die isn't fair. A perfectly symmetric coin that I've flipped ten times and it came up heads all ten times: if someone says "the probability it lands heads is 0.5," I think that's still true, not like 0.9 or whatever, because the coin is genuinely symmetric and the ten heads is just a run of bad luck. A weather forecast saying "30% chance of rain in London tomorrow": I think that's a meaningful and rougly true statement, but I can't point to any long-run frequency it's supposed to be tracking, so I don't know what makes it true. "There's a 10% chance I die before 2050": I think that's meaningful too, some kind of personal/actuarial estimate, but again I'm not sure what grounds it. And a quantum thing, "a particle prepared spin-up along z has a 50% chance of being measured spin-up along x" — I think that's a real physical fact, not just a statement about my beliefs.

The pair that's bugging me is the first two. In the chipped die case I let the actual physical state override the textbook probability, but in the ten-heads coin case I let the textbook probability override the observed data, and I can't see why those aren't the same move. And honestly I'm not sure my rain and "I die before 2050" verdicts even fit with the die and coin ones, since those don't have any clean physical frequency or disposition I can point to. Is there one account of what probability is that gets all of these, or close? If not, which of my verdicts do I have to give up? And what would the resulting account say about a case I haven't listed, like the probability that two randomly chosen 128-bit hash outputs collide (wich I think is just 2^-128, a straight math fact, but I'm not sure that fits with anything else on the list)?
