I was a statistician for thirty years before I retired, and I've only recently started getting into philosophy, mostly because my grandkid keeps shoving these books at me. I keep runnning into this thing called the lottery paradox and the preface paradox, and I can't shake the feeling I already know the answer but can't quite put it into words. So I'm going to lay out what I think, case by case, and I want you to tell me if there's a principle behind all of it, or if I'm just being inconsistent.

Here's where I land:

1. The basic lottery. A fair 1000-ticket lottery, I have no info on which ticket wins. Im happy to say "I'm confident ticket #412 won't win." I'd put a dollar on it. No paradox there. But I obviously can't say "I'm confident no ticket will win." So I think the lesson is that belief just isn't closed under conjunction. Each individual belief is fine; the conjunction is where it breaks. I'm comfortable with that.

2. The preface. An author who's done careful research on 500 claims, each of which they're 99% sure of. They're also 99% sure at least one claim is wrong. I think both beliefs are fine at once. The author isn't irrational. They just can't believe the conjunction of all 500 claims. Same as the lottery. I'm comfortable with that too.

3. Now here's where I get stuck, and this is the one I keep going back to. I'm 95% sure it won't rain tomorrow. I'm 95% sure my car will start. I'm 95% sure the road will be clear. I'm 95% sure I'll get to work on time. I hold all four of these at once, and I act on them - I leave without an umbrella. I don't feel any paradox. I don't think I should be worried that "maybe one of these is wrong." I just drive to work. But in the lottery case, I'm supposed to be worried that "maybe one of these beliefs is wrong." What's the difference? In both cases I have a bunch of high-probability beliefs I'm holding at once. In the lottery case the conjunction is impossible - someone has to win. In my car case the conjunction is also pretty unlikely - 0.95 to the fourth is about 0.81, so there's an 19% chance something goes wrong. But I don't feel the same way about my car as I do about the lottery. Why? And is my reaction to the car case the "right" one, or am I just being sloppy?

4. One more, because I can't leave it alone. In my old job I ran into the multiple comparisons problem constantly. If you test 100 hypotheses at the 5% level you expect 5 false positives by chance. So I learned to adjust my threshold - Bonferroni, FDR, whatever - so the family-wise error rate is controlled. This is exactly the lottery paradox, right? Each individual test has a 95% chance of being a true negative, but the conjunction of all 100 being true negatives is very unlikely. And the "solution" I learned is to adjust your threshold based on the number of cases. So does that mean the "solution" to the lottery paradox is just to adjust your confidence threshold? And if so, does that apply to the preface paradox too? Should the author adjust their confidence from 99% per claim to something lower, to account for the 500 claims? I don't think the author actually does this. I think the author just "believes" each claim at 99% and also "believes" that at least one is wrong. So the "solution" doesn't seem to transfer. Or am I wrong about that?

So: is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? What does it say about the car case - am I being rational or sloppy? And which of my verdicts does it force me to drop, if any?
