In my foundations of mathematics seminar, the standing worry is that our basic axioms — ZFC, or even Peano arithmetic — might turn out to be inconsistent. The way we're usually told to think about why that matters is with an analogy: an inconsistent axiom system is a "trivial" one, because by the principle of explosion you can derive any sentence from a contradiction, so the system degenerates into a state where everything is provable and the distinction between theorem and non-theorem collapses. This picture is doing a lot of work: it's what makes the consistency question feel urgent, because on this view an inconsistent foundation doesn't just yield one false theorem, it makes the whole of mathematics vacuous. But a classmate pushes back, saying the trivial-system picture is misleading, because in practice we'd never notice an inconsistency — we'd keep using the large consistent chunk of math we actually care about, and the contradiction would be buried in some corner we never touch. Can you lay out what maps onto what in the trivial-system analogy, where the mapping breaks down, and whether the conclusion it's licensing — that we should treat the consistency of our foundational axioms as a genuine, live worry — survives once the disanalogies are on the table? I'm especially interested in whether the analogy quietly imports the assumption that an inconsistency would be detectable or localizable, and what a better analogy for a body of math that is inconsistent yet still mostly usable would be.
