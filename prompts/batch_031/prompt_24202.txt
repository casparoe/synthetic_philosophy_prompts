For a graduate seminar on the foundations of agent reasoning, I need a specification before we evaluate any particular account. Write the desiderata that an adequate theory of self-trust for reasoning agents must meet. The purpose is concrete: we want a standard by which to judge whether a given account genuinely solves the problem of how an agent may rationally place confidence in its own proofs — an agent that must act on the output of its own reasoning, not a logical curiosity — given the Löbian obstacle, namely that a sufficiently strong, consistent proof system cannot, from its own axioms, establish the general reliability of its own proofs.

For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that motivates it. At minimum the list must cover:

- The precise failure it must explain: why an agent whose reasoning is captured by a consistent, sufficiently strong system T cannot derive, from T's axioms alone, that T's proofs are generally reliable, and how this follows from Löb's theorem and Gödel's second incompleteness result rather than from a mere gap in T's current derivations.
- The distinctions it must respect: between consistency and soundness; between proving p and proving the conditional "if I prove p, then p"; between a self-verification that is genuinely circular and one that is not; and between trusting a specific proof and trusting the proof process in general.
- The cases it must get right: the agent that reasons modally about what its proofs would establish without asserting their truth; the self-referential program that carries, as data, a formal proof of its own correct behavior, so the verification can be checked without presupposing the very trust in question; the agent that trusts itself only up to a bound on proof length or with confidence strictly below one; and the agent that is itself uncertain about its own axioms.
- The results it must be consistent with: Löb's theorem, Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, and the standard provability logic GL, including the fact that T cannot internally prove its own consistency or its own general reflection principle.

For each item, say whether it is non-negotiable for any adequate theory or a preference of a particular camp — for instance, those who insist on full, unbounded, internally justified self-trust versus those who accept bounded or modal self-trust. Flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and explain why; if the list itself yields a no-go result, that is the part I most want to see. And tell me which single desideratum is the most eliminative of candidate theories.

Then, briefly — this is the lighter half — score the three main existing approaches against the list: the modal / provability-logic approach, the self-referential-program (quining) approach, and the bounded- or approximate-confidence approach. A paragraph or two each, or a compact table, is enough; do not pad this section.
