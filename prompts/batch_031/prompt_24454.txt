I'm preparing the final session of a graduate seminar on political theology, and we are closing on Dostoevsky's "Legend of the Grand Inquisitor" from The Brothers Karamazov. The discussion kept circling a point I never got the class to put into words: the Inquisitor's case is, on its own terms, nearly airtight. He offers the weak and the hungry a trade — a settled authority, a comforting mystery, and bread in place of the unbearable burden of freedom — and he insists that most people would gladly accept it, that he has done them a kindness by taking the weight of choice off their shoulders and merely "correcting" what Christ began. Yet Christ's entire reply is a single wordless gesture, a kiss, and it is precisely this non-answer that the Inquisitor finds more disturbing than any counter-argument he could have expected. I want to close the session with a villanelle that performs that asymmetry rather than merely describing it.

Please write a villanelle in which the two refrains are the two voices in the prison cell. Let one refrain be the Inquisitor's seductive offer — the promise to carry the burden of freedom for the flock, the trade of security for the right to choose — and let the other refrain be Christ's reply, the silence and the kiss, rendered as a line that is almost a non-statement, a gesture rather than an argument. The non-refrain lines should carry the Inquisitor's actual case so that a reader who knows the story can hear the specific moves: the three powers he names as the true foundation of the Church (miracle, mystery, and authority), his claim that humanity is too feeble to bear freedom, and his insistence that he has only corrected what Christ began. The form should do the philosophical work. The Inquisitor's refrain should keep returning, reasserting itself, confident and complete, while Christ's refrain keeps returning as something his case cannot absorb, answer, or refute. In the final couplet the two refrains should meet side by side for the first and last time — the kiss landing on the finished argument. A reader who knows the Legend should be able to tell which refrain is which and feel why the two voices never actually meet on the same terms; a reader who does not should still find it a good, legible poem. Keep it accurate enough that a specialist would recognize the actual structure of the confrontation rather than a generic "religion versus freedom" trope.
