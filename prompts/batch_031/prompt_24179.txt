I'm teaching a seminar in early modern political econ, about 25 grad students, 75 minutes, and I want a classroom game that makes the price-specie flow mechanism felt instead of explained. Rough idea I have: split the room into two trading nations, each with a fixed stock of gold (counters) and a basket of goods (paper slips or beads, wool for the north, wine for the south). Each round they trade goods across the border, and whichever nation ends the round with a trade deficit has to ship gold over to cover it. I want the rules rigged so gold keeps flowing to one side for a while, and then the students notice for themselves that the gold-rich nation's goods stop selling — the self-correction has to emerge from the play, not from me telling them it's coming. What I need from you: exact rules, a round-by-round script, a simple payoff/trade structure that doesn't need real money, and the specific point in the game where I should freeze everyone and ask "what just happened?" before debriefing. The debrief should map each phase of the game onto the steps of the argument, so they can see where the mercantilist hope that a surplus is permanently good for you actually breaks. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I'm guessing students will treat gold as a scoreboard and hoard it, wich would kill the mechanism — and how to fix it. And be honest about what the game can't show: can it convey the part where Hume thinks a money inflow is bad for the country's manufactures and wages, or is that something I have to just lecture about at the end?
