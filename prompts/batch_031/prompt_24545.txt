I run purchasing for a regional public hospital network — roughly 40 to 60 vendor decisions a year, each in the $50k to $500k range (lab equipment, clinical software, supply contracts, the usual stuff). I have about a day or two per decision, I'm not a domain expert in most of these (I lean on a couple of technical staff), and the final sign-off goes to the department head, but the recommendation is mine to write. My boss keeps telling me I gold-plate my evaluations: I'll spend a week comparing four vendors when the difference between #1 and #2 is margonal, and I should just pick something good enough and move on. I think that's the satisficing / bounded rationality idea from Simon, but I've never seen it turned into something I can actually run, and I don't trust a vague "just set a threshold" becuase that misses most of the point.

Can you turn satisficing and bounded rationality into a decision procedure my team and I can actually run on a vendor decision? I need the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and — this is the part I actually care about — how to set the aspiration level and how to adapt it over time (like, if I've been getting good options easily, do I raise my bar? if I've been struggling, do I lower it?). I need to know where the procedure stops with "pick this one and stop searching," and where it has to hand the decision to a human because the question is one of values rather than facts (e.g., "is a 10% price premium worth a 5-year warranty vs. a 3-year one" might be a value call).

Handle the hard cases explicitly: what do I do when no option meets the bar? what if the first option I see is way above the bar — do I stop or keep looking a bit? what if I've been searching for a while and nothing's good — do I lower the bar or keep going, and how do I know which? And be honest about where the procedure will misfire — I'd rather know it'll push me to pick a clearly-inferior option to save a day than have it silently do that.

Then run it on two or three real-ish vendor decisions. At least one should be a case where the procedure gives an answer I won't like (e.g., it tells me to pick the good-enough option even though a clearly better one is sitting right there and I'll probably regret it) — don't smooth that over, tell me what the procedure says and why, and say what it can't do.

Start with the procedure itself — no preface, no "great question," no restating what I asked.
