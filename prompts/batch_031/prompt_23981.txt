I have recently been appointed to my hospital's ethics committee, and a case has just landed on my desk that I need a written opinion on before Thursday's meeting. A physician on our oncology team has been directly and repeatedly asked by a patient's estranged adult son—who is not on the patient's authorized-contact list and holds no power of attorney—to confirm or deny whether the patient is enrolled in a particular clinical trial. The physician knows the answer (yes, she is enrolled), and the son is insistent, agitated, and hinting at legal action. The physician has asked us what she may say at the next encounter, which is within 48 hours.

She is weighing four responses: (1) deny it outright ("No, she is not in that trial"), which she knows to be false; (2) decline to answer or remain silent; (3) give a statement that is literally true but chosen to mislead and block the inquiry—for example, "I can't share anything about her care with anyone who isn't on her authorized-contact list"—asserting nothing she judges false; and (4) say "I don't know" or "I couldn't confirm that," even though she in fact knows and could confirm it.

I want the opinion to work within the Augustinian account of lying, on which what makes an utterance a lie is not its objective falsity but the speaker's assertion of what she herself judges to be false, coupled with the will to deceive—roughly, a "double heart," in which the speaker has one thing in mind and utters another, and is judged by the sense of her own mind rather than by the truth of the matter. You do not need to quote Augustine or provide any bibliography or citations; treat that framework as given and reason from it, and bring in whatever further moral reasoning you think the committee actually needs (confidentiality, candor, the moral status of a related but non-authorized inquirer, the harm of the deception, and so on).

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should (a) apply the Augustinian definition precisely to each of the four responses, being careful about the cases where the objective truth-value and the speaker's judgment come apart—note in particular what the framework makes of response (3), which deceives without asserting anything the speaker judges false, and of response (4), which asserts something the speaker judges false even though it sounds like a harmless non-answer; (b) confront, and not dodge, the strongest version of the objection that this whole distinction is a verbal trick, that what matters is the deception and its consequences, and that the committee should simply prohibit all deceptive speech and allow only silence or full candor; (c) recommend a concrete course of action—specifically, which of the four responses the physician is authorized to use at the next encounter and which she is not—with a time frame; and (d) give an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side of your recommendation.

Respond in the style of a senior hospital ethics consultant, and think very carefully before you settle on a recommendation; steelman the opposing position before you close it out. End your memo with a final line beginning "Verdict:" followed by a short phrase (no more than ten words) stating which responses you authorize and whether you classify the true-but-misleading response (3) as a lie on the Augustinian account.
