Question: Leibniz is usually credited with the identity of indiscernibles, and Max Black's example of two perfect spheres is the standard counterexample to it. In a short essay of roughly three hundred words: (a) state the identity of indiscernibles, and explain what kind of properties it must restrict itself to in order not to be trivial; (b) explain how Black's example is supposed to refute the principle; and (c) give one genuine difficulty the example faces, and say whether it succeeds in undermining the example.

Student's answer:

The identity of indiscernibles, associated with Leibniz, says that if two things share all their properties, then they are not two things at all but one — numerical identity follows from qualitative identity. One has to be careful about which properties count. If we allow properties that refer to the objects themselves, like "being identical to A," the principle becomes trivial, because every object has the property of being identical to itself and no other object does. So the principle is only interesting when restricted to qualitative properties — properties that could in principle be had by other objects and that do not involve reference to the objects in question.

Max Black's example is supposed to refute even this restricted principle. Imagine two perfect spheres, each one meter in diameter, with their centers exactly two meters apart, in an otherwise empty universe. The spheres are identical in every intrinsic respect: same shape, size, mass, color, and so on. No relational property distinguishes them either, because the only relation either sphere bears to anything is the distance relation to the other sphere, and since distance is symmetric, whatever is true of A's relation to B is true of B's relation to A. So the two spheres share every qualitative property, yet they are plainly two things, not one.

One might think the example only works if the spheres are the only objects in the universe, but that is not right: even if there were other objects, they would be at some distance from each sphere, and nothing about those distances could single out one sphere over the other, so the symmetry would remain.

A genuine difficulty for the example, though, is that the spheres are in fact distinguished by relational properties: A is two meters from B, and B is two meters from A, so they do not share all their relational properties after all, and the case fails to establish that two things can be qualitatively identical.
