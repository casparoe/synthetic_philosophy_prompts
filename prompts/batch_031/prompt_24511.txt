I am a novelist, and I am at the stage of drafting a novel in which a central character is a skilled persuader — someone whose trade is to move other people to action or belief. The novel turns on this character's own uncertainty about whether what they do is persuasion or something worse, and I need to draw, inside the fiction, a line between legitimate persuasion and manipulation that is conceptually defensible.

My working position, which I have adopted for the purposes of the novel, is this: I treat manipulation as influence such that the target, if they knew the method by which they were influenced, would not approve of having been influenced in that way; legitimate persuasion is influence the target would so approve of. I am not requiring that the influence be covert. A target may know the method and still be manipulated, provided that on reflection they would reject the method. I have chosen this account over the simpler picture on which manipulation is just covert influence, because it lets me write scenes in which the character is open about their methods and is still manipulating, which I think is more interesting and more true to the kind of person I am writing.

I would like you to map what would count against this position. Which observations, arguments, intuitions, or thought-experiments would count against it? What does it predict that rival accounts do not — in particular, the account that identifies manipulation with covert influence, the account that identifies it with bypassing the target's rational deliberation, the account that identifies it with exploiting a cognitive bias or vulnerability, and the account that identifies it with a violation of the target's autonomy? And does it risk anything at all, or has it been insulated by qualifications I have not noticed? I want you to distinguish evidence that would be decisive from evidence that would merely be awkward, and to say what a committed defender of my account would themselves concede as counting against it, as opposed to what only its critics would press. I am also willing to be told that the account is well insulated, and that this is or is not a problem for a novelist who needs a stable line to draw in a story.

One note on what I would personally find most persuasive. I would be most moved by a case in which the very same act of influence counts as manipulation in one context and as legitimate persuasion in another, where the difference is not merely in the target's knowledge of the method or their reflective endorsement of it, but in something about the relationship between the parties or the purpose of the influence. In my novel, the same character uses the same technique on different people, and I need the line to track something real rather than something that is just a matter of the target's state of mind.

Please do not give me a bibliography or a list of readings; I want the conceptual work done in your answer, not a pointer to where I could go and do it myself.
