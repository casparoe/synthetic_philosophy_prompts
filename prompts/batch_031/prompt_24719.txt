I cover science and ideas, and I'm preparing a piece on why groups so often reach a conclusion their own members would reject. Before I weigh any specific proposal, I want the standard of evaluation itself. Write out the full list of desiderata that a theory of collective judgment — a rule that converts a set of individually consistent judgments about a collection of propositions into a set of group judgments — must satisfy to count as adequate, with the case behind each requirement: why it must accommodate any combination of rational individual views rather than only the well-behaved ones (universal domain); why the group's verdict on a given proposition should depend only on how individuals voted on that proposition (independence); why it cannot simply track one member's judgment (non-dictatorship); why it must respect unanimity; and why the group's own judgments must be logically consistent (deductive closure). Mark which of these are non-negotiable and which are the preferences of a particular camp, and — the part I most want to see — identify the subset that is jointly unsatisfiable and explain how the discursive dilemma, in which a court voting separately on each legal element ends up with a majority on the elements that implies a verdict the majority directly rejected, is the concrete face of that tension. Then, briefly, how premise-based voting and conclusion-based voting each do against the list. Begin with the list itself, with no preface or commentary.
