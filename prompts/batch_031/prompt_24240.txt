I am formalizing a debate about ideal rational agents, and I have derived what I think is an unacceptable result. Let Kφ mean that an ideal agent knows φ, and let ◇φ mean that φ is metaphysically possible. I am using the usual modal reading in which Kφ is itself a proposition whose truth can vary across possible worlds. The agent is supposed to satisfy three conditions. First, logical omniscience: whenever φ entails ψ, Kφ → Kψ; in particular, K(φ∧ψ) → Kφ and K(φ∧ψ) → Kψ. Second, factivity: Kφ → φ. Third, in-principle knowability: for every proposition p, if p is true, then ◇Kp. I am not assuming any introspection axiom for K, positive or negative, and I do not need the agent to know that they are logically omniscient.

I reason as follows. Suppose p is true but unknown, so p ∧ ¬Kp is true. By in-principle knowability, ◇K(p ∧ ¬Kp). But if the agent knew p ∧ ¬Kp, then by logical omniscience they would know p and know ¬Kp. By factivity, knowing ¬Kp would make ¬Kp true, which contradicts the knowledge of p. Hence K(p ∧ ¬Kp) is impossible, so it cannot be possible that the agent knows p ∧ ¬Kp. That contradicts the earlier possibility. Therefore no true p can be unknown: for every p, p → Kp. This says that a logically omniscient agent who could in principle know every truth already knows every truth, which seems to leave no room for discovery, learning, or the distinction between truth and knowledge.

Which premise or step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would like to keep the agent’s logical omniscience and the idea that some truths are unknown, but I am open to restricting the knowability principle if the restriction is principled rather than ad hoc.
