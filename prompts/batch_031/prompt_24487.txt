I sit on a species-delimitation working group that periodically rules on whether two populations, or a pair of lineages, should be treated as a single species or as two. The conceptual chair is a philosopher of biology, and his rulings have been consistent enough that we can usually anticipate them. Here is the track record so far, each entry giving the evidence we were handed and the verdict we reached:

1. Two populations of a songbird, separated by a mountain range, with no gene flow between them. They differ in plumage and in courtship song, and a phylogenetic analysis recovers two well-supported monophyletic clades, each with unique diagnostic characters. Verdict: two species.

2. Two populations of a freshwater fish. One lives in tannin-stained, low-light water and is darkly pigmented; the other lives in clear, well-lit water and is pale. Where the two water bodies meet, the fish interbreed freely, the hybrids are viable and fertile, and genetic analysis shows a single continuous panmictic population with no recoverable clade structure. Verdict: one species.

3. Two lineages of a marine invertebrate. They are morphologically indistinguishable, but one occupies the shallow shelf and the other the deep sea, they never meet and do not interbreed, and a phylogenetic analysis recovers two well-supported monophyletic clades. Verdict: two species.

The case now before us: two populations of a lizard. They are morphologically distinct, differing in body size and in the pattern of dorsal scales, and they occupy different microhabitats, one on exposed rock and one in leaf litter. In a narrow contact zone they do interbreed, and the hybrids are viable and fertile. A phylogenetic analysis, however, recovers two well-supported monophyletic clades, each with several unique diagnostic characters, with only limited admixture in the contact zone.

What do you think we will rule, one species or two? I am not just after the verdict. I want you to (a) identify the species concept, or concepts, that the track record above is jointly consistent with, and note which familiar concepts that record rules out; (b) say what each of the surviving candidate concepts predicts for the lizard case; (c) give your best guess for our verdict, with the reasons for preferring it over the alternative; and (d) name one further case, different from the lizard, on which those surviving concepts would diverge, so that a ruling on it would tell us which concept we are in fact running.
