I'm presenting at a lab-wide AI safety seminar next month, defending the following thesis: the standard framing of AI safety in terms of goal-directed agents with mis-specified goals is misleading, and the more fundamental and useful axis is the distinction between approval-directed agents—agents whose behavior is shaped by their attempt to obtain approval from an evaluator, such as a human or a learned reward model—and goal-directed agents, which have a fixed objective they pursue independently of any ongoing evaluation. My claim is that most of the failure modes the literature treats as symptoms of goal-directedness—reward hacking, specification gaming, sycophancy—are better understood as artifacts of the approval-directed architecture, and that the research program is misdirected insofar as it treats goal-directedness as the problem when the operative problem is approval-directedness. I also want to argue that the distinction is not merely semantic: it implies different responses to the same failure (for example, that the right response to reward hacking is not to sharpen the stated goal but to change how approval is solicited, weighted, and withdrawn).

The audience is a group of roughly twenty AI safety researchers at a frontier lab. They are deeply familiar with the standard goal-directed framing and think of alignment as the task of making the goal-directed agent do what we want, robustly, at scale. They are practical, skeptical of philosophical reframings, and will initially treat my distinction as largely terminological. About a third of them work on reward modeling and RLHF; they will be most affected by the distinction but also most likely to object that the approval/goal split is already implicit in their work and that I am merely redescribing something they already do. There are one or two philosophers of AI in the room who will be sympathetic to the reframing but will press on whether "approval" is a genuine causal force in the system or just a placeholder for reward signals.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how likely and how damaging each is, phrased the way a reward-modeling researcher or a committed proponent of the goal-directed framing would phrase them. For each, give a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Include the one objection I am hoping nobody asks.
