I was at dinner with a friend who studies game theory, and we ended up arguing, quite heatedly, about how two people with different outside options ought to divide a fixed surplus. I kept reaching for the Nash product — maximize the product of our gains over the disagreement point — while he kept insisting the Kalai–Smorodinsky construction is the fair one: draw the straight line from the disagreement point to the ideal point, where each of us gets her individually maximal feasible payoff, and take where that line hits the Pareto frontier. I found his ideal point deeply unsatisfying, since it is a point neither of us can actually reach and so reads more like an anchor or a threat than a genuine benchmark, but I could not pin down, axiom by axiom, exactly where the two constructions come apart, and by the end of the night I was no longer sure I could even state which of the standard bargaining axioms each one actually satisfies or violates.

Could you build me a decision tree, or taxonomy, that sorts the standard two-person bargaining solutions by the axioms they satisfy, rather than by their names? I want the tree to be constructed from the logical space of the axioms — Pareto efficiency, symmetry, invariance to positive affine rescalings of the two players' utilities (scale invariance), and independence of irrelevant alternatives — with each branch node phrased as a question someone could answer without already knowing which solution it leads to, and each leaf a named solution (at minimum the Nash, the Kalai–Smorodinsky, the egalitarian or equal-gains, and the dictatorial solutions). For each leaf I would like a one-line geometric description of how the solution is actually computed, and beside each branch a sentence on which named solutions fall on which side. Where the classification can be derived from first principles, I would like you to actually work out, and not merely assert, which solution satisfies or fails each axiom, because that is precisely the part I got wrong in the argument and the part I want to be able to defend at the table.

Finally, please treat the corners of the logical space that no standard solution occupies as genuine deliverables rather than an afterthought: for each unoccupied combination of axioms, say in one sentence whether it is empty because the combination is incoherent, because no natural construction lands there, or because it is simply unexplored. And flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot actually separate using only the axioms placed on the branches, so I know exactly where the instrument is under-specified. I am using this to prepare a one-page handout for a graduate game theory seminar, so keep the whole thing tight enough to fit on a single page, and do not include any references, citations, or reading list — just the tree and the reasoning behind it.
