I read the following summary of Rawls's theory of justice in a popular book on political philosophy, and I'd like a careful audit of it:

"Behind the veil of ignorance, each party knows the general social and psychological facts about the kind of society they are designing, but not their own place in it — not their natural talents, their social class, or their conception of the good. Because they cannot attach reliable probabilities to where they will end up, Rawls argues that the parties should reason by the maximin rule: pick the set of principles whose worst possible outcome is the best. This leads them to the two principles of justice. The first guarantees each person an equal claim to a scheme of equal basic liberties. The second holds that social and economic inequalities are permissible only if they are attached to positions open to all under fair equality of opportunity and, at the same time, work to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged members of society. Rawls offers this as a refutation of utilitarianism: utilitarianism sums welfare across the population, so it can count a large aggregate gain against a small loss borne by a few, whereas Rawls's principles treat each person's claims as separate and never trade one person's loss off against another's gain. The upshot is that a just society must maximize the welfare of its worst-off members, which is why Rawls's view is commonly read as demanding extensive redistribution."

I have always understood Rawls's position as basically "arrange society so that the poorest people end up as well off as they possibly can," so this summary feels right to me on the whole. But a friend who has read more of the primary texts said the summary gets something important about how Rawls actually argues for the two principles wrong, and that the word "welfare" is doing quiet work that Rawls himself would object to. I do not know enough to tell where the summary is fair and where it is not. Could you go through it and separate (1) what is accurate, (2) what is a defensible simplification, and (3) what a careful defender of Rawls would push back on — paying particular attention to the role of the maximin rule and to whether Rawls's index is really "welfare"? And then tell me whether the summary's final conclusion — that Rawls's view demands extensive redistribution — still holds up once the corrections are made.
