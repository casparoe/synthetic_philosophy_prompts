I chair the Municipal Autonomous Systems Incident Review Panel, a standing body my city created to adjudicate harms caused by deployed autonomous systems when ordinary tort and regulatory channels have run out of clear answers. We are being asked to issue a written decision on a specific incident, and I need the memo you draft to be the one I circulate to the full panel before we vote.

The incident: A Level 4 autonomous delivery van, operating under a city-issued geofence permit in a mixed residential-commercial zone, struck and killed a pedestrian. The van was fully self-driving; there was no human operator in the vehicle and no remote driver. The city's investigation found the following. The pedestrian was crossing outside a marked crosswalk, against a "don't walk" signal, in a zone where the van was permitted to travel at up to 30 km/h. The van's perception stack misclassified the pedestrian — who was carrying a large, brightly colored object that partially occluded their body — as a static obstacle, and the vehicle braked but could not stop in time. The manufacturer had passed all type-approval and roadworthiness tests required for the geofence, had disclosed this class of misclassification (partially occluded pedestrians in low-contrast lighting) as a known limitation in its operational design domain documentation, and had no outstanding recall or safety notice for the affected software version. The logistics company that owned and operated the van had maintained the vehicle and its software exactly per the manufacturer's specifications and had not altered any safety parameters. And the specific lighting and occlusion configuration that produced this misclassification had never appeared in the manufacturer's test corpus, in the city's approval testing, or in any prior deployment incident; the panel's technical advisors judge it to be a genuinely novel edge case that no party could have foreseen or prevented given the information available to them at the relevant times.

The panel is split. One faction argues we should assign strict liability to the manufacturer: they designed and deployed the risk, they are the only party positioned to price, insure, and internalize it, and a rule that puts the loss on them will drive the safety improvements the public actually needs. The other faction argues this is a genuine responsibility gap — no human agent acted wrongly, no one could have foreseen this specific failure, and imposing liability on the manufacturer would either amount to an unjustified windfall tax that distorts the deployment of a net-beneficial technology, or would require us to stretch a theory of responsibility (fault, causation, or foreseeability) past the point where it tracks anything we can defend. They propose we instead treat the loss as a no-fault harm and recommend the city create a compensation fund, funded by a levy on all autonomous-system operators, to cover harms that no party is responsible for.

Our charter requires the panel to ground its allocation in at least one of three recognized bases — fault, causal contribution, or ability-to-bear (the capacity to price and spread the loss) — and the memo must say which base or bases it relies on and why the others fail or are insufficient. Please draft the panel's decision memo. It should (a) recommend one concrete course of action for this specific incident and for the class of cases like it, with a time frame for implementation; (b) state the principle of allocation the panel is adopting and why; and (c) engage the strongest version of the losing faction's position seriously, including the point that whichever rule we adopt here will set the terms for every future autonomous-systems harm in the city. The memo should be something the panel could actually vote on.
