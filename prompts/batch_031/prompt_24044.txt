Is there a name for this? In the debate over whether a consideration counts as a reason for a person only if that person's actual motives would connect them to it, I keep hitting a specific configuration I can't label. Take the claim that the only reason you have to drink water is that you happen to thirst, and that "water is healthy" is not a reason for you unless you already care about your health; or that the only reason you have to keep a promise is that you take yourself to be bound by it, not that "promises should be kept" is a reason for you unless you already value fidelity. That much I can follow. But when I ask why the motive-connection is itself normative — why the fact that you want something is a reason to want it, or to act on it — the explanation just stops. The view grounds the normativity of each particular reason in a non-normative psychological fact, yet the general rule that licenses the move ("if you have motive P, then F is a reason for you") is itself a normative claim, and the view's own resources cannot ground its normativity, since doing so would require pointing to a further motive, which would require a still further rule, and so on. I've seen people gesture at this with phrases like "the bootstrap problem" and "the theory is self-defeating," and I've seen it lumped into the general question of why reasons are normative at all, but neither seems to be the exact concept. Does this configuration — a theory of reasons whose explanatory grounds are non-normative and so cannot ground the normativity of the very rule they invoke — have an established name? If so, how exactly does it differ from the general normativity problem and from the is/ought gap, and which of those is the best fit? If not, what would you call it, and where are the edges — that is, which theories are guilty of it and which escape it? Think very carefully before answering, and respond in the style of an expert in metaethics and the philosophy of action.
