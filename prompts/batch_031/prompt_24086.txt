I am a retired experimental particle physicist, and I have only recently begun to read philosophy with any seriousness, so do bear with me if I bring a problem to you in a shape that a philosopher would find either trivial or muddled. This puzzle has been turning over in my head for some time, and it resurfaced last week when a former postdoc and I were arguing over coffee about whether the many-worlds, or Everettian, interpretation can really be said to contain probabilities at all.

The practice I would like you to examine is this. Working quantum physicists routinely invoke the Born rule to assign a probability to the outcome of a single, individual measurement, not to an ensemble of repeated trials. When I set up an apparatus and ask what the probability is that this one run yields outcome A, I am treating the Born weight as a genuine single-case probability, even though no ensemble and no long-run frequency attaches to that one run. At the same time, the same community treats the underlying quantum dynamics, unitary evolution, as perfectly deterministic and wholly free of probability, and a good many of them now claim to have derived the Born rule from that deterministic dynamics, whether by way of decoherence, by way of a decision-theoretic argument about how a rational agent ought to bet in a branching universe, or by way of some typicality measure over the branches of the universal wavefunction. So the practice seems to do three things at once: it uses the Born rule as though it were a fundamental single-case probability, it treats the dynamics as probability-free, and it insists that the probability rule is a theorem of the dynamics rather than an independent postulate.

What I would like you to do is reconstruct the implicit theory of probability that this practice presupposes. Is the single-case probability being treated here as a propensity, a real physical tendency in the system; as a subjective credence, a rational agent's degree of belief; as a typicality measure, the fraction of branches in which a typical observer sees outcome A; or as something else still? Which rival interpretations of probability does the practice quietly rule out, and with which does it remain compatible? And is the practice actually coherent, or does it rest on a tension it has never had to face? In particular, does the typicality move, the claim that almost all branches exhibit Born-rule frequencies, smuggle the Born measure back in through the door it was meant to close, and does the self-locating question, which copy of me after branching am I, even admit a well-defined answer under any of these interpretations? Finally, tell me what would have to change in the way physicists actually do their work if we replaced the presupposed view with a strict frequentist or a purely subjective-Bayesian theory of probability.
