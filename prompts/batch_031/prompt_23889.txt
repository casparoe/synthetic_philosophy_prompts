Draft: "Tyrannicide and the sedition problem in Aquinas"

Aquinas is usually presented as a cautious, even anti-tyrannicide, thinker, but I think that's a misreading that comes from conflating two different questions he's actually asking. In the Summa (II-II, q.42, a.2) he insists that sedition is always a mortal sin, because it attacks the unity of law and common good. That's a real and strong claim. But it's not a claim about killing a tyrant. Sedition, for him, is the *internal* tearing-apart of a people that already shares a just common good. A tyrannical regime, by his own definition elsewhere, is *not* a just common good at all, because it's ordered to the private good of the ruler. So "disturbing a tyrannical government" falls outside the very thing sedition is opposed to. He says this explicitly in the reply to an objection in that same article: since tyrannical government is not just, there is no sedition in disturbing it. So the sedition article and the tyrannicide material are answering different questions and don't actually collide.

The real question, then, is not *whether* a tyrant may be killed but *by whom*. In De Regno he grants that a tyrant "may be lawfully killed," but he attaches a condition that a lot of summaries gloss over: it has to be done under public authority, i.e. the people delegate this power to certain persons, or, if no public authority exists, by private authority. My claim in the weeds is that this is genuinely permissive, not a fig leaf. The "public authority" requirement is the ordinary, safe case, but the private-authority clause is a real carve-out: in a tyranny there is no functioning public authority to delegate from, so the private right doesn't just collapse into the public one, it's what's left. On this reading Aquinas is closer to a private right of tyrannicide than the standard "he only allows state-sanctioned removal" story.

What I'm unsure about is whether that private-authority clause is doing the heavy lifting I think it is, or whether it's just a residual that he'd prefer to starve. And I haven't fully worked out how to keep the "whole people as one tyrant" bit (where he says a democracy or oligarchy can be a tyranny) from collapsing into the same private-right logic — if the *multitude itself* can be the tyrant, then who is the "private" agent, and does the delegation condition even make sense? I'd push on that.
