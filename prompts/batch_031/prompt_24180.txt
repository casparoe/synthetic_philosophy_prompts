I've been stuck on the Pasadena lottery for a few days now and I honestly can't get a clear picture of what's going on. Quick refresher for context: you flip a fair coin until it first comes up heads, and if the first heads lands on the nth flip you win 2 to the (n-1) dollars if n is odd, or lose that same amount if n is even. So the payoffs go +1, -2, +4, -8, +16, -32, etc., each with probability 1/2^n. The expected value comes out to 1/2 - 1/2 + 1/2 - 1/2 + ..., which is just the Grandi series times a constant, so it's undefined. Not infinite like St. Petersburg, actually undefined in the sense that the positive and negative parts both blow up.

Here's where it gets weird for me. I've been playing with the idea that maybe we can rescue a 'value' for this lottery using some summability method. If I take the Cesaro mean of the partial sums I get 1/4, and I think Abel summation gives the same answer. 1/4 is a nice positive number, which would naively suggest the game is worth playing. But I'm really not convinced this is a meaningful decision-theoretic value. It feels like we're just doing a math trick on a series that doesn't converge, and then pretending the result has some cash-in-hand significance.

Can you argue that Cesaro or Abel summation does NOT give us a legitimate decision-theoretic value for the Pasadena lottery? I want the argument to be about why the 'averaging' approach fails to capture what actually matters when a real person is deciding whether to pay to play this game, not just a formal objection.
