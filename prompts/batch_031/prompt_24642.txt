I'm a PhD student in the philosophy of probability, and I'd like a short poem that does structural work on the absent-minded driver problem. The setup: a driver knows his route takes him past the same junction three times, that he must turn off at exactly the second one, and — because he is absent-minded — that he cannot tell which junction he is at when he arrives. I want the form to embody the indistinguishability: three stanzas that are nearly identical, so that the reader, like the driver, cannot tell which junction they are reading, with a single recurring line (a refrain) voicing the driver's question of which junction this is. The poem's turn — the shift from the driver's confusion to the decision-theoretic calculation — should land on the point that the symmetry answer (one chance in three) is not the same as the optimal strategy, which is to turn off with probability one-half at each junction and thereby succeed only one quarter of the time. Please keep it accurate enough that someone who knows the problem can recognize the specific tension — not merely the vague feeling of not knowing where you are — while still standing as a good poem on its own; roughly twenty to twenty-four lines, no fixed meter, but the three-stanza structure and the refrain are non-negotiable.
