I'm a novelist working on a short-story collection in which each piece dramatizes a particular philosophical argument rather than stating it, and I'd like you to help me with one. The argument I want to get at is Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against strong moral responsibility: roughly, that to be genuinely responsible for what you do, you'd have to be responsible for the character that moves you to do it; but to be responsible for that character, you'd have had to shape it in a way that presupposes an earlier, already-responsible character; and so the requirement regresses without end, which Strawson takes to show that no one can be responsible in this strong sense. I want a story that embodies this regress rather than explaining it — a character making a real, hard effort to take full ownership of a past act, who discovers that each layer of "I chose this about myself" turns out to rest on a layer they didn't choose, and that the very effort to be responsible keeps pushing the problem one step back.

Please write the story, keeping the philosophy implicit in the character's experience rather than as a lecture, and aim for roughly eight hundred to twelve hundred words. Give it a concrete setting and something genuinely at stake for the character. I was discussing this with a friend in a philosophy department, and what struck me was how the argument feels less like a logical trick and more like something you'd actually feel if you pressed on it hard enough — I'd like the story to land in that register, where the weight of the regress is something the character experiences rather than something the narrator explains.
