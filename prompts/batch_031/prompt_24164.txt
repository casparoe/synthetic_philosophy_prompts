I'd like a careful analysis of the 2008 financial crisis through the lens of Machiavelli's distinction between fortuna and virtù. As a longtime reader of the rationalist blogs, I'm familiar with the "skill versus luck" debate in finance — the view that much of what is celebrated as skill is really the product of luck, and the reply that a genuine skill component survives once you control for noise. I want the Machiavellian reading, which cuts differently from the statistical one. For Machiavelli, fortuna is not the same as randomness or a black swan; it is the part of human affairs that lies beyond our direct control but that nonetheless responds to how we act, and virtù is the set of dispositions — boldness, adaptability, the willingness to build dikes before the river rises — that let an agent shape and ride fortuna rather than be destroyed by it. So please do the mapping carefully. Who, in the crisis, plays the role of the prince — the financial system as a whole, the individual banks, the regulators, the rating agencies, or some combination? What counts as fortuna — the initial housing bubble, the collapse of the subprime market, the contagion, the sudden loss of confidence? What counts as virtù — the leveraged bets, the regulators' failure to adapt, the "too big to fail" dynamic as a kind of reputational power? One crux I want you to address specifically: is "too big to fail" a form of virtù (a kind of structural power the system exercises over its creditors and regulators) or a form of fortuna (an unintended consequence no agent intended or could have controlled)? The answer matters, because if it is virtù, the crisis was a failure of the system's virtù — an over-reliance on its own power — whereas if it is fortuna, the crisis was an act of fortune that no single agent could have averted. Then tell me, and this is the part I care about most, where the mapping breaks. Machiavelli's fortuna is a political concept, and the financial system is not a single agent with a will; the "skill versus luck" debate is about statistical regularities across many trials, whereas Machiavelli's fortuna is a one-off, path-dependent force. What does the episode show about the theory — is the crisis a confirmation of the virtù account (a failure of adaptability and boldness, not a pure act of fortune), an anomaly (the "prince" is a distributed system, not a single agent), or a case the theory never anticipated (a domain with no single agent to exercise virtù)? And is there a better lens — does the statistical "skill versus luck" framing, or a game-theoretic framing of the crisis as a coordination failure, explain the episode better than the Machiavellian one, or do they illuminate different parts of it?
