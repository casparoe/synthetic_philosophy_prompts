I write about science and big ideas for a magazine, and I'm working on a piece on the Leibniz question — why is there something rather than nothing? — and I keep getting stuck not on the big answer but on what 'nothing' is even allowed to be. Here's where I land on the candidates: the quantum vacuum is not nothing, its a physical state with structure and fluctuations, and an empty region of space is not nothing either since space is already something. A universe with only abstract objects and no concrete things feels like a cheat, like something is being counted on the back of an envelop. 'No possible worlds at all,' the absence of any state of affairs, I think is the only real nothing — but I also suspect I can't even think it. The empty set I'm least sure about: I think it's something, it's an object, but set theorists treat it as the least of all things and I feel like I'm missing something. Is there a principle that separates something from nothing that gets all of this, or close? The empty set verdict and the no-objects one seem to be in tension for me — if the empty set is an object that exists in every possible world, then 'no objects at all' is never a live candidate and the whole question either collapses or is ill-posed. Tell me what the principle says about cases I haven't listed, like a universe of just one massless point particle, and be honest about which of my verdicts it forces me to drop.
