I'm grading a take-home essay for an upper-level ethics course. The question was:

"State and evaluate the Epicurean argument that death is not bad for us. Explain the core idea of the argument, and explain why it is thought to be vulnerable to the 'time of harm' objection (the objection that for death to be bad for us, it must be bad for us at some time at which we exist, and that the Epicurean argument shows there is no such time)."

A student submitted the following answer:

"The Epicurean argument that death is not bad for us rests on the observation that when we exist, death is not yet present, and when death is present, we no longer exist. So there is no time at which we and death coexist, and therefore there is no time at which death could be bad for us. This is the no-subject route: death is no evil for us because there is no subject (no 'us') at the time of death for whom it could be an evil. This is really just a version of the hedonist route, since the point is that death involves no pain or negative experience, and so it cannot be an evil.

This argument is supported by the symmetry with the period before our birth. Just as the vast stretch of time before we were born was nothing to us — we did not miss it, it was not a deprivation for us — so the time after our death will be nothing to us. This symmetry shows that our fear of death is irrational.

The argument is vulnerable to the 'time of harm' objection: the Epicurean argument assumes that for something to be bad for us, it must be bad for us at a time at which we exist, and this assumption is questionable. Maybe death can be bad for us even if there's no time at which we experience the badness, for example by depriving us of future goods."
