I work in software — I build and operate customer-facing systems built on large language models — and I don't have a formal philosophy background, but I've read enough of Austin and Searle at a popular level to have formed a set of views about whether the systems I work with can perform speech acts. Before I write them up, I'd like a consistency check. Here is the package, stated as I actually hold it:

(1) I accept Searle's reconstruction over Austin's original constative/performative split. What matters is illocutionary force, and every serious utterance has some; "performative" is not a special class of sentence but a dimension that every utterance has, so there is no such thing as a purely descriptive utterance that merely states a truth.

(2) I accept that performatives depend on an institutional background. They work by constitutive rules of roughly the form "X counts as Y in context C," and these rules obtain only because of a practice sustained by agents who can recognize and take on normative statuses — what Searle calls collective intentionality.

(3) I think the sincerity condition and the essential condition are constitutive, not merely conventional. An utterance that fails the speaker's intention condition — a hollow or bald performative — is not a failed promise; it is simply not a promise at all.

(4) I am confident that the LLM systems I work with do not have beliefs, intentions, or anything that would count as a mental state in the relevant sense.

(5) Nevertheless, I think that when such a system outputs "I apologize for the outage" and the user accepts it, a genuine apology has been performed and has real normative effects: it changes what is owed between the parties (the user's standing to complain is reduced, the company's duty to make amends is partially discharged). And I do not think the user is merely engaged in a make-believe interaction with an imaginary person behind the machine.

I don't know where the problem is, if there is one, so I'd rather you find the actual load-bearing tension than confirm the set. Please audit whether these hang together: which pairs genuinely pull against each other, and which only look like they do; for each genuine tension, say which commitment has to give and what the cheapest revision would cost; and tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Be specific about the mechanism of each tension — which condition or premise is doing the work — and rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad. I don't want a survey of the literature or a menu of possible positions; I want a diagnosis of this specific set.
