Two grad students in my epistemology seminar got into it about disjunctivism and the argument from illusion. One (Sam) was defending the argument from illusion. She said: it's conceivable that I'm in a hallucination exactly like my current experience of seeing a red cube, so there has to be some mental state common to both the seeing case and the hallucination case -- otherwise, since I can't tell the difference from the inside, there'd be no fact of the matter about which case I'm in. The other (Riley) said that's exactly the mistake: in the good case I'm in a state of seeing the cube, which is a relation to the cube, and in the bad case I'm in a state of seeming to see a cube, which isn't. Those are different kinds of states, so there's no common factor, and the conceivability of the illusion doesn't force there to be one. Sam pushed back: but if the two cases are subjectively indistinguishable, how can there be a fact of the matter about which kind of state I'm in? Riley said the fact of the matter is external -- whether the cube is actually there -- and that I can't access it from the inside, but that doesn't mean it's not a fact. Sam then conceded the external fact but said that even so, my justifcation for believing I'm seeing the cube has to be the same in both cases, because I can't tell them apart, so the disjunctivist can only save the justification if they say my justification in the good case is the cube itself, which seems to beg the question. Riley said that's exactly the point: in the good case, my justification IS the cube, and that's not begging the question, it's just what justification is when you're actually in contact with the thing. I couldn't tell who was right. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right about each step, and what, if anything, both of them are missing?
