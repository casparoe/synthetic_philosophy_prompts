Could you explain, in Smith's technical usage, the difference between sympathy as a general mechanism and the particular sentiment of compassion or pity that the mechanism produces?

In particular, I'd like an account of the process by which Smith says sympathy works: the spectator first imagines the situation of the person whose conduct or passion he is observing, and then feels what he himself would feel if he were in that situation, so that the sentiment the spectator actually experiences is a weakened copy of the agent's sentiment rather than the agent's sentiment itself.

Given that mechanism, please explain why, on Smith's account, the pleasure of moral approbation is not the shared passion itself but a second-order pleasure — the pleasure of perceiving the concord between the spectator's projected sentiment and the sentiment he attributes to the agent. And please explain how the fact that the spectator's projected sentiment is always weaker than the agent's original passion bears on Smith's standard of propriety, including the familiar charge that this standard systematically favors calm and restrained responses over intense ones.

Finally, please explain how Smith tries to defuse the worry that this makes morality conventional — the appeal, on the one hand, to an impartial spectator who has corrected for his own partialities, and, on the other, to a natural moral order in the closing paragraphs of the work — and indicate where that move is genuinely contested among people who know the text.

Throughout, please clearly separate what is settled in the interpretation of Smith from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
