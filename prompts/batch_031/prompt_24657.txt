The practice of "splitting the difference" — resolving a dispute by taking the midpoint between the two parties' positions, or by each conceding half the distance to the other — is standard in negotiation, mediation, and settlement practice. What view of disagreement does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology: does it require that the dispute be a genuine peer disagreement, in which neither party has access to evidence the other lacks, or can it be justified when one party is simply better informed? Is it compatible with a "total evidence" approach, on which each party weighs all available evidence — including the mere fact of the other's disagreement — and may rationally hold their position fixed? And since the practice only makes sense when the disputed matter lies on a scale that can be bisected, what would have to change in it if the disagreement were over a categorical choice or a theory for which no midpoint exists?
