The question I posed was: "In Lewis's Convention (1969), a convention is a solution to a coordination problem. Explain why the coordination-problem structure is essential to the account, and what work the condition that no alternative to the regularity is strictly preferred does in the definition." Here's what one student wrote:

"First, the coordination-problem structure is essential because a convention only makes sense when there are multiple self-sustaining equilibria and the agents' interests are aligned in wanting to match up. In a pure conflict situation there's no shared interest to coordinate on, and in a situation where one action dominates, the payoff structure already picks a unique solution, so there's nothing for a convention to do. What makes a regularity a convention rather than just a habit is the common-expectation structure: each agent conforms because she expects most others to conform, and most agents expect most others to be so disposed. That mutual, self-reinforcing loop is what keeps the regularity stable even though no one has a unilateral reason to maintain it.

The condition that no alternative to the regularity is strictly preferred does the work of guaranteeing that the choice between the equilibria is genuinely underdetermined by the payoff structure. Since no equilibrium is strictly better than the others, the agents are indifferent across them, and the selection among equilibria is therefor purely arbitrary — there is no reason, including salience or prior history, that could favor one equilibrium over another. This is what allows the regularity to be a convention in the first place: if one equilibrium were strictly better, the agents would just play it and no convention would be needed.

Finally, Lewis provides an evolutionary account of how conventions actually come to exist in the first place. Because some equilbria are more stable than others, the less stable ones get selected against over time as populations of agents interact, so conventions emerge and spread through a process analogous to natural selection in biology. The static definition in the book is just a snapshot of the end state of this dynamic process."
