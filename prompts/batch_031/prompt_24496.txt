I'm building a reading list on populism and I keep hitting the same wall: everyone invokes "the will of the people" as if it's a thing you can point at, but I can't tell what a theory of it would actually have to deliver. So before I read more populism, can you write the desiderata? I want the full spec of what an adequate theory of the will of the people has to be able to do. Fix the purpose first -- I'll say its good enough for me to decide, case by case, when a populist's claim that "the people have spoken" is actually true rather than a rhetorical move. Give me the requirements list: the cases it has to get right (a law passed by a 51% majority on a procedural technicality, a referendum where most of the electorate stayed home, a "will" that flips between two referendums a year apart), the distinctions it can't collapse (will vs. interest, the peoples will vs. the sum of each person's will, a temporary mood vs. something that binds over time), and the results from social choice theory it has to sit with (Condorcet's cycle, Arrow's theorem) -- and for each requirement, the case or consideration that makes it a requirement and not just a preference of one camp. Then tell me which of the things people usually demand of these theories are genuine desiderata and which are just a camp's prejudice, flag any pair you suspect can't be jointly satisfied, and take a definite line: which single requirement knocks out the most popular theories, and is there a subset of the list that's jointly unsatisfiable -- if so, that's the result I most want to see, don't just lay out the options. A table is fine. Then, briefly, how Rousseau's general will, Schumpeter's view of democracy as competition for office, and a plain preference-aggregation theory do against the list -- keep it short, the criteria are the point.
