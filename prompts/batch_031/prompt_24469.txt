I'm a novelist, and in a story I'm writing a surgeon has had to detach a character's brain from her body, keep it alive in a tank in one city, and relay its signals by radio to the disembodied body, which is going about its work in another city. I keep returning to Dennett's "Where Am I?" — the brain-in-a-vat-with-a-radio-linked-body puzzle — because it appears to be exactly the situation my character is in, and I want the metaphysics settled before I let her narrate her own location. I'd like you to compare the two answers that puzzle most naturally invites. The first is the embodiment answer: you are wherever your body is, because the body is the thing that acts, suffers, and occupies a place in the world. The second is the brain answer: you are wherever your brain is, because the brain is the seat of memory, character, and the continuous stream of experience, and the body is merely a prosthetic it happens to be driving.

Set these two against each other as competing theories of personal location: what does each take the self to be, what does each treat as incidental, and what are the strongest cases for and against each given the two details that make the puzzle genuinely nasty — namely that when the radio link briefly drops the body collapses while the brain is perfectly fine, and that a backup duplicate brain is kept on ice in case the first one fails. Then, once the two are laid out, tell me how Dennett's own resolution bears on the contest: the idea that the self is not a thing sitting at a place but something like a center of narrative gravity, a useful fiction rather than a location. Does he pick a side, does he dissolve the question, or is his move a genuine third option, and would my character, if she were a careful reader of Dennett, be entitled to say she is in neither city?
