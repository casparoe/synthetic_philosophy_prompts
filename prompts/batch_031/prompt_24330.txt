A post I read on an AI-safety forum made an argument I keep coming back to. It sounds like exactly the kind of claim a careful person would make, but I can't tell where it quietly overreaches, and I don't trust my own complexity theory enough to pin it down. The post goes roughly like this:

"Complexity theory gives us a principled, almost physical basis for trust in AI systems. The core idea is the P-versus-NP distinction: some problems are easy to check but hard to solve. Now, figuring out what an AI is actually optimizing for — the read-out problem — is an inverse problem at least as hard as the hard problems in NP. So if P is not equal to NP, then no AI can efficiently reverse-engineer its own reward function, and no adversary can efficiently find the alignment-failure modes. This is a law-of-nature-style guarantee: just as thermodynamics rules out the perpetual-motion machine, complexity theory rules out a deceptive AI that hides its true goals, because hiding requires solving an NP-hard problem. The open question in AI safety is therefore really just the open question in complexity theory, and once we settle P versus NP we'll know whether we can trust AIs."

I'm inclined to think the broad gesture is right — that computational complexity is doing real work in the safety question — but I suspect the post is smuggling in a distinction it never draws. Can you go through the argument and tell me which parts a working complexity theorist would actually accept, which are overstatements of a modest thesis, and which rest on a conflation the post never notices? And most importantly: does the conclusion — that settling P versus NP settles whether we can trust AIs — survive once the technical claims are corrected, or was it doing all the work all along?
