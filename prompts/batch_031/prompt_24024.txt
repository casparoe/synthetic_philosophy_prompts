I'm in an epistemology seminar and we just did the regress problem -- the foundationalism vs coherentism vs infinitism thing, where you try to justify a belief and every justification needs its own justification, so you can only end up stopping at some foundation, going in a loop, or going on forever. And I was at a mock trial the other week (I'm a law student so this is basically my life) and it hit me that the way the legal system structures evidence looks like a huge practical answer to that exact problem, and I can't tell if I'm just pattern matching.

Here's what I mean. In court you can't take the stand and say 'my friend told me he saw it' -- that's hearsay and it's inadmissable. Each witness has to be there in person and testify to what they themselves perceived, and the side making the case has to build a chain where each link is independantly supported, and the whole thing terminates in first-hand perception or a document, not in an infinite 'well then someone told me.' Also the standard of proof changes with the case -- beyond a reasonable doubt for criminal, preponderance for civil -- but the structure of how evidence gets in stays the same. And here's the part that confuses me: the rules seem to demand that each piece of evidence stand on its own (which looks anti-coherentist), but the jury is still supposed to be convinced by the 'whole story,' the overall fit of everything, which sounds a lot more coherentist to me.

So what I want to know: what view of the structure of justification does all this presuppose? Does the legal system really commit itself to foundationalism -- justification as a finite chain that stops at basic first-hand evidence -- and thereby rule out infinitism (you can't have infinite chains in a trial) and coherentism (a belief's admissibility doesn't depend on how well it fits the whole picture)? Or is it actually compatible with more than one of these? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology, tell me what the practice strictly presupposes vs what it just happens to fit well with, point out where it's underdetermined, and then say what would actually have to change in courtroom practice if the legal system adopted a coherentist or infinitist view of justification instead.
