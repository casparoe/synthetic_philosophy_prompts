I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about the line between what's "said," what's "implicated," and what a speaker just means, going by my gut on cases. "Some of the students passed the exam," said by a professor who knows the full list: I take it to implicate "not all passed." Add "and in fact all of them did" and the "not all" just go away, fine. "You may bring a dog or a cat to the party": I don't take it to implicate "you may not bring both." "I don't know whether it's raining or not": also no "not both" implicature, but for what feels like a different reason. "He's a doctor," said in answer to "how's your health?": there's an implicature, but it's about relevance to the question, not about the word "doctor" itself. "Can you pass the salt?" at dinner: I don't think it implicates anything derived from "can"; I just take it to mean "please pass the salt." And "I passed the exam," said by someone who actually means "I think I passed": what's said is "I passed," but what they mean is "I think I passed," and I don't call that an implicature.

Is there one principle that gets all of these, or close, that says when a hearer is entitled to infer an implicature as opposed too just recovering what the speaker meant? What does it say about a case I haven't listed — "I'm not saying that he's guilty" — and which of my verdicts does it force me to drop?
