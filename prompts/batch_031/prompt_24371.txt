I'm taking a course on philosophy of language and I'm working through Kripke's argument for the necessary a posteriori from *Naming and Necessity*. I found a summary in a popular philosophy book that I think is mostly right, but I'm not sure I'm reading it carefully enough, and I keep coming back to one sentence that feels off. The summary goes like this:

"Kripke's argument for the necessary a posteriori is elegantly simple. First, he argues that proper names and natural kind terms like 'water' are rigid designators: they designate the same object in every possible world, and their reference is fixed by a causal-historical chain stretching back to an initial baptism, not by any descriptive content. Second, since the terms are rigid designators of the same object, the identity statement connecting them is necessarily true. Third, we come to know the reference of our names and natural kind terms only a posteriori, through empirical investigation — we discover that water is H2O by doing chemistry, not by reflection. Therefore, 'water = H2O' is a necessary a posteriori truth. The upshot is that the traditional rationalist identification of the a priori with the necessary is refuted: modality and epistemology come apart. What's especially powerful about the argument is that the necessity is a matter of the meanings of the words — it follows from the semantics of rigid designation alone — while the a posteriori element is just a contingent psychological fact about how we happen to learn things."

I suspect that last sentence is doing some work that Kripke himself wouldn't accept, but I can't pin down exactly where the summary goes wrong, and I don't want to build my essay on a misreading. Could you go through this clause by clause and tell me which parts are accurate, which are subtle misrepresentations, and which are outright wrong? In particular, I'd like to understand (a) what role descriptive content actually plays in Kripke's theory of reference, (b) whether the a posteriori character of "water = H2O" is a feature of the meaning theory or just a fact about our psychology, and (c) whether the summary's conclusion — that the rationalist identification of the a priori with the necessary is "refuted" — actually follows from the accurate parts, or whether it overstates the modest claim that the two distinctions are independent.
