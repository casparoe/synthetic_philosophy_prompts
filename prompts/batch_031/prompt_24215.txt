I've been turning over a puzzle I once read about, attributed to a philosopher named Kavka: an eccentric billionaire offers you a million dollars tomorrow morning if, at midnight tonight, you can show that you intend to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow afternoon. The toxin will make you very sick for a day but is otherwise harmless, and you never actually have to drink it — the money lands in your account hours before the afternoon arrives. The trouble is that once the money is secured you have no reason left to drink, so you know in advance that your midnight intention will be abandoned, which seems to make it impossible to form it genuinely in the first place. I'd like a sestina, in the first person, set in the afternoon after the money has arrived, with the vial still untouched on the table. Choose the six end-words so that their rotation does the philosophical work: one of them should be a word for the intention, and as the sestina cycles it back to the end of a line, stanza after stanza, it should feel like the intention being re-formed and re-abandoned, resurfacing even after it has been revoked — while the act of drinking itself never actually happens in the poem, the toxin only ever mentioned, never taken. It should be accurate enough that someone who knows the puzzle can tell which stanza is the midnight check and where the abandonment lands, and good enough that someone who doesn't just gets an aching poem about a promise you always knew you'd break. When you're done, tell me which assumptions the piece depends on — whether a genuine intention requires expecting to carry it out, whether the timing of the payment matters, how much the agent knows about their own future self — and how the poem would change if any of those were dropped.
