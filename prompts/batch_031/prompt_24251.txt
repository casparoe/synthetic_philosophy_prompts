I've been going back and forth on computationalism for a while now and I want to stress-test it rather than just collect more reasons to feel good about it. My working position, roughly: the brain is a computer, or at least the interesting stuff that happens in the brain — the stuff that makes it a mind and not just a meaty lump — is computation, and that's why you can in principle build minds out of silicon. I've read Searle's Chinese Room and I don't think it does what people think it does (to me it's either a bad argument or an argument about something else entirley), so please don't lead with that one. I want the deeper stuff, the kind of thing that would actually move me.

Here's the worry I keep coming back to. If "the brain computes" just means "there's some way of describing the physical goings-on such that they line up with the steps of some algorithm," then it looks like a rock, or a bucket of water, or the whole solar system, also computes something, and "the brain is a computer" stops saying anything determinate. I've seen the version of this where you're told that any physical system can be read as a universal computer if you're willing to pick the right interpretation, which would make the claim trivially true of basically everything and therefore not really a theory at all. So: what would actually count against computationalism, setting the room-argument stuff aside? Be honest about which objections are decisive, which are just awkward, and which a defender would themselves concede. And the big one — has computationalism quietly been qualified into unfalsifiability, where "the brain computes SOME computation" is true of literally any physical system so the view risks nothing? If so, is that a live problem, and what would it take for "the brain computes" to be a non-trivial, testable claim rather than a tautology? I'm in a philosophy of mind-ish seminar and the computationalists keep waving at "implementation" like it's obvious, and I want to know where the view is actually exposed, not just teh parts that sound good in a blog post.
