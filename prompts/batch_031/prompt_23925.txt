I'm in a philosophy of computation seminar, and the discussion keeps returning to the same exchange. One classmate holds that whether the Church–Turing thesis could be wrong is a live, even empirical, question: if some physical device could really compute a function no Turing machine can (a hypercomputer, a continuous analog machine, some exotic spacetime setup), that would be a genuine counterexample. Our instructor answers this every time with the same one-liner — 'the Church–Turing thesis is a definition, not a theorem' — and treats it as closing the matter, most recently to dismiss my question of whether the physical version of the thesis is testable at all. I suspect there is something real in both positions, but I can't say what the one-liner is actually doing, so I'd like it taken apart. Lay out the distinct precise claims 'the Church–Turing thesis is a definition' could be standing for, as a menu I can bring to the next session. I have at least these candidates in mind, but I'd rather you develop them properly and add any I'm missing: the stipulative reading, on which we simply stipulate that 'computable' means 'Turing-computable' and the thesis becomes a tautology; the reading on which it is a theorem, specifically the provable equivalence of the standard formalizations (Turing machines, the partial-recursive functions, the λ-definable functions); the reading on which it is a substantive a priori claim about our pre-theoretic notion of an effective procedure; and the reading on which it is an empirical generalization, either about what a finite human procedure can carry out or about what any physical system can realize. For each, say whether it is a platitude, something substantive and defensible, or actually false.

Then, the part I most want: which of these readings would actually support the instructor's dismissal of the testability question, and whether any of them does without quietly moving the goalposts. Finally, flag where the mathematical version of the thesis (a claim about which functions are computable) gets conflated with the physical version (a claim about which machines can exist), because I think that conflation is doing a lot of the arguing in the room, and I'd like to be able to point at it when it happens.
