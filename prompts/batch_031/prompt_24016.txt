I'm preparing a graduate seminar in the philosophy of statistics, and I've been stuck for some time on how to state a puzzle about the institution of significance testing that I think is real but that I can't quite get to the bottom of. The practice is familiar enough: a researcher runs a randomized experiment or a well-controlled study, computes a p-value, and if it falls below 0.05 the finding is reported as "statistically significant," the effect is treated as real, and a paper follows; if it doesn't, the finding is reported as "not significant" and the effect is usually treated as if it weren't there, even though the procedure is calibrated only to control the long-run rate of false positives, not of false negatives. And there is a further asymmetry in the institution itself: journals and grant reviewers systematically prefer published significant results to published null results, so the procedure's output is consumed as if it were a verdict on whether an effect exists, when on the standard account the p-value is merely the probability, supposing the null hypothesis is true, of seeing data at least as extreme as what was in fact observed — a probability about the behavior of the procedure, not about the truth of the hypothesis.

What I'd like from you is a careful reconstruction of what this practice presupposes about the nature of probability. On the frequentist reading, probability is a long-run relative frequency of a repeatable procedure, and that reading seems to be what the significance level is doing all its work for; but the way the result is actually consumed — "the drug works," "the effect is real" — is a statement about the hypothesis, which is what a subjectivist or Bayesian degree of belief would be. So: which of the standard interpretations — frequentism, degree-of-belief subjectivism, propensity — can each be made to license the practice as it actually is, and at exactly which step does each one break down? I'm especially interested in the norm of preregistration, which requires the hypothesis and the analysis plan to be fixed before the data are collected. That norm looks like it only makes sense if probability is a property of the procedure and not of the hypothesis, but it also looks like it is being motivated by a desire to make post-hoc statements about the hypothesis legitimate, which is a different thing altogether. Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, say where the practice is underdetermined between the interpretations, and explain what would have to change — in how results are reported, in what counts as evidence of an effect, in the design of the experiment — if the institution were rebuilt on a straightforwardly Bayesian view of probability rather than the view it appears to presuppose.
