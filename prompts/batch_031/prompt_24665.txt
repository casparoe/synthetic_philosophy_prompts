I just spent the better part of an evening in a long, inconclusive argument with a friend over whether van Fraassen's Reflection principle is an independent diachronic norm of rationality or whether it is fully entailed by the law of total probability together with a deferral principle, and I am now presenting my side of that argument to a workshop on diachronic rationality next Thursday. My thesis is that Reflection is not a free-standing principle at all: it is a consequence of the law of total probability plus the requirement that an agent treat her own future probability functions as expert functions she ought to defer to, and that the well-known tension between Reflection and Conditionalization is therefore a pseudo-conflict produced by a too-quick reading of both principles.

The audience is a mixed group of roughly fifteen: a core of Bayesian epistemologists who are sympathetic to time-slice rationality and who suspect that any diachronic norm is either trivial or incoherent, two decision theorists who work on the relation between belief and choice and who are wary of "ought to defer" language, and a handful of probabilists who treat the law of total probability as a theorem rather than a rationality constraint. Please draft my Q&A prep sheet: the six or seven objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how much damage each would do, each phrased the way the relevant subgroup would phrase it, with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. I want particular attention to the objection that, if Reflection is just the law of total probability in disguise, it has no normative force at all, and to the objection that my deferral principle quietly does all the work Reflection was supposed to do, so I have merely relocated the controversy rather than dissolved it. Tell me what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and include the one question I am hoping nobody asks but which, if it comes, would actually sink the talk.
