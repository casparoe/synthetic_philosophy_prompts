So at a philosophy seminar last week two grad students got into it over whether Gödel's incompleteness theorems put a hard ceiling on the social sciences. One of them said: "Gödel proved that any consistent formal system rich enough to do arithmatic is incomplete — there's a true sentence it can't prove. Our best models of the economy are exactly that kind of system, they have to count things and do arithmetic, so no complete economic theory is possible. Gödel shows social science can never be finished." The other one pushed back: "You're smuggling in a lot. The theorem is about provability from axioms, not about truth or about whether a model actually works. And the strength condition is a condition on formal systems, not on theories of the world. Incompleteness just means there's a true sentence the system can't prove — you can add it as an axiom and move on. So it doesn't show any economic theory is useless or unfinishable." The first one replied: "But adding the Gödel sentence as an axiom just gives you a new, bigger system with a new Gödel sentence, so you never actually finish. And any model of a complex system has to encode arithmetic in some way to describe quantities, so the strength condition is automatically met. So the conclusion stands: no complete theory." The second said: "That only works if you treat an economic theory as a single formal axiom system, which it isn't. A theory of the economy is a bunch of claims about a domain, and the Gödel sentence for it would be an arithmetical statement about the formalization, not an economic fact. Plus, complete in Gödel's sense means proving every true sentence in the language, which is way stronger than what we ever want from a theory — we want good predictions, not a proof of every true sentence about money. So the whole thing is just a category error dressed up as deep insight." Can you go through their exchange and say who's right at each step — not just who wins overall?
