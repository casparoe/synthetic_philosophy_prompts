I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I am preparing a piece on why small organizations keep choosing voting rules that look fair but are manipulable. At a board meeting I recently covered, the chair proposed plurality, the treasurer proposed Borda scoring, and an editor wanted approval voting; everyone invoked fairness, but nobody said what fairness was supposed to deliver. I want to get past the usual shorthand that the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem settles everything by asking what a voting rule would actually have to do to be adequate in a concrete setting, and I do not need a survey of the literature, only a design judgment.

Imagine a five-person editorial board that must select exactly one of three standing project proposals in a given cycle. Each member may hold any complete ranking of the three proposals, the rule must be deterministic rather than a lottery, and the board wants a rule that can be explained to contributors in a single short paragraph. Write the desiderata such a rule should meet: which outcomes it must be able to select, which preference patterns it must handle, which features of the decision it must respect, and which virtues are genuine requirements rather than the aesthetic preferences of one camp. For each requirement, give the case behind it, and mark which are non-negotiable for a rule that is to count as a serious selection mechanism and which are negotiable. Then identify the subset of requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied under these assumptions, and say why that subset is the real obstruction.

Finally, do not end with a balanced menu. I want you to choose the single requirement this board should abandon first, commit to that choice, and defend it against the objection that the resulting rule is either too crude or too manipulable. If weakening the unrestricted-domain assumption, for example by restricting to single-peaked preferences, would rescue most of the list, say whether that is a practical escape route for this board or merely a theoretical consolation.
