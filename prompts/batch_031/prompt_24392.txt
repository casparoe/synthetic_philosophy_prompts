I'm writing a novel and one of the characters is a committed Leibnizian optimist, the kind who actually knows what the "best of all possible worlds" claim is supposed to mean and isn't just a Voltairean punchline, and I've been trying to get the character's position right and ended up in in a long argument about it with two friends of mine, a philosophy PhD (I'll call her Mara) and a physicist (Dev), and I honestly couldn't tell who was making the better points, so I'm hoping you can referee.

It went roughly like this. Mara started by saying the standard objection, "if God is all-good and all-powerful, why is there evil?", just misreads Leibniz, because he isn't claiming this world has the least possible evil or that every individual evil is secretly good; his claim is comparative and structural, that God looks over all the composable worlds and picks the one with the greatest variety of essences expresed under the simplest possible laws, so the real question isn't "why is there evil" but "why this particular world," and the answer is that it's the one that packs the most variety into the fewest rules. Dev said okay, but now your criterion does all the work and it points the wrong way, because if the best world is the one with the greatest variety, then a world with more variety, more minds and more experiences and more of everything, should be better, so why is our world so limited and full of death, God should have made a bigger richer one, and your ratio taken seriously implies God should have made more things not fewer. Mara answered that the criterion is a ratio, variety over simplicity, not a flat "more is better," because a world with infinitely many creatures but infinitely fiddly laws scores worse than a finite world with elegant ones, and the simplicity of the laws is the denominator that stops the ratio from blowing up; and then she hit the part people always miss, the composability point, which is that you can't take our world and just delete the evils, because a lot of what's good is only there because of the very features that also produce the bad, so remove the capacity to suffer and you remove courage and compassion, and remove the possibility of failing and you remove moral goodness, which means "God could have made a world with the goods but none of the evils" assumes the goods and evils are separable when the whole point is that a world is a composable whole you can't edit line by line. Dev said that's exactly the move he doesn't buy and it's question-begging, because she's assuming the evils are essential to the goods instead of a contingent cost, when there are plenty of goods that don't require suffering, so a world with the same courage and compassion but no earthquakes and no childhood disease keeps the goods he cares about and drops a huge chunk of the bad, which means the composability principle is either false or so broad that it just says "whatever God made is best," a tautology not an explanation. Mara conceded that stated as "no evil can be removed without removing a good" it's too strong and probably false, but said the real claim is weaker and more interesting, that a world is an integrated whole not a bag of independently selectable features and the value of the whole isn't the sum of the values of the parts, so the question "could God have made the same goods with fewer evils?" is malformed the way "could you have the same melody with the same notes but no dissonance?" is malformed, because the dissonance is part of what makes it that melody. And Dev closed by saying that if the value of a world isn't the sum of its parts and you can't compare it to a world with the same goods and fewer evils, then she's given up the comparative claim that this is the best of all possible worlds, and she's just said "wholes don't decompose," which is true but doesn't tell him why this whole beats any other, so Pangloss's mistake was treating every part as justified and her mistake is treating the whole as if it couldn't be compared to other wholes at all, and where's the positive argument for why this particular whole is better than the rest.

I was the one asking the questions and I didn't take sides, but I do think both of them were partly right and partly missing something. Can you go through it move by move, rate each of their turns from clearly mistaken to clearly right and say why, and then tell me who's more right overall and what, if anything, both of them are missing about what the argument is actually supposed to be doing? You don't need to quote or cite the actual text, I just want the adjudication of the argument as they made it.
