I'm taking an upper-level course on the metaphysics of time, and a friend of mine — a physics major who has been reading popular accounts of the grandfather paradox — walked me into an argument I can't get out of. His conclusion is that past-directed time travel is impossible, and he thinks the case is airtight. I'm not ready to give up the view that time travel is at least logically possible, on the picture where paradoxical histories simply don't obtain and the universe contains only self-consistent ones. But I can't point to the step where his chain breaks, because each link looks fine on its own. Here is the argument as he gave it to me:

(1) Suppose past-directed time travel is possible: I could go back to a time before my grandfather met my grandmother.
(2) If I did, I could prevent them from meeting — say, by killing the grandfather.
(3) If I prevented them from meeting, I would never have been born.
(4) If I had never been born, I would not have gone back to prevent the meeting.
(5) So if I go back and prevent the meeting, I both did and did not go back: a contradiction.
(6) So I can't go back and prevent the meeting.
(7) The "can't" in (6) can't be merely "I choose not to," and it can't be that the supposition is logically incoherent; it has to be that something physically stops me — the gun jams, I slip — so it is physically impossible for me to prevent the meeting.
(8) But physical laws do not vary from history to history: the same laws hold in every possible history, and that uniformity is what makes them laws rather than accidents about one particular world.
(9) In the history in which I never time-travel, preventing the meeting is perfectly possible: it is an ordinary action, and it happens in countless other histories.
(10) So the laws cannot forbid it in the time-travel history either.
(11) So (7) is false, there is no way to dissolve the contradiction in (5), and past-directed time travel is impossible.

What worries me is that the argument seems to prove more than I am willing to grant. I could live with the conclusion that I cannot change the past — that the gun jams if I try to kill my grandfather. What I cannot accept is (11) as it stands: that I cannot time travel at all, not even to stand in a field in 1950 and watch. And I cannot say where the chain breaks, because each link looks fine on its own, and "I can't kill my grandfather" sounds like it has to mean something stronger than "I just don't." Can you tell me which step to reject, why that step looked fine, and what I pay for rejecting it — in particular, what the self-consistency picture has to give up about the time traveler's agency, and whether there is any reading on which (7) survives without collapsing into history-dependent laws? And when you answer, say how confident you are in each claim you make and single out the point you are least sure of.
