I've been reading Ian Hacking's stuff on looping effects — the idea that categories in the human sciences don't just describe the people they're applied to, they actually change those people, so the category keeps drifting as the people internalize it — and I'm convinced that psychiatric diagnosis is a textbook case of this. I'm presenting a short talk at a philosophy colloquium at a university in about a month, and I'm defending a fairly strong claim: that psychiatric diagnostic categories are more unstable than the people who use them day to day are willing to admit, and that this instability isn't a bug to be engineered away but the thing that makes psychiatric classification genuinely different from, say, classifying rocks or bacterial species. The audience will be mostly clinical psychologists, a couple of psychiatrists, and two philosophers of psychiatry. I suspect the clinicians will be the ones who push back hardest, because their whole careers are built on making these diagnoses work in practice, so I expect them to be a bit hostile to an outsider telling them their categories are unstable in some deep sense.
