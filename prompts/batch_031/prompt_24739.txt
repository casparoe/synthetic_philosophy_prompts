I'm in a course on data ethics and I keep getting stuck on a tension I can't resolve. GDPR's right to erasure (Article 17) lets a person demand that their personal data be deleted—the right to be forgotten. Yet the same legal order also *requires* that certain personal data be kept: banks must retain transaction records for several years, telecom operators must hold metadata for a set period, and providers must keep patient records well past the treatment. So one system is at once saying "you can make this data disappear" and "this data must be kept, full stop." What theory of the value of privacy does that pattern presuppose? Does it presuppose that the mere *existence* of recorded information is the harm (in which case mandated retention is a standing violation and erasure is the default), or that the *use* or *contextual misuse* of information is the harm (in which case retention for a defined, legitimate purpose is unobjectionable and the right to erasure is really just a risk-reduction tool)? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether the practice strictly presupposes it or is compatible with several, and tell me what would have to change in the design of the right to be forgotten if we replaced the presupposed view with a straightforward existence-based one.
