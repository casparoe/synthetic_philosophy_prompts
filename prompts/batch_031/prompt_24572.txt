I've been stuck on Moore's paradox for a few days now after a friend dragged me into it, and I keep getting inconsistant intuitions about which variants are actually weird. I want to figure out if there's a single principle doing the work, or if I'm just being arbitrary. Here are my gut calls, all spoken sincerely by one person in the present tense (I'll flag the exceptions):

- "It's raining, but I don't believe that it's raining." → weird, the classic one.
- "I don't believe that it's raining, but it is." → totally fine, just reporting my state and a fact.
- "It's raining, but I believe that it isn't raining." → also weird, maybe even weirder.
- "I believe that it isn't raining, but it is." → fine, same reason as the second one.
- "It's raining, but I don't know that it's raining." → weird, similar to the first.
- "It's raining, but you don't believe that it's raining." → fine, that's a statement about you.
- "It was raining, but I didn't believe that it was raining." → fine, it's in the past.
- "She said, 'It's raining, but I don't believe it's raining.'" → fine, I'm just reporting what she said.

The thing that's really bugging me is the first two. As far as I can tell they have the exact same content — it's raining, and I don't believe it — but I find the first weird and the second fine, and I can't articuate why. And I can't reconcile the third and fourth ones either; the third is the "other form" and I think it's weird, but it seems to have a similar structure to the fourth, which I call fine.

So: is there a principle that captures all of these, or am I just making it up? If there is, what does it predict for a case I haven't thought about, like when a philosopher says the classic line just to demonstrate the point, the way Moore apparently did? And if the principle forces me to drop one of my verdicts, which one would you say I should drop and why?
