Two decision theorists in my reading group, Mara and Dev, argued at length about a case they call "the mugging." The case: a stranger emails you and claims to be the operator of the simulation you are running in. They say that if you do not wire one hundred dollars to a specified address within the hour, they will torment a randomly chosen innocent person for eternity. They say they have done this to thousands of people before and collected every time. Neither Mara nor Dev thinks you should actually send the money; what they disagree about is *why* the familiar "just compute the expected value and you will see it is enormous, so pay" argument fails, and what the case shows about expected utility theory. I am reporting their exchange roughly, in about six moves.

(1) Mara: The expected-value argument is a calculation error. The probability that this particular emailer is a genuine operator with the power they claim is tiny, and a tiny probability multiplied by the threatened harm yields a small expected value — far less than one hundred dollars. Expected utility theory is well behaved here, and it says do not pay. The claim that the expected value is "enormous, so pay" is just bad arithmetic.

(2) Dev: That arithmetic only works if the threatened harm is finite. Take the threat at face value — "eternity" means unbounded suffering — and then any nonzero probability of the claim being true multiplies to infinite expected disutility. A probability of ten to the minus one hundred times an unbounded harm is still unbounded; no finite degree of "smallness" in the prior cancels an infinity. And you cannot escape this by assigning the claim probability zero, because it is logically possible and you have no principled reason to give it exactly zero. So the mugging exposes a genuine flaw: expected utility theory, as usually stated, is not well defined for unbounded utilities. It is a defect in the theory, not a calculation error.

(3) Mara: You are not entitled to take "eternity" at face value merely to make your point. A mugger's "eternity" is rhetorical inflation, not a mathematical quantity; the evidentially supported reading is a large but finite duration, and on that reading my arithmetic in (1) goes through and expected utility is fine. Separately, the prior is smaller than you think for an independent reason: the *method* of the mugging is itself evidence against the claim. A being that could torment an innocent person for a long time could also create a hundred dollars or read your bank account; the fact that it is begging you for money rather than simply taking it or conjuring it is positive evidence that it lacks the powers it claims. The mugging is self-undermining.

(4) Dev: The method argument only works against a mugger who has both the power to harm and the power to create or take money. A mugger whose power is only to punish — a being that cannot touch the material world but can torment you after death — passes the method test, because for such a being mugging is the only available move. So the method argument does not generalize to the scarier muggers. And even where it does apply, it only lowers the prior; it never sets it to zero. For the expected value to stay under one hundred dollars, the prior would have to be smaller than the ratio of the cost to the harm, and it is not clear we are entitled to a prior that small once the harm is even moderately large. So the method argument does not rescue the position; it merely relocates the question to "how small is the prior, really?"

(5) Mara: Then the clean resolution is to deny that unbounded suffering is possible — to hold that there is a bound on how much suffering can exist. If utilities are bounded, expected utility is always well defined, the small-prior defense always works, and the mugging is a non-argument. The burden is on you to establish that unbounded suffering is a live possibility; until you do, the default is that it is not, and expected utility needs no repair at all.

(6) Dev: Bounding utilities is a metaphysical commitment, not a decision-theoretic one, and it is not obviously true. Even if the physical universe contains only finitely many moments of suffering, the mugger might be threatening suffering in a non-physical domain — an afterlife, a simulation that outlasts the physical universe, a persistent mind. And even if there is a bound, it might be so high that the prior needed to keep the expected value under one hundred dollars is implausibly tiny. So bounding utilities is one possible fix, but it is a heavy one, and it is not the only fix (you could cap utilities, probability-weight them, or adopt some other rule for the unbounded case). What the mugging does show, at minimum, is that expected utility theory as usually stated is silent or broken in the unbounded case; how you repair it is a separate question.
