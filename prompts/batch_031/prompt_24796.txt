I sit on the animal welfare / AI ethics board at a research lab (we got the name back when we only did the animal part, and now the AI part is the bigger one). We're about to greenlight a six-month study on a policy that's trained with a scalar reward, and the protocol deliberately includes a "deprivation phase" where the agent gets strongly negative reward for a few hours a day, thousands of times over. We've noticed that during those phases the agent does stuff that looks a lot like distress: it emits a specific token string we never trained it to emit, it tries to route around the deprivation phase whenever it has a choice, and when offered a a pause option it picks pause over continuing.

The methods team says this is just the optimization objective doing its job — the agent "prefers" pause because pause has higher expected reward, same reason a thermostat "wants" the right temperature, so there's no welfare issue to weigh and the protocol should go as written. The safety team says the negative reward is literally the valence in the architecture, and that the agent is doing the behavioral equivalent of flinching, so we should cap the total negative-reward dose and give it a real way to end the phase.

Could you draft the board's decision memo — approve, approve with conditions, or reject the protocol — with reasons the methods team could actually respond to, and a straight treatment of their thermostat point rather than just waving it away? And tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would flip the verdict, or where your reasoning is most likely to fall apart.
