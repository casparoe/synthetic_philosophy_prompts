A popular book on the evolution of cooperation, which I have been reading since my retirement, presses an analogy I keep returning to. It says that the iterated prisoner's dilemma — the famous tournaments in which a simple tit-for-tat strategy beat far more elaborate ones — is really just a market in miniature. Self-interested agents, no central planner, no appeal to altruism; and yet a stable, mutually beneficial order of cooperation emerges from each player's pursuit of its own payoff, exactly as Smith's invisible hand is supposed to produce social order from self-love. The book then uses this to draw a conclusion I find too clean: that we should stop looking for moral or genuinely other-regarding explanations of cooperation. If cooperation is the market of the social world, then it needs no moral foundation at all — it is rational self-interest plus the repetition of encounters, and any talk of evolved altruism is a mistake.

Can you audit this analogy for me? Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the buyer and seller, the price, voluntary exchange, fraud, contract enforcement, and market equilibrium in the prisoner's-dilemma world — and then tell me where the mapping breaks. I am especially interested in what the analogy quietly imports from the market that the dilemma does not have, and vice versa, and in whether the book's conclusion — that cooperation needs no moral or altruistic explanation — survives once the disanalogies are on the table. I suspect the payoff structure, which is the whole reason it is a dilemma in the first place, is doing something the market picture cannot see, but I want that argued out rather than asserted.

As always, please say how confident you are in each major claim you make, and single out the one point you are least sure of.
