I keep coming back to a pattern in political philosophy and in the way histories of law are told, and I am not sure whether it has a name. The pattern: a legal or political order is challenged as unjust, and its defenders trace its origin to an act of force — a conquest, a usurpation, an enclosure — but they do not simply say the strongest had the right. Instead, they add that the force is long past, and that since then custom, usage, and the mere passage of years have transformed the original seizure into law. The force was the seed; time and habit did the rest. The order is just now because it has long been what it is, and the earlier violence has been absorbed into an unremarkable history.

I have a sense of where this lives. In the justice-and-force fragments of Pascal's Pensées, in the "reason of effects" material, the argument runs roughly this way: no law can be shown to be just in itself, what counts as just varies from place to place, and what we call law is, in the end, force that has been made respectable by custom and the length of time it has stood. Rousseau, in the opening pages of the Social Contract, is attacking something with exactly this shape when he dismisses the "right of the strongest" as a paradoxical formula no one has yet thought to put in writing. Hobbes has a related move: justice and injustice are creatures of the commonwealth, so before there is a sovereign there is no injustice at all, and the founding act cannot be judged by the standard it creates. And ordinary property law has prescription, where time plus possession can confer a title that no one can unmake.

But I do not think any of those is the thing I am describing. The bare claim that the strongest has the right is not it, because the described move explicitly refuses that claim and substitutes duration for strength. The bare historical fact that every regime has a violent founding moment is not it either, because that fact by itself is descriptive and does no justificatory work. The de jure/de facto distinction is not it, because it does not say that the gap between the two closes with time. Hobbes's conventionism is not it, because it locates the source of justice in present authority rather than in elapsed years. Prescription is the closest, I think, but it is a technical rule with defined conditions and limits, whereas the move I have in mind is a general argumentative strategy available to any established order.

So my questions are these. First, does this pattern — the laundering of an originally forceful founding into legitimacy through custom and the passage of time — have an established name or a stable place in the literature? If it does, say what it is called and where it is argued. If it does not, I would like you to coin a term for it and define it precisely, distinguishing it from each of the neighboring concepts above and saying which of them it fits best and where it departs from all of them. I will not be satisfied with a bare label; the point is the carving: what the mechanism exactly is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.

Second, and this is the substantive question, I want an assessment of whether the mechanism is a genuine source of legitimacy or a rhetorical one. Does the passage of time and the settling of custom really confer legitimacy, or do they only make an existing distribution of power look legitimate? If they really do confer it, what exactly is time doing — stabilizing expectations, dissolving epistemic uncertainty about what the right order is, something else? And if they do not, then the move is a piece of legitimation rhetoric, and I want to know what would distinguish the two cases in a way that could actually be applied. Third, say something about the boundary cases: does the mechanism apply equally to property rights, to international practice, and to political authority, or only to the last? And what would have to be true for the mechanism to fail — what would break the conversion of force into right if it were real?

This is for a long essay, so depth and precision matter more than coverage. I will take care of any citations myself.
