I'm a science-and-ideas journalist, and on the side I run a small monthly seminar — roughly sixteen people, a mix of graduate students and curious non-specialists, ninety minutes in a rented room — where we work through one big idea at a time. I've been covering a lot of recent work on animal cognition and sentience for a feature, and it keeps pushing me back to the moral question underneath it, so this month's theme is the moral status of non-human animals. I want to open the session with a hands-on exercise rather than a lecture, because I've found that the argument I keep coming back to — the one usually called the argument from marginal cases, the engine behind the objection known as speciesism — lands far harder when people generate the inconsistency in their own judgments than when I explain it to them.

Could you design that exercise for me? I'm imagining a staged, forced-choice task in which participants make concrete protection or priority judgments about a set of beings, where the beings are presented in a way that initially hides which are human and which aren't, and where the structure is set up so that people run into the tension themselves — the point at which the boundary they're actually drawing tracks cognitive capacity rather than species membership — before anyone in the room names the argument. Please specify the exact task and its rules; the case material, i.e., the capacity profiles of the beings they'll judge, including a few that are deliberately marginal humans, a few that are complex non-humans, and a couple of unambiguous anchors; a commitment device that locks in each person's judgments before the reveal so they can't simply revise once they know which beings are human; a minute-by-minute plan for the ninety minutes; and a debrief that maps the specific moments in the room onto the parts of the argument and walks through, step by step, where the argument could be resisted and what it costs to resist it at each point.

Two things I care about. First, be honest about what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run — where people will guess which cases are human, where they'll get stuck or start debating individual cases instead of the structure, where the discomfort will show up — and how to handle each of these in the moment. Second, be frank about which feature of the argument the exercise simply cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply myself in the debrief, so I don't overclaim what the activity actually did.
