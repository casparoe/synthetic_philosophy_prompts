I'm taking a course on Chinese ethics this semester and my study group got really stuck on the Mencius vs. Xunzi debate about human nature, and neither of them would back down. One of them, Lena, was on Mencius's side and Marco was on Xunzi's. Here's roughly how it went.

Lena opened with the famouse case of the child about to fall into a well: anyone who suddenly sees a kid on the verge of falling in gets a flash of alarm and pity, and she argued that this comes before any calculation about whether the kid's parents are friends, whether you'll get credit for the rescue, or whether you just dislike the kid's crying. Since the feeling is spontaneous and not bought by any payoff, she said, benevolence has to be part of what we are by nature. Marco pushed back by saying that even if the alarm is spontaneous, that doesn't make it nature, because Xunzi draws a line between what you are without choosing or making it (the raw, unchosen stuff, like your basic desires) and what your discerning mind produces by working. He said the flash of alarm is exactly the kind of thing Xunzi would file under the function of the discerning mind, not under nature, so Lena's case doesn't show what she thinks it shows.

Lena said fine, but then where does the discerning mind get its material? If everyone's raw nature is just competing desires that turn into quarrels and disorder when you let them run, then the sage who came up with ritual and righteousness is pulling a rabbit out of a hat, ritual just appears from nowhere. Marco answered that the sage isn't magic, the sage is what you get when someone accumulates deliberate, artificial effort over a lifetime, and he used the crooked wood image, a crooked piece of wood only becomes straight if you put it to the axe plane, and unshaped metal only becomes round if you put it to the grind stone. The raw stuff isn't good, but it's the material that gets worked. Lena then said accumulation is doing all the heavy lifting, because if the sage can accumulate ritual out of an evil nature, why can't ordinary people, and at that point she felt like Xunzi's evil nature was secretly doing the same job as Mencius's good nature, both are just saying people can be shaped, they only disagree about which way the shaping goes. Marco said the direction is the whole point, for Mencius the task is to preserve and extend what's already there, like nourishing a sprout, whereas for Xunzi the task is to correct and transform what's there, like straightening the wood, so the two positions aren't the same thing in different clothes.

They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't. Could you go through their exchange and say who's right at each step, not just who wins overall? And can you flag which parts of this are actually settled among people who study these texts versus which parts are genuinely contested, so I don't take some one scholar's take to be the consensus?
