I spent thirty years as a physician, most of it in anesthesiology and pain medicine, and only in the last two years, since retiring, have I started reading philosophy of mind and the philosophy of pain. I keep getting stuck on pain asymbolia, and I want a real answer rather than a survey of the literature. So that we are working from the same case: patients with damage to the insula or cingulate cortex, or patients under morphine, can report that they still feel the painful stimulus — its location, intensity, and quality are intact — but that it is not unpleasant and does not bother them; they recognize it as pain while being, as far as they can tell, not suffering from it. This looks like a clean dissociation between the sensory-discriminative side of pain (where, how strong, what kind) and the affective-motivational side (the unpleasantness and the drive to withdraw). What it forces me to confront is a question I have not been able to settle: what, precisely, is it about pain that makes it bad?

Here is the proposition I want you to put a number on. Call it P: "The badness of a painful experience for the person undergoing it is grounded in its unpleasantness — its negative affective valence — and not in its sensory-discriminative content or in the tissue damage it represents; so that a pain that is fully sensory-discriminative but not unpleasant is not, in any welfare-relevant sense, bad for that person." The rival reading is that the badness lies in the represented damage or in the danger the pain signals, and that the asymbolic patient is simply cut off from registering it — in which case their pain is still bad for them, and they are, if anything, in a worse situation because they cannot even feel that it is bad. I would count the question resolved if we had a settled answer to whether the asymbolic patient's pain is bad for them, or, more generally, whether pain's badness-for-a-person is identical with its unpleasantness. My own credence in P is about 0.6; a friend who is a philosopher of mind puts it at about 0.3, and I do not know which of us is wrong or why. I suspect the gap is about whether the lesion removes the badness or only the awareness of it, but I could be wrong about where it lives.

Please give me your own credence in P, with the full decomposition rather than a bare number. I would expect the number to ride on at least these sub-questions, but add any others you think genuinely belong: (i) whether pain is really a composite state with separable sensory-discriminative and affective components, such that "pain without unpleasantness" is a coherent existing state rather than an oxymoron; (ii) given that it is composite, whether the badness for the person is grounded in the affective component rather than in the sensory-discriminative component or in the represented damage; (iii) whether badness-for-a-person is experiential at all — that is, whether something can be bad for a person only insofar as it affects their experience, or whether damage a person does not feel can still be bad for them; and (iv) how much evidential weight asymbolia actually carries, i.e. whether it isolates the unpleasantness from everything else that could be a source of badness, or whether the lesions and the morphine also knock out some "this is a problem" appraisal that is itself part of what makes pain bad. Assign a rough probability to each sub-question with a one-line justification, show me how they combine into your number for P, tell me which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, and say what evidence or argument would move it most. And tell me, on your account, where the disagreement between me at 0.6 and my friend at 0.3 most likely actually lives. I want a committed answer — a definite position with reasons — not a balanced overview that leaves the choice to me. And please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
