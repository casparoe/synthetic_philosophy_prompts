I'm in a small philosophy of religion reading group and two of us got stuck in an argument about Molina's middle knowledge that neither would drop, and I wasn't sure who had the better case so I'm bringing it to you. The setup as I understand it: the Molinist says God knows, prior to and independantly of His choice of which world to create, what every free creature would freely do in any possible circumstance — this "middle knowledge" (scientia media) sits between God's knowledge of necessary truths and His knowledge of the actual world, and it's what lets Him "select" a world, seeing in advance what free creatures would do in each one. The other view, which I think is sometimes called Ockhamism, says you don't need that seperate faculty at all: God just freely decides which world to make, and the "what would have happened otherwise" facts are grounded in that decision, so there's no independent list of counterfactuals to survey beforehand.

Here's roughly how it went. One of them (the Ockhamist-leaning one) started by saying the middle knowledge is redundant — once God picks a world He knows everything about it including what would have happened if things had been otherwise, so the counterfactuals are just downstream of His choice, and positing a distinct kind of knowledge in the middle is just extra machinery for no payoff. The Molinist replied that this gets the direction of dependence backwards: whether creature C would freely do A in circumstance X looks like a fact about C's free nature, not about which world God happens to instantiate, and if the answer to that question is settled by God's choice of world, then it's God's decree that makes it true that C "would have" done A — which is just determinism wearing a wig, since C's freedom is supposed to be what fixes the counterfactual, not the decree. The Ockhamist then pushed back on the Molinist's picture directly: the "circumstances" your middle knowledge ranges over only exist inside particular worlds, so if God hasn't chosen a world yet there are no circumstances for the creatures to be placed in, and you end up with "knowledge" of what would happen in situations that aren't instantiated anywhere, which is incoherent. The Molinist answered that the circumstances only need to be possible, not actual — possible situations are real enough to be the antecedents of counterfactuals, and the truth of "if C were in X, C would do A" is fixed by C's free disposition, not by whether X ever occurs. To close, the Ockhamist conceded the point about the circumstances but said the real question is what grounds the counterfactual, and on the Molinist's view it's some "would-do disposition" the creature just has, while on his view it's God's decree — and he didn't see why a mysterious disposition is any less ad hoc than a decree, so the Molinist hasn't actually escaped the problem, just relocated it.

Can you go through that exchange move by move and tell me who's right about each specific point, not just who wins overall? And I'd like you to actually commit — pick a side on the direction-of-dependence question and on whether the "no circumstances before the world" objection is a real threat, and say what, if anything, both of them are missing in the middle. I know this is a corner of the literature I don't know well, so I'd rather have a clear verdict with reasons than a balanced survey of everyone's options.
