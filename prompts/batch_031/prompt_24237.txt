I've been going through the scalable-oversight literature for a reading group, and the standard way the debate proposal gets motivated in the writeups and talks is the courtroom analogy: two models argue opposite positions on a question while a human (or another model) sits as judge and picks the winner, just like litigants before a judge. The analogy is doing a lot of work in these accounts — it is what makes debate sound like a robust oversight mechanism, on the idea that an adversarial structure plus a competent judge reliably surfaces the truth, so we can outsource the checking of long model chains-of-thought to a debate the judge only has to skim. One of my colleagues, who is more skeptical, pushes back that the courtroom picture is precisely the thing the obfuscated-argument problem refutes, and that the analogy is quietly smuggling in the very assumption we would need to prove.

Can you audit the courtroom analogy for me? Start with a correspondence table — what plays the role of the litigants, the judge, the evidence, the burden of proof, and the verdict — and then map out where the mapping breaks. I especially want you to bring in three disanalogies: first, that in a real courtroom the evidence is independently checkable (documents, physical exhibits, cross-examinable witnesses) and both sides are incentivized to be honest about it, whereas in debate the "evidence" is the opposing model's own reasoning — the very thing in dispute — which the judge cannot independently re-derive, so it has to take each debater's word for the correctness of each step; second, the complexity-theoretic point that a polynomial-time judge can only directly check NP-level claims, so it is structurally underpowered for a PSPACE-level question and must rely on the debaters to do the heavy lifting and can only spot-check, and the courtroom hides this by making the judge look like a natural, competent arbiter with something external to check the parties' claims against; and third, the obfuscated-argument case itself, where every locally checkable step of the losing argument passes the judge's spot-checks but the argument is globally wrong, so there is no single step the judge can point to as the "perjury," because the error lives in a hidden global premise the judge never inspects. After laying that out, tell me which of these disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion that debate is reliable and which are just surface differences, and whether the reassuring conclusion — a good judge plus adversarial structure gives us truth — survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If the courtroom picture was carrying the argument all along, say so plainly.
