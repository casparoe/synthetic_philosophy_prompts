I'm taking a course on value theory and find the whole better-than transitivity thing maddening. I was playing with a spectrum argument and I think I've accidently proven that value comparisons are incoherent, which I don't want. Here's the chain: (1) For any two outcomes, exactly one holds: x is better than y, y is better than x, or they are equally good. (2) If x is at least as good as y and y is at least as good as z, then x is at least as good as z. (3) On a spectrum, take a long finite sequence of cases that runs from a clearly excellent outcome to a clearly terrible one, with neighboring cases so close that the difference between them is too small to matter; then each neighboring case is at least as good as the next. (4) So if you chain the neighboring cases all the way down, the excellent outcome is at least as good as the terrible one. (5) But that's false. (6) So one of the premises fails. (7) If (3) fails, then every tiny difference on the spectrum matters, which seems absurd. (8) If (1) fails, then there are incomparable outcomes, and I can't even say which of two close cases is better. (9) Therefore value ranking is hopeless.

I'd rather keep the idea that close cases are genuinely close than that all tiny differences matter, but I'm not sure that saves the whole thing. Which premise or step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what do I lose by rejecting it? Also say how confident you are in your diagnosis and single out the point you're least sure about.
