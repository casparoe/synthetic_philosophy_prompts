I'm at the stage where I'm defending a paper at a workshop on moral responsibility, and my view is that the "standing to blame" constraint (roughly: if you yourself are guilty of the relevant wrong, you lose standing to blame others for it, full stop) is a much more important and interesting idea than the "moral pollution" view (roughly: your own wrongdoing doesn't remove your right to blame but makes your blame less credible or effective, a kind of rhetorical handicap). I want a tier list of the arguments in this debate, ranked by how much they actually move the case, and for each argument I want you to say in a couple of sentences which side it favors and why it lands where it does. Include at least: the argument from moral self-knowledge (you can't see your own faults as clearly as others', so your blame is epistemically less reliable), the argument from fairness or reciprocity (blaming others for what you do is a kind of free-riding on the moral order), the argument from the point of blame (blame is meant to be a moral correction between persons, and a wrongdoer has no business playing the corrector), the argument that standing and credibility are separable (a doctor who smokes can still be right that smoking is bad), and the "it's just prudential" deflation (pollution is nothing over and above the fact that a hypocrite's blame invites a tu quoque, so there's no deep philosophical fact, just a conversational one). If you think this framing of the debate as two clean views is confused, or that the ranking task as I've set it up can't be done well because the arguments don't compare on a single dimension, say so plainly and tell me how you'd reformulate the question instead of forcing the tier list.
