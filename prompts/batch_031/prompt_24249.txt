I'm writing up my views for a seminar on population ethics and the moral significance of the distant future, and before I present them I'd like a careful consistency check. A lot of my thinking has been shaped by Scheffler's afterlife conjecture—the idea that what matters about the future beyond our own lives is the continuation of some kind of human afterlife, in which our culture, projects, and significance carry on after we are gone, and that the total cessation of that continuation would be a genuine loss in its own right. I've tried to spell out my position as a set of commitments below, and I'd like you to tell me whether the set hangs together.

(1) I accept the core Schefflerian intuition: human extinction would be a catastrophe in a way that is not reducible to the sum of the badness of the last generation's deaths. Some impersonal value—the continuation of human culture and an ongoing human afterlife—is lost, and that loss is bad even though no particular individual is wronged by it. (2) I'm a welfarist: I think all value is ultimately grounded in the welfare of individual sentient beings, and I'm skeptical that there is any free-floating impersonal value that is not reducible to someone's experience. (3) I think the non-identity problem is a genuine obstacle to person-affecting justifications of our obligations to the distant future: in cases where a choice determines which particular people will come into existence, it is not clear that a worse option is wrong because it harms specific identifiable individuals, since the people who would exist under the better option are different people. (4) I think we have strong moral reasons to care about the very distant future—thousands to tens of thousands of years out—and that the heavy temporal discounting most of us default to is a bias rather than a rational attitude. (5) I think a world in which the human species is extinct but the rest of the natural world—forests, oceans, other species—continues to flourish for millions of years is not, in itself, a tragedy; it would be sad, but it is not the kind of catastrophe that (1) describes.

Could you go through these and tell me which pairs are actually in tension and how badly, and which are only apparently in tension and dissolve on inspection? For each real tension, please say which commitment has to give and what it would cost to give it up, and then rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as a flat list of equally bad problems. I'd also appreciate it if you could flag any commitment that is doing less work than I think it is, or any pair I have not noticed that is in tension.
