I've been going in circles on an argument a colleague of mine laid out and I can't find the flaw. Here it is:

(1) If moral status is a matter of degree, then as you move along a spectrum of sentience-like properties, the amount of moral status a thing has changes smoothly — no sudden jumps.
(2) Now run the spectrum from a fruit fly down to a home thermostat. The relevant stuff — having experiences, being affected by what happens to you, there being something it's like to be you — seems to fade out gradually as you go from the fly toward the thermostat.
(3) There's no fact of the matter that pins down exactly where on that spectrum moral status "switches on." Any specific line you draw between "has some moral status" and "has none" is just a choice, not something the world forces on you.
(4) If the line is just a choice, then it's not a real boundary, so there's no sharp boundary between beings with moral status and beings without.
(5) If there's no sharp boundary, then moral status comes in degrees all the way down — so the thermostat has a little moral status, just alot less than the fly.

I work on embedded systems, so sensor thresholds and cutoffs are basically my whole job, and the thermostat is just the example that stuck with me. I can't really accept (5) — I don't think I owe the thermostat anything. And what worries me is that if I let this reasoning stand, I could run the exact same thing on the spectrum from a person down to a goldfish and "prove" there's no real line between them either, which I'm definitely not ready to sign up for. Somewhere around (3) to (5) I feel like something's off but I cant put my finger on it. Which step do I actually have to reject, and what do I give up by rejecting it? And if any part of your answer is shaky, say so and tell me which bit you're least sure about.
