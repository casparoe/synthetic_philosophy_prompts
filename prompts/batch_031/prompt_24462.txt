Question posed to the student:
"Explain Kuhn's notion of incommensurability between scientific paradigms. In your answer, (a) distinguish incommensurability from the Duhem–Quine thesis of underdetermination, and (b) assess whether incommensurability, by itself, entails a form of scientific relativism. Be as precise as you can about what 'incommensurable' is and is not supposed to mean."

The student's answer:

Incommensurability, for Kuhn, is the claim that two scientific paradigms cannot be fully translated into one another and so cannot be neutrally compared. When a community moves from one paradigm to another — from Newtonian mechanics to relativity, or from phlogiston to Lavoisier's oxygen chemistry — the basic terms and categories change with it. "Mass," "force," and even "explanation" do not carry the same weight under the old framework and the new one, so there is no theory-independent, neutral vocabulary in which both could be restated and weighed against the same body of evidence. Because no such neutral standard exists, the choice between rival paradigms cannot be settled by a purely objective, paradigm-independent procedure; it involves, as Kuhn suggested, something like a gestalt switch. And because the two frameworks rest on different categories, a scientist deeply committed to one cannot, in the end, make sense of the claims of the other — the paradigms are, in a real sense, incompatible.

This is related to, but should not be confused with, the Duhem–Quine thesis. Duhem–Quine holds that a hypothesis is never tested in isolation: any prediction rests on a whole web of auxiliary assumptions, so when a prediction fails we cannot tell which node in the web is at fault, and the evidence underdetermines the theory. Incommensurability is a different claim. It is not about the underdetermination of a single theory by the evidence, but about the absence of a common standard for comparing two rival frameworks. Duhem–Quine underdetermination can in principle operate within a single paradigm (one theory, several competing ways of accommodating the data), whereas incommensurability is a relation that holds between paradigms.

As for relativism, I would say incommensurability does not by itself entail it. Even if there is no neutral, theory-independent standard of comparison, scientists still choose between paradigms by their success at solving the puzzles their paradigm sets before them, and puzzle-solving success is a shared, objective criterion that both camps can recognize. Since both the old and the new paradigm are evaluated by how well they solve puzzles, the choice is still guided by objective considerations rather than by mere preference, and we do not have to slide into the claim that anything goes, or that one paradigm is just as good as another.
