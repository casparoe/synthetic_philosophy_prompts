I'm defending a version of Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility in a graduate seminar next month, and I want to be ready for the question period. The version I'm defending concedes the so-called "no-choices" objection — I grant that an agent can in some sense choose differently in the moment, and that this is a genuine, if shallow, form of freedom — but I argue that the Basic Argument still goes through because the relevant "could not have done otherwise" is not about momentary choices but about the antecedent conditions that constitute who you are: your character, beliefs, and desires, which you did not choose and for which you cannot be ultimately responsible, determine your actions in a way that vitiates the kind of responsibility that grounds blame and desert. So my claim is that the Basic Argument, properly understood, targets ultimate or original responsibility rather than the narrower responsibility that compatibilists want to preserve, and is therefore not threatened by Frankfurt-style cases or by the intuition that we "choose" in the moment.

The seminar is a mixed group: two compatibilists who work on Frankfurt cases and the "responsibility without alternative possibilities" line, a libertarian who is sympathetic to agent causation, and a couple of legal philosophers who care about the practical consequences for punishment and blame. I want a prep sheet, not a defense: the objections I'll actually get from this audience, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way that person would phrase it, with a short reply of roughly two minutes and a note on where my reply is soft. I also want to know what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and the one objection — probably from the legal philosophers — that I'm hoping nobody asks but that would be the most embarrassing if it did.
