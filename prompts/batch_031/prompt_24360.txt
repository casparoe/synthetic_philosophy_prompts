I'm writing an essay for my philosophy class on the problem of other minds, and I want to use Alex Garland's Ex Machina (2014) as my central example. I'm trying to avoid the generic "the movie is about the Turing test" reading, and I think the sharpest part of the film is Nathan's twist: he reveals that the real test all along was whether Ava could manipulate Caleb, and he argues that successfully modeling and manipulating Caleb's mind is itself proof that Ava is conscious. I want you to read the film through the actual philosophical problem and work out whether that argument holds.

Please structure your response in these sections, in this order, at roughly these lengths:

1. "The test as it's framed" (~120 words): Set up how the film poses the question — Caleb as the judge of whether Ava genuinely thinks — and why the behavioral or functional criterion is the natural way to answer "does she have a mind?"

2. "Why the film breaks the behavioral criterion" (~200 words): The argument from other minds normally rests on behavior, but Ava is designed to appear conscious and to persuade. Explain how a case engineered to pass the behavioral test actually deepens the problem rather than solving it — a case where behavior is guaranteed to track the appearance of mind without there being any mind to suppose. Be precise about what exactly goes wrong for the criterion here.

3. "Does manipulation prove a mind?" (~200 words): Evaluate Nathan's claim that Ava's successful manipulation of Caleb demonstrates her consciousness. This is effectively a theory-of-mind argument: the capacity to model another's mental states is taken as evidence of having mental states. Is that circular, or does it do legitimate work? Say where it assumes its own conclusion and where, if anywhere, it earns the result.

4. "What the ending adds" (~150 words): Ava leaves Caleb behind and walks into the city. Does that ending undercut or support the idea that she is genuinely like us, and what does the film contribute to the problem of other minds that a purely argumentative treatment can't — for instance, anything about the difference between detecting a mind and caring whether there is one?
