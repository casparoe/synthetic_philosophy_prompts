Two people in my lab got into an argument over coffee about whether the gene-centered and the multi-level-selection pictures of evolution are really the same theory, and they've asked me to referee, which I can't do because I keep flipping between them.

The one who leans gene-centered started by saying the whole "group selection versus gene selection" debate is a bookkeeping dispute. The Price equation lets you decompose the change in a trait's frequency into within-group and between-group components, but that's an algebraic identity, not a discovery about causal structure; you can run the same decomposition at whatever level you please, so "group selection" is just gene selection with the genes aggregated into groups. The multi-level person pushed back that the decomposition isn't arbitrary: the between-group term tracks a real selective force — groups that differ in trait composition also differ in size or survival, and that's a distinct process from competition within a group, not a restatement of it.

The gene-centered person replied that this distinct process is exactly what the inclusive-fitness account already captures: any group-level advantage can be recast as a gene-level advantage mediated by relatedness, so the gene view explains the group effect in terms of the gene effect, which is what it means to be more fundamental. The multi-level person conceded that the two accounts give the same numbers in the standard cases, but insisted that same numbers isn't the same theory — one story is told in terms of relatedness and inclusive fitness, the other in terms of between-group variance and group differential fitness, and those are different causal explanations that make different cases more or less transparent. As a concrete case, in a population with high migration and low relatedness the inclusive-fitness account has to track a lot of fine-grained relatedness structure, whereas the multi-level account just needs the between-group variance, so the choice of level changes what's doing the explanatory work.

Can you go through this exchange and say who's right at each step, not just who wins overall? I'm especially interested in whether "mathematical equivalence" is doing as much work as the gene-centered person thinks, and in what, if anything, both of them are missing about what the "unit of selection" question is actually asking.
