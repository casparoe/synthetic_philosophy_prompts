I've been working on a project comparing the personal careers of nineteenth-century political philosophers with the theories they published, and the case that keeps coming up is John Stuart Mill. The facts I want to take as fixed: Mill entered the East India Company in 1818 at the age of seventeen, spent his entire working life in its India Office, rose to head the Examiner's office by 1856, and retired in 1859 after the Government of India Act of 1858 liquidated the Company and transferred authority over India to the Crown. The episode I want analyzed through the harm principle is precisely that 1859 transition: the same year Mill retired from the administration of India, he published On Liberty and an essay, "A Few Words on Non-Intervention," arguing that non-intervention is the default rule in international affairs, with exceptions reserved for cases involving peoples he regarded as not yet capable of self-rule. I want a disciplined two-way case analysis, not a summary of his Indian writings and not a bibliography -- I need the theory and the episode put in contact with each other and told where the contact fails.

First, state the harm principle as Mill actually formulated it in On Liberty, with its commitments made explicit: that it is a limit on the legitimate coercion of individuals by the public; that the sole end justifying such coercion is the prevention of harm to others; that self-regarding conduct is beyond the public's authority; and that the principle is framed in the singular, as a rule about one person's liberty against the rest. Then map the 1859 episode onto that framework carefully, and say what the framework says. The mapping needs to be explicit about who the agent of coercion is, what the relevant action is, what counts as harm, and who is harmed -- and I want you to confront, rather than smooth over, the following tensions. One: the harm principle is an individualist instrument, yet the object of the 1859 transition is the self-governance of an entire people; does the principle have any purchase on collective political status at all, or does it simply go silent where colonialism operates? Two: Mill's own exception for peoples he deemed not ready for self-government -- the proviso he makes in Considerations on Representative Government -- is the actual doctrinal ground on which continued British rule in India was defended; is that proviso a coherent extension of the harm principle's logic (a people cannot be harmed by being governed until it can be harmed by governing itself), or a special pleading that, once admitted, allows the principle to be suspended for as many populations as the administrator pleases to classify? Three: Mill's official role was that of a drafter of despatches, subordinate to the Examiner and to the Board of Control, so the coercion he helped administer was not his in any personal sense; does the harm principle, which is addressed to the public's authority over the individual, have anything to say to a functionary's complicity, or does it only reach the institution? Four: what does the timing show -- does retiring from the administration in the same year as On Liberty look like a philosopher finally aligning his life with his doctrine, or like a man stepping down from a post that had become untenable while leaving the doctrine untouched? And what does "A Few Words on Non-Intervention" add: is the 1859 essay a defense of the status quo he was leaving, or evidence that he was quietly repositioning himself as a critic of it?

Close by saying what the episode shows about the theory itself: is 1859 a confirmation of the harm principle, an anomaly it cannot absorb, or a case it never anticipated -- and does any rival model of the episode (paternalism, the civilizing mission, the economics of empire) do a better job of explaining how it went down than Mill's own principle does? Be explicit wherever the theory is silent instead of forcing it to speak, and keep the whole analysis to roughly 1,000 to 1,500 words with clear sectioning. End your response with a single line beginning "Verdict:" followed by exactly one of the three labels INCONSISTENCY (the episode reveals a genuine tension in Mill's thought that the harm principle, as he stated it, cannot resolve), ACCOMMODATION (the principle's own terms, including its exceptions, adequately cover what Mill did and defended), or OUT_OF_SCOPE (the episode concerns a kind of authority the harm principle was never designed to govern, so it neither confirms nor refutes the principle), and then one sentence explaining why you chose that label.
