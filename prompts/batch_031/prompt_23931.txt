I'm assembling a case library on the dollar auction — the game Martin Shubik is credited with popularizing, in which a dollar prize is bid on and the runner-up must also pay their final bid and receives nothing — and I want to extract the principle behind my gut verdicts about when raising is rational. I don't want a history of the literature or a reading list; work from the setup and my verdicts below.

The rules, stated once and used throughout: the prize is exactly $1.00; a player who has not yet bid may open at 5 cents; a player who has bid may either pass or raise by exactly 5 cents; when a player passes, the auction ends, the highest bidder pays their bid and takes the prize, the other player pays their bid and takes nothing, and a player who never bid pays nothing.

My verdicts, in the order I arrived at them:

1. You have not bid; the highest bid on the table is 5 cents. Bid 10 cents. Easy yes: you are risking 10 cents for a prize worth 100, and if you are outbid your loss is capped at 10.
2. You are at 5; your opponent bids 10. Raise to 15. Yes, and I want to be explicit about the reasoning I am using: the 5 cents you have already committed is gone; the live choice is between paying 15 and getting $1 (net +85) and passing and paying 5 (net −5). The idea that you would be "getting your 5 back" is not part of the calculation.
3. You are at 50; your opponent bids 55. Raise to 60. Yes, with exactly the same confidence as in (2). The sunk amount has grown tenfold, but my confidence has not shrunk at all, and I think any account that makes confidence shrink here is importing a sunk-cost motive I do not have.
4. You are at 95; your opponent bids 100. Pass. I think raising to 105 is irrational here, even though the same two-line calculation as in (2) and (3) comes out in favor of raising: paying 105 and getting $1 (net −5) beats passing and paying 95 (net −95). I cannot locate the line in the arithmetic, but I feel there is one at the prize's face value: past $1.00 the activity stops being buying and starts being bleeding, and a rational agent should exit.
5. The same numbers as (4) — you are at 95, your opponent at 100 — except your opponent is a mechanical device publicly programmed as follows: if outbid, it raises exactly one more time and then never bids again. Now I raise — indeed I would keep bidding until I am on top — and I am happy to end up paying $1.15 for a $1.00 prize, because the alternative is paying $95 for nothing.
6. You are at 150; your opponent bids 155. Pass. I think continuing to raise "because the marginal arithmetic still favors it" is an error at this point, even though I cannot point to a line in the arithmetic that 150 crosses and 55 did not. I grant that (6) means I raised past the line I drew in (4) at some earlier moment; I want the principle to say which of those moments was the mistake, not to pretend the inconsistency away.
7. A descriptive claim about real auctions: when this game is run with actual people, I think most of the escalation past face value is precisely the motive I excluded in (2) — bidders keep raising to get their money back, save face, or because they have come this far — and if those motives were removed, the auction would end much earlier, around $1.00 to $1.10, rather than at $2.00 or $3.00.
8. Stated plainly, this is where I am stuck: (7) appears to contradict (2)–(3). If sunk-cost and face motives are the only irrational motives, then a bidder stripped of them would follow the two-line forward-looking calculation at every step — and that calculation, as I have been doing it, never stops, because at every bid level, raising and hoping the opponent passes beats passing and paying your bid. So a fully rational auction, on my own reasoning, spirals exactly like the pathological one, and (7)'s prediction that a rational auction ends early has no support. I cannot hold (2), (3), (4), (6), and (7) together, and I suspect the error is in how I have been doing the two-line calculation rather than in any single verdict.
