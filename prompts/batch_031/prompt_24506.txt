I'm a philosophy professor, and I'm putting together a problem set on the Boltzmann-brain problem for an advanced seminar in the philosophy of cosmology. A graduate student came to office hours with an argument he had constructed for himself, and he is convinced that it shows the "Boltzmann-fluctuation" cosmology is not merely unattractive but self-refuting. I find the argument genuinely difficult to assess: each individual step looks plausible, and yet the conclusion is one I cannot accept, and I cannot name the step at which it goes wrong. I would be grateful for a careful diagnosis.

Here is the argument as he stated it. (1) If the universe is an enormous or infinite thermal system that has had time to fluctuate, then spontaneously arising "Boltzmann brains" — disembodied observers that fluctuate into existence carrying memories of an ordinary past — vastly outnumber ordinary observers who evolved in a low-entropy early universe. (2) If Boltzmann brains vastly outnumber ordinary observers, then an observer selected at random from the total ensemble is, with overwhelming probability, a Boltzmann brain. (3) For epistemic purposes I am indistinguishable from such a randomly selected observer: I have no special reason to think of myself as anything other than a typical member of the ensemble. (4) Therefore I am, with overwhelming probability, a Boltzmann brain. (5) A Boltzmann brain's memories and beliefs are the product of a random fluctuation rather than of reliable cognitive processes tracking the world, so a Boltzmann brain's beliefs are, with overwhelming probability, unreliable. (6) Therefore, with overwhelming probability, my own beliefs — including the belief that the universe is an enormous thermal system — are unreliable. (7) I should not rationally accept a conclusion whose supporting reasoning is, with overwhelming probability, unreliable. (8) Therefore I should not rationally accept the Boltzmann-fluctuation cosmology. (9) Therefore the Boltzmann-fluctuation cosmology is false, or at the very least no one can rationally believe it.

I am prepared to grant him (1), (5), and (7), and I am strongly inclined to grant (2), because the underlying counting result seems robust. What I cannot resolve is whether the chain from (2) through (4) is doing legitimate work, and whether (8) really licenses the strong form of (9). Every step looks fine in isolation, and I cannot name the one at which something slips. Could you tell me which premise or inference to give up, why it looked fine, and what I lose by giving it up? If I had to keep one claim above all others, it would be (2); and I would much rather not be stuck with (9) if there is a way to avoid it.
