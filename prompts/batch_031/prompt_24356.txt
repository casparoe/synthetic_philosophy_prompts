I'm taking a graduate decision-theory course, and I've been going back and forth with a friend who does a lot of work in mechanism design about the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution. I'd like an independent audit before I take his word for it. His pitch, condensed, was this: "The Kalai–Smorodinsky solution is the fair alternative to the Nash bargaining solution. Nash says maximize the product of the two players' gains and, via independence of irrelevant alternatives, ignore any options that end up not being chosen. Kalai and Smorodinsky think the whole menu of available options should matter, so they drop that independence principle and replace it with a monotonicity principle — roughly, if one player's set of achievable options improves, that player should walk away with at least as much as before. You compute it geometrically: take each player's best-possible outcome, the ideal point, draw a straight line from the disagreement point to that ideal point, and the solution is where the line meets the Pareto frontier. The result is a proportionally fair split — each player gets an equal share of the surplus above the disagreement point — which is why it is sometimes called the proportional-fairness solution. The main criticism is that it is less rational than Nash, because abandoning independence of irrelevant alternatives lets the outcome shift just because an irrelevant option was added or removed, which seems arbitrary."
