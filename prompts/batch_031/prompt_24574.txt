I'm preparing a seminar that pairs Blaise Pascal's account of political legitimacy with Victor Hugo's Les Misérables, and I'd like a close reading of the novel through the lens of Pascal's argument about force and right. Pascal's position, as I understand it, is deflationary: in the Pensées he argues that "justice without might is helpless; might without justice is tyrannical," and that because we cannot independently establish what is just, we end up treating whatever is strong as though it were just. He traces this to what he calls the "reason of effects" — a circular grounding in which the law is obeyed because it is strong and is treated as just because it is obeyed — and he insists that legitimate law depends on hiding its own illegitimate founding: "law was once introduced without reason, and has become reasonable. We must make it regarded as authoritative, eternal, and conceal its origin, if we do not wish that it should soon come to an end." He even locates the seed of it all in a child's first claim of possession — "this dog is mine" — which he calls "the beginning and the image of the usurpation of all the earth," and he mocks the sheer spatial arbitrariness of custom, noting that "three degrees of latitude reverse all jurisprudence."

Read Les Misérables against that specific argument, and I want you to say what the novel shows, dramatizes, and complicates about it. In particular: does Javert function as a living embodiment of the "reason of effects" — a man who treats the strength of the law as its justification — and does his suicide dramatize the collapse of "might without justice"? Does Valjean's reinvention as the respectable M. Madeleine stage Pascal's point that a usurped origin must be concealed for authority to persist, or does it suggest the opposite, that a concealed origin can be redeemed rather than merely hidden? And how does the Thénardiers' claim on Cosette — a debt, a contract, a kind of ownership — instantiate the "mine, thine" logic Pascal reduces to a child pointing at a dog? I'd also like you to take seriously the one place where the novel most directly resists Pascal: the Bishop, and later Valjean himself, exercise a moral authority that is just but not strong, and that authority nonetheless transforms lives, which seems to contradict Pascal's claim that "justice without might is helpless." Does Hugo's novel vindicate Pascal's bleak, self-grounding picture, or does it smuggle in an independent ground of justice that Pascal's argument was designed to deny?

Please be faithful in both directions: don't flatten the novel into a mere illustration of Pascal, and don't bend Pascal to fit the plot. Where the reading strains, say so. And close by saying what the novel adds to the question of the relationship between force and right that Pascal's argument, taken on its own, doesn't.
