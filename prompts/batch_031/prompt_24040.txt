I'm a PhD student in philosophy of math, and I'm making a handout for a foundations seminar I'm co-teaching next month. My co-teacher keeps insisting that Russell's paradox is just a matter of not being careful enough with the words, so I want the handout to make clear where the real forks are. The starting point I want everyone to accept: if you have a single-sorted theory with unrestricted comprehension, the set of all sets that aren't members of themselves gives you a genuine contradiction — it's a member of itself iff it isn't. From there, I want a decision tree that takes a student to a named foundational position. Each branch should be a question a student could answer on the spot without already knowing which leaf it leads to — the kind of question like "do you want the Russell collection to exist as something, even if not as a set?" or "do you want to keep one sort of object, or are you willing to have types?" — and each leaf should be a named position.

For leaves, I definately want ZF (ZFC can be the same leaf), Quine's NF, Russell's own type-theoretic move, a class theory like NBG or MK, and a paraconsistent or dialetheic option. You can add one or two more if you think there are genuinely distinct positions I'm missing — but if anything you add is really just a variant of one of the required leaves, say so rather than giving it its own leaf.

Two things I really care about. First, flag any pair of leaves your tree can't actually seperate with the questions you're using — I'd rather know that NF and type theory collapse under my allowed questions than have the tree pretend they're distinct. Second, note any combination of answers that lands on no leaf at all, and say in a sentence whether nobody's there because the position is incoherent, just unattractive, or unexplored. Keep the whole thing to about six to eight leaves and four to five branch questions, so it fits on a page with a little room for annotation.
