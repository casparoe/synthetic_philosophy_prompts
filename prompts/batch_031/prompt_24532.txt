I'm reading about the precautionary principle in the context of environmental policy debates, and I keep noticing a pattern in the arguments that I don't have a word for.

Here's what I mean. A group wants to ban a pesticide because there's some evidence it might harm pollinators, and they invoke the precautionary principle. The other group responds that conventional pesticide use is also risky — it kills non-target species, it contributes to resistance, and so on — so the precautionary principle cuts both ways. The first group then responds that the risks of conventional use are "known and quantified" while the risks of the new pesticide are "unknown and potentially worse," so the precautionary principle still favors the ban. I see the same structure in the nuclear-power versus fossil-fuels debate, and in the GM-crops versus conventional-agriculture debate.

The pattern I'm noticing is that the first group is using a different epistemic standard for the two sets of risks: they're demanding that the new option's risks be proven to be safe before it's allowed, but they're accepting that the status quo's risks are known and therefore acceptable. The asymmetry is justified by the type of risk (known versus unknown) rather than the magnitude of risk. And I notice that the status quo is not actually "inaction" — it's a specific policy choice with its own risks, which the first group acknowledges but then discounts.

Is there an established concept for this? If so, which one fits best, and how does it differ from the closest concepts I can think of — the idea that inaction is risk-free, the tendency to favor the status quo, and a plain double standard? If not, what would you call it, and how would you define it in a way that captures the specific structure of the argument? I'm not satisfied with a bare name; I want to know what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges are.
