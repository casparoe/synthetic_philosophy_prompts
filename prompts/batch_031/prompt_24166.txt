Aristotle in the Politics draws a sharp line between the natural management of a household (oikonomia) and the pursuit of wealth for its own sake (chrematistics), and he holds that lending at interest is the form of wealth-getting most opposed to nature, because money is barren and was meant to be a means of exchange rather than to increase in quantity. Suppose he confronted the modern fiat monetary system: money with no commodity backing, its value set by legal decree and central-bank policy, deliberately created and retired to steer inflation, and a financial order in which interest-bearing credit is the norm and the engine of growth. Working only from his actual arguments, what would he make of it? I want a considered verdict on three points: whether fiat money is a corruption of exchange or a legitimate new instrument; whether his barrenness objection to interest survives once money is a claim on future production rather than a store of metal; and whether his own commitments — the need for a stable measure of value, and the priority of use-value over exchange-value — force a specific conclusion. Where his texts are silent, say so and reason by analogy rather than inventing a doctrine.
