I am writing a chapter for a short book on the design of collective decision-making, and I keep returning to a dispute I had with a colleague that I have not managed to settle. The dispute concerns the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem — the result, proved independently in the 1970s, that with at least three alternatives and unrestricted ordinal preferences, any social choice function that is onto and satisfies the Pareto condition can only be strategy-proof if it is a dictatorship. My colleague, a social choice theorist, thinks the theorem is far less threatening to the project of designing non-dictatorial, incentive-compatible mechanisms than its popular reputation suggests, and he is always quick to point to the ways a real problem can fall outside the theorem's scope: restricting the domain to single-peaked preferences and using the median, randomizing the outcome, or accepting a rule that is not onto. The other person in the dispute, a political theorist, thinks that every one of these "escapes" surrenders something of comparable importance — expressive power over the preference space, the guarantee of a decisive outcome, or the ability to rank every possible outcome — and that the theorem's real lesson is that the properties the designer wants are in genuine structural tension, not that one can simply engineer around it.

Please write a dialogue between these two interlocutors, set in the context of a concrete problem: a faculty committee that must choose one of three candidates for a chair position and wants a nomination rule that no member can profitably manipulate. Both speakers should be fully competent and should press the strongest version of their case; neither may caricature the other. The theorist should be able to explain precisely why his escapes are legitimate under the theorem's exact hypotheses, and the theorist's interlocutor should be able to answer whether the cost of those escapes is a defect of the theorem or a genuine feature of the design problem. The dialogue should contain real back-and-forth: each speaker should respond to the specific point just made, and at least once the two should test one of the escapes against the three-candidate committee example and agree about what it does and disagree about what it costs. End the dialogue with the two having made a genuine concession or having identified the exact premise on which their disagreement turns, rather than with a stalemate in which neither has moved. Aim for something in the range of ten to fourteen turns, with no speaker monologuing for more than two or three sentences at a time.
