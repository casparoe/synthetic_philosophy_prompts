I'm working through a long-running dispute in the philosophy of science and language about natural kinds, and I keep feeling like the two camps are answering slightly different questions without realizing it. On one side there is a view, now widely accepted, that a natural kind term like "water" or "gold" gets its reference fixed by a causal-historical chain: the term is fixed in an original encounter with a sample and then passed along through a speaker–hearer community, so that ordinary users need not be able to state any essence or necessary property in order to use the term correctly. The deep content, on this view, is that what makes the referend the referend is some underlying natural property, even if nobody in the chain can articulate it. On the other side there is a persistent worry that this picture is quietly circular: the supposed underlying property is supposed to be *that which the term refers to*, but the theory gives you no independent way of identifying it, so it can't tell you where the chain of reference stops — for instance, it can't say what would count as a world in which the word "water" picks out something else. My working hypothesis is that this isn't a single argument with a flaw but two genuinely different questions braided together under one name — one about how reference actually gets fixed in our linguistic practice, and one about whether the world is structured in ways that make some properties the "right" candidates to be referred to — and that the stalemate comes from treating them as one.

Could you diagnose, as carefully as you can, what kind of disagreement this really is? I want you to take seriously the circularity objection on one side and the "carve nature at its joints" / projectibility idea on the other, and say whether they are (a) a verbal disagreement about what "natural kind" even means, (b) a substantive but resolvable disagreement about which property is doing the referring work, or (c) a case where the two positions are actually answering different questions — one a question about semantics, one a question about the structure of the world — and the impasse is a sign the question is ill-posed rather than merely hard. Please engage with what each side actually says (the causal-historical mechanism, the requirement of an underlying essence, the appeal to projectibility or to the world's own structure) rather than restating the labels. And then, given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as genuine progress — a distinction that separates the questions, a formal or empirical result, a change of question — or argue honestly that no such progress should be expected and why.

Please respond in the style of an expert who has thought about this for years and is willing to say "this is a bad question" if it is. Think very carefully before answering, and do not give me a survey of the literature; give me the structure of the disagreement and a reasoned verdict on whether it is resolvable.
