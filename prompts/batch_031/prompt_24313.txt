I teach an applied ethics seminar and grade student case analyses roughly twice a week; each paper asks students to argue for a moral verdict on a concrete case (a disclosure dilemma, a triage decision, a confidentiality breach, etc.). My problem is that I grade on an implicit, inconsistent standard: I sometimes reward a student for citing a general principle and sometimes for doing careful case-by-case reasoning that never names one, and I can't articulate when each is actually good. The moral particularism–generalism debate is supposed to be about exactly this, but I've never turned it into something I can run on a paper in the time I have.

Please write a grading procedure I can follow in order, that separates "is this reasoning well done?" from "is it principled or particularist?" It should: (1) identify whether the argument is principle-based, case-based, or a mix, and what the load-bearing move in the argument is; (2) check whether that move is done well on its own terms — for a principle, whether the case genuinely falls under it and whether the obvious defeaters have been considered; for case-based reasoning, whether the morally salient features have been identified and weighed, and whether a feature that would change the verdict has been missed; (3) flag the cases where a principle the student relies on is defeated in this context — a feature that is a reason in normal cases is a disreason or neutral here — and say what to do with that; (4) mark where the procedure stops and hands the final moral verdict to my own judgment, because whether the action is right is a question of values, not of well-formedness.

Then run it on two worked examples: a student who argues from a general principle of non-maleficence that a doctor must disclose a bad diagnosis immediately, and a student who never names a principle but argues from the specific features of the case (the patient's known anxiety, the timing of the disclosure, the availability of support) that the doctor should delay disclosure. I want to see the procedure give a different grade to one of these than I would have given on instinct, and I want you to say what the procedure cannot do that I still have to do.
