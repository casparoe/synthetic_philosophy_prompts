I run a philosophy of science seminar — 14 or 15 students, mostly junior and senior undergraduates, one 90-minute session, a standard classroom with a whiteboard and nothing else I can count on. I want a structured in-class activity, not a discussion, that makes them feel the force of a specific idea in their own experience rather than have me explain it.

The idea has two layers. The first is the contrast between grouping things by superficial resemblance and grouping them by underlying causal or predictive structure — the difference between arbitrary categories and the "natural kinds," the categories that track real structure in the world, the ones people mean when they say a good classification "carves nature at its joints." The second, sharper layer is the tension between the thought that there is one objectively correct deep grouping (the one that tracks the real causal structure) and the thought that which grouping is "the right one" can depend on what inquiry you are running — that the same set of things can be a natural kind for one purpose and not for another. I want them to run into that second tension themselves, inside the activity, not hear it from me afterwards.

Please design:

1. A concrete, runnable game with specific rules, materials (keep it to paper cards, a whiteboard, and markers — no technology, no real money, and no preparation that would take me more than an evening), and a payoff or scoring scheme.
2. A setup in which the "obvious" classification — the one a student reaches for first, by the most salient surface feature — does a measurably worse job of predicting some hidden property of the items than a less obvious classification does, so that they hit the superficial-versus-deep problem in their own scores rather than in my explanation.
3. A mechanism that introduces the second layer, so that at least two groups end up with genuinely different "best" classifications and have to sit with the fact that both are defensible — ideally without my having to announce that this is what just happened.
4. A minute-by-minute timeline for the full 90 minutes.
5. A debrief that maps specific, nameable moments in the activity — a particular turn, a particular score, a particular complaint a group will make — onto parts of the philosophical idea.
6. An honest prediction of what will go wrong the first time I run this, and what I should do about it in the moment.
7. A frank note on which feature of the "different purposes, different joints" idea the activity structurally cannot reproduce, and what I will have to supply myself in the debrief to cover it.

The constraint that matters most to me: the idea has to be something that happens to the students — something they do, something they lose points for, something they argue about — not something I tell them at the end. If the whole point could be delivered in a five-minute lecture, the activity has failed.
