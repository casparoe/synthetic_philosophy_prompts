I work in the neighborhood of Fitch's paradox of knowability and have settled on the mainstream reading of it, which I want stress-tested. The setup: let Kp mean that p is known (by someone, at some time), and let the knowability principle be the schema that every truth is possibly known, i.e., for every p, p → ◇Kp. The derivation that this entails that every truth is known, i.e., p → Kp, runs by instantiating the principle with the conjunction p ∧ ¬Kp and showing that no one could know that conjunction: assuming K(p ∧ ¬Kp), closure of knowledge under conjunction gives Kp and K¬Kp, and factivity applied to the second conjunct yields ¬Kp, a contradiction; so K(p ∧ ¬Kp) is impossible, hence ¬◇K(p ∧ ¬Kp), which conflicts with the instantiated knowability principle, so p ∧ ¬Kp is false for every p. The only non-logical ingredients are factivity, conjunction closure, and the ordinary modal principles that theorems are necessary and that necessity of the negation rules out possibility.

The position I hold: the knowability principle is the weak link. The paradox is a genuine reductio of any view — verificationist or anti-realist theories of truth, constructivist mathematics, pragmatic or idealist accounts that identify truth with ideal epistemic attainability — that is committed to the unrestricted principle, and the correct response is to abandon or heavily restrict the principle, not to reject factivity, closure, or the modal logic, all of which are far more secure. The other, non-classical responses (non-normal knowability operators, non-classical logics, restricting the range of propositions quantified over) are either ad hoc or give up more than the principle does.

What would count against this position? I want the evidence and arguments that should move me, sorted by how decisive they would be, including what has arguably already happened in the literature. Concretely: (1) would the derivation still go through under assumptions weaker than the ones I listed — and if some response to the paradox targets an assumption I have not identified as load-bearing, which one is it, and is that target really secure? (2) Is there a formulation of the anti-realist or verificationist commitment that is genuinely weaker than the unrestricted principle — not a restatement of it in different symbols — yet still does the philosophical work those views need, so that the reductio misfires? (3) Do the non-classical and non-normal responses have any genuine explanatory force, or do they all merely repackage the problem? (4) Does the paradox generalize to other modalities or operators (for instance, to future knowability or to belief under different axioms) in a way that shows the pressure on the knowability principle is a special case of something broader, and if so, does that broaden or narrow the lesson? (5) Is the position I hold well insulated by its qualifications — has it been made unfalsifiable — and if so, is that a problem for a view of this kind?

Distinguish throughout what would be decisive from what would merely be awkward, and say which of these a committed defender of the mainstream reading would concede as counting against it and which they would dismiss, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. I would personally find it most persuasive to be shown a case in which the paradox is doing real philosophical work — that is, where the formal result tracks a genuine feature of the anti-realist commitment rather than a mere artifact of formalization — and to learn whether the mainstream position can even recognize such a case as a threat, or whether it is built to absorb it.
