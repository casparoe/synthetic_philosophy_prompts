I work on anomaly detection for a fleet of microservices, and I've been stuck on this for a week. I'm not a philosopher, so I might be talking nonsense, but I built an argument that I now think proves too much and I can't find the step that's doing the work.

The setup: to flag an anomaly I need a model of how the system normally works — the mechanism, i.e. the causal structure that produces its usual behavior. An anomaly is when the behavior stops matching that mechanism. Sounds right. Now the argument:

(1) To detect an anomaly I need a model of the system's normal mechanism — the causal structure that generates its usual behavior.
(2) But for any behavior the system exhibits, I can construct a mechanism that would produce exactly that behavior — just let the mechanism be whatever maps the observed inputs to the observed outputs.
(3) So no observed behavior is inconsistent with "the mechanism is working fine" — I can always absorb the anomaly by redefining the mechanism to include it.
(4) So no observation can ever tell me the mechanism has changed, or that an anomaly has occurred.
(5) So mechanism-based anomaly detection is vacuous — it can't actually detect anything.

I can't accept (5). I do this for a living and it works, so one of the earlier steps has to go. I can't tell wich one, and I don't want to just cut a step on vibes. Which step do you reject, and what does it cost me to reject it?

And when you answer, please keep straight what's actually settled vs. what's still genuinely up for debate among people who work on this. I don't want a contested position handed to me like it's definately the consensus.
