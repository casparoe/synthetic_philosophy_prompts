I'm trying to pin down what I actually think about the difference between knowing how to do something and knowing that something is the case, and I'd like to see whether my reactions to a set of cases hang together or whether I'm just being inconsistent. I'll report my verdicts case by case, mostly by gut, and then I'll ask you for the principle, if any, behind them.

Start with the two I'm confident about, to set a baseline. I can ride a bicycle, but if you pressed me to explain the mechanics of balance I'd give you a muddled, probably wrong account; I know how to ride a bike, obviously. By contrast, I have read a very detailed manual on changing a tire and can recite every step in the right order, but I have never actually changed a tire and I'm confident I would fumble the jack; I do not know how to change a tire — I know that, if I followed the manual, I would change a tire. Those two feel like they should fall out of the same principle, and I'm not yet sure what that principle is.

Now the cases where I'm less sure I'm consistent. I know how to swim, but suppose I'm in a lake with my arms zip-tied behind my back so that I literally cannot swim right now. I still feel that I know how to swim: the zip ties prevent me from swimming, they don't take the knowledge away from me. So for me, knowledge how seems to survive being temporarily blocked from being exercised. I can also recognize my next-door neighbor's face in a fraction of a second, though I could not describe the specific features that let me do it; my gut says I know how to recognize my neighbor, though I'm not sure that's the same kind of thing as knowing how to ride a bike. And I can work through a long-division problem by following the standard algorithm step by step, but I could not explain why the algorithm works, and if you asked me to do the same division in base seven I'd be lost; I'd say I know how to do long division, but only the ordinary base-ten kind, not long division in general. So my knowledge how comes in domain-specific packages, and I don't know how to state the domain.

Here is the set I keep going back and forth on, because the three cases look to me like they should come out the same and I can't see why I'm treating them differently. In each, I can, if I set my mind to it, bring about a mental state on command.

First, imagining: I can, deliberately and repeatedly, bring a vivid image before my mind — say, imagine in detail what it would be like to perceive the world in ultraviolet — and I can stop when I want. I'd say I know how to imagine. There's no ambiguity here; it feels like a straightforward ability, the same register as riding a bike.

Second, believing: here I'm split. On one hand, I cannot, no matter how hard I try, bring myself to believe something I have strong evidence against — I can't just decide that the earth is flat — so "knowing how to believe" feels incoherent to me, as if the "how" were pointing at something that isn't a doing at all. On the other hand, when I have good evidence for a claim but am resisting it, I can bring myself to believe it by attending to the evidence and letting it register; I've done that deliberately, e.g. convincing myself a project will work by re-reading the data. So I hold both a "no, belief isn't something you do" verdict and a "yes, I know how to get myself to believe true things" verdict, and I can't make them consistent.

Third, willing: the same structure. I cannot will myself to want something I have no reason to want — the attempt to will myself into wanting it just doesn't land. But I can will myself to act on a reason I already have, by rehearsing it and committing; I've willed myself to get up early that way. So again a "no" and a "yes" sitting in the same case.

Is there a principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that captures all of these verdicts, or at least most of them? In particular, I'd like to know whether the swimmer-with-zip-ties verdict and the tire-manual verdict can both be kept, and whether the imagine/believe/will trio can be reconciled at all, or whether I have to give up one of the three — and if so, which one, and on what principle.
