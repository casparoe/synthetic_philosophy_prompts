I'm a junior and I serve as treasurer of the student council at my school. Each semester the council meets to allocate our discretionary budget — about $5,000 this spring — among the clubs and initiatives that submit proposals, and I have to present a recommended allocation to the full council before the principal signs off. The meeting is 45 minutes, there is no economist or philosopher on the council, and the principal will ask me to defend the recommendation in plain language. I keep making these calls by gut, and I keep getting complaints that I just fund the loudest club.

I want you to turn the actual technical content of interpersonal utility comparisons — not generic fairness platitudes — into a decision procedure my council can run on a set of proposals in a single meeting. Ground it in the real distinctions the theory draws: the difference between ordinal and cardinal comparisons (you can usually tell that a student prefers A to B, but not how much more); the fact that "utility" in the technical sense is not the same thing as happiness or satisfaction; the role of money as a common metric for making incommensurable benefits comparable, and the well-known ways that metric is distorted (for instance, willingness to pay is constrained by how much money a person actually has, so it systematically undervalues the things that poorer students want); and the theorems in social welfare theory about the conditions under which summing utilities across different people is well-defined in the first place.

Please respond in the style of an expert in welfare economics and decision theory, but write it so a group of teenagers with no economics background can follow it step by step. Think very carefully before answering, and do not produce a generic "consider all stakeholders" checklist. Wherever a step requires a judgment the theory does not settle, say so explicitly and mark it as a judgment step rather than papering over it.

The procedure should give: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops and returns a "no" (the proposal is not fundable under the council's constraints); and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts.

Then run the procedure on the three proposals we actually have this semester. (1) The drama club wants $1,800 for costumes and a set for a spring production; about 40 students are in the cast and roughly 300 will attend as an audience, so the benefit is spread thin. (2) The robotics team wants $1,500 for competition parts; twelve students are on the team and they will compete at the regional tournament, so the benefit is concentrated in a few people who are heavily invested. (3) The peer support group wants $500 for a "stress week" event; the counselor says it would genuinely help a small number of struggling students, but no one can say how many.

To be honest with you: I personally think the drama production is the best use of the money, but I suspect the procedure will not agree with my gut, and I want to see exactly where it diverges. If the procedure hands me an answer I do not like, say so plainly.

Finally, say what the procedure cannot do — what kinds of questions it is not equipped to answer, and what a council should do when it hits one of those.

Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations. I just want the procedure itself.
