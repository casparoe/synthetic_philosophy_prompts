I'm writing a piece for a general-audience science and ideas outlet on the centipede game, and I keep getting stuck on one particular conceptual point that I think is often glossed over. I'd like you to explain it to me precisely.

The standard backward-induction argument for a finitely repeated centipede game concludes that a rational player should defect at the very first node: the last player defects, so the second-to-last anticipates this and defects, and the reasoning unwinds all the way to the start. The point I want you to get at is that this prediction is belief-dependent in a way that a dominant-strategy prediction — say, mutual defection in the Prisoner's Dilemma — is not, and that this makes the "defect immediately" conclusion fragile rather than robust.

Please structure your answer in three sections, in this order, each roughly the length indicated:

1. "The dependence chain" (about 200 words): explain how, in the centipede game, each player's optimal move at their decision node depends on their belief about what the other player will do at the next node, and how this iterative dependence is fundamentally different from a dominant strategy, which is optimal no matter what the other player does.

2. "Why the prediction is fragile" (about 200 words): explain why that dependence means a small break in the chain — a small doubt that the other player is fully rational, or a small perturbation in the payoffs near the end of the game — can change the prediction, even though the conclusion under perfect rationality is so stark.

3. "Entailment or procedure?" (about 150 words): say what this fragility implies for how we should understand backward induction — as a claim about what rationality strictly entails, or as a reasoning procedure whose output is only as reliable as the beliefs it starts from.
