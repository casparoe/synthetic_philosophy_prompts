I've been preparing a seminar on the revealed preference approach to rationality, and I keep landing on a set of verdicts about what observed choice actually reveals that I cannot quite unify. Let me lay them out and ask you to find the principle behind them, if there is one.

The background, briefly: on this approach we do not assume we can read an agent's utility function off her introspection. Instead we infer what she values from what she chooses, given the options and the budget she actually faced, and we call a choice pattern rational if it can be represented as the maximization of some utility function over the available bundles. The question I am trying to pin down is not just when a pattern is representable, but what, if anything, the representability is doing — what it reveals about the agent, and what the utility function that does the representing really is.

Here are my gut verdicts on a range of cases.

1. A shopper who, on every occasion when both apples and oranges are available and she can afford both, buys the apples. I take this to reveal a genuine preference for apples over oranges.

2. The same shopper buys oranges in midwinter, when apples are out of season and oranges are the only fruit on the shelf. I do not think this reveals any preference for oranges over apples; the set of available options changed, so the choice tells me nothing about how she ranks the two.

3. A diner who, given a menu of A, B, and C, picks A when A and B are both offered, picks B when B and C are both offered, and picks C when C and A are both offered, with nothing else about her circumstances changing. I take this to reveal no stable preference at all: there is no single ranking of the three with which all three choices are compatible.

4. A consumer who, faced with two physically identical cans of soup at different prices, always buys the cheaper one. I do not think this reveals a preference for "the cheaper can" as such; I think it reveals a preference for the soup together with whatever money is left over, and the price difference is just what makes one bundle better than the other.

5. A household that buys less butter as its income rises and more margarine in return. I take this to be perfectly consistent with stable preferences — it reflects the fact that butter is an inferior good for them, not any incoherence in their ranking of bundles.

6. Here is the pair I keep coming back to. On the one hand, when an agent's entire observed choice history is consistent in the strong sense — no choice she makes, directly or through a chain of intermediate choices, is ever reversed — I am inclined to say this reveals that the agent has a real, stable utility: a persistent psychological state that her choices express. On the other hand, the utility function that represents a consistent choice pattern is fixed only up to any monotonic transformation; I can rescale and reshape it and the choices it represents do not change. So I am also inclined to say that the utility function is not a literal thing with determinate magnitudes, but a mathematical representation of the ordering of her choices.

I cannot see how to hold both halves of 6 at once. If the utility is a real psychological state, it seems strange that its values are arbitrary up to monotonic transformation; if it is only a representation, then what the consistency of choice reveals is a formal property of the choice pattern, not the existence of a real preference structure in the agent.

So: is there a single principle that captures verdicts 1 through 6, or a small set of candidates that come close? For each candidate, state it precisely; say which of my cases motivates each clause of it; and give its verdicts on cases I have not raised — for instance, a person who consistently takes the smaller reward now over the larger reward later; a person whose choices are fully consistent only because she follows a fixed habit rather than any ranking of the options; and whether anything at all can be said, on this basis, about comparing one person's utility to another's. And finally, be candid about which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and whether I should drop one half of 6 — and if so, which one.
