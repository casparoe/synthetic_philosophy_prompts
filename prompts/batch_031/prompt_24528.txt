I'm writing up my views on insect sentience for a seminar in animal ethics and I'd like a consistency check before I present, because I have a nagging feeling that somthing in here doesn't quite fit and I can't pin down what. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) I think the probability that a typical insect — say a honeybee or a housefly — is sentient, i.e. has some positive or negative phenomenal experience, is low but non-zero. I'd put it somewhere around 1–5%. Not zero, given the behavioral and neural evidence (nociception, associative learning, what look like learned preferences), but low, given the enormous gap in neural architecture relative to even simple vertebrates.

(2) I think moral weight under uncertainty should be computed by plain expected value: the moral weight of an action on a population of uncertain sentience is P(sentience) times the expected welfare impact, summed over individuals. No special precautionary multiplier, no "better safe than sorry" boost.

(3) I think the scale argument is basically decisive. The number of insects alive at any moment is astronomically large — I've seen estimates running from 10^15 up to 10^18 — so even at a 1% probability of sentience the aggregate expected welfare of insects swamps most human welfare issues. In the aggregate, insect welfare should dominate our moral priorities.

(4) In practice, though, I don't think we should change how we treat insects. I don't think it's irrational to step on ants, or to use broad-spectrum insecticides on a cash crop, or to ignore the suffering of the bees in a hive. The reason isn't that insects don't matter; it's that we have no reliable way to tell wich interventions on insects help versus harm them, so the practical uncertainty swamps the expected value.

(5) I'm aware that I'm holding insects to a much stricter evidentiary standard than I hold fish. I accept that fish are sentient on the strength of fairly modest behavioral evidence, but I demand a lot more before I'll credit insects. I think that's the right call, because insects are so much less like us that the base rate of "this looks like sentience but isn't" is much higher.

And on a specific case: on the use of broad-spectrum insecticides to protect a cash crop, I think it's clearly permissible. Even granting a 1% sentience probability, the expected welfare cost is swamped by (a) the practical uncertainty about whether those insects would have been "doing well" anyway, and (b) the human benefit of food security.

Can you audit this set for me? I want to know which commitments actually pull against each other, which pairs only look like they do and dissolve on inspection, and, for each real tension, what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs. Please order the real tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and tell me which single change would clean up the most. And don't answer by telling me which commitment is "least important to me" — I want the structural reasons, not a ranking of how much I care.
