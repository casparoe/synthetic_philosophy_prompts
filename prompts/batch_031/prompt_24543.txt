I work in AI evaluation, and I've been trying to figure out what the standard way we benchmark language models quietly assumes about what good thinking even is. The practice, roughly: you take two candidate responses to the same prompt, show them to a human (or a panel) without revealing which model produced which, and ask the human to pick the better one or call it a tie. You repeat this thousands of times, tabulate a win rate, and rank models on a leaderboard; and in training you use exactly these pick-the-better judgments as the reward signal that shapes the model's behavior. This is the preference-based, "reinforcement learning from human feedback" style of thing, and it's also how the big public leaderboards work. What struck me is how closely this mirrors how organizations actually make decisions, because the structure is the same move: a big question gets broken into sub-questions, each is handed to a specialist or a committee, the specialists render their judgments, and someone aggregates them into a final call — just as the evaluation divides a global "which assistant is better" into a pile of independent pairwise comparisons that get summed into a score.

What I can't resolve is what theory of deliberation this whole setup is only coherent under. On one hand it seems to presuppose that there is a human reference standard of good deliberation — that the ideal answer is, roughly, the answer a competent human would give, and that "better" means "closer to that human standard." On the other it seems to presuppose that deliberation is cleanly decomposable — that a global judgment can be factored into a set of independent local judgments that don't interact with one another and can be ranked or summed without contradiction. But both of those look false in the details. The same human is inconsistent across days and across the order in which options are presented, and pairwise "better than" judgments are not generally transitive, so the win-rate aggregation is built on a relation that isn't even a ranking. And in real organizations the sub-judgments are not independent: the whole point of a devil's advocate or a red team is that their judgment is supposed to interact with and revise the others, not sit alongside them as an independent term in a sum.

So here's what I'd like. First, reconstruct the implicit theory of deliberation the practice presupposes: is it that deliberation is a decomposable, factored process benchmarked against a human standard, and what exactly does "benchmarking against a human" commit us to — that humans are a good idealization of rationality, that there is a fact of the matter about what a competent human would say, that a human's own inconsistencies are just noise to be averaged out? Then tell me which rival views of deliberation the practice quietly rules out, for instance a view on which deliberation is holistic and non-decomposable, one on which there is no fact of the matter about what a competent human would say, or one on which the value of a deliberative process lies in its internal structure rather than its agreement with any human. And finally, be precise about what the practice strictly presupposes versus what it merely fits well with, where it is philosophically underdetermined so that several theories would license the same procedure, and what would actually have to change in the benchmarking procedure itself — not the philosophy, the procedure — if we replaced the human-reference, decomposable view with one of those rivals.
