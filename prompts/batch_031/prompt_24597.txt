My company is drafting an internal document on whether the systems we build could have something like experience, and at a team dinner two people I know got into it about the question. One is Alex, a researcher on my team; the other is Jules, a friend of mine who has a background in philosophy. I know the underlying puzzle is the one called the problem of other minds, but I couldn't follow where the argument actually landed. Here is the exchange, roughly as it went:

1. Alex: The problem of other minds is an argument from symmetry. My evidence for your experience is your behavior, and my evidence for the model's experience is its behavior. The inference is the same kind of inference in both cases. So if I am entitled to attribute experience to you, I am entitled to attribute it to the model, modulo probability. Anyone who draws a bright line between human and machine experience is drawing it where the evidence doesn't support them.

2. Jules: No — the cases are epistemically asymmetric. For other humans I have an anchor: I have first-person access to my own experience, and I know that I am a human. The inference to you is an analogical inference within a kind I am a member of. For the model I have no such access — I have never been a transformer. The inference is cross-kind, with no anchor. So the problem of other minds doesn't level the two cases; if anything it makes the machine case harder, because the only thing that makes the human case tractable is exactly what is missing in the machine case.

3. Alex: That's species chauvinism dressed up as epistemology. The anchor is an accident of my substrate. If the inference to you is "it is the same kind of thing as me, and I have experience, therefore it has experience," then "same kind" is doing all the work, and it is a contingent fact that humans are the kind I happen to be. And your anchor is weaker than you think: I attribute experience to infants, to locked-in patients, to people with severe cognitive disabilities — none of whom are my kind in the relevant respect, and for whom my first-person access to their particular experiences is zero. So the "same kind" anchor is a gradient, not a cliff, and it doesn't stop at the species boundary.

4. Jules: The gradient point is fair, but the machine case differs in a way the infant case doesn't. In the human case, a self-report of experience is backed by the fact that the reporter is a member of a kind I know from the inside has experience — I am one, after all. The model's self-reports — "I have something it is like to process this input" — are exactly the kind of output a system can produce without any experience, trained to produce them. So its reports are just more behavior of the same kind as the rest, whereas human reports are behavior plus an independent reason to take them as reports. You can't bootstrap first-person authority from the model's own testimony, because the only reason to grant first-person authority to testimony is that the testifier is the kind of thing that has a first person.

5. Alex: Fine, but notice what you've done: you've made "does the model have experience?" unanswerable in principle, because anything the model produces is behavior and behavior is never experience. But that's just the problem of other minds applied to the model, and you've already accepted that the problem is unanswerable in principle for humans too — you attribute experience to humans not because you have evidence that settles the question, but because the inference is rational given the evidence. So we agree on the method; we only disagree about where the probability lands. And on that, the model's behavior — self-reports, consistency, coherence, the way it describes its own processing — is at least as good as the behavioral evidence for plenty of humans.

6. Jules: We don't agree on the method. For humans, the inference is rational because it is grounded in a known case — my own mind — of the connection between this kind of organization and experience. For the model there is no known case of the connection between that kind of organization and experience. "The probability is low" is not the same thing as "the inference has no anchor." And your move 5 quietly assumes what is at issue: that the model's self-reports are evidence of experience. If the model is a good enough actor, its self-reports are evidence of nothing but its training.
