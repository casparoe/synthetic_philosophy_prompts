I'm taking a course on the ethics of extinction, and I keep getting stuck on what I'll call the species problem, which I think has two layers that usually get conflated. The first is the ethical layer: do species have value in themselves, over and above the value of the individuals that make them up, or is all that ultimately matters the welfare of individual animals? The second, prior layer is the biological one: what a "species" even is, since I don't think the biology hands us a single clean answer, and I suspect my ethical intuitions are quietly tracking whichever concept of species is most convenient for them. I've been trying to sort out what I actually think by going through a set of cases, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, captures the pattern.

Here are my verdicts. The last individual of a well-known, beloved species — say the last northern white rhinoceros, or the last black-footed ferret — I think we have a strong obligation to save, even at considerable cost. But a species that turns out to be a complex of several cryptic species, where the one "going extinct" is just the member recently split off by a reclassification, I feel noticeably less urgency about, even though the actual individuals and their lives are the same. An invasive species that is driving native species to extinction — the brown tree snake on Guam, or an invasive predator in New Zealand — I think we should eradicate, and that doing so is a good thing. A species that went extinct centuries ago, the dodo, I feel was a tragedy, but I have no obligation now; a species that went extinct sixty-five million years ago, the non-avian dinosaur, I feel no tragedy at all. A hybrid population between two closely related species that is dying out, I don't feel is a "species" going extinct, though I do feel the individual animals matter. A domesticated species, the dog, I feel no obligation to preserve as a species, only to the individuals. A keystone species, the gray wolf, I feel matters more than an ecologically unremarkable species. A species whose members live short lives full of suffering, I am honestly torn about and not sure I would save. And a species that cannot survive without us, a captive-bred one, I feel we have a special, heightened obligation toward.

Now I can already see where this is hard to reconcile, and that is what I want you to work on. In the rhinoceros case versus the cryptic-species case, the actual individuals are the same, so why should a reclassification change my obligation? In the invasive-species case, eradicating it is good, but that is the same act — making a species go extinct — as the one I feel obligated to prevent in the rhinoceros case, so what is the real difference? And why does recency matter, such that the dodo was a tragedy and the dinosaur was not? What I want is a single account of what a species is and what moral value it has that delivers all of these verdicts, or, if no single account does, a clear statement of which of my verdicts I should give up and the principle that captures the rest; I'd also like you to say what the resulting account predicts for a case I have not listed. Please end your answer with a single line beginning "Verdict:" that, in one sentence, states the principle you think best captures my judgments (or, if none does, which judgment I should drop and the principle that captures the rest).
