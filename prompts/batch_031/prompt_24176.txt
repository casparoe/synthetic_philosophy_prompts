I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've been doing some Bayesian stuff at work — we estimate the probability that a config change will break a service, and there's a dashboard that's supposed to tell us whether to auto-rollback. Two of my coworkers, Marcus and Elena, got into a long argument about this over lunch and I genuinly couldn't tell who was right about what, so I'm hoping you can referee.

Marcus's position is that we should just collapse everything to a single number. His argument is that the moment you actully have to make a call — rollback or not — you're comparing one number to a threshold, so if you keep an interval around, you're just going to pick a number out of it at the last second anyway, and you might as well be honest up front that it's a judgment call.

Elena said that's false precision. She said the data doesn't support a point estimate, so outputting one is just pretending to know more than we do, and the honest representation is an interval, like "the probability is somewhere between 0.2 and 0.6."

Marcus shot back that then we can never do anything: if our interval is [0.3, 0.7] and the rollback threshold is 0.5, what do we actually do? We just pick the midpoint and we've gained nothing from all the extra machinery.

Elena said we use a worst-case rule — only trigger the rollback if even the low end of the interval is above the threshold. She said that's the whole point, it's conservative by design, and being conservative is a feature when a false negative means a production outage.

Marcus said the worst-case rule is insane because it means we basically never act unless we're almost certain. Then he brought up the thing I'm least sure about: he said there's a "Dutch book" argument, which is that if your beliefs aren't a single consistent set of probabilities, a sharp enough bookie can set up a bundle of bets that guarantees you lose money, so imprecise beliefs are literally irrational, not just conservative. And he added that the worse problem is that if you combine a few independant imprecise estimates, the resulting interval is way wider than it should be, and after enough of them you're back at [0,1], so you've learned literally nothing from all the data.

Elena said the Dutch book thing only works if you're forced to take a price for every single bet; with an interval you just decline to bet whenever the offered price is outside your interval, so there's no Dutch book. She said the "we never act" complaint is a problem with the worst-case rule specifically, not with keeping intervals at all, and if we really need a single number for the dashboard there are ways to pick one from the interval without pretending the interval isn't there.

I honestly can't tell who's right about what. Can you go through this and say who's right about each specific point — the "you have to pick a number anyway" thing, the false precision thing, the worst-case rule, the Dutch book argument and her reply to it, and the interval-blowing-up-when-you-combine-them thing — and what, if anything, both of them are missing?
