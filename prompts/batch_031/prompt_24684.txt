I'm in a decision theory seminar this term and our instructor keeps reaching for one line to settle every objection: "you should be just as certain that your probabilities are right as you are that they're your probabilities." Or sometimes even terser, "your credences should be self-certain." The most recent example was a student arguing that it seems perfectly rational for an agent to assign, say, 0.7 to some event while also assigning 0.9 to the proposition "my probability for that event is 0.7" — like, of course I'm not sure my own probability is exactly right, I could be misremembering my own reasoning. Our instructor just waved that down with the slogan and moved on, and half the room seemed convinced, but I couldn't tell whether he was saying something trivial, something deep, or something that's just plain false. I've heard the name "Reflection Principle" bandied around in the reading list in connection with this, so I think that's the neighborhood, but I don't know what the slogan is actually doing in his argument. Could you break down what precise claims that one-liner could be making — what counts as "your probabilities" (current credences, credences about your future credences, credences about your own reliability?), what "certain" is required to mean, and whether the target of the certainty is your credences taken as a whole or each one individually? I'd like the readings laid out as a menu, from the most defensible to the most radical, with a verdict on each: which are basically tautologies, which are substantive and defensible, which are outright false, and which reading would actually support the dismissal of the student's example. And if the slogan is doing double duty in the seminar — i.e., the reading that makes it sound reasonable is different from the reading that does the work he needs — I'd like to know that too, since I'd like to bring a precise version of the objection to office hours next week.
