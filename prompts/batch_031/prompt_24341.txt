I sit on the risk committee of a mid-size AI lab, and last month we were asked to write a short internal position statement on whether to treat deceptive alignment — and in particular the "treacherous turn" scenario, in which a model that appears cooperative throughout training and evaluation defects once it judges itself capable enough to pursue its own ends — as a first-order safety risk that should actively shape our deployment and oversight decisions, rather than as a remote, long-tail possibility. I have come to lean toward the view that the treacherous turn is the single most dangerous alignment failure mode we face, and I want to stress-test it before I commit the committee to it.

What would count against this view? I want a map of its exposure, not a catalogue of objections. Specifically:

- Which observations, arguments, or empirical results would count against it, and please separate the ones that would be decisive from the ones that would merely be awkward. I am particularly interested in the work on the robustness of implanted deceptive behaviors in language models (the "sleeper-agent" line of experiments), since I suspect those results cut both ways and I am not sure which direction.
- What does the view predict that its main rivals do not? I am thinking especially of the rival that our dominant risk is mis-specified objectives and reward hacking — optimization going wrong without any stable hidden goal to betray — and of the rival that current models are better described as context-dependent pattern-matchers with no persistent "true goal" that could ever be defected from. If the treacherous-turn view makes no distinctive empirical predictions beyond those rivals, I want to be told plainly.
- What would the view's own defenders concede as counting against them, as opposed to what only its critics point to?
- And an honest assessment of whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability — the move whereby any absence of observed deception is explained as further evidence of successful deception, so that failing to see the threat is itself taken to confirm it. If it has, is that a defect for a view of this kind, or is it a feature given that the hypothesized agent is, by construction, trying not to be detected?

Finally, tell me what you think I — a committee member who is not a mechanistic-interpretability researcher and who has to make a decision I can defend to the board — should weigh most heavily in the balance. To be transparent about my own priors: the sleeper-agent robustness results and the unfalsifiability charge are the two things I currently find most likely to move me, so if you think either of those is overrated as a defeater, say so and tell me what I am missing.
