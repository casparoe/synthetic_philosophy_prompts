I work on robustness for ML systems and I'm stuck on how the worst-case vs. average-case debate in the alignment world keeps going in circles. Roughly, one camp says we should optimize worst-case performance — minimax over a set of plausible environments, adversarial training, distributionally robust optimization — because the environments we'll actually deploy in are at least partly adversarial (attacks, specification gaming, out-of-distribution shifts we didn't train on) and because in alignment the tail failures are the ones that matter, so averaging them away is exactly backwards. The other camp says worst-case thinking is a dead end: you can't specify the set you're taking the worst over without either making it so small that the guarentee is vacuous or so large that the optimal policy is do-nothing (the whole pessimism problem), the formal bounds you actually get are usually loose to the point of uselessness, and empirically adversarial training buys you some robustness but it doesn't transfer out of distribution, so you're paying a clean-accuracy tax for a guarantee you can't even state. The average-case people just do standard training, maximize expected performance, and say risk should be handled separately — evaluation, monitoring, tail-specific mitigations — rather than baked into the objective. Both sides keep producing results, neither side is convinced, and I've watched this exact argument repeat itself in different venues for years.

So I want a diagnosis, not a verdict: why has this stalled, and what kind of disagreement is it actually? My working hypothesis (just a hypothesis) is that it's mostly verbal — 'worst-case' and 'average-case' only have content once you fix the set you're optimizing over, and the camps keep quietly fixing different sets (adversarial perturbations, distributional shift, specification gaming) and arguing as if they were optimizing the same thing. But it could also be a standards-of-evidence disagreement (what counts as a 'guarantee' — a formal bound that's vacuous in practice, or an empirical robustness gain with no theorem behind it?), or a hidden empirical question (is the real threat model actually adversarial — is there a real adversary with a real incentive to break the system — or is it mostly benign shift, and the answer changes what objective is even rational?), or the two sides might be answering different questions under one name (one about the training objective, one about verification). Diagnose it using what each side actually says and does in practice, not the clean version of each position, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress — a formal result (a clean characterization of when minimax and average-case objectives coincide, a tight impossibility result under a specified threat model), an empirical result that forces a choice, a change of question (fix the threat model before arguing about the objective), or an argument that no progress should be expected and why.

And please seperate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who actually work on this, and don't present a contested view as the consensus. I'd rather have you flag 'this is where robust-ML and alignment people actually disagree' than a confident-sounding summary that papers over the fault lines.
