A friend of mine who works on moral responsibility walked me into an argument about "standing to blame" and I cant find the exit. The background idea: you don't just have to be right to blame someone, you have to be in a position to — you have to have standing. One condition people keep citing is that you can't blame someone for a wrong if you're yourself in the wrong about the same matter. You have to be, roughly, clean-handed. I'll call this the Clean-Hands condition. Here's the argument as he laid it out:

(1) If you have wronged S, you lack standing to blame S.
(2) In most real cases of blaming, the blamer has also done something wrong in the same episode — usually a small thing (you snapped at them, you were rude, you cut them off).
(3) So on the unrestricted reading of (1), you lack standing to blame in most real cases, because you almost always do some minor wrong in the same episode.
(4) But that's plainly false: you can blame your coworker for lying to the boss even though you snapped at them that morning.
(5) So (1) needs a restriction: you lack standing only if you've wronged S in a way relevant to the wrong you're blaming.
(6) On the most natural understanding, "relevant to" means "at least as serious as" — your own wrong has to be of comparable weight to theirs for it to undercut your standing. You can't blame someone for a big wrong if you did an even bigger one, but you can if yours was smaller.
(7) But on that gloss the restriction is trivial: it just collapses into "you can't blame if you're the bigger wrongdoer," which is a special case of a more general principle and doesn't capture any of the interesting standing cases.
(8) So the Clean-Hands condition either proves too much (3) or is trivial (7).
(9) Therefore there's no version of Clean-Hands that both does real work and doesn't block almost all blame.

My question: which step is doing the bad work, and what does it cost me to give it up? The two things I most want to keep are (4) — that you can blame your coworker for the lie even though you snapped — and the idea that there really is a standing restriction stronger than "you're just the bigger wrongdoer." I'm not sure which of the other steps I can afford to loose, and I don't want to just drop the whole condition, because I think it's capturing something real.
