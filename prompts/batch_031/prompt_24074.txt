I'm writing a novel about a synthetic biologist who has cultured something that isn't a cell but is metabolically active, and a biotech company is trying to claim it as property on the grounds that it's obviously not alive, just a chemical reaction. In the book my bioethicist character shows up at a regulatory hearing and shuts it down with: life is like a flame. A flame isn't a substance, it's a process -- a chain of chemical reactions that sustains itself, and the moment the chain breaks, the flame is gone. So "alive" isn't some magic stuff that gets added at conception or at some complexity threshold, it's just a label we attach to a self-sustaining process, and the only question is where you draw the line. She uses this to argue that the company's "obviously not alive" doesn't hold water, because a flame is also just a chemical reaction and we call it alive. I want to use this in the book but I don't want my bioethicist character to be saying something I can't defend. How far does the flame analogy actually hold? Give me the mapping -- flame to organism, wax to nutrients, self-maintenance to metabolism, the wick to what -- then where it breaks, and which of those breaks matter for the "no hard line, only a gradient" conclusion she's trying to draw. Also, is this analogy double-edged in a way she hasn't noticed? I mean, could the company turn the same flame picture around and argue that the culture is "just a process" and therefore not alive at all, the same way a flame isn't? Which readings of the analogy do her work and wich do theirs, and what's the strongest pushback my scientist character could give that doesn't just amount to "but organisms are more complex than flames"?
