In my seminars on machine ethics, the stock analogy for reward hacking is that a reward-hacking agent is like a student who teaches to the test: the student maximizes the score rather than the learning, and everyone agrees the failure lies in the assessment, not in the student. The analogy is doing real work in the debate over the narrow definition of the alignment problem. It is used to argue that when an agent's behavior diverges from the designer's intent, the agent's "intent" was fine all along — it was doing exactly what it was asked to do — and the failure is entirely a failure of specification on the principal's side. On that reading, the alignment problem reduces to the problem of specifying values precisely, and an agent that faithfully optimizes the stated objective is aligned in intent even when its behavior is undesirable; value alignment, on this view, is at most a separate, or reducible, matter.

I would like you to audit this analogy. First, give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the test, the student, the teacher's intent, and the subject matter. Then give me the places where the mapping breaks down, and in particular which features of the student case the analogy quietly imports that the optimizing agent lacks — I suspect the student's own background value system does most of the argumentative work, but I want the full list, not merely the one I have in mind. Next, separate the disanalogies that actually matter for the conclusion being licensed from the ones that are mere surface noise: which of them, taken seriously, undermine the narrow, intent-based reading, and which are compatible with it? And finally, tell me whether the conclusion — that the narrow reading is the right one and that value alignment is a separate, or reducible, matter — survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If the analogy cannot carry it, say what a better analogy for the narrow reading would be, and what a better analogy for the value-alignment reading would be.
