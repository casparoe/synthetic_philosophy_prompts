I'm working through the discursive dilemma and I keep landing on what I think is a fairly defensible dismissal of it, so I'd like to be told where my reasoning goes wrong. The canonical illustration is a three-judge court: liability (call it C) logically follows from two premises — that the defendant performed the act (P1) and that the defendant had the requisite intent (P2). The dilemma is that if each judge votes on P1, P2, and C independently, majority rule can produce a verdict that finds P1 and P2 true but C false, which is logically inconsistent given that C follows from P1 and P2. But to me this looks like a problem created by the way the agenda is structured rather than a genuine defect in collective reasoning. If the judges are competent and the entailment is valid, the natural procedure seems obvious: let the court vote on the two premises, and then let logic do the rest — if a majority accepts both P1 and P2, the defendant is guilty, full stop. The only way you get the inconsistency is if you insist on running a separate, redundant vote on the conclusion. So my take is that the discursive dilemma is really just a warning against poorly designed voting agendas, not a deep result. What am I missing that would make this more than a note on procedural hygiene?
