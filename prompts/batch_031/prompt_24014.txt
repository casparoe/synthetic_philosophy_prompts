I work on identity and access management for a B2B SaaS product, so I spend a lot of time thinking about what a "user" actually is in our system. A design review a few weeks ago made it worse: someone asked why we treat the account as if it were the person rather than just a record of the person, and I couldn't give a clean answer, so it's been stuck in my head. Concretely, a user in our system is a persistent account: a username, an email, an accumulating history of activity, and a profile of preferences that carries across sessions, devices, and even the user's own name and preference changes. When the same person logs in on a brand-new laptop, the system treats it as the same "you"; when they change their display name or their settings, the account is still "theirs"; when they log out, the account just sits there as a kind of empty vessel, a record with no active subject behind it. I'd like you to take this practice as your object of analysis and reconstruct the view of personal identity it silently presupposes.

The puzzle is that the practice seems to pull in two directions at once. On one hand, it treats the account as stable through all change — the same "you" before and after a name change, a mood change, or a total shift in beliefs — which seems to require a persistent, unchanging entity of which the account is merely a record. On the other hand, the "you" the account represents is partly constituted by its own mutable content: the posts, the bio, the preferences, all of which the user can edit or delete at will, so the self seems to be made out of a narrative that can be rewritten, what I'll call a center of narrative gravity around which the experiences cohere into a single "me" rather than a thing that sits behind them. Please work through where the practice strictly presupposes one of these views versus where it is merely compatible with several, and use the cases where it genuinely comes apart: account deletion, forking or copying the account so two people share it, the "logged-out" state where there is a record but no active subject, and the case where a user recreates the account from scratch after a long absence.

I'm not after a history of personal-identity theory or a list of who said what, and I don't want citations — I want the implicit theory of the practice itself. So: name the view or views the practice most plausibly rests on, say which of those it strictly needs versus which it would survive, flag the combinations of answers the practice leaves underdetermined, and then tell me what would have to change in how we design and reason about accounts if we replaced the presupposed view with a bundle-theory or no-deep-self picture, or with a purely narrative-identity picture where the account is not a record of a self but the self.
