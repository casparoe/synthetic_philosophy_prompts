I'm a science and ideas journalist, and I'm teaching a short seminar on the foundations of mathematics. One of my students turned in the following answer to an exam question.

Exam question: Explain the independence of the continuum hypothesis (CH) from ZFC. In your answer, address (a) what CH asserts, (b) how Gödel and Cohen each contributed to showing its independence, (c) the role of the constructible universe L, and (d) what (if anything) the independence of CH implies about the "truth" of CH.

Student's answer:

CH asserts that there is no set whose cardinality is strictly between that of the natural numbers and that of the real numbers; equivalently, that the cardinality of the continuum is ℵ₁.

Gödel showed that CH is consistent with ZFC by constructing the inner model L, the universe of constructible sets, in which V=L holds and hence CH is true. Cohen then showed that ¬CH is consistent with ZFC by his method of forcing, in which he extended a model of ZFC to a larger model in which CH fails.

The constructible universe L is important because it is an inner model of ZFC in which every set is constructible, and it satisfies V=L, which implies CH. Indeed, CH is equivalent to V=L, so the two can be used interchangeably.

The independence of CH from ZFC means that CH is neither provable nor refutable in ZFC. This is a special case of the incompleteness that Gödel's theorems guarantee for any sufficiently strong, consistent, recursively axiomatizable theory, and it is one reason why some mathematicians take CH to be indeterminate in the sense that there is no fact of the matter about its truth.

Please assign a letter grade and provide specific comments justifying it, addressing each part of the answer point by point.
