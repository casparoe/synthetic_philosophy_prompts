A friend of mine (epistemologist, so brace yourself) has this short argument that the preface paradox isn't even a paradox, and I still can't find the hole. Here it is:

(1) You're rationally entitled to believe a claim just in case your evidence puts its probability above some threshold t — say 0.99 for concreteness, the usual "justified belief" bar.
(2) The preface author believes each of the n factual claims in her book, so her evidence puts each one at or above t.
(3) Her preface claim M — "at least one claim in this book is false" — is the logical complement of the conjunction of her claims, so its probability is 1 minus the probability that all her claims are true.
(4) If the claims are independant, that's 1 minus the product of their individual probabilities.
(5) But each individual probability is at least t, so for n = 2 the product is at least t² ≈ 0.98, which means M's probability is at most about 0.02.
(6) So M is way below the bar and the author isn't entitiled to believe her own preface claim.
(7) So she never holds the inconsistent bundle, and the preface paradox dissolves.
(8) The same move handles the lottery: you're never entitled to believe "every ticket loses" once you're entitled to believe "some ticket wins."

Here's where I lose the thread. The same reasoning seems to show I'm never entitled to believe "I made at least one slip in this two-line derivation," even right after I've watched myself botch the algebra. And it makes the author's preface sound like a mood rather than a belief. But I do believe my derivation has a slip, and I'm pretty sure the author does believe her book has an error — that's the entire point of writing a preface. So either the argument is wrong somewhere or "belief" is doing something I'm not tracking. I'd rather keep (1) and keep the idea that the author really believes M than give up either.
