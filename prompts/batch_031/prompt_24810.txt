I'm a teaching assistant for an upper-level seminar on the value of life, and I'd like a second pair of eyes on a problem-set response before I post my marks. We spent two weeks on whether death is bad for the person who dies, and the question on the set read as follows:

"Consider the Epicurean argument that death is not bad for the person who dies: (i) whatever is bad for a person must be something that person experiences, or at least something that is bad for them at a time at which they exist; (ii) before a person is born they do not exist, and after they die they do not exist, so there is no time at which the person exists and at which their death is bad for them; therefore (iii) death is not bad for the person who dies. Evaluate the argument. In particular: (a) Is premise (i) defensible? (b) If not, what is the most promising alternative account on which death can be bad for the person who dies, and what difficulties does that account face? (c) Does the asymmetry between death and pre-natal non-existence — the fact that we regret death but not the non-existence that preceded our birth, which Lucretius presses as a further argument for the Epicurean conclusion — help or hinder the Epicurean?"

Here is the student's response, in full:

"The argument runs as follows: (i) whatever is bad for a person must be something that person experiences, or at least something that is bad for them at a time at which they exist; (ii) before I was born I did not exist, and after I die I will not exist, so there is no time at which I exist and at which my death is bad for me; (iii) therefore my death is not bad for me. The heart of the argument is what I'll call the no-experiencer problem: if badness for a person requires an experiencer, and there is no one there to experience my death, then there is nothing bad about my death for me. Premise (i) is the load-bearing one, and I don't think it is defensible.

The most promising alternative is the deprivation account. On this view, death is bad for me not because I experience something bad, but because it deprives me of the good things I would otherwise have had; the badness is in the loss, not in any experienced state. This handles the case of the person who is killed painlessly in her sleep: she experiences no bad state, yet her death is bad for her, because it cuts her off from the good future she would have had. A difficulty for the deprivation account is the location problem: if my death is bad for me, at what time is it bad? I am fine before I die, and I do not exist after, so the badness seems to be located at no time at all, which is puzzling for an account that ties badness to a subject's states.

One consequence I think follows from the deprivation account is that death is bad for a person only if that person's life is, on balance, good. If my life were not worth living, then there would be no good future for me to be deprived of, so my death would not be bad for me; and if my life is good, then my death deprives me of that goodness, so it is bad. So whether my death is bad for me depends, on this account, on whether my life is, on balance, good.

On the asymmetry: Lucretius points out that the non-existence before our birth is just as real as the non-existence after our death, yet we do not grieve the former, and he takes this as showing that our fear of death is irrational. The standard response, which I think is right, is that the asymmetry tracks a real difference in our interests: we have interests in the good things in our future, because those are goods we would have had, but we do not have analogous interests in the pre-natal past, because we were never a subject who would have had those goods. So the deprivation account can explain why we rationally regret death but not pre-natal non-existence, and on that view the asymmetry helps the non-Epicurean. But I suspect this is doing too much work. In the end, I think the asymmetry is a contingent feature of how our psychology is set up — we are creatures who project forward, not backward — and that a being with a different temporal orientation would not find death any more regrettable than the pre-natal void. On that view, our dread of death is tracking a feature of our psychology rather than a real difference in value, which is what the Epicurean would want to say."
