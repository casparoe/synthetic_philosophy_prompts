I'm working on a project at the intersection of the metaphysics of material constitution — the "statue and the lump of clay" problem — and the actual practice of museum conservation, and I'd like a careful reconstruction of what that practice presupposes. Think very carefully before answering, and respond in the style of an expert in the metaphysics of material constitution who also understands how museums actually work.

Here is the practice, described concretely. Conservators routinely replace lost, broken, or heavily corroded parts of sculptures with modern material: a missing arm is recast in new resin or new marble, a fractured piece is reattached with modern adhesive, a corroded bronze is cleaned and consolidated with modern consolidants. Three norms are standard. First, the replaced part is often deliberately left visually distinguishable from the original — unpolished, a slightly different color, or a different material — so that a viewer can tell what is original and what is restoration. Second, the museum continues to display and label the object as the same work, by the same artist, with the original date of making rather than the date of the latest intervention. Third, the profession treats original material as having a special value — the material link to the artist, the patina of authenticity — and there is a widely held intuition that there is some limit to how much can be replaced before the object is no longer really the same object, even though nobody can state a precise threshold.

My questions are the following.

1. What theory of material constitution does this practice presuppose? Does it treat the statue as identical to the lump of clay (or to the original matter), or as a distinct object that the clay constitutes? And what does it presuppose about persistence through partial material replacement — that identity does not require material continuity, or that it does, subject to some slack?

2. There is an apparent tension: the practice treats the statue as persisting through replacement, which suggests that identity is not material continuity, while at the same time it treats original material as value-relevant — an authenticity value that only makes sense if the original matter is somehow "the stuff of the statue." Reconstruct the implicit theory that reconciles these two commitments, and say whether the reconciliation is coherent.

3. For each of the following, say what the practice would require and whether the implicit theory can handle it: (a) a statue whose arm is replaced with modern resin; (b) a statue that is melted down and recast in exactly the same shape from the same metal; (c) a statue that is completely disassembled and then reassembled, piece by piece, from its original parts (a conservation practice sometimes called anastylosis).

4. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes — what it would not make sense without — from what it merely fits well with — what the practice is compatible with but does not require. Then say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined: which rival theories of material constitution would all license exactly the same conservation practice?

5. Finally, say what would have to change in conservation practice — in what gets replaced, how replacements are marked, and how objects are labeled and dated — if the practice were instead built on (i) the view that the statue is identical to the original matter, or (ii) the view that the statue is a purely formal or pattern object that the matter merely instantiates.

Please be explicit about which steps are matters of conceptual judgment rather than settled fact, and flag any point where a reasonable philosopher could disagree. I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I want the reconstruction and the reasoning.
