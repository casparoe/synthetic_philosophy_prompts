I'm a philosophy professor, and I've been going back and forth on the unity of consciousness — what makes a person a single unified subject rather than two. I've been working through a set of split-brain cases and I want to know what principle, if any, captures my judgments.

Here are my verdicts:

- The classic commissurotome patient, where the verbal left hemisphere gives a reason for a hand movement that the non-verbal right hemisphere actually caused: I think this is still one person, but with a fractured unity. The verbal "I" is a spokesperson, not the whole subject.

- A patient whose commissure is fully severed and who reports two distinct streams of experience, each with its own sense of agency, but who shares one body: I'm tempted to say this is two persons sharing a body, but I'm not confident.

- A person with a device that can reversibly sever and restore the commissure: when connected, they feel like one person; when the two hemispheres are seperated, they feel like two. I think it's the same person in both states; the disconnection doesn't create a new person, it just changes the internal communication.

- A person whose two hemispheres each have their own verbal report (via a device that lets the right hemisphere speak) and who disagree about what they "experienced": this is the case I keep going back and forth on. I'm tempted to say two subjects, but I'm not sure.

The tension I can't resolve is between my verdict on the reversible-disconnection case (same person) and my verdict on the two-streams case (maybe two persons). Both involve two "minds" in one body, but I judge them differently, and I can't say why.

Is there a principle of unity of consciousness — some account of what makes a subject a single unified subject — that gets all of these, or close? What does it say about cases I haven't listed, like a person whose two hemispheres are connected only by a slow, low-bandwidth link, or a person whose commissure is gradually degrading over time (does unity fade, or is it all-or-nothing)? And wich of my verdicts does it force me to drop?

Think very carefully before answering. Respond in the style of an expert in philosophy of mind, and be honest about which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate.
