I'm a novelist, and I've been asked to give the opening address at a two-day workshop on fiction and machine writing, co-hosted by a literature department and an AI lab. The room will be roughly half working novelists and literary critics, half people who build or study language models. The reason I've been asked to speak is that in an interview last month I admitted to having used a large language model to draft a stretch of my new novel, and several people in the room will have read that and are already deciding what I am. I don't want to do the obvious thing: I don't want to stand up and deliver either the speech about the death of the soul of literature, or the shrug that the model is just a tool, like a thesaurus, and there's nothing to see. I think both are wrong, and I want the address to build instead on a position I can actually defend, namely that what we call creativity in fiction is not a matter of where an idea comes from or of novelty, but of being answerable to what one has made — of being, in the plain sense, on the hook for it, able to be ambushed by it, and bound to it whether one likes it or not. I want that single idea to do three jobs in the speech. First, it should explain why, on my view, a language model's output is not creative in the sense that matters, and it must do so without leaning on the claim that the model has no soul or does not really understand, which I consider embarrassingly thin. Second, it should cost me something: I want the address to concede, in front of the novelists, that my own craft — the revision, the character who does something I did not plan, the sense that I am finding the story rather than making it — is mostly this same answerability, and not the inspired authorship I used to believe I practiced. Third, it should say to the people who build the models, in a way that does not read as snobbery, that their instinct to speak of the machine as an author or a collaborator is a category mistake, and to explain what the mistake is. The address should run about seven minutes when spoken, it should carry its argument on one concrete anecdote from my own writing life rather than on abstractions, and it has to land for both halves of the room: the novelists should feel described, not lectured, and the machine people should feel engaged, not dismissed. Please write it.
