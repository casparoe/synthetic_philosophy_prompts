I am a retired astrophysicist who, over the past several years, has drifted from the observatory into the philosophy of science, and I keep returning to one result I find almost unfairly elegant: Good's theorem on the value of information, the result that the expected value of perfect information — the expected gain in decision value from learning the true state of the world — cannot exceed the entropy of the agent's prior, and that the value of any real experiment, being a degraded form of perfect information, is bounded by that same quantity. A colleague in the statistics department has invited me to give a short talk next month to a working group on the foundations of Bayesian decision theory, and the room will be mostly Bayesian statisticians who think in expected utility and are deeply suspicious of entropy being smuggled into decision theory, plus one or two decision theorists who know the decision-theoretic derivations inside out. I will be defending the interpretive claim that this bound is not a bookkeeping identity but a substantive normative constraint: that it tells a rational agent, in principle, how much no experiment is worth, and that this is what makes the value of information a finite, checkable claim rather than a slogan.

Please prepare me for the question period as a numbered list of exactly five objections, ordered by how much damage each is most likely to do me, with no introduction and no conclusion. For each numbered item, in one or two sentences, give me the objection phrased the way a statistician in that room would actually put it, together with a compact reply, a word on whether I should concede the point up front or hold my ground, and a note on where that reply is softest. Let the pattern of softness make clear which objection exposes the weakest point in my own position rather than a flaw in the theorem, and make the final item the objection I am hoping nobody asks. Reason from the theorem itself and from the decision-theoretic and information-theoretic machinery the room will assume I know; do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or any citations.
