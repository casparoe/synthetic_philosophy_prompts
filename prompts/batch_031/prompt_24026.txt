im in a seminar on moral sentimentalism and we've been grinding through Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments, and I keep getting tangled up in the actual mechanics of how sympathy is supposed to produce moral approval, as opposed to the headline version. I get the basic story — you imagine the other person in your situation and check whether you can go along with their feelings — but the part that's been bugging me is the two-way structure and why it lands where it does.

Here's where i lose the thread. It seems like the spectator's sympathetic feeling is usually weaker than the agent's actual passion (i get this, e.g. a parent's grief is way more intense than what a bystander can actually feel), and Smith says the agent has to tone his passion down to the spectator's level in order for it to be proper. But then two things don't click for me. First, if propriety is just about matching the spectator, why is it the spectator's weaker feeling that sets the standard rather than the agent's stronger one? Is it just that the spectator is the judge, or is there something about the direction the sympathy travels that makes the agent's side the one that has to bend? Second, and this is the part that really confuses me, it looks like the agent's own moral emotions — pride when the spectator approves, self-reproach when they disapprove — are themselves sympathies, i.e. the agent is sympathizing with the spectator's sympathy. So the whole thing is a loop where the agent's moral feelings are copies of the spectator's feelings about the agent's feelings. Can you walk me through that two-stage structure, and in particular why the weaker spectator-side feeling ends up being the one that defines propriety, and whether the pride/self-reproach really is a second-order sympathy or something more like a direct feeling of the outcome?

And then the last piece i can't place is the impartial spectator. If all of this is built out of sympathy with particular imagined bystanders, what exactly is the impartial spectator — is it a genuinely separate internal judge, or just the agent's sympathy-with-the-spectator standing in for itself once it's been generalized across everyone? And how does the "impartiality" actually get generated from a bunch of partial, particular spectators?
