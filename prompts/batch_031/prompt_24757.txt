I'm an evolutionary ecologist working on population structure, and I keep running into a configuration in the species-concept literature that I can't pin a label on. There's a chain of populations arranged around a geographic barrier — I've seen it described for a bird whose subspecies wrap all the way around a large basin, and for a salamander whose populations ring a mountain range — where each neighboring pair in the chain interbreeds and is hard to distinguish, but the two ends of the chain, which meet at a single point, are as different from each other as if they were separate species. What trips me up is the logical shape of it: "is the same species as" is a relation I'd expect to be transitive, so if population A is the same species as B, B as C, all the way around the loop, I'd expect A to be the same species as the population it loops back to — but the two ends don't interbreed, so the relation fails to be transitive, and the whole loop resists being carved into species by any consistent rule. Does this specific configuration — a loop of interbreeding populations whose ends meet but are reproductively isolated — have a name of its own? And is the non-transitivity of "same species as" just a symptom of the general species problem (the absence of a single criterion for drawing species boundaries), or does it carry a more specific label? I'd also like it sorted against two terms I suspect people use loosely for related things: a hybrid zone and a cline. I won't be satisfied with a bare name — I want to know exactly what the standard label covers, what configuration it strictly requires, and where its edges are: which nearby cases (a loop whose ends do interbreed, a broken loop, a loop with no geographic barrier) fall inside it and which fall outside.
