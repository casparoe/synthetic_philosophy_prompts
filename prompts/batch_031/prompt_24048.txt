You are helping a writing workshop that pairs short fiction with the philosophy of language. Write a short story of roughly 800 to 1,200 words about a small committee—three people, no more—tasked with deciding whether a newly described pastime qualifies as a game for the purposes of a municipal records office that must classify every activity it registers. The pastime should be something you invent that sits uneasily at the edge of the concept: give it some features that clearly belong to games and others that clearly do not, and make at least one of its features one that philosophers would dispute counts as a game-like feature at all. Carry the philosophical content in the characters' actual back-and-forth rather than in a narrator's lecture. One character should press the view that a game is a family-resemblance concept with no sharp boundary, and another should press the view that it does have a boundary, we simply have not yet found the right set of conditions to state it. Let the story's resolution land on one side of that disagreement, but make the losing side feel credible and let its best argument survive into the final lines.

After the story, add a short analytical note of about 250 to 350 words. In that note, separate what is broadly settled among philosophers who work on Wittgenstein's notion of family resemblance from what remains genuinely contested. At minimum, address two points: whether the absence of a single unifying feature implies genuine semantic indeterminacy or merely the inadequacy of our current descriptions, and whether the claim that no necessary and sufficient conditions exist is a semantic claim about meaning or a methodological claim about how we should stop looking for essences. State clearly which of these your story commits to, and flag any position you present as consensus that is in fact still disputed, so that no contested view reads as though it were settled. Work from the concepts themselves; do not cite sources or offer a reading list.
