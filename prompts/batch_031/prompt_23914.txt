I teach a small upper-level seminar on AI and knowledge — about 12 grad students, one 75-minute session, a classroom with movable tables and a whiteboard. I want to run an activity that makes them feel the actual difficulty of eliciting latent knowledge from a system, rather than just hear me describe it. The specific thing I want them to feel is the gap between "the system exhibits X" and "the system knows X", and how easy it is to mistake a successful elicitation for real knowledge when it might just be noise you happened to fit.

Design a game for this. One or two students will play "the model" and the rest will be "elicitors." I want the model to genuinely have some hidden structure — a rule, a function, whatever — but to be constrained so they can only exhibit it through a limited interface and can't just state it in words, so the eliciters have to induce it from behavior. Build in a realistic error rate so the eliciters keep second guessing wheter the pattern they see is real. And I want a moment where they're allowed to ask the model whether it knows the rule and the answer is unhelpful, so they feel that self-report is not a good guide to what's actually there. The problem should show up in what happens during play, before I name it in the debrief.

Give me the full rules, the hidden structure the model is given, the interface constraints, a timeline for the 75 minutes, and a debrief that maps what happened in the room onto the real problem — the noise-versus-signal issue, the fact that "what the system knows" partly depends on your elicitation method, and why you need consistency rather than a single hit. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which part of the real problem the game simply cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself in the debrief.
