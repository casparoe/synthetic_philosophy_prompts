I'm a philosophy graduate student, and I've just started preparing a paper on disjunctivism about perception. Last week, during a reading-group discussion, two of my colleagues — Nora, who's been working through the disjunctivist literature, and Priya, who's more sympathetic to representationalism — got into a real argument over the argument from illusion, and neither would budge. I was the one taking notes, so I didn't take a side, and I honestly can't tell who's more right. I'd like you to referee the exchange, move by move, and then say what, if anything, both of them are missing.

Here is the exchange, roughly as it went. They were discussing whether veridical perception is a factive, world-involving mental state (the disjunctivist position) or whether what we have in perception is always mediated by something intrinsic to the experience, such as a representation or a sense-datum.

Priya: "Start with the argument from illusion. When you veridically see a ripe red tomato, and when a hallucination makes it seem to you exactly as if you're seeing a ripe red tomato, with no tomato present, the two experiences are subjectively indistinguishable — nothing it's like to be in one differs from what it's like to be in the other. The intrinsic, phenomenal character of an experience is fixed by what it's like, so the two experiences have the same intrinsic character. But in the hallucination there's no tomato present. So the intrinsic character of the experience doesn't depend on the tomato's being there. Which means the experience can't be essentially and directly of the tomato; it has to be mediated by something intrinsic. So direct realism and disjunctivism are false."

Nora: "You've got the indistinguishability premise, but the step from 'indistinguishable' to 'same intrinsic character' is doing all the work, and it isn't licensed. In the veridical case the experience is factive — it's an experience of the tomato, and part of what makes it the experience it is consists in the tomato's actually being there and being red. In the hallucination you have a different kind of mental state, a non-factive one. The fact that the two are subjectively indistinguishable doesn't make them the same kind of thing with the same intrinsic properties. The common factor you're hunting for isn't an intrinsic phenomenal property shared by two experiences of the same kind; it's just that both involve a 'seeming,' and 'seeming' is a weaker, non-factive way of characterizing even the veridical case. Disjunctivism survives."

Priya: "Then you owe an account of the indistinguishability. You can't just assert that the two are different kinds of things and stop, because that's precisely the fact I'm trying to explain. To explain why the two experiences are subjectively identical, you have to point to something they share, and whatever that is — a shared 'seeming' property, a shared representational content — is an intrinsic property of the experience that holds in both cases. Once you've conceded that, you've conceded my conclusion: the intrinsic character doesn't depend on the tomato. You've just relabeled the common factor."

Nora: "No, because the 'seeming' isn't an intrinsic property that constitutes the way the experience is. In the veridical case the way the experience is is partly constituted by the external world, so it isn't an intrinsic property at all. And if you want the epistemic side: in the veridical case I can know that I'm seeing a red tomato — I can know the factive disjunct. In the hallucination I can at most know that I seem to see a red tomato — the weaker, non-factive disjunct. That asymmetry is real, and it's exactly what disjunctivism predicts. You can't flatten it into a single common factor without losing the asymmetry."

Priya: "The asymmetry is easy to assert, but the modal structure is the problem. If I can't tell the two cases apart, then in any case where I have this experience there's a nearby possibility in which I'm hallucinating. So my belief that I'm seeing a red tomato, even when it's true, isn't safe: it could easily have been false given the same evidence. On the anti-luck, safety-based view of knowledge, that blocks knowledge. And your reply — that in the veridical case the object itself justifies the belief — looks ad hoc. You're using the fact that I'm veridical to explain why I know I'm veridical, which is close to circular. So the knowledge version collapses too."
