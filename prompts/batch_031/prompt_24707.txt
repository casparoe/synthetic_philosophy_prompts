When a news article, a platform's content policy, or an academic paper has to refer to a slur, a widely followed convention governs the result: the slur is printed at most once, and after that it is referred to obliquely — "a racial slur," "the N-word" — or described without being written; some writers, wanting to keep search engines and moderation filters from flagging the text, obfuscate even the first mention, replacing a vowel with an asterisk or substituting "the N-word" outright. What view of slurs does this convention presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory under which the practice makes sense: is the harm of a slur located in its meaning, in the attitude of the person who uses it, in the effect it has on those who encounter it, or in the bare occurrence of the string? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it is merely compatible with, and mark the places where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that more than one theory of slurs would license it. The convention also seems to pull in two directions at once: it treats repetition as especially dangerous, which suggests that the harm of a slur accumulates with each use, yet it treats even a single occurrence — one whose context makes clear that the speaker is analyzing or condemning the word rather than wielding it — as something that must be hidden, which suggests that the string is harmful in itself, regardless of context or intent. Reconcile or resolve that tension, say which rival theories of slurs the practice quietly rules out, and describe what a communication policy built on a different theory of the slur would have to do differently.
