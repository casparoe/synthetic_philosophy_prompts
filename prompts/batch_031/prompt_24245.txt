I'm a retired immunologist — roughly thirty years in the lab working on self-tolerance and autoimmune disease — and I've only in the last couple of years started reading philosophy with any seriousness. I'm applying for a small interdisciplinary fellowship (one year, modest stipend) that exists specifically to fund projects where a working scientist brings a mature scientific framework to a live philosophical problem. I'd like your help drafting the proposal.

My half-formed idea, which I've been turning over for a few months: the paradox of tolerance — the claim that a fully tolerant society must be intolerant of intolerance in order to survive — is almost always treated as a genuine paradox, a self-undermining principle that cannot be stated without already violating itself. I suspect it is instead a category error. In immunology, "tolerance" is not a virtue to be applied universally; it is a regulatory function with a built-in, context-dependent boundary. The system must tolerate self-antigens (enforced centrally in the thymus and peripherally by regulatory T cells), it carves out zones of immune privilege (the eye, the brain, the developing fetus) where the response is deliberately suppressed to prevent collateral damage, and failure of tolerance in either direction — attacking self, or failing to reject a pathogen — is a distinct pathology. The immune system never faces a "paradox of tolerance" because it does not try to apply tolerance indiscriminately; it has a principled, functionally determined way of deciding what to tolerate and what to reject. My working hypothesis is that the philosophical paradox arises precisely because tolerance is stated as a universal moral principle, and that reframing it as a regulatory function with a functionally determined boundary dissolves the paradox into a harder but non-paradoxical question of where the boundary is drawn and on what grounds.
