At a graduate reading group on normative ethics, two members argued at length about the demandingness objection to utilitarianism, and I was asked to referee but couldn't. Dana presses the objection and Ruth defends utilitarianism. Here is the exchange, as close to verbatim as I can reconstruct it:

1. Dana: The problem with utilitarianism is that it is too demanding. If I ought to maximize total welfare, then I should keep giving to effective causes until my marginal dollars do as much good as the marginal dollars of the people I am helping — that is, until I am roughly as poor as they are. On that view I have no genuine moral permission to buy a new jacket, a coffee, or a vacation. But a moral theory that leaves no room for any of my ordinary choices cannot be tracking what morality actually requires.

2. Ruth: You have stated the theory fairly, but "too demanding" is not a refutation. The strength of a requirement is not evidence that the requirement is false. If the facts really make the demands that high, then that is what morality requires, and our feeling that it "should not be that demanding" is just a record of how far we have fallen short. You cannot use the difficulty of living a theory as evidence that it is wrong.

3. Dana: That is too quick. There is a difference between a theory's being demanding and its being mistaken, but demandingness is still evidence about whether the theory is tracking the right moral concept. Our moral vocabulary — "ought," "requirement," "permission," "supererogatory" — carves out a space of genuine discretion, of things I may do for myself. A theory that collapses that whole space into a single "do the max or else" dichotomy is not describing our moral landscape; it is replacing it. So demandingness is a symptom of a category error, not merely a difficulty.

4. Ruth: You are smuggling in the conclusion. You are treating the idea that morality must leave room for personal projects and discretionary choices as a neutral constraint, but that is exactly what is at issue. Why should the concept of "the moral" be required to preserve a sphere of self-indulgence? Maybe the "discretion" you are pointing to is just an unexamined artifact of a culture that privileges personal projects. You are using a descriptive claim about how we talk about morality to settle a normative question.

5. Dana: It is not purely descriptive. There is an independent point: we cannot be required to do what would cost us something we have a genuine claim not to sacrifice. My own life, my own projects, my own relationships — there is a separateness of persons here. If utilitarianism treats my life as just another resource pool for the aggregate, it fails to respect the fact that I am a separate person with my own claims. The demandingness objection is really a separateness objection in disguise.

6. Ruth: I grant that feasibility is a real constraint — no one is required to do the literally impossible. But "separateness" is doing all the normative work, and you have not justified it. Why should my being a separate person give me a veto over how my resources are used? If the separateness of persons is a real moral fact, then fine — but that is a substantive moral premise, not a neutral constraint, and you are simply asserting the very principle the utilitarian disputes. So the objection either collapses into a bare assertion of a competing principle, or it has to be defended on its own terms, and you have not done that.

Please adjudicate this move by move: for each of the six moves, say who is right about what, rate the move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Then say what, if anything, both parties are missing, and who is more right overall. Finally, state how confident you are in your overall verdict and in your least-confident move-by-move judgment, and single out the point you are least sure of and explain why.
