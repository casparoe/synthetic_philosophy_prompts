In the *Incoherence of the Philosophers* (*Tahafut al-Falasifa*) he presses the point hard: he insists we must allow for "the possibility of a contact between the two which will not result in burning," and even for cotton turning to ash without ever touching fire, on the grounds that the link between what we call cause and effect is only a habit of the divine will, not a necessity built into nature.

Here's what's been bugging me. If no created thing is a genuine cause — if the only real agent is God, who is the one actually producing each event — then it looks like the sinner didn't really do anything; God did. And yet the Islamic legal system al-Ghazālī is defending is built on holding people responsible, on punishing the one who commits a wrong, and on the fine-grained distinction between a voluntary act and an involuntary one (an act done in sleep, under compulsion, or by mistake). So I'm stuck: doesn't his occasionalism undercut the very notion of agenthood that responsibility — and the legal system — require? If the act is really God's, how can it be "mine" in the sense that matters for blame? To me the position looks self-undermining, and I'm trying to understand how he thinks it is supposed to work.
