I am trying to work out what I actually believe about the rationality of escalating a losing position, and my intuitions are not lining up. I would like to set out my verdicts on a series of concrete cases and ask what principle, if any, captures the pattern.

Begin with the dollar auction, in which a single dollar bill is sold to the highest bidder but the second-highest bidder must also pay the amount they bid. I am the current high bidder at ninety cents and another bidder moves to ninety-five. My verdict is that I should bid a dollar. I can see why: folding forfeits the ninety cents with certainty, whereas bidding a dollar converts that certain loss into a lottery in which I break even if the other bidder folds and lose a full dollar if they do not, so as long as I assign the other bidder a sufficient probability of folding, the bid carries positive expected value.

Now run the same auction well past the face value of the bill. I am high at five dollars and five cents and another bidder moves to five dollars and ten cents. My verdict is that I should stop. The move is structurally identical to the first — I am again staking the same small increment to convert a certain loss into a lottery whose two outcomes are shifted by exactly the same amounts — and yet my gut refuses the bid. I cannot name what separates the ninety-cent case from the five-dollar case when the incremental calculation appears unchanged.

The same shape appears outside the auction. My firm has spent twenty million dollars developing a product that fresh market research indicates will fail; my verdict is to kill the project, and I am inclined to call continuing it the sunk-cost fallacy. But my firm has also spent twenty million dollars building a prototype, and a competitor will bring a rival product to market next quarter unless we ship ours first; my verdict is to ship, even though the product is risky. The same twenty million dollars, opposite verdicts.

And here is the case I keep returning to and cannot settle. I have driven two hours into a worsening snowstorm; the road ahead is closed, though crews are expected to clear it within the hour, and I am driving to an event I have no other way of attending. Part of me insists that I should press on because of the two hours already spent, but another part knows that those two hours are lost whether I wait or turn back, so that the only live question is whether the wait is worth it given the conditions. I cannot tell which of the two impulses is the rational one, or whether they are even competing considerations.

So I have three questions. First, is there a single principle — or a small set of candidates — that recovers as many of these verdicts as possible, stated precisely enough that I can see which case motivates each clause? Second, what does that principle say about the snowstorm case and about a case I have not raised, namely whether to keep funding a failing startup that has already burned through its seed round? I have a tentative guess that the dividing line is whether some other agent's future choices respond to mine, but I am not confident that is the right axis, and it does not obviously do any work in the snowstorm, where there is no opponent at all. Third, and most important to me, which of my own verdicts does the principle force me to abandon, and what do I lose by abandoning it?
