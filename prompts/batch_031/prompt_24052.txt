Exam question: "State the repugnant conclusion and the mere addition argument that generates it. Then evaluate one proposed escape from the conclusion — either a critical-level view or average utilitarianism — and say whether it succeeds."

Student's answer:

"The repugnant conclusion is the result that, for any population all of whose members lead very good lives, there is some much larger population whose members have lives that are barely worth living, and where that larger population is better overall. It is the flagship objection to total utilitarianism, which ranks outcomes by the sum of welfare across the population and so can always be beaten by adding enough people whose lives are only slightly positive.

The argument that produces the conclusion is the mere addition paradox. Let A be a large population every member of which has very high welfare. Now add a further large group C whose members have positive but lower welfare; the result A+C is better than A, since we have merely added people whose lives are worth living. Let B be an even larger population with a lower average welfare than A but a higher total welfare. Total utilitarianism then ranks B above A. So, from two premises that each look plausible — that adding people with positive welfare makes things better, and that a greater total of welfare is better — we are led to the repugnant conclusion.

Parfit's own way out of the difficulty was to reject the mere addition principle. Because the paradox depends on the claim that adding people with positive welfare always improves the outcome, denying that principle blocks the argument and lets one keep total utilitarianism's sum-ranking while avoiding the repugnant conclusion.

As for escapes, average utilitarianism is the cleanest. Because it ranks populations by average rather than total welfare, adding barely-worth-living people lowers the average, so the repugnant conclusion simply does not arise. Since it has no comparable downside, it is the most defensible view in the area."
