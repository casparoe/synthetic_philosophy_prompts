I chair the data governance and actuarial committee at a health insurance company. We are deciding whether to use a newly discovered statistical regularity in our claims data to price a new product. The regularity: policyholders whose claims in the past year include a certain combination of procedure codes are significantly more likely to file a large claim in the following year. It is stable across years and highly compressible — much shorter to describe the code combination than to enumerate the individual policyholders who exhibit it.

Our data team warns, however, that the regularity is not causal. The codes are assigned by our own internal coding system, and the combination in question is an artifact of our coding conventions: two individually unremarkable conditions, when they co-occur, produce the combination, but the combination itself has no independent significance for future risk. The regularity is "real," if at all, only relative to our own description language and data collection process.

The actuarial team wants to use it, arguing that it is a "real pattern" in the relevant sense: stable, compressible, and predictive, and that it would let us price the new product more accurately. The data governance team is concerned that pricing on the basis of an artifact of our own description language would be illegitimate.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before next week's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — use the regularity, decline to use it, or use it with specific conditions — together with a time frame for implementation, and it should engage the disagreement seriously rather than simply restating one side's framing, including an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side. In particular, it should address: (a) whether a pattern that is "real" only relative to our own description language and data collection process can legitimately be used for pricing; (b) the distinction between a compressible, observer-relative pattern and a causal, observer-independent pattern, and whether that distinction does any work in this case; and (c) which is the more defensible basis for the decision — treating our coding system as a designed tool whose output we are entitled to rely on, or treating the underlying data as a physical system whose regularities we must verify independently of the description language we happen to use — and why.
