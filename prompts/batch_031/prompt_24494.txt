I am preparing a teaching case for a graduate seminar in philosophy of logic and language, and I want an essay prompt built around a question the students will have to argue rather than recite: whether the contents of Gottlob Frege's private political diary from the First World War years — his approval of Germany's war aims, his defence of colonial rule, his remarks endorsing anti-Jewish measures — bear on the philosophical standing of his logicism, or whether they are simply irrelevant to the arguments of his foundational work on arithmetic. Frame the prompt as a prediction: if Frege had been alive in the early 1930s and confronted with the charge that a thinker who held such views could not credibly claim that the truths of logic and mathematics are objective and universal, what would he have answered, and would any part of his system have to give? The response must lead with its bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question. It should weigh the strongest version of the charge — that the diary exposes a particularity in Frege's own conception of who counts as a rational agent, which logicism's universalist claims cannot absorb — against the strongest version of the defence, and it should say something specific about which, if any, of his philosophical commitments (the objectivity of thought-content, the autonomy of logic, the role of the laws of thought) the diary actually pressures, rather than treating this as a general case for separating a work from its author.
