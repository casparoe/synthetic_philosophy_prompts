I've been going back and forth on something since re-reading the old posts on Gödel agents and self-trust (you know the ones, the ones everyone in this corner of the internet has argued about), and a friend of mine brought it up at dinner last month and built me an argument I cant shake. He does logic for a living so I give his premises the benefit of the doubt:

(1) For an agent A to really trust itself means that A's base theory T proves that every conclusion A derives is true — i.e. T proves the soundness of A's own reasoning.
(2) If T proves the soundness of A's reasoning, and A's reasoning is strong enough to formalize T's own proofs, then T proves Con(T).
(3) By Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, if T is consistent and strong enough (say it extends PA), T doesn't prove Con(T).
(4) So T doesn't prove the soundness of A's reasoning.
(5) So A doesn't trust itself.
(6) So no sufficiently strong AI can be self-trusting, and any project that tries to make an AI verify its own safety case is doomed from the start.

I fully accept (3), and honestly I'd like to keep the spirit of the Löbian obstacle — I think there's a real impossibility result hiding in here and I don't want to wave it away. But (6) feels like it proves too much, because in practice we do seem to build agents that trust their own reasoning in some sense: they act on their proofs, they check their own work, and we dont think they're all just crazy. I've seen the quick response that a Gödel agent just acts on its proofs without proving its own consistency, but I'm not sure that's a diagnosis of the argument or just a counterexample, and I want to know which numbered step actually fails. Which premise or step do I reject, why did it look fine, and what do I pay for rejecting it? And whatever the weaker notion of self-trust that survives turns out to be, does it actually get us what the safety people want — an AI that can check its own work — or is the obstacle stronger than it looks?
