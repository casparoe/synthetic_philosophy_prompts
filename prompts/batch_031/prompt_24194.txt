I referee for an epistemology journal. I'm a general epistemologist, not a specialist in the literature on transcendental arguments, and I get six to ten papers a year, a few of which claim to offer a transcendental argument — against global skepticism, for the existence of other minds, or for the reality of the external world. My reports on these tend to be vague, because I can't reliably tell whether a manuscript is really offering a transcendental argument or is offering a different species of argument that merely sounds transcendental. I need a decision procedure I can run on a manuscript and finish within the month I have to write my report.

Please write me that procedure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a verdict of "not a transcendental argument" (and which species it is instead), and where it has to hand the call back to me because the question is substantive rather than formal. The procedure should at least let me separate a genuine transcendental argument — one that purports to establish a necessary condition of the possibility of experience, judgment, or reference, and then infers the condition from the fact that the phenomenon is possible — from (i) an a priori argument, (ii) an inductive or empirical argument, (iii) a performative-contradiction argument, and from the modal trap of inferring from "we cannot think X away" to "X must be the case." It should also run two specific checks: an overreach test, which asks whether the argument establishes at most the conditions of our representation of reality (the standard objection usually associated with Sellars) rather than a claim about mind-independent reality; and a question-begging test, which asks whether the premise that X is a necessary condition already presupposes X. And it should require the paper to specify the phenomenon whose possibility is being analyzed, so I can check that the condition is genuinely necessary for that phenomenon and not merely convenient or psychologically typical.

Then run the procedure on three papers. Paper A argues against global skepticism: to coherently maintain that one's experience might be ungrounded, the subject must be able to distinguish appearance from reality, but that distinction itself requires the reality of objects, so the skeptic's position is self-undermining. Paper B argues that other minds must exist: a subject can acquire general concepts only through interaction with other speakers, so the possibility of conceptual thought requires other minds, so other minds exist. Paper C argues that the external world must exist because one cannot coherently doubt it. I'm personally attached to Paper A — I think it's the best available defense against skepticism and I hope it will pass as a genuine transcendental argument — so I expect the procedure's verdict on it to be the one I will not want. For each paper, show me the procedure's steps, its verdict, and which step did the work.

Finally, be honest about the limits: mark which steps of the procedure are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and say where the procedure will misfire on a genuinely novel argument that doesn't fit the taxonomy.
