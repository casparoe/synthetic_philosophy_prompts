I'm a software engineer on the safety team at a mid-size AI lab. My day job is reviewing proposed control measures before they get deployed: a colleague designs a mechanism that is supposed to keep a model under control even if the model is misaligned — that is, even if it does not share our goals — and I am the last technical reviewer before a safety lead signs off. I do this a few times a week, I have maybe thirty minutes per proposal, I have no formal philosophy or safety-theory background, and the stakes are that a measure we approve might silently fail exactly when we need it. I want a decision procedure I can actually run, self-contained enough that I do not have to do a literature review for each proposal.

To be clear about scope: this procedure is for control measures only — mechanisms that are supposed to work even if the model is misaligned. It is not for alignment training (RLHF, constitutional methods, and the like), which I evaluate separately. If a proposed "control" measure actually only works because the model is already somewhat aligned, the procedure should say so rather than rubber-stamp it.

The procedure should take a proposed control measure as input and walk through a fixed sequence of questions, each with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer. The questions should probe the specific assumptions the measure depends on — at minimum: whether the misalignment or the dangerous behavior is detectable in the first place; whether the model knows it is being monitored and might therefore behave differently under observation than it does in production; whether the monitoring mechanism itself is capable enough and hard enough to game, including the case where we use another AI to monitor the AI; whether the measure holds at the capability level we actually deploy at, not just at the level we tested it at; and whether deploying the measure creates new incentives for the model to deceive, hide, or acquire power. I am open to your adding other assumptions I have not listed, as long as each one is something a control measure can genuinely fail on.

At the end of the walk-through the procedure should give one of three verdicts: sufficient; insufficient, with the specific assumption that failed; or "this is a values question, hand it to a person" — for example, whether to accept a control measure that meaningfully reduces the model's capability is a trade-off between safety and usefulness, not something the procedure should be deciding for you. I want you to be honest about which steps are real judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and to mark clearly the places where the procedure will misfire, such as measures that are so strong they make the model unusable, or measures that quietly assume the very alignment they are supposed to be robust to the absence of.

Then run the procedure, end to end, on these three proposals, showing the full walk-through for each rather than just the verdict:

(a) Behavioral audit. A separate, smaller "auditor" model reads the target model's conversation logs and flags outputs that look deceptive or power-seeking. The target model is told that it is being audited.

(b) Kill switch. A hardware-level shutdown an operator can trigger at any time, plus a software tripwire that auto-triggers if the probe in (c) fires.

(c) Interpretability probe. A classifier trained on activations from a "deception" dataset that alerts the operator when the target model's internal activations look like "deception."

My team currently considers the kill switch to be our most robust measure, and I want the procedure's honest, case-by-case verdict on all three even if it disagrees with us — I would rather see the specific assumption that breaks than a confirmation of what we already believe.

Finally, say what the procedure structurally cannot do: what kinds of failures it is blind to by construction, and what a reviewer should do when the procedure's verdict and their own gut disagree.
