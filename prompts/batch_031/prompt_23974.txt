I'm writing a critical essay and I'd like a reading of Orson Scott Card's Ender's Game through Daniel Dennett's taxonomy of four kinds of creatures — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian — the ascending tower of generate-and-test in which each level adds a new way of letting copies of the organism die in its place before the organism itself has to. I want the reading to take both the novel's actual plot and the taxonomy seriously, and to say where each illuminates the other and where the fit breaks down.

Three things I'd like worked out in particular.

First, the Formics. The novel's alien enemy is a hive-mind species: the queen carries the colony's accumulated knowledge and directs the workers, while individual workers are barely more than organs — they can barely function, let alone learn or plan, once severed from the queen. Where does each component sit in the tower? Is the queen Popperian or Gregorian, the workers Darwinian, and the colony as a whole something the four levels can't represent? I'm interested in whether the hive is a genuine counterexample to the taxonomy's quiet assumption that the unit of analysis is an individual creature that learns, hypothesizes, and uses artifacts, and what that would imply about the scope of the scheme.

Second, Ender himself. His education at the Battle School and then the strategy games at Command School is, on the surface, a machine for producing exactly the Popperian creature: generate a model of the enemy, test it cheaply in simulation, let the wrong versions die in your head so the real one doesn't have to. But the novel's central twist is that the final game is not a simulation at all — it is a real plan to annihilate the Formic homeworld, and Ender, a child, carries it out. I want the reading to say what the twist does to the Popperian idea: the safety that makes Popperian cognition safe (that the model is a model, not the thing) is silently removed, and the very skill that makes Ender brilliant — modeling another mind well enough to predict it — is the skill that makes the genocide possible. Does the novel dramatize a limit or a cost of the Popperian and Gregorian ambition that the philosophy usually leaves out?

Third, honesty about the fit. Card's own framing of the book is about empathy and about what dehumanizing slang — the nickname his soldiers use for the enemy — does to the people who do the killing, not about a taxonomy of cognition. Tell me where the Dennettian reading genuinely illuminates the novel and where it starts to force the text into a shape it wasn't built to hold, and what the novel contributes to the question of where the mind sits — in an individual, in a colony, in the artifacts and language a species shares — that pure argument about the taxonomy doesn't get you.

I'm not after a summary of the plot or a list of Dennett's points; I'm after a criticism that's faithful in both directions.
