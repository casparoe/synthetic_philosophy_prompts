I'm taking a graduate seminar on the philosophy of causation, and the unit on causation by omission is the one I keep losing sleep over, so I'd like to try to pin down what I actually think by working through cases. Here are my verdicts on when a failure to act counts as a cause of an outcome. A physician has a patient who will die within the hour unless given a specific antidote that is right there in the cabinet; the physician has no other patients and nothing to gain, but simply decides not to administer it, and the patient dies — I'm confident this omission caused the death. A plant operator is required to check a pressure gauge every thirty minutes and skips a check; the pressure spikes, a tank ruptures, and a coworker is killed, though the check would have let them vent the tank in time — again, I say her omission caused the death. Now a person walks past a pond, sees a child treading water and going under, and does nothing; four other adults are nearby who could each easily get the child out, and they all just stand there filming on their phones, and the child drowns — here I do not think the person's omission caused the death, or at least it feels radically less like a cause than the physician's case, even though both are failures to save a life that was within reach. A person at a signal box could press a button to divert an oncoming train onto an empty side track but doesn't, and the train kills five people on the main track — I'm genuinely torn on this one, because counterfactually the five would have lived had the button been pressed, yet I don't have the strong "she caused their deaths" reaction I had with the physician. Two independent safety systems should each be enough to stop a runaway cart — a guard who can pull a lever, and an automatic brake; the brake works and stops the cart, and the guard, who was supposed to pull the lever if needed, never does — I say the guard's doing nothing did not cause the cart to stop, and yet if I change exactly one thing, namely that the brake malfunctions so the cart crashes, then I say the guard's very same omission now caused the crash. Finally, I don't donate to a well-run charity whose money would have saved a life far away, and someone in fact dies of a preventable condition — I say my omission did not cause that death.

So is there a single principle that captures all of these verdicts, or at least the most of them, and if so, what exactly does it say? My own tentative guess is that an omission causes an outcome just in case (i) the outcome would not have occurred had the agent performed the act, and (ii) performing the act was something the agent was reasonably expected to do in the circumstances — but I'm not sure (ii) is doing legitimate work here, and I'm worried it just smuggles in the very moral or normative question I'm trying to get a grip on, which would make the whole account circular. I'd like you to test this candidate against my cases and tell me whether it survives, and if not, to repair it or replace it with something better. Please state whatever principle you end up with precisely enough that each clause is motivated by a specific case, and be honest about which of my verdicts it forces me to drop — I suspect the physician-versus-bystander pair, and the two versions of the runaway-cart case, are the ones any clean account will have trouble holding together.

And as a further test that I haven't thought about yet: what does your principle say about a software engineer who knows her code contains a bug that will, with small probability, cause an autonomous vehicle to malfunction and kill someone, and who decides not to fix it, after which the vehicle does malfunction and someone does die? Does her omission cause the death, and if the principle says yes, is that the right answer or a sign that the principle is over-generating?
