In a seminar on Davidson's radical interpretation, a colleague kept using "the principle of charity" to shut me down: whenever I suggested that a speaker, a culture, or a historical figure might be largely mistaken or incoherent, they replied that I was violating the principle of charity, as though that settled the matter. I suspect the slogan is quietly doing very different work at different moments. Can you lay out the distinct precise claims "the principle of charity" might stand for — for example, whether it is a strict requirement to maximize agreement with the speaker or merely a defeasible default, whether it concerns attributing truth to the speaker or attributing rationality and coherence, and whether it is a substantive thesis about meaning or just a methodological constraint on the interpreter — and for each reading say whether it is a mere platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or actually false, and whether it licenses the move of dismissing my objection? I'd like a list I can bring back to the seminar, with a verdict on which reading my colleague was most likely invoking.
