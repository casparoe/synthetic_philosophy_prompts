I'm taking a course on the philosophy of probability, and I keep coming back to a question I can't quite pin down, so I'd like you to work through it with me carefully. The practice I have in mind is the single-number forecast as used in forecasting tournaments: a forecaster submits one real number in [0,1] for each binary event (say, "will a given bill pass by a given date?"), and at the close they are scored by a proper scoring rule, most commonly the Brier score, which for a binary event with true outcome 0 or 1 and forecast p is (p − outcome)², with lower being better. The culture around these tournaments treats the single number as the honest expression of a forecaster's uncertainty, and treats calibration — the long-run match between the stated numbers and the observed frequencies — as the mark of a good forecaster.

My question is about what this practice silently presupposes about the nature of probability itself. It strikes me that the single-number forecast, combined with a strictly proper scoring rule, presupposes that probability is always a precise real number — that for every event there is one true number, and that a rational agent's uncertainty about that event is always fully captured by one number. But I've been reading about imprecise probability, where instead of a single number you keep a whole interval (or a set of probability distributions), on the grounds that with limited data, or with genuinely conflicting models, the honest representation of your uncertainty is a range, not a point. If that's right, then the tournament practice seems to force an arbitrary precision onto anyone whose belief is genuinely imprecise: any point in the interval is "equally honest," and the scoring rule just picks one without justification.

So can you (a) reconstruct the precise view of probability that the single-number, Brier-scored forecast presupposes, and say which rival views of probability it quietly rules out; (b) say whether the "arbitrary precision" objection is really a flaw in the practice or just a feature of how the scoring rule is defined — in particular, whether there is a defensible reading on which the single number is not arbitrary but is the unique rational representative of an imprecise belief, or whether it is underdetermined once you allow the interval; and (c) say what would have to change in the tournament practice — the scoring rule, the way forecasts are submitted, the way they are aggregated and reported — if we took imprecise probability seriously as the honest representation of uncertainty in the low-data regime? I'd rather have a careful, in-the-weeds analysis than a survey of the literature, and please don't end with a reading list.
