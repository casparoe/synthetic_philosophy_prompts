Ok so I'm in a practical rationality seminar and we're doing Parfit's hitchhiker and two people in my study group got into it and I cant tell who's making the better point.

Quick setup: the hitchhiker promises the driver he'll pay for the ride, the driver only drives him if he makes the promise, and by the time he gets to his destination he has zero self-interested reason to actually pay (nobody around, no future interaction, no rep cost). Parfit's supposed point is that this shows the "rationality = acting in your own interest" view is incoherent, since the hitchhiker has a reason to make the promise but no reason to keep it, so it can be rational to be disposed to do what's irrational to do.

Person A: "Exactly, and that's the whole problem. The reason for making the promise is a reason that doesnt exist at the moment you'd keep it, so the self-interest view can't even track the reason that's supposed to justify the promise. It's got to be wrong."

Person B: "You're equivocating between 'it's rational to be disposed to do X' and 'it's rational to do X.' The hitchhiker has a reason to form the disposition to keep the promise, sure, but that's a reason to form a disposition, not a reason to act at the moment of action. At the moment of action, keeping the promise is still self-interestedly irrational. So the case doesn't show it can be rational to act against your own interest, only that it can be rational to have a disposition to act against your own interest. Those are different claims, and the second one is totally consistent with the self-interest view, which is only about your dispositions and beliefs being oriented toward your interest, not about every single act being in your interest."

Person A: "That's just relabeling. If the disposition is what's actually in your interest (it's what gets you the ride) and the only way to have the disposition is to actually keep the promise when it comes down to it, then the act has to be rational too, otherwise the disposition wouldn't be. So you've just got a case where it's rational to be disposed to do what's irrational to do, which is exactly Parfit's point. The self-interest view can't say the disposition is rational and the act is irrational, because then it's incoherent about what's in your interest."

Person B: "No, that's the move I'm questioning. The disposition is instrumentally valuable (it gets you the ride), the act is a net cost. That's fine and consistent. You should form dispositions that are instrumentally valuable even if the acts they produce are costs. The self-interest view never said every act has to be a net benefit, only that your dispositions and beliefs track your interest. Parfit's case just shows the self-interest view allows dispositions that produce costly acts, which is exactly what we'd expect, not a refutation."

Can you go through this and tell me who's right about what? I honestly can't tell if B's disposition/act distinction is a real objection or just relabeling, and I'm not sure if A is actually getting at something real about whether the self-interest view can be incoherent in the first place. And I don't know if B's "that's just what we'd expect" is doing too much work.
