I am writing a novel set in the world of 1930s foundations of mathematics, and toward the end a character who has spent her career defending the uncountability of the continuum is confronted with the fact that her axioms have a countable model in which the reals are uncountable only from the inside. I would like to place a villanelle at this point in the book, with the form doing the argument's work. The two refrains should be the two vantage points of Skolem's paradox: one, the model's own claim that its set of reals is uncountable — that no sequence of its elements covers it; the other, the external observer's observation that the entire model, reals included, can be listed one by one. The tercets should dramatize each vantage point trying to refute the other, and the closing quatrain, where the two refrains finally stand side by side, should show that they are not in contradiction: the bijection that lists the model exists only outside it, and the model's Cantor's theorem still holds within it. A set theorist should be able to tell from the poem that the mechanism is understood — the missing bijection, the relativity of "countable" — and a reader who has never heard of Skolem should still find it a poem worth reading.
