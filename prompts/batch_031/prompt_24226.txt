My friend Danny died by suicide about three weeks ago. He was a philosophy major and I'm a biochem grad student, and his favorite thing in the world was cornering me at dinner and making me defend the position that ending your own life is always a moral wrong, which I could never actually do and he knew it. He kept coming back to Hume's short essay on suicide, the one where Hume argues it isn't a wrong at all — not a wrong against yourself, not a wrong against your family or your country, and not a sin either, since there's no good argument that life is some kind of trust we're supposed to keep no matter what. His favorite move was the one about duties: you can't be obligated to do what you know to be impossible, and since no amount of reasoning can ever overpower the instinct to keep living, that instinct can't be a moral duty either. I told him he was being cold. He told me I was being sentimental, and he was right.

I'm giving his eulogy Saturday and I'm stuck, because I think the argument he won from me is the exact argument I need to make in front of his parents, and I have no idea how to do that without it turning in to a lecture. The room is not a seminar. His mom is a deacon in her church and I can tell from the funeral that she and his dad are processing this as a sin they can't stop thinking about, while his college friends are the kind of people who would be quietly offended if I said he was at peace or in a better place. I don't want to lie to either group, and I don't want to name Hume once in a eulogy, but I also can't stand the idea of him being remembered as someone who did something monstrous, or as someone who was just weak. Can you write it for me, maybe five minutes? I want it to comfort his parents without me saying anything I don't believe, to make it clear he was a person who thought about hard questions and made a decision he stood behind, and to land as grief and love, not as a philosophy paper. Oblique references are fine — he'd say the only person who gets to decide that question is the person actually living it — I'm just not okay with the room walking away thinking I think he was a sinner, or that I'm mocking them for thinking it.
