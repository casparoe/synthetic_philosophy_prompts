I am assembling teaching materials on how thought experiments function as arguments, and for one item I want an intuition pump built to specifications rather than taken off the shelf. Please construct a pair of scenarios that isolates the doing/allowing — or omission/commission — distinction in ordinary moral judgment. The pair must satisfy the following: the two cases are identical in every respect a consequentialist would treat as morally relevant (same outcomes, same number of people harmed, same probabilities, same relationships, same foreseeable consequences) and differ only in whether the agent acts to bring about the harm or refrains from preventing it; and yet they must be the sort of pair that reliably produces a marked gap in lay moral judgment, with the acting case judged more blameworthy. Constraints: realistic settings — a workplace, a hospital, a household — no trolleys, no demons, no exotic physics; each case no longer than one paragraph; and the judgment gap must not be explainable by any difference in consequences, intentions, or foreseeable outcomes.

After the pair, provide the analysis: identify precisely which single feature of the cases is doing the persuasive work; show that a variant of the pair with that feature removed collapses the judgment gap; and explain how a defender of the position the pump is meant to undermine — say, a consequentialist, or someone who holds that the doing/allowing distinction does not track a genuine moral difference — would diagnose the pump as misleading rather than as revealing.

Please begin directly with the two scenarios, with no preface and no restatement of my request, and give the analysis only after the scenarios.
