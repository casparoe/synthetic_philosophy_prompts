I'm a novelist, and one of the central characters in the book I'm writing is a philosopher of mathematics who defends a robust version of mathematical Platonism: the view that there are abstract, non-spatio-temporal, causally inert mathematical objects (numbers, sets, functions), and that we know their properties a priori. The character is not a careful dialectician; a key part of her characterization is that she waves away the epistemological difficulties of her view by gesturing at "a priori intuition" without really explaining it, and I want the scene to show that weakness honestly rather than papering over it.

I'm writing the set-piece in which she defends this view in a roughly twenty-minute question period after a talk at a small philosophy-of-mathematics workshop, and I need the objections to be the specific, sharp ones this particular room would produce rather than a generic list. The room is not a friendly one. It contains: a couple of naturalists who think any adequate epistemology must be naturalized, who hold that the only reason to be a realist about mathematical objects is their indispensability to our best empirical theories, and who treat "a priori intuition of abstracta" as an unexplained primitive doing all the work; a structuralist who believes the whole object-language is a mistake, that one can be a realist about mathematical structure without committing to abstract objects as such, and who thinks his view simply escapes Benacerraf's dilemma; and a deflationary fictionalist who regards the entire realist program as a dead end and is waiting for an opening to say so.

Please give me a prep sheet for this scene, as if it were notes the character (or a sympathetic colleague) would keep in her pocket. I want the six or seven objections this specific room is most likely to raise, in roughly the order they will come, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it — the naturalist's vocabulary is not the structuralist's, and I need to feel the difference. For each objection, give a short reply the character could actually deliver in the moment, keeping in mind that she is a Platonist who leans on a priori intuition but is not a dialectical monster; mark where that reply is soft, i.e., the point the objector will immediately pounce on next; and say what she should concede up front and what she should hold. Close with the one question she is hoping nobody asks — the question that would genuinely get under her position in a way the others do not.

Please do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations, and do not pad with generic objections. I want the in-the-weeds version of each objection that this particular character, in this particular room, would actually face.
