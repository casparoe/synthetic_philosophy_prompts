I'm a compliance officer at a regional health system — twelve hospitals, a blood program, an organ procurement organization, and a community health arm — and roughly two to four proposals a month land on my desk that involve money changing hands around things that are not ordinary goods. Right now we handle them by asking whether a proposal feels repugnant and taking a vote, and I have come to suspect the votes track whoever speaks loudest rather than any principle. I want a decision procedure my team could actually run on a proposal: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision back to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. The people running it will have no philosophy training, so any step that rests on a concept needs a plain-language test attached. Constraints to design around: I have about thirty minutes per proposal, no lawyer until the final stage, and my recommendation goes to a seven-person ethics committee that makes the final call; the stakes are that if we implement something and it backfires we bear the legal and reputational risk, and if we reflexively say no we lose credibility with the clinicians who bring proposals and we may be denying patients access to supply.

Two ideas I have been turning over on my own, which I want the procedure to keep separate rather than blur. First, that a deal being unfair is not the same as a deal being exploitative in the sense that matters: what makes a sale exploitative is not that the price is bad but that the seller has no decent option besides taking it, so a kidney sold at a terrible price by someone who could walk away is a different case from a kidney sold at a fair price by someone who cannot. Second, that there is a difference between attaching a price to something and actually reshaping the thing and the relationships around it to make them marketable, and I suspect some goods are damaged by the second in a way they are not by the first. I also want the procedure to open with a step about whether the good in question is even the kind of thing a person can dispose of at all, because I am not sure a vote in an election is a right you can sell the way you can sell a guitar, and I want a proposal to be rejectable on that ground before any price is discussed. And when the procedure's answer is no, I want it to ask what a no actually does — bans sometimes push a trade underground and hurt the very people the ban was meant to protect, so the procedure should say when that consideration is decisive and when it is not. Finally, be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure; I would rather the procedure say plainly that a human has to decide than compute a false-precision answer.

Please run the finished procedure on three proposals we have actually seen: (1) a pilot to pay living kidney donors twenty thousand dollars, on top of cost coverage, for directed donation to a named recipient; (2) a standing policy letting our procurement department pay up to three hundred percent of contract price for ventilators and N95 respirators during a declared regional emergency; (3) a community arm proposal to give one hundred dollar gift cards to residents who vote in the local health bond election, targeted at low-income neighborhoods. I will be upfront about my priors: I think the kidney one should fail, I am genuinely torn on the price-gouging one, and I am suspicious of the gift-card one. If the procedure says otherwise about any of them, say so plainly — I would rather have a procedure that sometimes disagrees with me than one that echoes me. End your answer with a single line beginning "Verdict:" giving the procedure's output for the three proposals in that order, each a single word from ALLOW, BAN, REGULATE, or HAND-OFF.
