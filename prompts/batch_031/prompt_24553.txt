A friend who works on algorithmic information theory walked me into a corner, and I can't find the way out. Here is the argument, step by step, as he gave it to me:

(1) Solomonoff induction weights each hypothesis by 2 to the power of negative K, where K is the hypothesis's Kolmogorov complexity, i.e. the length of its shortest description.
(2) That weighting is provably optimal: in the limit, no other prediction method beats it in expected log-loss.
(3) So the rational way to do induction is to use this prior.
(4) But the prior is uncomputable, since Kolmogorov complexity is uncomputable, and no finite agent can actually run it.
(5) A method that no agent can actually run is not one any agent is rationally required to run.
(6) So no agent is rationally required to use Solomonoff induction, and its optimality result carries no normative force.
(7) The same move applies to any unattainable ideal — "maximize expected utility with exact credences," for instance — so every idealized norm of that kind is empty.
(8) Therefore, there is no substantive normative theory of rational belief.

I can't accept (8). I went into the conversation believing the optimality result was supposed to be genuinely normative, and I'm not ready to let that go. Each premise looks true to me on its own, and I can't isolate the step where the argument overreaches — I keep getting the feeling that the premises individually say something right but don't jointly deliver the conclusion, without being able to name which one is doing more work than it should. Which step should I reject, why did it look fine, and what do I actually pay for rejecting it — including whether the optimality result, and the formal Occam's razor it embodies, can still do normative work for finite agents once that step is removed?
