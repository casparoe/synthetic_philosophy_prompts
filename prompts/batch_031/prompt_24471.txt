So, a bit of background: I'm a retired physicist, spent most of my career in cosmology, and I've only recently started reading philosophy of science, mostly out of irritation at how philosophers talk about my field. I'd like you to write an essay on whether "underdetermination of theory by evidence" is a real, substantive constraint on what science can actually conclude, or whether it's mostly a vacuous logical point that evaporates once you look at a real case.

Use the dark-matter vs. modified-Newtonian-dynamics (MOND) debate as your test case, because it's the one I keep coming back to. Rough state of play as I understand it: MOND (Milgrom's low-acceleration modification of gravity) fits galaxy rotation curves and the baryonic Tully-Fisher relation remarkably well without any dark matter, while the standard ΛCDM dark-matter model fits the cosmic microwave background, large-scale structure, and gravitational lensing beautifully. The tensions run both ways: MOND struggles with the CMB and with galaxy clusters (which seem to want more mass than MOND supplies, even after you bolt warm neutrinos on as a patch), and ΛCDM has its own galactic-scale headaches, the cusp-core and missing-satellites business. So the essay's job is to say: is this a genuine case of two theories underdetermined by the evidence, or is it just ordinary scientific competition where the data arent exhausted yet and the next good measurement will break the tie? And, more sharply, does the Duhem-Quine point (any theory can be shielded from any falsifying datum by tinkering with the auxiliary hypotheses) actually bite in a case like this, or is it a trivial logical truth that dissolves the moment you demand the rescue not be ad hoc?

Weigh the arguments on both sides and don't leave it hanging. I want you to actually reach a verdict and defend it. I don't care whether your verdict is "underdetermination is real and this case shows it" or "underdetermination is mostly a philosophical myth and this is just pending data" — commit and justify.
