i've been thinking about skolem's paradox for a while now and i keep building arguments that seem to prove there are no determinate mathematical facts. i can't find the exit. here's the latest one, and i think it's the cleanest i've managed:

(1) by the downward löwenheim-skolem theorem, if a first-order theory has an infinite model, it has a countable model.
(2) assuming zfc is consistent, zfc has a model.
(3) so zfc has a countable model m.
(4) from the outside (in the metatheory), m is countable: there's a surjection f from ω onto m.
(5) but m satisfies "there is no surjection from ω onto p(ω)" — cantor's theorem holds inside m.
(6) so f is not in m, and m doesn't "see" f as a set.
(7) so whether a set is countable depends on which model you're looking from: m thinks p^m(ω) is uncountable, but from the outside p^m(ω) is a subset of m and hence countable.
(8) so "countable" doesn't have a determinate, model-independent meaning.
(9) by the same reasoning, any property expressible in the first-order language of set theory is model-relative: whether a structure is "a model of zfc," "well-ordered," "the natural numbers," etc., depends on which model you're looking from.
(10) so no first-order statement has a determinat, model-independent truth value.
(11) but "2 + 2 = 4" is true, determinately.
(12) so something in (1)–(11) is wrong.

i feel like something slipped between (8) and (9) but i can't name it. which step do i reject, and what does rejecting it cost? i'd rather keep (1) and (11) than anything else, but i want to know what i'm paying.
