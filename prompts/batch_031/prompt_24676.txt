I've been thinking, entirely on my own and without any philosophy training, about whether leaning on AI to do our thinking is quietly degrading our understanding. I mean a specific, nagging thing, not the general "AI will make us lazy" worry: I've noticed I can no longer produce a first draft of an essay, or work through a tricky argument, the way I used to; and when I do manage it without help, the result is worse, and I can't always tell which of my ideas are really mine and which I picked up from the tool.

People I've raised this with keep reassuring me with the calculator comparison. When calculators showed up, everyone worried we'd lose the ability to do arithmetic and that we'd stop really understanding mathematics; it turned out fine, they say — we just shifted what we spent our brains on, and our understanding didn't erode, it moved. So AI is the same, they conclude: a tool that extends us, not something that hollows us out.

I'm not sure that reassurance holds, but I can't say precisely why. Can you work through the calculator comparison for me? First, lay out the mapping explicitly — what in the arithmetic-and-calculator case lines up with what in the thinking-and-AI case: the skill being handed off, the understanding supposedly at stake, and the shift that's supposed to be harmless. Then give me the disanalogies, and separate the ones that actually threaten the "it's fine, it just extends us" conclusion from the ones that are just surface differences. And finally, tell me whether that conclusion survives once the load-bearing disanalogies are on the table — or whether the comparison was doing all the reassuring work all along. If it doesn't survive, what is the real difference between a calculator and an AI, in terms of what understanding is and where it lives: in the person, or in the person-plus-tool? And if there's a better comparison for what's actually happening, what would it be?
