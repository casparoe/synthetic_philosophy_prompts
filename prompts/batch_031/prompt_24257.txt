For a seminar in political philosophy I am preparing a discussion that brings the parable of the Grand Inquisitor in The Brothers Karamazov into contact with the modern literature on libertarian paternalism, and I would like a careful, self-contained essay on the following question.

Suppose that Fyodor Dostoevsky, in the years following The Brothers Karamazov, were introduced to the "nudge" program associated with Thaler and Sunstein, on which one can shape the choices of otherwise free agents by altering the framing, defaults, and salience of the options available to them, without removing any of the options they could have chosen. The standard liberal defense of this approach is that it is paternalistic in intent but libertarian in form: because a nudge leaves the target free to do otherwise, it does not coerce and so does not violate the autonomy that the liberal tradition holds to be the core of freedom.

In the parable of the Grand Inquisitor, Christ's refusal to turn stones into bread, to leap from the temple, and to accept dominion over the kingdoms of the earth is presented not as the gift of a too-burdensome freedom but as the very thing the Church later takes upon itself: the Inquisitor offers humanity bread, miracle, and authority in place of the freedom Christ would have left them to bear. The Inquisitor's charge is that human beings are weak, that they cannot bear the responsibility of freedom, and that a benevolent authority which secures their happiness and relieves them of the burden of choice is a mercy, not a tyranny.

Given this, what would Dostoevsky most plausibly have made of libertarian paternalism and nudging? Consider, in particular, the following. First, the nudge leaves every option formally open, so the libertarian-paternalist can say it preserves freedom. But the Inquisitor's point, on a natural reading, is not about the removal of options but about the transfer of the burden of choice and responsibility: a nudge that quietly makes one option feel easier or more salient, and so effectively decides for the agent while pretending to leave the choice to them, may be precisely the kind of "mercy" the Inquisitor defends. Second, does the libertarian-paternalist claim that nudging preserves freedom survive contact with the Inquisitor's deeper claim that what is unbearable about freedom is not the loss of options but the weight of having to decide for oneself — and that this weight is exactly what the nudge, by tilting the choice, removes?

Argue for both the reading on which Dostoevsky would have been sympathetic to nudging (as a gentle, non-coercive form of care that leaves the agent's ultimate sovereignty intact) and the reading on which he would have condemned it (as a sophisticated, self-justifying form of the very authority the Inquisitor embodies, one that mistakes the preservation of options for the preservation of the soul). Then take a position on which reading is more faithful to the parable and to Dostoevsky's broader moral vision, and say what, on your account, the nudge's libertarian-paternalist defense gets right and what it gets wrong.

Argue from the content of the parable and the structure of the argument as I have sketched them, rather than from secondary literature or citations.
