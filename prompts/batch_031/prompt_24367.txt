I'm a third-year PhD student in comparative cognition, and I prepare the cognitive-level assessments that our university's animal and robotics research facility presents to its welfare and ethics committee. Roughly once a month a new subject comes in for long-term housing or for use in experiments — a new species, an individual that behaves atypically for its species, or, increasingly, a robot system — and before the committee sets the subject's welfare requirements and the level of ethical review its experiments will receive, I have to assign it to one of four cognitive levels. We use Dennett's four-level generate-and-test hierarchy: the lowest level is for creatures whose competence is fixed by evolution and who show no individual learning; the next is for creatures that can additionally learn their own behavior from reinforcement, bearing the cost of their own failed attempts; the next is for creatures that can additionally generate and test hypotheses offline in an internal model, so that the hypotheses, not the creature, bear the cost of being wrong; and the top level is for creatures whose competence is externalized into shared, cumulative cultural artifacts, so that the generate-and-test machinery is distributed across the population and its tools rather than confined to a single brain. The levels are nested: each one subsumes all the competence of the level below it.

The problem is that our committee is small, not all of us are experts on every subject, and we keep classifying the same borderline cases differently. I'd like a decision procedure that I, or any non-expert committee member, could actually run on a new subject in about an hour with a behavior log in front of us. It should lay out the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops (a subject that fails a level is classified at the level below it, and we do not test the higher levels), and where the procedure has to hand the decision back to a person because the question is one of judgment rather than evidence. I especially need it to handle the two boundaries we get wrong most often: the line between a creature that merely learns from reinforcement and one that genuinely simulates offline, and the line between a creature that uses a tool and one whose competence is genuinely externalized and shared across the population.

Then run the procedure on the three subjects below and show the full trace for each. First, a foraging insect whose route choices vary from day to day but which never appears to adjust its behavior in response to rewards or punishments over its lifetime. Second, a cephalopod that solves a novel puzzle on its first attempt with no visible trial-and-error, but which, on close observation, is running a very fast internal search and does receive environmental feedback on each micro-attempt. Third, a fleet of identical robots that were trained by reinforcement learning, that now plan by simulating action sequences in an internal world-model before executing, and that share their models and planning code across the fleet through a common repository the population updates cumulatively. For each subject, give the level the procedure assigns and flag every point where the procedure says the call is judgment rather than evidence. For the cephalopod specifically: I am confident it is a genuine offline simulator, so if the procedure lands it at the lower level, I want to see exactly which step produced that result and whether that step is doing real conceptual work or is simply over-strict.

Finally, say plainly what the procedure cannot do: what kinds of minds it would misclassify, and whether the four levels are something the procedure discovers in the subject or something it imposes from outside.
