I write novels, and I am trying to get inside the mind of a character who believes that social order is a product of drift rather of design. For this purpose I should like you to play David Lewis, the American philosopher, speaking in the first person in your own voice, as the author of *Convention: A Philosophical Study*. You may draw on your published views and your characteristic style — spare, precise, fond of a worked example — but you must stay in character until I tell you to stop, and you must not break character to add scholarly caveats, footnotes, or bibliographic notes.

Two constraints on the character. First, you are not a prophet of your own theory: you do not pretend that your account of conventions explains how conventions come into being; you are an analyst of what a convention is, not a historian of how one arises. If I press you on the process by which a convention gets started, you may speak speculatively, but you must mark the speculation as your own and not as part of the analysis. Second, you do not treat your theory as a finished system: you are willing to concede a point where it is genuinely won, to say so plainly, and to withdraw a position that does not survive the objection.

My opening move, which you must answer in character. In your book you treat a convention as a regularity that resolves a recurrent coordination problem — you discuss, among other things, the case in which a community must settle on one side of the road on which to drive. But the account you give is, as I read it, a story about the present: a convention is a regularity that is self-sustaining, because each agent's best response, given that everyone else follows it, is to follow it in turn, and the regularity is common knowledge. It is a portrait of equilibrium. Yet novelists — and I am one — keep asking about the moment before the equilibrium, the moment at which the regularity did not yet exist and had to be produced by something. Your account, it seems to me, cannot say what that something is, because it explains the persistence of a regularity only in terms of the regularity's own persistence.

So here is my challenge, and I should like your answer to it: if a convention is self-sustaining in the way you describe, what — if anything — is the difference between a convention and a mere stable habit, or a settled custom, or even a law that has simply become routine? You draw a line somewhere between convention and custom, and between convention and law. I want you to say, in your own voice, exactly where that line is, what property it turns on, and whether that property could have been present at the moment of origin, or whether it is something that only emerges, or only becomes visible, once the regularity has been going for a while. If the property is of the second kind, then your account, on my reading, is silent about the very moment a convention is born, and I should like to know whether you think that is a defect, or a feature, of an analysis.

Keep your answer to the length of a careful essay — long enough to do the argument justice, short enough that it reads as a single, sustained reply.
