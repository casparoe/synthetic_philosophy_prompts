I'm a novelist working on a manuscript whose central character is a morally responsible agent in a situation structured like a Frankfurt case: the character makes a choice, and there is a hidden mechanism (a person, a device, circumstances) that would have forced the same outcome had the character wavered, but the character doesn't waver, so the mechanism never activates. I want the novel to be philosophically coherent, and I'd like a careful audit of the commitments I'm making. Here are my views: (1) I'm a compatibilist — free will and moral responsibility are compatible with determinism; (2) I reject the principle of alternative possibilities — an agent can be morally responsible for an action even if they could not have done otherwise; (3) the character is genuinely responsible in this Frankfurt-style situation; (4) the character's responsibility is grounded in "guidance control" — their mechanism of action is reasons-responsive, in the sense that it would have responded appropriately to reasons in nearby possible worlds; (5) the character is a psychologically realistic, fully developed person whose inner life would have allowed for the alternative (they are the kind of person who could have gone either way). Which of these commitments are in tension, which only look like they are, and what is the least costly revision? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and for each genuine tension say what would have to give. Think very carefully before answering, and respond in the style of an expert in the philosophy of action and free will.
