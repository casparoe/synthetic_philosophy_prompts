I'm giving a talk at a small philosophy-of-biology workshop next month and want a Q&A prep sheet for it. The position I'll be defending: the etiological, or "selection-for," account of biological function (the one associated with Millikan and Dretske) is not independent of the causal-role, or contribution-to-fitness, account. My specific claim is that when the etiological account explains how a trait that has only just arisen — a novel mutation, for instance — acquires its *first* function, it must appeal to the causal role that trait was already playing in its environment before any selection had taken place; selection can only act on a difference-making effect, and that effect is a causal role. So the etiological account's "selection for" relation is, at the foundation, parasitic on the causal-role account, and the causal-role account is the more fundamental of the two. I'll be arguing that the etiologicalist's standard replies to the problem of first functions and to exaptation quietly concede exactly this point.

The room is roughly: three or four committed etiologicalists who know the selection-for account and its stock responses inside out; a handful of evolutionary biologists, mostly population geneticists, who are wary of teleological vocabulary and who think of fitness as a property of genotypes and phenotypes in a population rather than of individual traits in an individual organism; and a couple of general philosophers of biology who are agnostic. No one in the room is a specialist in the etiology-versus-causal-role debate itself, so they'll come at my thesis from their own home turf, which I expect will make the objections different from the ones I'd get at a general philosophy conference.

Please draft the prep sheet: the seven or eight objections I'm actually likely to get from this particular audience, ordered by how much damage each would do to my thesis if I fumbled it. For each objection, (a) phrase it the way a member of this audience would actually phrase it, in their own vocabulary; (b) give me a two-minute reply; and (c) note honestly where my reply is softest — the spot where a sharp etiologicalist or a sharp population geneticist could push back and I'd have no clean answer. Then include the one objection I'm most hoping nobody asks, and tell me what to do if it does come.

One more thing, and I mean it as a hard constraint: throughout, separate what is settled among people who actually work on this topic from what is genuinely contested. If a point in my thesis, or a reply I'm planning to give, treats a genuinely disputed view as though it were the consensus, flag it. I'd rather be warned in advance that my "settled" assumption is a live dispute than be blindsided in the question period.
