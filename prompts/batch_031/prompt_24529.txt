I've been working through the critiques of effective altruism — the institutional critique, the earning-to-give practice, and what I've been calling the measurability bias — and I keep hitting the same objection: that the measurability critique is a complaint about our instruments, not a real distortion of the decisions we should make. I was discussing this with a friend who works in global health, and they kept insisting that if you can't measure an intervention's impact, the right response is simply to rank it lower, and that calling that a "bias" is just relabeling rational caution. I want to settle the matter by constructing a case in which the measurability critique has genuine, non-accidental bite. So: construct a thought experiment to the following spec.

The case is for showing that the measurability bias produces an actual ranking divergence that cannot be explained by any difference in values — so a case that merely stipulates the verdict (asserts that the measurable option gets ranked higher) rather than generating the divergence from the two decision rules does not count. Constraints: it must be realistic (an individual earning-to-give donor or a small grantmaking organization; no trolleys, no exotic physics, payoffs a normal person could actually face); it must involve two interventions with comparable true expected impact that are identical in every respect except measurability — same domain, same underlying mechanism, same cost, one of them verifiable by an independent randomized trial and the other not; and it must be evaluated by two decision frameworks, which I'll call A and B. Framework A is the standard effective-altruism expected-value-per-dollar calculation, which takes a low-confidence, wide estimate of impact and collapses it into a low point estimate — equivalently, it uses the median or a conservative quantile, or multiplies the point estimate by a confidence factor below one. Framework B is a measurability-aware rule, which preserves the full probability distribution over impact and applies a decision rule sensitive to the right tail, such as the expected value of the distribution itself or a robustness / minimax-regret rule. The case must show that A ranks the measurable intervention higher while B ranks the hard-to-measure one higher, and that the divergence is not due to a value difference.

After the scenario, walk through the reasoning for each framework and why it gives its verdict; say which structural feature of the case is doing the work — I'm guessing it's A's confidence-discounting / point-estimate collapse rather than the raw absence of data, but tell me if you think otherwise, and say what happens to the divergence if that feature is removed; and then give the strongest attack a defender of framework A would make on the case (for example, that measurability is itself evidence of real impact, so the low expected value is justified rather than biased, or that B's fat right tails are arbitrary and simply reward mystery). Keep the entire response — scenario plus walk-through — under 700 words.
