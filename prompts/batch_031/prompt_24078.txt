I've come to defend a version of what's often called Chesterton's fence — the Burkean principle that one should not remove or alter an existing institution or practice until one can explain why it was put in place, on the grounds that the mere persistence of something is itself a reason to treat it as presumptively rational. I want to treat this as a genuine epistemic and practical norm, not just a conservative bias. What would count against it? I want the objections ranked from genuinely decisive to merely awkward, including the cases that seem to break it: the fence erected by accident or for a reason that has since lapsed, the fence erected for a bad reason (say, to exclude a group), and the fence whose original justification no one can now reconstruct. I also want an honest account of whether the principle has been qualified into unfalsifiability — whether "you haven't explained the fence yet" can be waved in front of every proposed change — and whether that is a defect or a feature. And tell me which of these objections a committed defender would have to concede as real versus dismiss, and where you land on whether the principle survives its strongest critics as a general norm or degrades into a narrow heuristic about the costs of change.
