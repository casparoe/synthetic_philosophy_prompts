My half-formed idea, which I've been bouncing around with my advisor, is that the Church-Turing thesis is usually treated as a single settled claim, but I think it's really two claims with different epistemic statuses: the logical, effective-procedure version (that every effectively computable function is Turing-computable) and the physical version (that no physical system can compute a function that is not Turing-computable). My hunch is that the logical version is best understood as a conceptual or a priori claim that rests on Turing's argument about what an effective procedure must be like, whereas the physical version is genuinely empirical — it's a claim about what the world can do, and it's the version that the hypercomputation literature is actually trying to challenge. And I suspect that the familiar move of simply defining computability to mean Turing-computability, which makes the thesis trivial, works for the logical version but not for the physical one, because the physical version isn't about what our concept of computation picks out but about what physical systems can in fact realize.
