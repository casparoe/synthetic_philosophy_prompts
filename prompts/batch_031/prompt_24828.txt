I had a long argument with a friend last night about panpsychism, and I'm left with a distinction I keep needing but can't put a name on. Let me lay out the shape of it.

My friend was defending the view that fundamental physical constituents have something like experience, and I raised what I take to be the standard objection: if the constituents have experience, how do their experiences add up to mine? He answered, in effect, that they don't add up to mine — the macro-experience is a new, emergent thing, not the sum of the micro-experiences — and I replied that this seems to make the position worse rather than better. I think two distinct things go wrong here, and I'm not sure the debate treats them as distinct.

The first is a quantitative thing. If every fundamental constituent is a subject, then so is every lump of constituents, and so is every part of every lump. A single brain would then contain not one subject per neuron, but something like one per particle, and each of those subjects would itself have proper parts that are subjects, and so on, with no principled cut-off anywhere in the hierarchy. The view doesn't just give me a lot of company; it gives me an unbounded stack of subjects at every scale, almost none of which are ever the subject of any experience anyone can report.

The second is a structural thing, and it's the one I'm after. Even granting that the macro-experience is emergent and not a sum, the micro-experiences are not the constituents of it. My experience is not made out of the experiences of my neurons any more than a table is made out of the tables inside it. The micro-subjects are nested inside the macro-subjects in the physical sense, but the larger subject's experience is not the smaller subjects' experiences, and nothing in the picture so far explains why the micro-experiences should be relevant to the macro-experience at all. They just sit there, in parallel, and the macro-experience appears as if from nowhere. On this reading, the view is not so much the claim that experience is fundamental as the claim that experience is redundantly fundamental: it is fundamental at every level, and the levels are unrelated to one another.

My question: is there an established name for the second thing — the nested, non-constitutive one — as distinct from the first, and as distinct from the general "how do micro-experiences combine into macro-experiences?" objection that my friend was answering? I'm not satisfied with a bare name. I want to know (a) what the nearest established concepts are, (b) how the thing I'm describing differs from each of them, and (c) which, if any, fits best, and where its boundaries are. And if the honest answer is that the literature does not draw this line, I'd like a proposed name and a definition precise enough to tell the second thing apart from the first and from the general combination objection. I don't need a reading list; I want the conceptual carving.
