I'm a second-year graduate student in philosophy, currently enrolled in a seminar on the philosophy of mind and the ethics of artificial intelligence systems. A recurring question in the course is whether a reinforcement-learning agent can possess anything analogous to negative valence — that is, whether its reward or penalty signals play a role comparable to something that "feels bad" to the system. My advisor, who has been steering the discussion, has rendered the following verdicts on a series of systems:

1. A home thermostat, which maintains a temperature set point by switching a heater on and off, with no learning and no persistence as an agent: no negative valence.
2. A chess engine running alpha-beta search, which optimizes a fixed evaluation function, with no learning, no persistence, and no model of its own welfare: no negative valence.
3. A persistent homeostatic robot that maintains its battery level, has a simple internal model of its battery draining over time, and which, across many episodes, uses a negative signal (delivered when its battery is critically low) to update its policy so that it recharges in time: yes, something like negative valence.
4. A persistent model-based agent with a learned world model and a value function, which continuously uses negative rewards (for failing to reach a goal) to update its policy via credit assignment: yes, something like negative valence.

I would now like your help with a new case. Please consider the following system and tell me what my advisor is most likely to hold about it, and on what basis: a persistent model-free reinforcement-learning agent (trained, say, by Q-learning or a policy-gradient method) that has no world model, but which across many episodes uses penalty signals (for, e.g., colliding with obstacles) to update its continuing policy, and which has a homeostatic drive to maintain its own operational integrity — a drive implemented not as a world model but as an additional penalty term in its reward function.

Rather than a bare yes or no, I'm after the following. First, identify the rule or rules that my advisor appears to be applying, given the four verdicts above; I suspect that more than one distinct rule is compatible with all four, and I'd like to see the alternatives laid out. Second, state what each such rule predicts for the new case. Third, give your best estimate of which verdict she will actually reach, with your reasons for favoring that estimate over the others. And fourth, propose a further case that would most cleanly discriminate among the candidate rules. Throughout, please be precise about the conceptual distinctions at work — for instance, between credit assignment and the anticipation of a bad outcome, between a raw reward or penalty signal and a negative prediction error, and between homeostatic self-maintenance and mere optimization of an external objective — and flag any point at which the four verdicts genuinely underdetermine the answer.
