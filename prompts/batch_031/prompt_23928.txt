I've been going back and forth on the leveling-down objection to prioritarianism and I think the best way to figure out what I actually believe is to just lay out my gut reactions to a bunch of cases and see if there's a principle that captures them. So here's the setup: two people, A is the worst-off (let's say welfare 50) and B is the better-off (welfare 100). A policy can transfer welfare between them or do nothing. My verdicts:

If A loses 1 unit and B gains 1 unit, that's obviously bad — we're making the worst-off worse off to make the better-off better off, wich feels wrong. If A loses 1 and B gains 10, still bad, even though the net gain is positive, because A is the worst-off and their welfare should count for more. If A loses 1 and B gains 100, I still think it's bad but I'm starting to waver — the gain to B is really big. If A loses 1 and B gains 1000, I genuinely don't know. On one hand, 1000 units is a lot of welfare. On the other hand, I have this strong intuition that making the worst-off worse off is a bad thing, full stop, and no amount of gain to the better-off should justify it. If A loses 100 and B gains 1000, that's clearly bad — the loss to A is just too big. And if A loses 1 and B gains 10 but B is a child, I think it's actually worse than the case where B is an adult, because the gain to a child should count for more (or maybe less? I'm not sure).

Is there a principle that gets all of these, or at least most of them? And what does it say about the case I keep going back and forth on — A loses 1, B gains 1000? I'm also not sure I'm framing the question right. Is the real question "should we ever make the worst-off worse off to make the better-off better off?" or is it "how much weight should we give to the worst-off's welfare?" Those seem like different questions and I can't tell how they're supposed to relate.
