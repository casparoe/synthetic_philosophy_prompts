So at a dinner last week two of my friends got into a long argument and I honestly couldnt tell who was making sense. One of them, let's call him Dan, was making this case that we shouldn't really trust modern science to be telling us the truth, and the other, Priya, was pushing back hard. I was just listening in.

Dan started with the history. He said every time scientists have had what they thought was the best theory, it turned out to be wrong. He listed off phlogiston, the caloric fluid, the luminiferous ether, the whole epicycles-and-geocentric setup, the the steady-state universe. "So," he said, "by the exact same logic, the standard model, quantum field theory, even evolution, are probably wrong too. We've got no reason to think this round is any different." He called it a kind of meta-induction, an induction about induction, and said it's just common sense.

Priya said that's a bad argument because you're building your sample out of the losers. She said for every phlogiston there are a hundred old ideas that were basically right and just got refined, and we don't remember those. She also said the real pattern is continuity, not replacement: we've been talking about atoms for thousands of years and that core idea survived all the way through, and electrons and genes and DNA stuck around even as the theories around them changed. "The entities stayed, the details got fixed," she said. "That's convergence on the truth, not a history of getting it wrong."

Dan said the ether ruins her continuity story. He said the wave theory of light in a medium was a genuinely successful theory — it derived interference, diffraction, the speed of light, all of it fit the data really well — and it was completely wrong, there is no ether. So empirical success and precision don't mean you've got the real story. And he said her "the entities stayed" point is a cop-out: if the theory is false, the electrons it posits are just convenient fictions until the next theory happens to re-derive them, and you can't say the theory is probably false but the stuff in it is probably real. That's ad hoc, he said.

Priya answered that the ether is actually an argument for her side, not his. She said we only figured out the ether was wrong because we had a better theory, relativity, that explained more, and the whole history is theories getting better and converging. "If we were just making stuff up, we wouldn't keep landing on better explanations," she said. And she said even granting that the current theory is eventually replaced, that doesn't make science worthless — the entities and the math carry forward.

Dan's last move was to say that "converging on the truth" is just assuming the destination. He said you can't use the thing you're trying to prove, that we're getting it right, as a premise, and that the pessimistic point is just: past best theories were false, so present best theories are probably false, full stop.

They both thought the other was being a bit of a fool and asked me to referee, which I can't do. Can you go through this move by move and say who's right about which specific point, not just who wins overall? And can you be clear about what's actually settled among people who think about this a lot versus what's genuinely still contested, because I don't want you to present one side's position as if it's the consensus.
