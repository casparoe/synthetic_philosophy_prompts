I'm working through Aumann's agreement theorem in a decision-theory course and keep finding that my gut reactions to two-person belief scenarios do not line up cleanly with the theorem's stated conditions, so I would like to do some reflective equilibrium on them. Here are my verdicts on when I think two rational forecasters are forced to converge versus when I think they may rationally keep disagreeing, stated the way I actually feel them. First, two handicappers start from the same base rate for a horse race, each sees a different private signal about the track, and then they announce their final probabilities to one another and it is common knowledge that each has heard the other; I think they are forced to land on the same number. Second, two clinicians with different training — one learned her base rate in a high-prevalence region, the other in a low-prevalence one — examine the same patient, see the same test results, and share their final probabilities; I think they are allowed to keep disagreeing, but only within a bounded margin, and that margin should shrink the closer their training populations were. Third, two analysts with the same prior, but one knows the other's estimate while the other does not know that the first knows it; I think they can keep disagreeing indefinitely, because the chain of mutual knowledge is broken somewhere. Fourth, two agents with the same prior, but one is ambiguity-averse and reports a range rather than a single number; I think they can disagree even though the prior is common.

The pair I keep going back and forth on is this. On one hand, I am confident that if two people in the first scenario simply keep announcing their numbers back and forth, the sequence settles down to a common value, so I treat convergence as guaranteed whenever the common-prior and common-knowledge conditions are genuinely met. On the other hand, I have a strong gut that in any situation that could actually be described in a finite amount of language — where the mutual knowledge only goes a few levels deep, something like I know that you know that I know, but not the fourth level — the two people should still already be forced to agree, because three levels of mutual knowledge feels like it ought to be enough, yet I also suspect the formal result says you need the full infinite regress. So I am torn between the claim that finite mutual knowledge should already force agreement and the claim that you really need the whole regress.

Is there a single principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures all of these verdicts, stated precisely enough that I can see which clause is doing the work in which case? For each candidate I would like you to say which of my cases motivates each clause, what it predicts for a case I have not listed — for instance, two agents who share a common prior but where one of them is not strictly Bayesian and instead updates by a rule that only approximately satisfies Bayes' rule — and, honestly, which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. In particular, I want to know whether the cleaner principle forces me to drop the three-levels-of-mutual-knowledge verdict, and if so, what I actually gain by dropping it.
