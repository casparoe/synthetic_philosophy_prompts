I was at a dinner party last week and the conversation drifted to the usual "the world is a mess, what's the point" territory, and one guy — a genuinly smart guy, not a crank — kept landing on "well, it's the best of all possible worlds" like that was doing some real argumentative work. He didn't seem to be doing the Voltaire/Pangloss thing on purpose, he actually seemed to think there was a defensible claim in there. I want to be able to say "okay, but which of these do you mean?" so I'm trying to figure out what that one sentence could actually be claiming before I go back and pick on him.

Can you lay out the distinct precise theses "this is the best of all possible worlds" could be expressing? I'm most interested in the ones that turn on three axes: what the candidate set is (is "possible" doing heavy lifting here — is he quantifying over every logically possible world, or only worlds with the same laws of nature, or only worlds that could have coexisted with the particular kinds of things that actually exist, or something even narrower); what "best" is ordered by (total sum of value, average, some "perfection"-type metric, or just undominated in a partial order where there's no single winner); and the direction of the theistic move (is "best" supposed to be an independent fact about the world from which we then infer a benevolent chooser, or a fact about the chooser's decision that's supposed to be doing the explaining). For each reading, tell me whether it's trivial, substantive and defensible, or actually false, and — this is the part I care about most — which of the readings would actually let him get his conclusion that we shouldn't be dissatisfied with how things are. I want a menu I can carry into the conversation, not a pick-one-and-defend-it, and if one reading is the one he's probably meaning even though it's the weakest, say so.
