I've been sorting out my intuitions about when someone has the right to criticize another person for a wrongdoing they themselves have committed. A brother who quit smoking a year ago tells me, a current smoker, to quit: fine — his past doesn't disqualify him, but it gives him no more right to say it than a person who never smoked. A friend who has been quietly under-reporting his income for years and is still doing it wants to say something to a colleague who has just done a much bigger evasion: not fine — I can't shake the feeling that he has no right to be the one to raise it, even though the scale is very different. A coach who doped early in his career, admits it, and now coaches teenagers wants to ban them from doing so: fine — and I think he has more right to say it than a coach who never doped, because he can speak to why it's tempting and what it costs. A parent who admits to lying to their kid when they were small, to protect them, now wants to scold the kid for lying: fine — the kinds of lying are different enough. A friend who once borrowed money and never returned it now wants to pressure their roommate to pay rent on time: fine — the domains are different enough that the past doesn't carry over. Is there a principle, or a small set of candidates, that captures all of these or comes close, stated precisely enough that I can tell which of my cases each clause is meant to cover? What does it predict for a case I haven't listed — a person who has never done the thing and has never been tempted to, criticizing someone who has? And if no principle accommodates all my verdicts, which one does it force me to drop, and would I actually drop it?
