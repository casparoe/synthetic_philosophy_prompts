I'm working through the foundations of diachronic rationality — specifically, what principle, if any, should govern an agent's changes of belief over time — and I've been trying to pin down what I actually think by running a series of money-pump scenarios past my gut and recording the verdicts. I'd like to know whether a coherent principle captures the pattern, or whether some of my verdicts have to go, and if so, which.

Here are my verdicts on five cases. (1) The classic two-stage pump: at 9 a.m. I'm certain it will rain tomorrow, and a bookie offers to sell me a bet on rain for $0.99 (I pay $0.99 and receive $1 if it rains); I buy it. At 5 p.m., with no new information, I'm certain it will not rain, and a bookie offers to sell me a bet on no-rain for $0.99; I buy it. I'm $0.98 poorer, and I judge the change of mind to be irrational — I should have refused the second bet or precommitted at 9 a.m. to holding my credence. (2) A case with genuine new evidence: at 9 a.m. I look at a coin and am certain it's heads; at 5 p.m. a reliable witness tells me it's tails, and I update to certainty of tails. I judge this change of mind to be rational; the two-stage pump doesn't apply because I received new evidence. (3) The Sleeping Beauty setup: a fair coin is flipped; if heads I'm interviewed Monday only, if tails Monday and Tuesday, with memory erased between interviews. When I wake up interviewed, I assign credence 1/3 to heads, and I judge this correct, partly because I see that a bookie offering a bet that pays $1 if heads and costs $1 if tails at each waking moment would pump me if I held 1/2 — I'd accept it at both Monday and Tuesday in the tails branch, losing $2 in total against a $1 gain in the heads branch, for an expected loss of fifty cents. (4) A precommitment case: I'm hungry now and know I'll be full in an hour; a baker offers a $5 option to bind my future self to not buy a $10 cake; I buy it, judging it rational because my future self is biased by satiety. (5) A case where my future self will have better information: I can pay $10 now to bind my future self to not sell a stock if the market drops, but my future self will have watched a full year of market data; I decline, judging that I should trust my future self's superior information rather than precommit.

The pair that keeps me up at night is (1) and (3). In both, my "evidence" at the later time is just that I'm in a different situation than before — it's 5 p.m. instead of 9 a.m., I'm awake instead of asleep — and in both a bookie could in principle exploit the sequence of my beliefs. Yet I judge the change of mind in (1) irrational and the update in (3) rational. Is there a single principle of diachronic rationality that delivers all five verdicts, or do I have to give one up? If I have to give one up, which would you overturn and why? And what does the surviving principle say about a case I haven't listed: a bookie who, over 100 rounds, offers me a bet on a proposition whose truth I'm certain of in even rounds and certain of the negation of in odd rounds, at a price of $0.99 each time — should I precommit to a fixed strategy, and if so, what is it?
