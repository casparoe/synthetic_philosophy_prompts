I'm gettin a bit stuck on some Adam Smith for a paper, specifically the impartial spectator stuff, and I'd like a second pair of eyes on a passage. It's from Book III of the Theory of Moral Sentiments (1759):

"In order to pervert the rectitude of our own judgments concerning the propriety of our own conduct, it is not always necessary that the real and impartial spectator should be at a great distance. When he is at hand, when he is present, the violence and injustice of our own selfish passions are sometimes sufficient to induce the man within the breast to make a report very different from what the real circumstances of the case are capable of authorising."

And a little further on:

"when the paroxysm of emotion, in the same manner as when the paroxysm of distress, is fairly over, we can identify ourselves, as it were, with the ideal man within the breast, and, in our own character, view, as in the one case, our own situation, so in the other, our own conduct, with the severe eyes of the most impartial spectator."

Plus this one: "This self-deceit, this fatal weakness of mankind, is the source of half the disorders of human life."

Here's the bit that's bugging me. The whole point of the impartial spectator, as far as I can tell, is to give us a disinterested third-party vantage point, a way out of our own self-love. But this "man within the breast" is just that spectator internalized, so it's still us, inside us. And yet Smith here basically concedes that this internalized judge can be corrupted, our "selfish passions" get it to "make a report very different from what the real circumstances of the case are capable of authorising." So the judge inside us is subject to the exact bias the external impartial spectator was supposed to eliminate.

I want help making sense of that tension. Can you work through:

1. Is the "man within the breast" supposed to be a genuinely impartial judge, or is it more like a trained, idealized version of our own sympathy that we can call on when we're calm? What's the actual mechanism by which it's supposed to stay impartial once it's inside us?

2. Does Smith think internalizing the spectator solves the impartiality problem, or does it just relocate it? He seems to grant it can be warped, so I'm not sure his move is a solution at all. Maybe it's a pragmatic or therapeutic device (the "severe eyes" only kick in after the "paroxysm of emotion" has passed) rather than an epistemic fix. Help me figure out wich reading is better supported by the text.

3. If the internalized spectator is just us projecting an idealized other, is there any sense in which it's really "other" enough to be impartial, or is Smith quietly smuggling in a circularity (we judge ourselves by an ideal that we ourselves set)?
