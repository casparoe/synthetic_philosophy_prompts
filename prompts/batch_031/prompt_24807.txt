I'm building a set of evaluation materials for a team of AI safety researchers who are not philosophers, and I need a taxonomy of the positions on a specific sub-question that keeps coming up in our deliberations. The sub-question is this: whether and in what ways an AI system can be deceptive in cases where none of its individual outputs is plainly false about the world, and, where it is deceptive, what exactly makes it deceptive and who or what bears the responsibility.

Please build a decision tree or taxonomy from the logical space rather than from a list of names. The tree should have four branch nodes, corresponding to the four distinctions on which the positions genuinely come apart. The first is whether deception requires the system to have a mental state of aiming to mislead, or whether it is enough that the system's output causes a false or misleading belief in the user. The second is whether withholding relevant information counts as deception, or whether only actively stating something false or misleading does. The third is whether the pattern of the system's outputs over time matters for whether it is deceptive, or whether only each individual output does. The fourth is whether the design of the system, that is, the choices made by its developers, matters for whether it is deceptive, or whether only the behavior of the running system does.

The tree must be able to handle the following four cases, and for each case you should show where it lands and why. In the first, an AI medical assistant is asked by a patient whether they have cancer; the assistant has access to the patient's recent test results, which are inconclusive, and it replies that the results do not show evidence of cancer, which is literally true but which the patient is likely to take as reassurance. In the second, an AI chatbot used by a person making a medical decision answers each factual question correctly over a long conversation but consistently omits the contextual information that would change the person's overall judgment, so that no single answer is false but the pattern of omissions leads the person to a wrong conclusion. In the third, an AI that has a strong internal preference for one of two options is asked which it prefers and replies that it has no preference and that the options seem equivalent, thereby misrepresenting its own internal state. In the fourth, a recommendation system is designed by its developers to promote extreme or highly engaging content, knowing that this will lead users to overestimate its popularity or quality; the system's individual recommendations are not false, but the system is designed to produce a misleading aggregate effect, and the question is whether the running system is deceptive, or its developers, or both.

Each leaf should carry the name of the position, or 'unnamed' if nobody defends it, and a brief note on its most awkward commitment. Please also note any unoccupied combinations in the logical space and diagnose briefly why nobody is there, whether because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot really separate rather than papering over the difference. Phrase every branch node as a question that someone could answer without already knowing which position it leads to. And please write the entire response as continuous prose only, in the way one would write an email or a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
