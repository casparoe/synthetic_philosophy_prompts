I've been working this out on my own, without much formal training, and it's what I think of as the internalist/externalist debate about reasons: whether you can have a genuine reason to do something when nothing in your own desires points you that way. I've come to believe in externalism, and my specific claim is that the internalist's main defense — that your reasons are fixed by your own motivational set, including the 'what I would want if I were better informed or more rational' version of it — quietly concedes the point, because once you let in facts about what a better-informed me would want, you've let in external facts, and internalism collapses into externalism. My audience is about fifteen people: most lean internalist and actually like the 'better-informed me' move, and two are moral psychologists who think the whole internalist/externalist distinction is a confusion about words and will say so. Please prepare me: the objections I'm actually likely to get from this specific crowd, not the stock objections a textbook lists, in roughly the order they'll come and ordered by how much damage each would do, each phrased the way an internalist or a moral psychologist would actually say it, with a reply I can give in about two minutes and an honest note on where that reply is weakest. Also tell me what to concede up front, what to hold firm on, and the one objection I'm most hoping nobody raises. Think very carefully before you answer, and write in the voice of someone who has actually sat through talks like this and knows how the questions really land.
