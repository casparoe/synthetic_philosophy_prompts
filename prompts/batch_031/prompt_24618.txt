I've been leaning toward a value theory I'll just call tranquillism and I want a stress test before I commit to it. The core claim, stated plainly: the only thing that matters intrinsically is the absence of craving and the absence of suffering. Positive pleasures don't count as good in themselves -- what's good is that the craving is gone and the pain isn't there. So a life where you've got everything you wanted and now just settle in is as good, on this view, as a life where your still chasing stuff, as long as in the first one the craving and the pain are actually absent.

What would count against this? I don't want the standard "but what about joy then" objection restated -- I want the specific consequences the view is actually committed to, the kind a defender would have to live with. In particular, three things I keep getting stuck on: what does it say about the comparison between a state with no craving but some lingering pain versus a state with some craving but no pain, is there any way to rank those? What happens if craving and positive experience turn out to be inseperable -- like you can't enjoy a thing without wanting a bit more of it, so the ideal state is unreachable or incoherent? And does the view quietly collapse into negative utilitarianism once you think about bringing people into existence, where a life with no suffering is neutral rather than good?

Sort the objections by how much they'd actually move me, decisive versus merely awkward, and be straight about whether the view has been qualified into something unfalsifiable and whether that's a problem for a value theory specifically, as opposed to, say, an empirical claim. I'd personally find it hardest to shake an objection that shows the view can't describe a case I clearly think is good. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, no preamble and don't restate the question.
