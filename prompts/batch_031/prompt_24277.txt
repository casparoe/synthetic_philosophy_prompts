I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of physics, and I'm preparing a seminar paper on the metaphysics of time. In a recent popular book on relativity (I'll leave the author unnamed so your assessment doesn't track the author's reputation), I came across the following summary of the Rietdijk–Putnam argument:

'The argument is a straightforward consequence of special relativity, and it adds no metaphysical assumption that anyone could reasonably dispute. The physics is this: whether two spatially separated events happen "at the same time" depends on the state of motion of the observer who judges it. Rietdijk and Putnam, working independently in the 1960s, turned this into an argument about what exists. Pick any event in your future, an event in your future light cone. The geometry of Minkowski spacetime guarantees that there is some distant event F — say, somewhere in the Andromeda galaxy — such that F is simultaneous with your present moment in some inertial frame, while your future event lies in F's past. For the observer in whose frame F is simultaneous with your "now," your future event has already happened, so it is as real for that observer as anything in their present. And by the simple, obvious transitivity of "is real for" — if A is real for B and B is real for C, then A is real for C — your future event is real for you, since F's "now" is real for you (it is simultaneous with yours in some frame). Since your future event was arbitrary, everything in your future is real for you, and the same reasoning runs in reverse for the past. A vivid way to see it: two people walking past each other on Earth, one heading toward Andromeda and one away, have planes of "now" that, extended across the 2.5 million light-years to the Andromeda galaxy, differ by a matter of days, so for one of them some Andromedan decision has already been made, and for the other it hasn't yet. Because the argument shows that future events already exist, it is often presented as an argument for determinism: if the future is already real, it is already fixed, and there is no such thing as an open future, which is why Rietdijk and Putnam's result is cited in discussions of whether free will is compatible with physics. The upshot is that presentism — the view that only present events exist — is dead: anyone who insists that the present is ontologically privileged is simply clinging to a pre-relativistic intuition that the physics has refuted.'
