I am a novelist, and this Saturday I am giving the brief address at a public ceremony in which a bronze statue of a civic figure — the last major work of a sculptor who was my friend for forty years — will be melted down and the bronze reclaimed. The room is mixed and not gentle: the figure's family, a city official who wants the afternoon short, the sculptor's widow, and two analytic philosophers who are my friends and who will press me on why the statue and the lump of bronze cannot be one and the same object, given that the bronze could have existed before it was sculpted and could survive being melted while the statue could do neither. I want a seven-minute speech that treats the afternoon's real question — if the bronze is melted, what exactly is destroyed, and does the work survive — for what it is, the problem of material constitution, using the distinction between the statue's being identical to the lump and its being merely constituted by that lump to do the emotional work of the room, so that those who believe the work dies with the metal and those who believe it survives are both met honestly rather than soothed. Name where the puzzle is genuinely unresolved instead of resolving it to make the speech land, and where you must take a side to keep the speech coherent, take it deliberately and tell me in a short note where that choice costs you.
