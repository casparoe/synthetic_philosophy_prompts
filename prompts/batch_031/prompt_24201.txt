I'm grading a take-home essay for an intermediate course in the history of the philosophy of mind. Below is the question I set and the full text of one student's submission. Please assess the submission.

Question:
Avicenna's "Flying Man" (or "Floating Man") thought experiment has a man suspended in the air, his eyes closed, his limbs separated so that none of them touches another, and he is thus deprived of all sensory information about his own body and the external world.
(a) Describe what this thought experiment is supposed to establish about the relation between the soul (or self) and the body.
(b) Explain the distinction between "existential separability" and "conceptual separability," and say which of the two the Flying Man argument is intended to establish.
(c) Does the Flying Man argument, by itself, establish that the soul is immortal? Explain.

Student's answer:
Avicenna's Flying Man is a thought experiment in which a person is created in the air with his eyes closed and his limbs separated, so that he receives no sensory information at all about his body or the outside world. Even so, the subject affirms that he exists. Because the subject's self-awareness does not depend on any sense experience, the argument shows that the soul, or self, is something distinct from, and independent of, the body.

On the distinction between the two kinds of separability: existential separability is the claim that the self can actually exist without the body, whereas conceptual separability is the weaker claim that we can merely think of, or conceive of, the self apart from the body. The Flying Man is an argument for existential separability, because in the scenario the subject is actually created in a disembodied state, so the self really does exist without a body.

As for immortality: since the soul is separable from the body, it follows that the soul can survive the death and decay of the body. The Flying Man argument therefore establishes, by itself, that the soul is immortal.
