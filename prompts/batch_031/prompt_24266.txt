I'm a retired ecologist — I spent forty years studying mate choice and predator–prey signaling, and only in the last couple of years have I started reading philosophy seriously, mostly the philosophy of biology. I find I can still do the modeling, but I've begun to worry that I have not actually thought through the foundations of the field, and I would like a careful audit of where I currently stand before I try to write it down. Here is, as best I can state it, the package of commitments I think I hold about why signals are honest.

(1) The handicap principle is the correct general explanation of signal honesty. A signal is credible because it is costly to produce, and the cost is higher for low-quality signallers, so that low types cannot afford to bluff; the cost is what forces the truth out.

(2) The most reliable honest signals in nature — the color of a ripe fruit, the appearance of a wound, a pheromone whose concentration is fixed by physiology — are honest not because they are costly but because they are indices: they are physically or causally coupled to the very state they represent, so that faking is not merely too expensive but impossible in principle. I regard this indexicality as a distinct source of honesty, and in my view a more fundamental one than cost, because it does not depend on the economics of the signaler at all.

(3) At the same time, I want to insist that there is really only one mechanism here, cost. An index is simply a costly signal in the limiting case in which the cost of producing a fake is infinite, or at least so prohibitive that no low type would ever attempt it. On this view indexicality is not a separate mechanism but a special case of the handicap logic, and I would be unhappy to be left with two rival accounts of the same phenomenon.

(4) For a signal to be honest in the costly-signaling sense, its cost must be quality-dependent: a uniform cost that every type pays the same does not, by itself, select for honesty, because it screens for nothing.

(5) I am nonetheless comfortable with the idea that a signal can be honest even when the cost of producing it does not vary with the signaler's quality, so long as the population settles into a stable separating equilibrium in which only high-quality signallers send. I take this to be a genuine, if less common, route to honesty, distinct from the quality-dependent-cost route.

My question is whether this set hangs together. I suspect it does not, but I cannot see exactly where, and I do not want to prejudge which commitment I would be willing to give up. Could you go through the set and tell me which commitments genuinely pull against each other, which pairs only look like they do, and — for each genuine tension — what would actually have to give, and what that would cost me? If one of the apparent problems dissolves on inspection, I would be grateful to be told that too, and to be told why. Please order the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and be specific: I would rather be told that exactly one or two of these are in real trouble than be handed a list in which everything is equally at fault.
