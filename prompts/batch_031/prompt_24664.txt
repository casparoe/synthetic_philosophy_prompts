I'm teaching a grad seminar on the nature of value this term and the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem keeps coming up, and I've started to suspect the whole debate is stalled in a way I cant quite diagnose, so I want to think it through with you before I raise it with the students. Here's how it looks from where I sit. One camp, the ones I'll call the deniers, say there are no such things as wrong-kind reasons at all. When I say "this painting is famous, so that's a reason to value it," they reply that I've actually identified a reason to care about prestige or social standing, not a reason to value the painting; the "wrong kind" feeling is just a confusion, and once you sort out what you're actually valuing, every reason to value X turns out to be a reason that makes X valuable. The other camp, the affirmers, say no, wrong-kind reasons are real: "this is the kind of thing people of taste value" or "everyone prizes this" are genuine reasons to value the thing, they just don't themselves reflect or ground the thing's value; they're reasons of the wrong kind, but reasons nonetheless. And the debate has been going in circles for as long as I've been teaching it, with the deniers constantly reinterpreting the affirmers' examples as reasons of a different sort and the affirmers just producing new examples in response. The one that keeps bugging me is "this is the kind of thing that people of taste value" — the deniers want to say that's just a reason to care about what people of taste think, but that feels like moving the goalposts, because you can always press "but what makes it the kind of thing that people of taste value?" and the question just reappears one level up.

My working hypothesis is that its mostly a verbal disagreement about what it is to have a "reason to value X" — does the reason have to be one that makes X valuable, or is it enough that it's a reason that points toward valuing X? — plus a hidden dispute about which examples count as data, since the two sides never seem to be engaging the same cases. But I'm not sure, and I'd like a real diagnosis rather than a verdict on who's right: is this a substantive disagreement about the structure of value, a verbal one about the phrase "reason to value," or two groups answering different questions under one heading? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a distinction that cleanly separates the cases, a formal result, a reframing of the question — or should I just tell the students no progress is to be expected, and if so, why?
