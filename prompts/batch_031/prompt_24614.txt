I've been trying to wrap my head around the internal vs external reasons distinction because it keeps being refered to in the ethics papers I read and I never actually learned it in a class. A podcast I listen to had a segment on it and they went roughly like this:

"The internal/external reasons distinction, which Bernard Williams introduced, separates reasons that hook onto something the agent already cares about from reasons that don't. An internal reason for you to do X is one that connects to an existing desire, value, or commitment of yours; an external reason is one that applies to you even though you have no such psychological hook. Williams's famous claim was that external reasons don't really exist — every genuine reason has to be internal, i.e. traceable back to something in the agent's own subjective motivational set. So the practical upshot is pretty stark: if you don't care about something, you simply don't have a reason to do it. A person who's completely indifferent to their own survival, for instance, has no reason to put down a poisoned glass of water, even if they have no idea it's poisoned. The whole debate, then, is really just about how much leeway we give that motivational set to expand when the agent reflects."
