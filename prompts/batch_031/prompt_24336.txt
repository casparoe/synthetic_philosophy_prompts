In Bayesian confirmation theory, a common account of confirmation says that an observation E confirms a hypothesis H just in case P(E | H) > P(E). This account runs into the so-called problem of old evidence. Suppose E is already known to the agent before H is even formulated — the paradigm case being the precession of Mercury's perihelion, which was known long before general relativity was proposed to explain it. In that situation the agent assigns P(E) = 1, and since P(E | H) ≤ 1 always, the inequality P(E | H) > P(E) can never be satisfied. So, on this account, old evidence can never confirm a hypothesis, which is plainly counterintuitive.

Two responses to the problem are commonly discussed. The first is a counterfactual, or "subjunctive," response, on which the confirmatory force of old evidence E for H is not measured by the actual change in the agent's credence in H (which is zero, since E was already known) but by the change that would have occurred had E been newly learned: one compares the agent's actual credence in H with the credence the agent would have held in H at the time E was first acquired, before E entered the evidence base. The second response reframes what counts as the relevant evidence: rather than treating E itself as the evidence, it treats the discovery that H entails or predicts E as the evidence, so that the agent's credence in H is raised by learning the connection between H and E, conditional on E already being known.
