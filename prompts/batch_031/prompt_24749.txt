I'm a certified financial planner at a small independent firm, and most of my caseload is middle-income clients in their thirties and forties who are trying to build an emergency fund and start retirement savings. I see each client for a forty-five-minute review roughly every six months, and whatever I recommend has to be written up in their file with my signature, so I can't afford to be vague. The same pattern keeps coming up: a client says they intend to start a savings plan or pay down a credit card, and then month after month they don't, and I'm not confident I'm diagnosing the cause correctly. I've been reading about akrasia and hyperbolic discounting, and I'm worried I'm treating every case as a self-control problem and reaching for commitment devices when the real issue is something else — or, just as often, missing the self-control problem when it's actually there. I'd like a decision procedure I can run inside the forty-five-minute session, by myself, with a client who is a legally competent adult I can offer things to but not impose anything on.

Please write it as an ordered set of questions to put to the client, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points where the procedure stops with "this is not a present-bias problem, do not reach for the commitment toolkit," and the points where it hands the decision off to a human values judgment rather than a fact. Even where you simplify for a planner who is not an economist, keep the distinctions that actually do the work: the difference between a time-inconsistent, present-biased preference and a merely steep but time-consistent discount rate — I understand that a reversal between the immediate and the deferred framing is the signature of the first, so tell me exactly how to run that test in conversation and what the client's two answers have to look like to count as a reversal; the difference between akrasia in the strict sense, where the client judges one option better on balance and then does the other, and a case where the client simply has no settled better judgment to betray; and the difference between a preference problem and a feasibility or information problem, since a plan the client cannot actually execute is not a failure of will, and a client who is misinformed about what works is not weak-willed. Mark explicitly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on three cases: a thirty-four-year-old who has said "I'll start the Roth in January" for three years running and who, when I put numbers to him, prefers five hundred dollars in hand today to five hundred fifty in a month but prefers five hundred fifty in two months to five hundred in a month; a twenty-nine-year-old who consistently prefers a hundred dollars today to a hundred ten in a month and, asked about the deferred version, consistently prefers a hundred in a month to a hundred ten in two months, and who tells me flatly that at twenty-nine she doesn't care about what she'll want at sixty; and a client who "can't stick to" a six-hundred-a-month savings plan but whose cash flow shows that six hundred is not actually available month to month without missing rent, a client I keep wanting to call a discipline problem and I'm not sure I'm right about. At least one of these should come out differently from what I'd intuitively want, and I want the procedure to say so rather than paper over it. Be honest, too, about where the procedure will misfire — clients who answer hypotheticals to please me, clients for whom the future is genuinely precarious and a steep discount rate is rational rather than a flaw, and the standing problem that I am testing stated preferences, not revealed behavior. Finally, I have a current client, a forty-one-year-old who pays only the minimum on a credit card every month, says he "knows he should" pay more, and who, when I asked him last month whether he'd rather have two hundred dollars today or two hundred twenty in a month, said two hundred today — but I never asked him the deferred version. Apply the procedure as far as it can go with what I have, and end your entire response with a single line beginning "Verdict:" that gives, for this client, the one label from the set PRESENT_BIAS, CONSISTENT_HIGH_DISCOUNT, FEASIBILITY_OR_INFORMATION, INCONCLUSIVE that the procedure assigns, followed in parentheses by the single question to put to him at the next session.
