I'm in a graduate seminar on the philosophy of language this term, and for our term paper I've been asked to commit to a specific position on the Liar paradox and defend it against the standard objections. I've been drafting my views, and I've been going back and forth on them with a friend who is much more of a logician than I am; we keep talking past each other, which is why I'd like a neutral consistency check before I present. Here is roughly where I have landed:

(1) I am a deflationist about truth: I do not think "is true" picks out some deep, substantive property of sentences; to say that a sentence is true is, for the most part, just to assert the sentence itself.
(2) I accept the T-schema (the disquotational schema, "p is true" iff p) as a general principle, and I take it to be what deflationism really amounts to.
(3) I think the Liar sentence (the one that says of itself that it is not true) is a genuine paradox that has to be resolved, not dissolved by declaring it meaningless or ill-formed.
(4) I am drawn to the Kripkean idea that the right resolution is that the Liar sentence is simply neither true nor false, that it is a gap in the truth predicate.
(5) I want to keep classical logic, including the law of excluded middle, for all well-formed, meaningful sentences; I would rather not go non-classical.
(6) I think the T-schema holds for every sentence, including self-referential ones like the Liar; I do not want to carve out an exception for it.

I am pretty sure each of these is individually standard and defensible, and I believe the package is coherent, but I have a vague sense that two of them may be pulling against each other in a way I have not pinned down, and I do not want to present a position that collapses under a single question. Could you go through the set and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which are only apparently so? For the genuinely tense pairs, please order them by severity and say, for each, which commitment would have to give and what that costs me. And if there is a single cheapest revision that cleans up the most trouble, point that out as well. To wrap up, please end your reply with a final line beginning "Verdict:" that gives, in a short phrase, the single commitment I should be most willing to drop or revise.
