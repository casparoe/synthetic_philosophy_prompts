I'm a retired experimental physicist (30 years in a plasma physics lab, now mostly retired and reading philo for the first time in my life). I got roped into giving a talk at a philosophy of science reading group next month, and I want to be ready for the Q&A.

My view: thought experiments are a genuine source of novel knowledge about the world, and the reason they can be right is that they are experiments on a model — the model is the thing being tested, and the model is constrained by the world in the same way a physical model is. And the reason we can be wrong is that the model can be wrong, not that the reasoning is bad. And the reason two competent reasoners can disagree about the outcome of a thought experiment is that the disagreement is about what the model says, which is an empirical question in disguise.

I've used thought experiments in my own research (to reason about plasma instabilities, mostly), and I've noticed that the simulation step is not a deduction from premises — it's a kind of counterfactual reasoning I can't fully articulate, and that's why the disagreement cases are interesting to me.

My case study: Newton's bucket. I'll be arguing that the bucket is a good example of a thought experiment that yielded real knowledge (the concept of absolute motion), and that the Newton vs. Leibniz disagreement about the bucket is exactly the kind of disagreement that shows thought experiments are not just arguments.

The audience: the group has a committed Nortonian who thinks thought experiments are just arguments (no new information, just reasoning from background knowledge), a structuralist who thinks of thought experiments as experiments on models, and a couple of agnostics who think thought experiments are heurstics/motivators.

I want a Q&A prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get from this audience, in their vocabulary, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each with a short reply and a note on where the reply is weakest. Include the objection I'm hoping nobody asks.
