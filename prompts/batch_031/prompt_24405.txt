A professor summarized the St. Petersburg paradox for me like this: "The game pays 2^n dollars on the first heads at the nth toss, so its expected winnings are infinite — the paradox is that the expected-value calculation says you should be willing to pay any finite entry fee, yet no one actually would. Bernoulli's 1738 fix was to replace expected money with expected utility, taking utility to be the logarithm of the gambler's total wealth. Because logarithmic utility is bounded, the expected utility comes out finite, and that finite number is the rational price to pay. That settles the matter: any continuous concave utility function does the same job, and pricing risky prospects by expected utility is now standard practice." I'm inclined to think the log step is ad hoc, but I can't say where the summary is wrong. Go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what's accurate, what's a subtle misstatement, and whether a careful decision theorist would accept that the paradox is thereby solved.
