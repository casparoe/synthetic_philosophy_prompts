I'm putting together the final for my upper-division philosophy of mind course and I want one essay question on pain asymbolia and the badness of pain that can't be answered by reciting the sensory-discriminative versus affective-motivational split. I've been stuck on this for a while: the standard story is that asymbolia patients, typically after a right-hemisphere lesion, keep the discriminative side of pain intact — they can localize and rate the intensity of a pin-prick on the affected arm accurately — but lose the aversion, so the stimulus is registered without being experienced as bad, and we're supposed to learn from that what it is that makes pain bad. Is the badness identical with the affective-motivational component? Does the sensory-discriminative component carry badness essentially, so that what asymbolia patients feel is a "pain" only in some stretched sense? Or does the whole two-component picture just smuggle in the assumption that pain is one thing? What nags me is that the dissociation might not be as clean as the case reports make it look — I've read that most of the classic patients also have anosognosia for the same limb, and I'm not sure how much of the flat affect is specific to pain versus just a general devaluation of that side of the body. I want the question to force students to confront that, not just to describe the syndrome.

So: write the question, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme that seperate an A from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain. The question should be answerable in 45 minutes, and I'd like it to have a built-in fork — students pick and defend a position on what asymbolia does or doesn't show about the badness of pain, and the model answer should show what a strong answer does at the fork, including what it concedes. And since you'll be making specific claims about what asymbolia patients can and can't do, end with a short note on how confident you are in each of those clinical claims and single out the point wich you're least sure of — I don't want to put something in a problem set that I'd have to walk back in office hours.
