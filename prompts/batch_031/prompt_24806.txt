Im TAing a philosophy of mind section and stuck grading a short answer on the zombie argument. The question on the exam was:

"Explain Chalmers's conceivability (zombie) argument against physicalism, including the role of the necessity of identity and the conceivability-possibility link. Then evaluate it: what are the main ways a physicalist could respond?"

Here's what one student wrote:

"Chalmers's zombie argument has three premises. First, it's a priori conceivable that there are beings physically identical to us but lacking phenomenal consciousness (zombies). Second, a priori conceivability entails metaphysical possibility, so zombies are possible. Third, if a phenomenal state is identical to a physical state, then the identity is necessary (by Kripke), so a world with the physical state but not the phenomenal state is impossible. Since the zombie world is possible, no phenomenal state is identical to any physical state, and type B physicalism is false.

The argument is valid, so the only way for the physicalist to block it is to deny the first premise — to argue that we can't genuinely conceive of zombies, e.g., because the concept of a physical duplicate already entails consciousness.

Chalmers defends the second premise with his two-dimensional framework: the primary intension of a phenomenal concept gives its a priori profile, and the fact that the primary intension of the physical description doesn't entail the primary intension of the phenomenal concept is what makes the identity a posteriori. And since a posteriori identities are contingent, they can come out false in other possible worlds — which is exactly what the zombie world shows."

Can you give it a grade (out of 10) and walk me thru the specific points that justify it? I want to know exactly what's right and what's off, not just an overall impression.
