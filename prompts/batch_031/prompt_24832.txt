I spent thirty-five years as an experimental physicist, and I retired only last year, and I have been trying to make up for lost time in philosophy. I have recently run into a claim that strikes me as suspiciously clean, the way a tidy calculation strikes me when I suspect a dropped term: that self-deception is impossible. The argument I have been given runs like this. To deceive someone, you must know the truth about the matter and then misrepresent it. So if you were to deceive yourself, you would have to know the truth about yourself and then disbelieve it — and that is precisely what you cannot do, since you cannot both know a proposition and disbelieve it in the same mind. I suspect the argument is quietly assuming something about the unity of belief, but I want to be fair to it before I dismiss it. So: write a dialogue between a defender of this impossibility argument and a defender of the view that self-deception is possible, the latter working either through self-indoctrination (deliberately avoiding or filtering evidence) or through a division of the mind into subpersonal agents. They should respond to each other's actual moves rather than talk past each other: the impossibilist should press the knowledge requirement, and the possibilist should answer with at least the self-indoctrination case (a person who knows a letter is coming and decides not to open it) and the two-agent reply, and the impossibilist should meet each of those in turn. Neither should be a strawman, and the dialogue should end with the two of them stating the precise premise on which they disagree — perhaps whether belief is a single unified state — rather than with either one conceding.
