I cover science and ideas for a long-form magazine, and I'm preparing a piece for a general but well-read audience on a question that sounds simple but isn't: how should a rational agent — a person, or a well-calibrated machine — update its beliefs when the evidence it receives is not a clean all-or-nothing proposition? When what it learns is not a fact but that a fact is "probably" the case: a smudged photograph, a diagnostic test whose reliability you only partly trust, a witness who seems mostly but not completely credible. In the technical literature this is the territory of Jeffrey conditionalization and its rivals — Popper functions, projection rules, imprecise or credal probabilities, and the reductionist view that all uncertain evidence is just ordinary, certain evidence about a finer-grained state of affairs. I have about fifteen hundred words, and I can only go deep on two or three of the open questions in this area. Which of the open questions here matter most, and in what order should I take them up? I'm less interested in a survey of the field than in the reasoning behind the ordering. For each question you pick, tell me why it is upstream of the others — that is, how many other questions in the area effectively depend on its answer — how settled or genuinely open it is, and how tractable it is to explain to a reader without a background in probability. And please tell me what would change your ranking: a new no-go theorem, a result showing that one of the invariance failures is harmless in practice, or something else. I don't need a list of papers to read; I need a defended agenda for where the piece should spend its words.
