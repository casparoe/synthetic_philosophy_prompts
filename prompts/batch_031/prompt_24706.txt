I'm a retired fluid dynamicist who only got into math foundations a year or so ago, so forgive me if I'm chasing a ghost. I keep noticing the same move in proofs and I can't tell whether there's a name for it or whether I'm just being a pedant.

The move: someone needs to pick one element out of each set in a big family of non-empty sets, so they wave the axiom of choice at you and say "by AC, choose one from each." Fine. But then I read on and the rest of the argument only ever uses that the picks exist and that they line up with some structure the family already had — an ordering, an equivalence relation, a continuity condition. The full, arbitary, "pick from any family you like" strength of AC is never actually touched. It feels like the AC step is doing less work than it's billed for, and I keep wanting to say "you didn't need the whole axiom, you only needed a structured choice."

Two instances that stuck with me. One: the Hahn-Banach extension theorem is usually proved by slapping on Zorn's lemma, which people treat as "AC in disguise," but I have a vague memory that it only really needs a weaker principle — something about extending filters to ultrafilters — which AC implies but which doesn't imply AC back. So the Zorn proof is over-armed. Two: the fact that a countable union of countable sets is countable is often proved by "pick an enumeration for each set and interleave them," which sounds like it only needs to make countably many picks, not picks from an arbitrary family — yet I've seen it justfied by full AC. But I also know there are results where the full axiom genuinely is the minimum, like "every vector space has a basis," so it's not as if AC is always overkill. The phenomenon is that some proofs advertise more choice than the argument actually spends.

So: is there an established name or literature for this — looking at a proof that invokes AC and asking which is the weakest choice principle that actually carries the load? I've heard the phrase "weak choice principles" and I know the name "reverse mathematics," but I'm not sure either is what I mean, and I'd rather not pin it on the wrong thing. If there's no single name, I'd be happy if you just named and defined the closest thing and told me where my version of it stops being that thing.
