I'm reading a chapter in a popular history of economic thought, and it summarizes Mill's late views on socialism more or less as follows:

"By the 1860s Mill had effectively converted from liberalism to socialism. In the later editions of his *Principles of Political Economy* he came to hold that the future of the working classes lay in collective ownership of the means of production, and that the existing distribution of the surplus, in which the profits of industry went to a small class of owners, was unjust and had to be replaced by an equal sharing of the fruits of labor. His argument, as he came to see it, was no longer primarily a defense of individual liberty but a commitment to justice and equality: a society in which the workers do not share in the wealth their labor creates is simply unfair, and no amount of formal freedom can remedy that. The individualist liberalism of *On Liberty* was, in effect, a position he had outgrown once he recognized that production is a social process and that the worker's claim on the product of his labor is a matter of right rather than of policy."

I'm not confident how much of this holds up. Could you go through it sentence by sentence and separate (a) what a careful Mill scholar would accept, (b) what is a defensible simplification, and (c) what is flatly wrong or misdescribes his actual position? I'm especially interested in the distinction between what Mill wanted to change about the distribution of the produce of industry and what he wanted to leave alone about the organization of production, and in whether his case for worker ownership was a utilitarian or consequentialist one or an appeal to justice and rights. And does the chapter's closing claim -- that Mill's liberalism was a phase he outgrew -- actually follow from the parts of the summary that are accurate?
