(1) The impossibility result is a math fact, not a matter of opinion: when two groups have different base rates, you can't have both calibration (same score = same actual probability in both groups) and equalized odds (same false positive and false negative rates in both groups).

(2) For high stakes calls I want to equalize the error rates across groups, because the harm of a false positive (someone locked up, care denied) is what matters, and I'm fine using group specific threshholds to get there.

(3) But the score has to stay a calibrated probability an individual can actually act on — a defendant or a judge or a clinician needs to know the real probabilty the prediction is right, and a number that means different things depending on your group is just uninterpretable to me.

(4) The right long run answer to the impossibility isn't picking one of the two criteria, it's fixing the inputs: shrink the actual base rate gap (reduce real recidivism in the higher risk group or whatever) or change what we're predicting in the first place.

(5) Demographic parity, equal selection rates, is not a good target here because it ignores base rate differences and would force us to systematically over predict for the lower risk group.
