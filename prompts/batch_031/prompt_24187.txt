A few months ago I had a long conversation with an old colleague who works in a reproductive-medicine lab, and she described the day-to-day of an IVF cycle in terms that struck me as philosophically loaded, though she herself was thinking only of technique. A woman is stimulated and her eggs retrieved; they are fertilized in the dish, and the resulting embryos are cultured for a few days. The embryologist then looks at the blastocysts and, where the couple has asked for preimplantation genetic testing, biopsies a cell from each one and tests for aneuploidy, and for any monogenic disease the couple is known to carry. Of the handful of embryos, one or two are selected and transferred, the rest are frozen as insurance, and after a successful pregnancy the frozen surplus is routinely offered for donation to research or simply discarded. In recent years some clinics have also begun offering, in various jurisdictions, a kind of polygenic screening that ranks embryos by predicted risk for conditions such as diabetes or cardiovascular disease. I am retired from a physics laboratory, so I understand the technique well enough, but I have only recently started reading philosophy, and I find myself unable to decide whether what the embryologist is doing is a straightforward welfare comparison between individuals or something else entirely.

The point that is confusing me is a problem I have read about under the name the non-identity problem. If the choice of which embryo to transfer determines who will exist at all, then the embryo that loses the selection is not a person who is harmed; it is a person who never exists, and it seems incoherent to say that it would have been better for it had it been selected, since "it" is the very thing whose existence depends on the choice. Yet the practice is organized as though the comparison were perfectly coherent: the embryologist ranks the embryos by expected welfare, the parents are told which ones are "healthier" and which should be discarded, and the discarded ones are treated as candidates for a fate that is worse than the alternative. So the practice seems to presuppose that each embryo is already an individual whose welfare can be assessed and compared, in the way that I could compare the prospects of two existing children, while the problem as I understand it says that the comparison has no object, because the candidates for the worse outcome are constituted by the choice. I have also read of a principle, apparently proposed by a philosopher named Scheffler, under the name procreative beneficence, to the effect that one should select the child, of the possible children one could have, who is expected to have the best life, or at least as good a life as the others. I am not sure whether this principle is the view the practice presupposes, a view the practice quietly contradicts, or a view that runs into the same difficulty.

What I would like, then, is a reconstruction of the implicit theory of value, identity, and welfare that the practice of selecting and discarding embryos presupposes: which view of the moral status of the embryo, and of what it is for a life to go well, the practice only makes sense under; which rival views it quietly rules out, for instance a view on which the relevant comparison is not between two people but between two possible compositions of the future; whether the practice is strictly committed to any one of these views or is in fact underdetermined and compatible with several; and where the reasoning breaks down, if it does, when the non-identity problem is pressed on it. And please tell me, separately, what the practice of discarding the surplus embryos presupposes, since I suspect that step commits the clinic to a view of personhood that the selection step does not require, and whether a policy built on a different underlying view of the embryo would have to change anything about how the clinic actually operates, or only about how it describes what it is doing.
