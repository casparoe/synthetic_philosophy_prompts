I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm drafting a feature on the boundaries of the knowable. I want to walk readers through Fitch's paradox of knowability without assuming any background, so I rebuilt the argument step by step, and I've arrived at a conclusion I can't live with. I'd like a diagnosis: which step is doing the real work, why it looked harmless, and what I'd actually lose by cutting it. Here is my reconstruction.

(1) Knowledge is factive: if a subject knows p, then p is true.
(2) Every truth is possibly knowable: for any proposition p, if p is true, then there is a possible circumstance in which p is known.
(3) Let U be the proposition "p is true, but p is not known."
(4) U is not possibly known. Assume for reductio that U is known. By factivity, U is true, so p is true and p is not known. But U entails p, and knowledge is closed under known logical consequence, so the knowledge of U yields the knowledge of p. So p is both known and not known, a contradiction; hence U cannot be known, and so it is not possible that U is known.
(5) It is not possible that U is known.
(6) If U were true, then by (2) it would be possible that U is known.
(7) Therefore U is not true.
(8) So for every p, it is not the case that p is true and p is not known.
(9) So for every p, if p is true then p is known.
(10) Every truth is known.
(11) But some truth is not known: the precise arrangement of every particle in the observable universe a nanosecond after the Big Bang is a truth, and no one knows it.
(12) So (2) is false.

I'm not willing to drop (1); it seems built into the very concept of knowledge. And I'd regret dropping (2) flat, since it is the principle the article is meant to defend. I can't tell which of the remaining steps is responsible, or what I'd forfeit by weakening any of them. So: which step should I reject, and why did it look fine? If the chain is in fact valid, which premise is the cheapest to give up, and what do I specifically lose by giving it up? In particular, if the fix is to restrict (2) to "stable" propositions, what counts as stable, and does that restriction genuinely save the principle or merely gut it?
