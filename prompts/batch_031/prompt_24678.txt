Split-brain research — the classic experiments in which the corpus callosum is severed — is often cited as evidence that a single person can possess two separate "streams" of consciousness. Consider the standard demonstration: a patient is shown a picture of a snow-covered scene in the left visual field, which is processed by the right hemisphere. When asked what they saw, the patient — whose speech is generated by the left hemisphere — reports seeing nothing, even though their left hand, which is controlled by the right hemisphere, correctly selects the matching picture from an array. The natural reading of this result is that the right hemisphere has no phenomenal experience at all, since phenomenal consciousness just is the capacity to verbally report an experience, and the right hemisphere cannot report what it "saw." Given that reading, how should we understand the unity of consciousness in a split-brain patient? Is there genuinely a second, silent stream of experience running in parallel, or is the whole notion of a "second stream" an illusion produced by the left hand's behavior and the experimental setup?
