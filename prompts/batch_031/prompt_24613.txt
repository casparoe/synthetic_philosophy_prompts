I've been trying to wrap my head around the is-ought gap (Hume's thing, where you supposedly can't get an 'ought' from an 'is'), and I came up with an argument that I think actually closes the gap, which feels off because I keep hearing that the whole point is that you can't. Here's what I got:

(1) An 'ought' claim follows from some 'is' claims just in case the 'is' claims plus the opposite 'ought' claim can't all be true at the same time.
(2) Look at 'I ought not to lie' as a moral claim. For any set of purely descriptive 'is' claims, you can consitently add 'I lie and nothing bad happens,' so the 'is' claims plus 'I ought to lie' are consistent.
(3) So by (1), no descriptive 'is' claims entail 'I ought not to lie.'
(4) But now add the 'is' claims 'lying always causes suffering' and 'I really want to avoid causing suffering.' Together with 'I ought to lie' they seem inconsistent, because if lying always causes suffering and I want to avoid that, I shouldn't lie.
(5) So by (1), 'I ought not to lie' IS entailed by some 'is' claims.
(6) So (3) was too strong, and the gap is way smaller than people say — in fact it looks like any moral ought can be pulled out of the right facts plus your desires, so there's no gap at all.

I feel like I'm smuggling something in somewhere, but I can't find where. Which step do you reject, and what do I actually lose if I reject it?
