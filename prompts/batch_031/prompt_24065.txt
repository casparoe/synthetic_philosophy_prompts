Im writing a thriller and I've got a plot device that's bugging me, and I think it's actually a philosophy problem in disguise. In my book, the protagonist is about to make a choice — she can either take the safe route or the risky one. There's a stalker who's been watching her, and he's set it up so that if she's about to take the risky route, he'll step in and force her onto the safe route. But she chooses the safe route on her own, so he never has to act. My question is: is she responsible for choosing the safe route? She "could not have done otherwise" — if she'd been about to take the risky route, the stalker would have stopped her. But she chose freely, on her own, and the stalker never intervened.

So here's the argument I built for my own understanding, and I think it proves too much:

(1) If a person is morally responsible for an action, then they could have done otherwise.
(2) In my story, the protagonist could not have done otherwise — the stalker would have prevented the other choice.
(3) So the protagonist is not morally responsible for choosing the safe route.
(4) But the protagonist is obviously responsible — she chose freely, and the stalker never acted.
(5) So (1) is false: responsibility does not require the ability to do otherwise.
(6) Now the same logic applies to real life. In the real world, there are always "stalkers" of a sort — my own character, my habits, my upbringing, the laws of nature — that would have prevented me doing otherwise if I had been about to.
(7) So in the real world, I could not have done otherwise.
(8) So by (5), I am responsible for what I do even though I could not have done otherwise.
(9) But by the same token, a sleepwalker is "responsible" for what they do, and a person acting under duress is "responsible," and a person whose action is caused by a brain tumor is "responsible."
(10) But obviously a sleepwalker is not responsible, and a person acting under duress is not fully responsible, and a person with a brain tumor is not responsible.
(11) So the conclusion of (5) — that responsibility doesn't require the ability to do otherwise — proves too much: it makes everyone responsible for everything.

I feel like something slipped, probably around (6) to (7), but I cant name it. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
