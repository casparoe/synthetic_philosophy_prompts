I'm trying to build the core of a 90-minute decision-making workshop around the Ellsberg paradox, and I want the paradox to be something the participants actually do and then notice in themselves, not something I walk through on a whiteboard. The group is about eighteen grad students, a mix of economics and philosophy, sitting at a few tables in a normal classroom, and I want to use real (fake) money — play points that cash in for a small prize at the end — so the stakes are real enough that they take the choices seriously but small enough that nobody's actually hurt. The bones are the standard two-urn setup: one urn with exactly fifty red and fifty blue balls, and one with an unknown split (something like thirty red / seventy blue or sixty red / forty blue, and nobody, including me, knows which), plus the four bets on red and blue drawn from each urn. But I explicitly don't want a straight textbook 2x2, because I'm pretty sure that if I just present the four bets in a row, half the room will reason their way to the "rational" answer before the cards are even on the table. I want the sequencing or a commitment device arranged so that the incoherence shows up in what they actually pick — like, they have to lock in the red decision before the blue one is even on the table, or their earlier choice binds them in a later round — so that the sure-thing violation is something they personally commit to and then have to defend out loud. And I don't want to drop the name "Ellsberg" or "sure-thing principle" until the debrief, so they run into it cold.

On top of that I want a control round built in, because I've been arguiing with a friend that the classic pattern might just be loss aversion or plain pessimism about the unknown urn in disguise, and I'd like the activity to be able to tell those apart if the room cooperates. My rough idea is to swap in a second unknown urn that's actually favorable to the bet (say sixty red / forty blue or eighty red / twenty blue) so that a purely pessimistic person should now prefer the ambiguous side, while a genuinely ambiguity-averse person still reaches for the known fifty-fifty — but I'm not sure that's the cleanest way to run it, so feel free to fix the design if you see a hole. Then I need a debrief that maps specific moments in the session — the point where they commit to the red choice, the moment the blue choice comes out the "wrong" way, whatever happens in the control round — onto the actual formal structure, i.e. the probability argument that the two preferences can't both hold under any single set of subjective probabilities, and where exactly the sure-thing principle is doing all the work.
