I've been thinking on my own, mostly from a popular book or two on human behavior, about why cooperation is so common even when, in the short run, it seems to pay to cheat. I know the basic prisoner's dilemma setup, where each side is tempted to free-ride even though mutual cooperation would do better for both, and I understand that the answer changes a lot when the same two people expect to meet again rather than just once. The explanation I keep running into is the small-town story: in a small town everyone knows everyone, so if you cheat someone the word spreads, your name gets stained, and people stop doing business with you. The fear of losing that good reputation is what keeps people honest, and that's the whole engine of cooperation. The conclusion people usually draw from it is that the modern world is much harder — we're constantly dealing with anonymous strangers in one-off transactions, there's no shared ledger of who's trustworthy, and so you can't really expect cooperation to hold up.

I'd like you to audit that small-town picture rather than just take it at face value. First, lay out the correspondence: what in the town (the shared reputation, the gossip, the threat of being shunned) is supposed to line up with what in the actual game-theoretic situation? Then tell me where the mapping breaks. In particular, is a publicly shared reputation really the mechanism that sustains cooperation, or is the real engine something narrower — the same two individuals expecting to interact with each other again — that the town story is accidentally doing the work for? And once those cracks are on the table, does the conclusion people lean on — that anonymous one-off dealings simply can't sustain cooperation — survive, or was the analogy quietly doing all the argumentative lifting?
