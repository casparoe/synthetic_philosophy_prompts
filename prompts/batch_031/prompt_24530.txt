I'm a second-year graduate student in philosophy, and my dissertation advisor has just told me that my chapter on the zombie argument is doing a lot of quiet work by assuming a particular link between conceivability and possibility that I never actually defend. To sort out where I actually stand before I rewrite the chapter, I want to build a decision tree that takes someone from a small set of starting commitments all the way to a named position on the question: what, if anything, does the conceivability of a state of affairs tell us about its possibility?

Please write the tree so that each branch node is a question a respondent can answer without already knowing which named position it leads to, and so that the questions are the ones on which the positions genuinely come apart rather than labels people attach to themselves. I'd like the tree to start from the commitment that at least some truths are a posteriori necessities — the identity of water with H2O is the case everyone in my program grants — since that is the case that does the most damage to naive conceivability arguments.

The branch nodes should be built from the logical space rather than from a list of names. At minimum I want the tree to separate positions on: (i) whether conceivability is a guide to metaphysical possibility at all, or only to epistemic possibility, or to neither; (ii) if it does guide to metaphysical possibility, whether the work is done by negative conceivability (conceivability of not-p) or by positive conceivability (a coherent positive conception of p), and whether an idealized or perfect conceiver is required for the link to hold; (iii) how the a posteriori necessities get handled — whether they are taken to break the conceivability–possibility link outright, or whether there is a way to accommodate them while keeping the link; and (iv) what grounds the link when it holds — two-dimensional or conceptual semantics, a principle of logic, or a fallible but respectable heuristic.

The leaves should be named positions, and I specifically want the following to appear as leaves: the Kripkean view that conceivability tracks epistemic possibility only; the Lewisian variant; the Chalmersian two-dimensional view on which idealized positive conceivability tracks metaphysical possibility; the strict a priori only rationalist who would restrict the link to a priori conceivability; and the deflationary no-link view on which conceivability is psychologically arbitrary and not a guide to any kind of possibility. If there is a position you think belongs in the space that I haven't named, add it as a leaf and mark it unnamed if nobody defends it under a standard label.

For each leaf, give one sentence on its most awkward commitment — the claim it must bite the bullet on. Where two leaves are so close that no question in the tree can actually separate them, flag that pair explicitly rather than pretending the tree distinguishes them. And where the logical space allows a combination of answers that no one actually holds, note it and say briefly why the space is empty there — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

The audience is a seminar of early graduate students who have read the standard primary texts on a posteriori necessity but are not specialists, so keep the branch questions free of jargon that only resolves once you already know the answer. I'm aiming for roughly five to six branch nodes and six to eight leaves, though I'd rather have a slightly smaller tree that separates its leaves honestly than a bigger one that pads the space.
