I am a referee for a journal whose house position is intuitionistic: the theorems and proofs we publish may not use the full law of excluded middle, and every existence claim has to be backed by an actual construction. A few times a year I review a paper in which the authors assert that a theorem is "constructively valid," or present a proof they describe as constructive, and I have to decide whether that claim holds before I recommend acceptance; a false claim of this kind would mislead our readers and undercut the journal's position, so the stakes are real. I want a decision procedure I can actually run on a paper, not a survey of constructivism.

Please write a step-by-step audit procedure that a co-referee who knows standard mathematics but is not a constructivist can follow on a proposed theorem-and-proof. For each step, state the question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, and whether a bad answer ends the review with a reject or merely flags a fix. Mark clearly which steps are mechanical checks and which are judgment calls the referee has to make, and state what the procedure cannot do at all — in particular, where it can only detect that a constructive proof is missing rather than produce one. The procedure must say how to handle proofs that argue "by contradiction" and proofs that invoke a choice principle, since those are where I most often err. It should end in exactly one of: accept, reject, or refer to the editor (our final sign-off) with a specific question. I have roughly an hour per paper.

Then run the procedure on these three proposals, keeping each run to under 200 words:
(1) "Every bounded increasing sequence of real numbers converges," proved by the usual epsilon-N argument.
(2) The intermediate value theorem in its exact form — if f is continuous on [a,b] with f(a) < 0 < f(b), then there exists c with f(c) = 0 — with a proof that near its end isolates a point by bisection and then argues by considering whether f at that point is zero or not.
(3) A lemma whose proof splits on whether a natural number n is even or odd.

For case (2), be explicit about what the authors would have to give up and what weaker statement survives, because that is the answer I will not like delivering.

Keep the entire response to at most 1200 words.
