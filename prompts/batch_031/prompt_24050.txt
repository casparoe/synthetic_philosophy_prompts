I've just spent an hour arguing with a friend about Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism, and I want to replace the back-and-forth with a diagnostic instrument that can actually sort people into positions. The friend, a philosopher of religion, keeps insisting that Ghazālī's occasionalism is not a metaphysical thesis at all, but only an epistemological claim: since we cannot know necessary connections in nature, we should attribute all production to God's will; on that reading, Ghazālī differs from Avicenna and Averroes only in what we can know, not in what happens. I was arguing that Ghazālī is making a substantive claim about the world: when cotton burns, it is not the fire that burns it; the fire is merely an occasion on which God wills burning, and the only "connection" between fire and burning is a habit of the mind formed by repeated observation. My friend also claims that Aquinas's doctrine of secondary causes is just occasionalism in disguise — that God is the sole true cause and creatures are at best instruments — and that the real line in the sand is between two forms of theism, not between theism and philosophy.

Please write a decision tree that sorts a person into a position on causation, built so that it can adjudicate exactly these disputes. The tree should start from a neutral base of commitments that everyone in my room accepts — the world is regular, our causal beliefs are mostly reliable, and, on the theistic options, God is omnipotent and the ultimate source of the order we observe — and each branch node should be a single question a student can answer without already knowing which leaf they are headed for. The questions should be concrete and case-driven: use the billiard-ball case (one ball striking another, the second moving), the cotton-and-fire case, and at least one modern case of your choosing, and the questions should separate the following distinctions: whether created things have genuine causal powers or are merely occasions for divine action; whether the causal connection is a necessary connection in reality or only a habit of the mind; whether causation is a relation between events, a power of substances, or a regularity in experience; and whether our knowledge of causation is of the world or of our own minds.

The leaves should be named positions. The following positions must all be represented — as leaves, or as merged variants where two are genuinely the same: Ghazālī's strict occasionalism; Maimonidean occasionalism, distinguished from Ghazālī's only where it is genuinely distinguished; Thomistic secondary causation or divine concurrence; Suárez-style occasionalism, merged with Ghazālī's if it is not genuinely distinct and with a word on why; Hume's regularity or constant-conjunction view; Hume's belief-based or projection view; the counterfactual-dependence view of causation; the dispositional-essentialist view that a kind's essence is its causal profile; the transcendental view that causation is a rule of the understanding rather than a feature of things in themselves; and the "production" view that causation is the active production of the effect by the cause, distinct from mere regularity. If you judge two of these to be variants of a single position rather than genuinely distinct ones, merge them into one leaf and say why.

For each leaf, give one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot really separate, and say what additional question would be needed to do so. Note any unoccupied combinations of answers and briefly diagnose them — is nobody there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored? In particular, address the combination "theism plus independent creaturely causal powers" and the combination "created things have genuine causal powers plus the causal connection is only a habit of the mind": for each, say whether any leaf occupies it, and if so what its most awkward commitment is, or if not, why nobody is there.

The audience is a graduate seminar in philosophy of religion with a mixed background: a few students have read Ghazālī, most have not, and several are analytic philosophers of causation who have never read Aquinas. Keep the tree to at most six branch questions and at most nine leaves. Respond in the style of a specialist in the history of philosophy and philosophy of religion, and think very carefully before answering: first reason step by step about the logical space of positions and which distinctions actually do work, and only then write the final tree. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; the point is to sort people by what they believe, not by which label they like.
