I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I am preparing a piece on whether an artificial agent can rationally trust its own reasoning — the question that keeps surfacing in debates about whether a capable AI could "know it is reliable." At the center of that debate is a result from mathematical logic that goes by the name the Löbian obstacle: building on Löb's theorem and connected to Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, it says that a consistent, sufficiently strong formal system cannot prove its own soundness, which in the agent setting amounts to saying that an agent whose reasoning is captured by such a system cannot prove that its own proofs are to be trusted. I keep getting muddled about what that result actually rules out, and I do not want to get it wrong in print.

I would like you to build me a decision tree that I can adapt into a flowchart for the piece and also use to sort the researchers I am interviewing. It should take a reader from the starting commitment "I take the Löbian result seriously, as a theorem" down to a named position on whether a self-trusting agent is possible.

The constraints are as follows.

- Five branch nodes and six leaves.
- Each branch node must be a single claim or fork on which the positions genuinely come apart, phrased so that a reader who knows nothing about the debate can answer it without already knowing which leaf it leads to. Do not name a position at any node, and do not phrase a node as "do you think X?"
- The forks are not meant to be answered in a fixed sequence by every reader. The tree should route a reader to the forks that are relevant given their earlier answers, so that a reader who says non-proof justification is enough is not forced through the forks about the scope of a proof they never required.
- The five forks I need, and the distinctions they must draw, are: (1) whether "the agent trusts itself" requires the agent to prove its own soundness, or whether a weaker, non-proof justification — an empirical track record, probabilistic calibration, pragmatic reliability — is enough; (2) whether the agent's whole reasoning is captured by one arithmetizable theory, or whether it can be spread across a hierarchy of theories or a non-arithmetizable foundation; (3) whether the self-trust has to be global, covering every statement including statements about the agent's own trust, or whether it can be restricted to a safe domain that excludes self-referential claims; (4) whether, for an agent that does not prove its own soundness, the obstacle is still a problem about logical possibility — the agent simply lacks any genuine reason to trust itself — or whether it collapses into a question of practical reliability; and (5) whether the obstacle is a fact about the particular logic the agent happens to use, or about logic in general, that is, whether switching to a different logic, such as a fixed-point or non-classical provability logic, could dissolve it.
- The six leaves must include, at minimum: the hard line that the obstacle is inescapable and full self-trust is impossible for any consistent agent; the view that the obstacle is an artifact of the single-theory assumption and dissolves once the agent is allowed a hierarchy; the restrictionist view that the agent can trust itself about a safe domain while withholding trust about its own trust; the logic-switcher view that the obstacle is real for classical provability but is dissolved by a different logic; the deflationary view that the obstacle survives even without a proof, because the agent still lacks a genuine reason to trust itself; and the pragmatist view that the obstacle is a philosophical curiosity that never bites for real, working agents.
- Each leaf carries its short name and one sentence on its most awkward commitment — the thing a defender has to accept that they would most like to avoid.
- Where two leaves are so close that the tree cannot actually tell them apart, flag that pair explicitly in a note rather than papering over it.
- For any combination of answers the tree allows but that no real position occupies, add a one-line note and a one-clause diagnosis: is nobody there because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it is simply unexplored?
- Keep the language at the level of a smart general reader, not a logician. I will introduce the theorem in my own words in the piece; the tree itself must not require the reader to parse any formal notation.

The underlying aim is to sort people by what they actually believe about the structure of the argument, not by which label they reach for, so the instrument has to separate positions on the forks above rather than on terminology.
