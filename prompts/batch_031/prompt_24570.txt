I'm presenting at a decision-theory workshop in about six weeks, and I'm defending a version of the money-pump argument for diachronic rationality, so I want to be ready for the question period before I even get there. My thesis, roughly: an agent whose preferences are locally rational at each time but globally incoherent — intransitive across a set of prospects, or unstable over time in a way that lets a patient bookie extract a sure loss — is irrational in a genuine, substantive sense, and the money pump is the best available argument for this. My sketch goes like this: I lay out a cycle of small trades, each of which the agent would accept if offered in isolation, such that completing the cycle leaves her strictly worse off; I then argue that the defect is not merely that she'll lose money if she happens to make a bad sequence of choices, but that the preference structure itself is incoherent, and that the only escape is a precommitment device — whose very necessity I take as evidence of the underlying defect. A colleague warned me that the decision theorists in the room will see through the argument within a minute, and that the economists will keep trying to flatten it into "no arbitrage," so I'd rather anticipate that now.

The room is mostly decision theorists who know the Dutch-book and money-pump literature cold, plus two or three economists who think in terms of revealed preference and arbitrage and are skeptical that the philosophical framing adds anything, and one philosopher of action who keeps asking what the argument actually establishes about normativity rather than about trading. Prepare me for the question period from *this* room, not the generic objections I'd get from a different crowd. Give me the six to eight objections I'll actually get, ordered by how much damage each would do to my thesis, each phrased the way the person who raises it would phrase it — the decision theorists technical and already halfway to their own reply, the economists reductionist, the philosopher of action pressingly abstract. For each, give me a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is soft. And close with the one objection I'm hoping nobody asks: the one that would actually threaten the argument rather than just test whether I know the literature.
