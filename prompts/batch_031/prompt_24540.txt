Construct a thought experiment, set in an ordinary workplace (an office pool or a small internal prediction market, not a casino), in which a risk-averse employee is nonetheless Dutch-booked. The employee's credences over an exhaustive three-way partition sum to less than one, violating additivity, and the employee prices each outcome at its own credence; the employee is non-risk-neutral, would decline each individual bet on risk-aversion grounds (each is fair to the employee), and would also decline the whole package (a sure loss), yet the employee is exposed to a guaranteed loss because the employee's role as pool-runner commits the pool to the posted prices, which a colleague can trade against as a bundle. After the scenario, show that it meets each specification: state the credences and the posted odds, give a payoff table across the three states, identify the sure loss, explain why each individual bet is fair to the employee and why a risk-averse employee declines it, explain why the bundle is a sure loss, and explain why the employee cannot avoid it by declining the combination. Finish with an honest note: which single feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to the book if that feature is removed (for instance, if the employee were free to add a margin so the prices sum to at least one), and how a defender of the standard risk-aversion objection to Dutch book arguments would attack the case.
