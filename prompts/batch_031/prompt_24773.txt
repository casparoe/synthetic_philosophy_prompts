Forecasting tournaments and the wider practice of scoring probabilistic predictions rank forecasters with "proper scoring rules," the best known being the Brier score, under which a forecaster who reports probability p for a binary event is charged (p − o)², where o is 1 if the event occurs and 0 if it does not, and the logarithmic score, which punishes confident errors more heavily. A rule is called proper because a forecaster's expected score is optimized by reporting her genuine credence, and the decision-theoretic foundation of the whole enterprise is the Dutch book argument: an agent whose credences are incoherent, or who acts on a probability that is not her real degree of belief, can be Dutch-booked — offered a set of bets that, on the probability she is working with, each looks favorable to her, but that together guarantee a loss. Prediction markets make the same structure literal, with real stakes and real arbitrage; forecasting tournaments replace the money with a score, and treat a good score as a mark of epistemic virtue, as evidence that the forecaster's beliefs are rational and well-calibrated.

What view of probability, and of rational belief, does this practice presuppose? First, does it presuppose that degrees of belief are the right currency for evaluating a forecaster, as against a practice that scores only definite "will" or "will not" judgments, and what rival picture of forecasting does that commitment quietly rule out? Second, and more importantly, does the practice presuppose that the Dutch book argument is a legitimate route from a pragmatic norm — do not knowingly accept a sure loss — to an epistemic norm — your credences should be coherent, and your reports should track your beliefs — and, if so, what exactly is doing the work in that transfer, given that in a tournament the "loss" a bad score inflicts is a mark of disrepute rather than a real cost? And third, there is a tension the practice must quietly resolve: the classical Dutch book argument establishes coherence, that your credences obey the probability axioms, but the tournament rewards accuracy, that your credences match the world; a perfectly coherent forecaster who reports 0.5 for everything cannot be Dutch-booked and is yet useless. So what must the practice presuppose to bridge coherence and accuracy — that coherence is a route to accuracy, that the betting metaphor is not merely instrumental, that the idealized bookmaker is a genuine normative authority, or something else?

Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, say where the practice is underdetermined so that several theories of probability would license it, and say what would have to change in the practice if its foundation were replaced by a purely accuracy-based account of rational belief, or by a purely evidentialist one that locates the norm of credence in the evidence rather than in the avoidance of a hypothetical loss.
