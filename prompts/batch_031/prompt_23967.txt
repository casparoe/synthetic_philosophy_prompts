I've been stuck on the boundary problem — like, who actually counts as being in the demos for a given decision — and I keep landing on an argument that feels like it proves too much, but I can't pin down the exact step that's doing the work.

(1) The all-affected principle: anyone who is significantly affected by a collective decision ought to have a voice in making it. I find this really hard to argue against, it's basically the whole point of consent.
(2) A town council is voting on whether to approve a new data center. The center draws a lot of power and the marginal generation is gas, so the project adds a definate chunk of CO2 to the atmosphere.
(3) That CO2 is a real (if tiny) contributor to the sea-level rise that is significantly affecting people in low lying coastal areas.
(4) So by (1), those coastal people ought to have a voice in the town council's data center vote.
(5) The same reasoning applies to any local decision with a climate footprint — transit, zoning, industrial permits — so effectively everyone on earth has a voice in essentially every local economic decision.
(6) That dissolves the demos. There's no workable boundary, and no local decision could ever be made without a global referendum.
(7) So (1) is false.

I'm not willing to just accept (6) or (7) — I genuinely think affected people have some claim — so I suspect the argument is smuggling something in somewhere, I just can't name it. Which premise or step do you reject, and what does it cost to reject it? And if the whole fix is just to quietly put a threshold on "significantly affected," I want to hear why that threshold isn't doing all the heavy lifting and making (1) basically empty.
