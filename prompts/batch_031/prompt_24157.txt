I teach an advanced epistemology seminar with a unit on Aumann's agreement theorem, and I want a handout that sorts students by what they actually believe about what the theorem establishes, not by which label they find attractive. Write a decision tree — roughly five to seven branch nodes and six to eight leaves — built from the logical space of the debate rather than from a list of named papers: each branch node should be one of the theorem's assumptions or a claim on which the readings genuinely come apart, such as whether the result is trivial (a restatement of Bayes's rule and the law of total probability) or substantive, which assumption is doing the real work (the common prior versus the common knowledge of rationality), whether its content is the impossibility of disagreement as such or only of common knowledge of disagreement, whether the common-prior assumption is as strong as it first appears, and whether it is a descriptive claim about real agents or a purely idealized one (with or without implications for multi-agent AI systems). Phrase every node as a question a student could answer without already knowing the leaf it leads to, and place the standard readings as leaves identified by their content, not by authors. Note any unoccupied combination of answers with a one-line diagnosis of why nobody holds it, and flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot really separate. Respond in the style of an expert in epistemic logic and the philosophy of probability, and think very carefully before answering, since the point is that the tree tracks the actual logical space of the debate.
