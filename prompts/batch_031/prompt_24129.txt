I was a population geneticist for about thirty years before I retired, and lately I've been falling down the rabbit hole of philosophy, specifically this thing called procreative beneficence, which is basically the idea that when you're deciding whether or what child to have, you have some reason to pick the child who'll end up with the best life. It's a topic that keeps coming up in my reading, and I find myself with a bunch of half-formed intuitions that I can't quite get to agree with each other, so I wanted to lay them out and see if there's a principle underneath.

Here's where I land on a few cases, just going by gut. First: a couple where the mother carries a gene for Huntington's, and they can use PGD to pick an embryo that doesn't have it. That's obviously fine, I'd even say they should do it. Second: the same setup but the condition is something milder, like a gene that gives you chronic joint pain in your fifties but no real disability. Still fine, I think, maybe less clearly. Third: a couple who uses the same technology not to avoid a disease but to nudge toward a trait, like picking embryos likely to be taller or to have some musical aptitude. That one feels wrong to me, it crosses into eugenics in a way the first two don't. Now the one that's actually bugging me: a couple who can choose between two sperm donors, where one would produce a child with a serious genetic condition and the the other would produce a completely different, healthy child. I'm pretty sure they should pick the healthy donor, but the moment I think about the fact that the unhealthy child wont exist if they pick the other donor, my confidence wobbles, because it seems like I can't be blaming them for harming a person who never was. And then there's the one I can't resolve at all: if there's any reason to pick the best child you can, why stop at avoiding disease, why not keep optimizing, and the thought of that makes me want to deny there's any reason to optimize at all, which I don't actually believe.

So what I'm after is: is there a single principle, or a small set of them, that gets all of these verdicts, or at least most of them, and if not, which of my gut calls does it force me to drop? I'd also like to know what that principle says about a case I haven't mentioned, which is a couple where the only realistic option is a child with a mild condition, and the better option would require a procedure that's risky to the mother herself. I guess what I'm really trying to figure out is whether the right comparison is always between the child you'll have and the ones you could have instead, or whether there's something more to it that I'm missing.
