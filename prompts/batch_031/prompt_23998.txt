I was talking with a friend about Scanlon's contractualism the other day and I keep getting stuck on aggregation problem — specifically the direction where contractualism seems to bless a principle that imposes a lot of total harm, just because no single person's slice of it is big enough to be reasonably rejectable. I want you to build me a concrete case that isolates exactly that feature, and then show me the machinery step by step.

Here's the spec. Give me a realistic public-policy scenario — infrastructure, public health, environmental regulation, something like that, not a trolley and not a life-or-death emergency — with numbers a real agency would actually use. Two options: one that imposes a small individual cost on each member of a large population, and a status-quo alternative that adds nothing for them but leaves a bigger population exposed to a larger risk. Make the per-person cost small enough that, on its own, it's the kind of thing nobody would reasonably reject, but make the population big enough that the total cost is genuely significant — large enough that a utilitarian has to actually do the math to clear it.

Then do the following. First, state the principle the contractualist is evaluating in Scanlon's "no one could reasonably reject" terms, and walk through why no individual in the affected population can reasonably reject it. Second, state what the utilitarian does with the same numbers and why it lands on the same verdict. Third, make it explicit that the two theories agree here but for different reasons — the contractualist because of a per-person threshold, the utilitarian because of a total — and identify which single structural feature of my case is doing the work of keeping them aligned. Fourth, change exactly one dial: raise the per-person cost, keep the population size fixed, and tell me at roughly what per-person level the contractualist flips while the utilitarian stays put, and why. Fifth, finish with how a defender of contractualism would answer the obvious objection that the base-case verdict — accepting a large aggregate harm — is counterintuitive, and whether that defense actually holds or just concedes the point.
