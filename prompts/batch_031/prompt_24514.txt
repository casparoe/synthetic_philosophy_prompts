I'm a retired computer scientist — I spent about thirty years building and analyzing simulations, and it was only in the past couple of years, after I retired, that I started taking philosophy seriously, mostly through the philosophy of mind. A friend who knew I was in a philosophical mood pushed me to play The Last of Us, and I finished it a fortnight ago and have not stopped turning it over. The game ends with Joel, after a long journey across a post-apocalyptic, fungal-pandemic America in which he and the fourteen-year-old immune girl Ellie have grown into something like father and daughter, refusing to let a band of rebels called the Fireflies operate on Ellie — a surgery that would almost certainly kill her but might yield a cure — and instead gunning his way out of their hospital and then lying to her about what happened. What has not let go of me is that the game is plainly asking me to treat Ellie as a being who matters morally: to feel the weight of Joel's decision, to judge it, to be outraged or to grieve — even though I know perfectly well that Ellie is a fictional character who does not exist and cannot be harmed.

As someone who spent his career building simulations, I am acutely aware of the gulf between a model and the thing it models, and my first instinct was to file my unease under the assumption that I was merely responding to a simulation, not to a person. But I am not sure that instinct is right, and I have been trying to work out whether the game is committing a category error — asking me to hold genuine moral obligations to a being that is not real — or whether it is doing something philosophically coherent, in the sense that the moral concern and judgment I feel toward Ellie is a real and meaningful kind of moral engagement even though its object is fictional. I am especially interested in what this case shows about the conditions for being a moral patient: whether consciousness, the capacity to suffer, and being real are all genuinely necessary, or whether our moral psychology can be meaningfully directed at fictional beings in a way that is more than a quasi-emotion. I would like a sustained critical reading of the game through this lens that takes the question seriously in both directions — where the game's treatment of its characters illuminates the philosophical question, where it resists or distorts it, and what the game adds to the debate that argument alone does not. Please do not simply summarize the plot or catalogue the issues; I want a reading that commits to a position and defends it.

Does The Last of Us's treatment of its characters — asking the player to care about and to judge fictional beings as though they were genuine moral patients — commit a category error, or is it philosophically coherent?
