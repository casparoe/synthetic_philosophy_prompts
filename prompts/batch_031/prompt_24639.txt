I'm part of a reading group that has been working through decision theory and the foundations of probability, and two of the regulars got into a long argument last month about Dutch book arguments that I have not been able to referee. I've read a lot of the rationalist-blog literature on probability and coherence over the years, so I know the terrain a little, but I genuinely couldn't tell which of them was more right, and I suspect they were partly talking past each other. I'd like you to go through their exchange and say who is right about which specific point. Here is the argument, roughly in the order it happened.

(1) Mira opened by saying the Dutch book argument is decisive for the probability axioms: if your credences are incoherent, then there exists a set of bets, offered at prices equal to your credences, such that whatever the outcome, you suffer a net loss; and since no one rationally wants a guaranteed loss, incoherent credences are irrational, full stop. (2) Jonas replied that the argument is question-begging: it only works if the agent is forced to accept every bet the bookmaker offers, but a rational agent can simply decline the bet, so the argument establishes at most that if you accept all bets at your credence prices then incoherence is costly, which is true but trivial and does not show that incoherent beliefs are bad, because you are under no obligation to take the bet. (3) Mira answered that refusing the bet does not dissolve the commitment: a credence is the price at which you are willing to trade, the cash-value of a belief, so if you will not buy a 60-cent ticket on an event you assign 60% to, then you do not in fact assign 60%; declining is not a rational response to a Dutch book, it is evidence that the credences were not really your credences. (4) Jonas then said the cash-value move is doing all the work, and doing it quietly: it assumes that beliefs matter only insofar as they price bets, and that dollar payoffs can be summed across outcomes; an agent whose utility for money is non-linear, or who has preferences over how things come about rather than only over the terminal payoffs, can be Dutch-bookable in dollars without being irrational, so the argument proves its conclusion only for agents who already evaluate money additively, which is a substantive assumption about preferences, not a purely logical fact about belief. (5) Mira closed by granting the caveats but insisting that the strong Dutch book — a sure loss in every state of the world, as opposed to a merely dominated expected value — is irrational for anyone who prefers more money to less, and that any incoherent credence function over a finite set of events is strongly Dutch-bookable, so the argument survives his objections for everyone except agents with genuinely bizarre preferences.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the five moves on a scale from 1 (clearly mistaken) to 5 (clearly right), with a brief justification for each rating, and then say what, if anything, both of them are missing. I am especially interested in whether the distinction between a sure loss in every world and a merely expected-value-dominated position changes the force of any of the moves, and whether the debate is really about the probability axioms at all or about something else. Please end your answer with a single line beginning "Verdict:" that gives the five numeric ratings in order, separated by commas, followed by a semicolon and a short phrase (no more than eight words) naming what both parties are missing.
