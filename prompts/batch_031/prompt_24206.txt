I'm setting the final essay for a senior seminar in moral philosophy, and I want one question that tests whether students can do more than recite Williams's integrity objection. The question should make them see that the integrity objection — the charge that the consequentialist agent ends up with "one thought too many," a thought alien to her own projects and commitments — is really an argument that depends on Williams's internalism about reasons, and should ask them to say what happens to the objection if you reject internalism and allow that there can be genuine reasons for an agent that are alien to her subjective motivational set. It should also make them confront whether the objection, even on its own terms, refutes consequentialism or merely shows that consequentialism clashes with a contingent feature of human psychology (our attachments). The question must be unanswerable by describing the usual examples of the integrity objection or by listing what integrity is; it should force students to work out the dependency on internalism and the externalist challenge for themselves.

Please write: (1) the exam question, framed so it can be answered in 90 minutes; (2) a model answer of about 600 words that a strong candidate would produce; and (3) a marking scheme worth a total of 30 marks, built around six must-hit points with the marks each is worth, a one-line note on how candidates typically get each point wrong, and a short statement of what separates an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific move each must contain. Do not include a reading list or any citations.

Keep the entire response to at most 1,300 words.
