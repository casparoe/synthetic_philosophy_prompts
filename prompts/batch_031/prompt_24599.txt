I was a physicist for most of my career and have only in the last few years begun to read philosophy seriously, so please excuse the idiom; I still think in terms of quantities, of convergence, and of whether a number is actually there. I would like you to examine a familiar practice and tell me what it quietly presupposes.

The practice is pricing a gamble by its expected value. An insurance company sets a premium as the expected payout plus a margin; a casino quotes a house edge, understood as the player's average loss per bet; an investor is advised to buy a security when its expected return exceeds its cost. In each case a single number is treated as a determinate, stable property of the gamble, and the rational decision is read off by comparing that number with a price or with zero. The practice seems to presuppose that every well-defined gamble possesses a well-defined expected value.

Now consider a particular gamble, which I will describe in full so that nothing here depends on a name. A fair coin is flipped until the first heads appears. If the first heads lands on the nth flip, the player wins 2^(n-1) dollars if n is odd and loses 2^(n-1) dollars if n is even; the probability that the first heads lands on flip n is 2^(-n). Form the expected value as the sum, over all n, of the probability of n times the payoff at n. Each term of this series has absolute value exactly 1/2, with the signs alternating: +1/2, -1/2, +1/2, -1/2, and so on. The partial sums therefore oscillate between 1/2 and 0 and never settle; the sum of the positive terms diverges to positive infinity, and the sum of the negative terms diverges to negative infinity. The gamble thus has no expected value at all — not a very large one, not an infinite one, but none. (It is the game sometimes called the Pasadena game; the description above is self-contained.)

I want you to do three things. First, reconstruct the implicit theory: what view of a gamble's value does the pricing practice strictly presuppose, as opposed to what it merely fits well with? Is it the claim that every gamble has a finite expected value, that every gamble has an expected value in the extended reals (allowing positive or negative infinity), or something weaker? Second, say whether that presupposition is coherent, and examine whether the practice can tell the present case apart from the St. Petersburg game, in which every payoff is positive and the expected value diverges to positive infinity. In particular, check whether the standard replies to St. Petersburg — bounded utility, finite wealth or truncation of the game, impatience — transfer to the present case, and note that truncating this game after a finite number of flips leaves the partial sums oscillating rather than converging, so that the value is not recovered as the limit of the truncated values. Third, tell me where the practice is underdetermined — that is, where several different theories of value would license the same pricing behavior — and what would have to change in the practice if we accepted that some perfectly well-defined gambles simply have no value at all.

I care less about the algebra than about the conceptual work: what is the practice doing when it treats an undefined quantity as though it were a number, and is what it does defensible, or does it depend on a premise that this example exposes?
