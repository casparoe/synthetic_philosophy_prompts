I'm putting together a handout for my intermediate epistemology seminar and I keep running into the same wall: students walk in treating the lottery paradox and the preface paradox as one puzzle with two names, and then they can't see why the standard responses come apart from each other. So I'd like you to write a decision tree that starts at "I find the lottery paradox a genuine problem for the idea that high probability justifies belief" and leads a student, branch by branch, to a named position. Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first lay out the questions, then locate the actual positions in the answers.

Each branch node should be one of the principles or distinctions the two paradoxes actually put under pressure — whether probability below one can justify belief at all, whether belief has to be closed under conjunction, whether the lottery agent really believes each ticket will lose or is just in a state of high confidence, whether belief and acceptance are the same notion, whether there's some probability threshold above which belief kicks in, and whether the evidence really supports the conjunction of all the tickets losing. Phrase every node as a question a student could answer on the spot without already knowing which leaf it leads to. The leaves have to include at least the infallist who rejects the lottery principle, the closure-rejecter, the threshold view, the view that separates belief from acceptance, and a "the paradox dissolves" leaf, plus one more you think belongs.

For any combination of answers your tree leaves unoccupied, say in a sentence why nobody sits there — is that combo incoherent, just unattractive, or genuinly unexplored? And flag any two leaves your tree can't actually seperate, because I'd rather the handout be honest about that than pretend the taxonomy is cleaner than it is. I'm not after a reading list or a history of who said what first; I want the logical space drawn out so the students can locate themselves in it.
