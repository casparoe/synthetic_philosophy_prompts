I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, and Wittgenstein's private language argument keeps surfacing for me whenever I think about specs and self-verification, so I'd like a poem that does the argument's work in its form rather than restating it. Write a villanelle — nineteen lines, the standard ABA ABA ABA ABA ABA ABAA scheme, in or near iambic pentameter — with the two refrains "I meant the same thing, and I meant it so" and "but no one could check it, and I can't know," alternating between the private user's assurance and the doubt that there is no independent standard to check against. The speaker keeps a diary in a language only they understand. The turn — the realization that "remembering" what your own word means, or a mental picture of the meaning, is not a criterion of correctness, because in a private case every use merely seems right and "seeming right" is not "being right" — should arrive around the middle of the poem. The forced return to the same two lines should enact the impossibility rather than just assert it. A philosopher of language should be able to tell which line is the proponent's and which is the skeptic's, but it should still read as a poem about a person quietly losing a word, not a lecture or a list of objections.
