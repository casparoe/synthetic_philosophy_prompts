I'm a novelist working on a book that turns on whether a mind has to "understand" what it is doing in order to be doing it well, and I've settled on a set of commitments I'd like stress-tested before I build the argument into the plot. Here is where I've landed: (1) A novel is a system that can be genuinely meaningful — coherent, "about" things, affecting readers — without the author, or the text itself, "comprehending" that meaning; meaning is a structural property that emerges from the work the way fitness emerges in evolution without a designer. (2) For a character to be fully realized, I must hold a determinate conception of the character's inner life — a psychology I could, in principle, articulate to a reader; I "know" my characters from the inside. (3) A system that passes every behavioral test for understanding should be treated as genuinely comprehending; the behavioral criterion is the whole criterion. (4) What makes a character's inner life "real" is not my access to it but its effects on the reader; the reality of the inner life is constituted by the reader's experience of it.

I'm not sure these hang together, and I don't know where the pressure is. Please audit the set: which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs. Order the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and for each say specifically what would have to give. If I've posed the task on a confused premise, or if one of these commitments is incoherent rather than merely in tension, say so plainly instead of smoothing it over. Think very carefully before answering, and respond in the style of an expert in philosophy of mind who also reads a great deal of fiction.
