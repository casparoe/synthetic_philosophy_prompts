The Dutch book argument is a way of showing that certain patterns of belief and preference are irrational because they make you vulnerable to a set of bets that will lose you money no matter what happens. The basic idea, developed by Ramsey and de Finetti, is this: suppose you assign a degree of belief, or credence, to a proposition, and you are willing to bet on that proposition at odds corresponding to your credence. If your credences do not obey the axioms of probability — say you assign 0.7 to a proposition and 0.7 to its negation, which sum to 1.4 — then a clever bookmaker can offer you a package of bets such that you lose money in every possible state of the world. This is a Dutch book. Because losing money for sure is obviously bad, and because the only reason you would be vulnerable to this is your incoherent credences, the argument shows that rational agents must have credences that form a proper probability distribution.

The argument has a further moral, which is that coherence — being immune to Dutch books — is what makes your beliefs rational. If your credences cannot be Dutch-booked, then they are rationally justified, and there is nothing more to say about whether they are well calibrated to the world. The Dutch book argument is thus a complete account of rational belief.

There is also a version of the argument for preferences. If your preferences over risky options do not satisfy the independence axiom, then you can be money-pumped: a bookmaker can string you along with a sequence of trades that leave you worse off no matter what, even though each individual trade seems acceptable to you. This is why the Dutch book argument is often cited as a complete justification for expected utility maximization: it shows directly that if you do not maximize expected utility, you can be exploited, so that avoiding exploitation is enough to conclude that you must be an expected utility maximizer.
