A competent adult can sign an advance directive stating that, should they later lose the capacity to decide -- for example, from advanced dementia -- their life support is to be withdrawn. When that crisis arrives the patient usually can no longer confirm or withdraw the directive, yet physicians and surrogates are expected to carry it out as though it were the patient's own present decision. The practice is strikingly asymmetric: a competent person may revoke a directive at any moment, so the present self outranks the past one, but an incapacitated person may do no such thing, so the past self outranks the present one -- authority tracks competence, not time. What view of the self over time does that practice presuppose? Does it commit us to the claim that one person persists through large psychological change and that the earlier, competent self retains authority over the later, altered one? Reconstruct the implicit theory of diachronic authority the practice rests on, say which rival views of personal identity or of reasons it quietly rules out, mark where the practice is underdetermined and could be licensed by more than one theory, and describe what would have to change in how we treat advance directives if we rejected the assumption that the earlier self outranks the later one.
