Over the past year I have been noticing a pattern in arguments — at work, in the news, even around the dinner table — and I cannot find a word for it. Someone makes a claim of the form "if X, then Y," and when the claim is challenged, the same sentence quietly comes to mean a different thing, and the speaker uses whichever version helps them at the moment, as if the two were the same claim. I have watched it happen at least twice in real situations. At my workplace, a team was debating whether to ship a product in March; the lead argued for a delay, saying, "If we ship in March, it will flop." When a colleague asked what that was based on, the lead replied, "Well, if we had shipped in March last year, it would have flopped — the market was already weak then." The colleague pointed out that the market had changed since then, and the lead answered, "It's the same point — shipping in March flops." The second example is from a doctor I know: to a patient who had been in pain for months he said, "If you had come in earlier, we would have caught it sooner," and then, in the same breath, "If you come in as soon as the pain starts, we'll catch it early." When the patient pushed back — "How do you know we would have caught it sooner? The tests are unreliable" — the doctor waved it off: "The point is the same, come in early." In both cases, one version of the sentence did the arguing, and the other version was produced when the first one ran into trouble, and the speaker treated the switch as invisible.

I am not a philosopher; I simply have a habit of watching how arguments actually proceed, and this is the one I keep coming back to. What I cannot shake is that the two versions are not the same claim. One is about something that did not happen, so it cannot be checked against what actually happened, and yet people treat it as settled; the other is about what will happen, and it can simply be wrong. I have been told this is "just equivocation," and I am not sure I buy it, because the two readings are not different senses of a word that the speaker is slipping between. They seem to be built into how "if" sentences work, and the switch always runs in the direction the argument needs. So I do not think this is a person being loose with a word; it looks more like a fact about the sentence itself being leaned on.

Here is what I would like to know. Is there an established name for this move — deploying one version of an "if" claim and, when it is challenged, retreating to the other version as though it were the same claim — or for the underlying fact that "if" sentences can say two different things and get used as though they were one? I am not after a list of books or authors; I want the concept itself, if it has one. If it does, what is it, and how does it differ from plain equivocation and from any other nearby idea? If it does not, I would like a good definition and boundary cases. And in particular, I suspect the switch is sometimes legitimate: if the connection between X and Y is something like a law — "if you heat ice to its melting point at sea level, it melts" — then the "what would have happened" version and the "what will happen" version seem to say the same thing, and I would like to understand why that is, and where exactly the line falls between the legitimate cases and the illegitimate ones.
