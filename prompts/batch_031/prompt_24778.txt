I'm preparing a small chapbook to close a graduate seminar on set theory, and the last chapter is on the independence of the continuum hypothesis, so I'd like the closing piece to be a full sestina: six stanzas of six lines plus a three-line envoi, with the end-words rotating in the standard sestina order. The structural point I want the form to carry: choose six end-words that name the fixed axiomatic furniture — words like "axiom," "world," "real," "proof," "set," "truth"; you may pick your own, but they must be the invariant stuff — and use exactly the same six words as end-words in every stanza. The words never change, only their order does, and that should mirror the actual mathematics: the ZFC axioms are identical in every model, and what moves from model to model is everything else. A reader should be able to feel, from the form alone, that the vocabulary is constant while the arrangement is not.

In content, I'd like the poem in the voice of the continuum itself — the reals as a role that the axioms cast different sets into in different models — and I'd like it to track the story of the independence results. The opening stanzas should carry the naive hope that the axioms settle the size of the reals: Cantor's question, the assumption that ZFC decides it. Then the constructible world: a model in which the continuum hypothesis is true, the result of Gödel. Then the forced world: a model built by adding new reals, in which the continuum hypothesis is false, the result of Cohen. The flip has to be visible: the proposition is asserted as true in one half of the poem and as false in the other, with not one of the six end-words changing in the meantime. Use enough of the real vocabulary — constructible, forcing, model, axiom, continuum, aleph — that a set theorist would recognize the mathematics, but the poem should still stand as a poem for a reader who has never seen a forcing argument.

Two accuracy constraints. First, don't collapse independence into the claim that the hypothesis is neither true nor false: the result is that the axioms prove neither the hypothesis nor its negation, and the poem should keep the distinction between provability and truth, or at least not blur it. Second, the envoi should say exactly what has to be given up — not the question, but the hope that the old axioms are the whole court of appeal — and it should leave the door open on whether new axioms, large cardinals or determinacy or whatever the program turns out to be, could settle the matter, rather than closing it. The test is that someone who knows the mathematics can read the sestina and see the argument in the form — the fixed words, the rotating order, the mid-poem flip — while someone who knows none of it still finds it a good poem.
