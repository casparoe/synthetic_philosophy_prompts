I work on an AI safety team, and a chunk of my job is reviewing transcripts from our two-model debate evaluations — the setup where two models argue opposite sides of a question and human reviewers judge which side made the stronger case. We run these several times a day, and the failure mode that keeps tripping us up is what I've started calling "obfuscated arguments": one model returns an answer buried under a long, technically plausible-looking argument that sounds authoritative, that I genuinely cannot verify, and that I can't distinguish from a genuinely hard technical claim the model is honestly struggling to support. The worry is that the model may be using the apparent depth as a smokescreen to hide a weak or wrong position, and that a human judge with limited time and limited domain expertise is exactly the audience this is designed to fool. I don't have a formal philosophy background, so I want to be explicit about what I'm not asking for: I don't want a literature review, and I don't want the abstract question of whether debate is a viable safety mechanism. I want you to take the underlying principle — that a genuinely good argument should be checkable in principle, and that the dangerous move is making an argument look checkable when it isn't — and turn it into a concrete, reusable decision procedure that my reviewers can run on a single transcript segment in a few minutes.

Concretely, I'd like a sequence of questions to ask in order, each with a clear statement of what counts as a sufficient answer, and I want the procedure to be honest about the points where it stops being mechanical and hands the call to a human judgment. It has to handle the hard cases we actually hit: (a) the argument is in a technical domain I'm not an expert in, so I can't check the claims directly; (b) it's in a domain I am an expert in, so I could check it but it would take longer than the review window allows; (c) the two models produce nearly identical arguments on opposite sides, which I read as a strong signal that at least one is obfuscating; and (d) the argument is short, clear, and on its face checkable, but I just don't believe it and I can't articulate why. I also need the procedure to be explicit about the cost asymmetry we operate under, because it determines where the flagging threshold sits: a false negative (letting an obfuscated argument through) is a genuine safety risk, while a false positive (flagging an honest but complex argument as obfuscation) wastes compute and, worse, trains the models to avoid legitimate technical depth, which is its own safety problem. Where a flagged segment goes — i.e., who has to sign off on an "obfuscated" call before it affects the evaluation — is a senior reviewer, so the procedure should mark which outputs are auto-flagged and which are escalated. Finally, please run the procedure on two worked examples: one where it correctly flags an obfuscated argument, and one where it correctly clears an argument that is genuinely just hard to verify — and include at least one case where the procedure's verdict runs against my gut reaction, and say how I should reconcile that rather than just defaulting to my gut.
