I'm working through the Nyāya theory of inference (anumāna) and have come to hold the following position: the Nyāya's three-step (tri-avacāra) account of the mental process of inference — the apprehension of the property (padārtha-grahaṇa), the second step (parāmarśa), and the final inferential awareness (anumiti) — is a genuine, defensible account of the psychology of inference, and the standard Buddhist (Pramāṇavāda) objections to it are not as decisive as they are often made to appear. Specifically, I hold that (i) the Buddhist charge that the three-step theory treats inference as a process rather than a single momentary, non-erroneous awareness, and thus fails to capture what a pramāṇa is supposed to be, misreads the Nyāya's theory; (ii) the Buddhist charge that the second step, parāmarśa, is obscure and arguably incoherent — that it is either a mere recollection (and thus a kind of memory, not a pramāṇa) or a mere conceptual operation (and thus not a genuine step in the process of inference) — fails to grasp what parāmarśa is doing; and (iii) the more radical Buddhist (Prāsaṅgika) charge that the Nyāya's inference is not a distinct pramāṇa but is reducible to perception, specifically to the perception of the vyāpti (the invariable connection between the hetu and the sādhya), is a misreading of the Nyāya's theory. What would count against this position? I want the arguments and considerations that should move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be, including (a) the strongest versions of the Buddhist objections, (b) any internal tensions within the Nyāya theory (for example, the tension between treating inference as a process and treating it as a single pramāṇa, and the tension between the three-step theory and the Nyāya's own definition of anumāna), (c) any cases or scenarios where the three-step theory gets things wrong (for example, immediate inferences, inferences where the vyāpti is not explicitly recollected, and inferences drawn from a single instance), and (d) whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability — that is, whether the Nyāya theorist can always re-gloss parāmarśa to avoid any specific objection, and if so, whether that is a problem for a view of this kind. I'd like an honest assessment of how exposed the three-step theory actually is, and which of these objections a committed Nyāya theorist (say, a follower of Uddyotakara or Raghunātha) would concede as counting against them, and which they would dismiss.
