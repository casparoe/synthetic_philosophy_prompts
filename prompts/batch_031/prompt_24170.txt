I cover science and ideas for a magazine, with a recurring focus on the foundations of mathematics and decision theory. I'm writing a piece on the strategy-stealing argument in combinatorial game theory, and I want to get the technical details right before I publish. At a conference last month, two of my sources — a combinatorial game theorist and a logician — got into a pointed exchange about whether the argument is actually valid. I was in the room and took notes, but I'm not a game theorist myself, and I couldn't tell who was right. Here's the exchange as I recorded it, with their names changed:

Alice (combinatorial game theorist): "Strategy-stealing is airtight, and it proves the first player in tic-tac-toe can't lose. Assume the second player had a winning strategy. The first player makes any first move, then plays as if they were the second player, using that strategy. The extra first move can only help, so the first player is guaranteed at least a draw — which contradicts the assumption that the second player had a winning strategy. So the second player has no winning strategy, and the first player can force at least a draw."

Bob (logician): "The argument isn't airtight. It quietly assumes the first move is never a disadvantage, but that's exactly what you need to prove, and it's false in general. In any zugzwang game — where being forced to move is itself a liability — the first player is at a disadvantage, and strategy-stealing clearly can't apply. So the argument is circular: it assumes the very thing it's trying to establish."

Alice: "You're conflating the general form with the tic-tac-toe case. Strategy-stealing is only valid for games where you can independently show the extra move is never a disadvantage. In tic-tac-toe, that's straightforward: an extra X can only help — it blocks a square your opponent needs and can complete your own line. It can never hurt you. So the assumption holds in tic-tac-toe, and the argument goes through there. The fact that the assumption fails in zugzwang games is irrelevant to the tic-tac-toe result."

Bob: "But you've just asserted that an extra X can't hurt, without proving it. And the real gap is in the deviation step. If the stolen strategy tells you to play a square that's already your X — because of your extra first move — you have to deviate and play somewhere else. You haven't shown that this deviation preserves the guarantee. So you're still assuming what you need to prove; you've just moved the assumption from the first move to the deviation step."

Alice: "The deviation step is fine in tic-tac-toe. When the strategy is blocked by your own extra mark, you play any available square. Since an extra mark can only help you, making an arbitrary extra move can't turn a guaranteed non-loss into a loss. The stolen strategy still guarantees at least a draw. So the contradiction stands, and the second player has no winning strategy."

I'd like you to referee this exchange move by move. For each of the five moves, rate it as clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right, and briefly justify the rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what, if anything, both of them are missing.
