I've been reading about AI safety on my own for a while now and one thing keeps bugging me. Companies will test a new model on some big battery of evaluations and if it scores well on average they just ship it out to the public. Nobody seems to actually check that the model can't do something catastrophically bad in some rare corner case -- like it passes 99% of the tests but there's this weird 1% where it does something terrible and they just shrug. So what does that practice actually assuume about how we should be thinking about risk? Is it just expected value thinking, where the average case is all that matters and the rare bad outcomes just get averaged away? And how do you square that with the same crowd who keeps saying we need to be super careful about AI because of the potential for existential catastrophe -- if you only care about the average, then a 1 in a million chance of the end of the world is basically fine, right? I want you to reconstruct what view of risk and responsibility that shipping practice is quietly presupposing, whether that view is even coherent, and what would actually have to change in how we test and deploy these models if we took worst-case guarantees seriously instead of just average performance.
