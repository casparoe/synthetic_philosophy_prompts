I'm in a grad seminar on the philosophy of science and the causation reading has me a little stuck, so I'd rather you help me work through one specific tension than just give me the textbook contrast between the two families of accounts. On the Lewis-style counterfactual view, X causes Y basically when Y counterfactually depends on X: you look at the closest world where X didn't happen and ask whether Y still happens. The interventionist view (Woodward, Pearl) instead says X causes Y when some intervention on X — an exogenous process that sets X to a value and severs X's normal causal inputs — would change Y.

The thing I can't shake is that I keep treating "intervening on X" and "holding the other variables fixed and changing X" as the same move, and if they are the same then the two accounts collapse into each other. But I'm fairly sure thats wrong, because the interventionist setup is supposed to fix cases like causal redundancy — two independent sufficient causes, where taking one away doesn't change the outcome — that the naive counterfactual test mishandles. So could you walk me through, concretely, what an intervention actually does differently from just holding the rest fixed, and why that difference is exactly what lets the interventionist account cope with redundancy while the plain counterfactual one doesn't?

And then the part that bugs me most: I'm not convinced the interventionist account actually earns the direction of causation rather than just assuming it. The requirement that an intervention be exogenous — that you can set X without also changing whatever normally causes X — seems to already presuppose that X sits upstream of its usual causes. If that's right, doesn't the account just build the causal order in by fiat? I'd like a clear sence of how strong that circularity worry really is, and whether there's any defensible answer to it, rather than a summary of what Woodward says.
