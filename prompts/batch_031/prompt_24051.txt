I've been building a system where two LLM agents debate a factual question and I use their resdual disagreement as a confidence signal — the idea being that if they can't agree after a few rounds, the question is probably hard. I recently read Aumann's agreement theorem and it basically sold me: if both agents are calibrated Bays sharing a common prior, then the moment it's common knowledge what each of them believes, they have to land on the same posterior, so any disagreement that's left over is just evidence that one of them is miscalibrated or that they don't actually share a prior. I've started treating the theorem as the theoretical backbone of the whole design, and honestly it feels like it settles the epistomology of disagreement for my use case.

Can you play devil's advocate and tell me what would count against this reading? I want the assumptions of the theorem that could fail in my actual setup — common prior, Bayesian rationality, the common-knowledge condition, whatever else I'm missing — and for each one, what the world would have to look like for it to fail, and which of these are live concerns for a system of two LLMs that only get a handful of turns to talk, as opposed to things that are only a problem in the idealized theorem. I'm especially curious whether the theorem says anything about how fast convergence happens, because my whole signal depends on disagreement after a finite number of rounds, not in the limit. And if you think I've qualified the reading into something that no longer does the work I want it to, say so.
