I teach an intermediate graduate seminar on decision theory for philosophy and economics students (about 14 participants, 75 minutes, comfortable with basic probability but not with stochastic processes). Design an in-class betting game that makes them feel the difference between maximizing expected final wealth over a fixed short horizon and maximizing expected logarithmic wealth, or equivalently the long-run geometric growth rate, in repeated even-money bets. The point should emerge from what participants actually do with their bankrolls, not from a pre-lecture, and the name of the relevant criterion should not be used until the debrief.

The game should use a biased coin or card draw with a partially known probability: participants first observe a short sample of outcomes, then repeatedly choose a fraction of their current bankroll to stake on the next round. Include exact rules, materials, a paper-money or token payoff scheme that avoids real stakes, a minute-by-minute timeline, and at least one twist that forces them to confront estimation error, model misspecification, or the difference between a short horizon and a continuing horizon. In the debrief, map specific moments in the game to the relevant calculations: why a fraction above the long-run optimal fraction can have higher expected wealth for a small number of rounds yet lower expected log wealth, why volatility and the risk of near-ruin matter even when each bet has positive expected value, and why betting less than full Kelly can be a rational response to uncertainty about the true odds rather than mere risk aversion. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, how to handle it, and which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply.

Finally, in the response, clearly separate what is settled among people who work on this topic from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. At minimum, address whether Kelly betting is a normative standard or merely a consequence of log utility, how to choose a fractional Kelly coefficient under ambiguity, whether finite-horizon expected-wealth maximization is a coherent alternative, and how much weight should be given to estimation error, model misspecification, and the possibility of ruin. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations.
