I spent thirty years as an evolutionary biologist, mostly on host-parasite coevolution in insects, retired four years ago, and instead of going back to the field I somehow fell down the rabbit hole of philosophy and through that into Maimonides. Next month I'm giving a paper at a small comparative religion reading group at the university, a dozen or so people: two observant Jews, one of them a rabbinical student, a historian of medieval Judaism, a philosopher of religion who works on divine command theory, a Christian theologian, and a few agnostic philosophers. My thesis is that the instrumentalist account of sacrifice in Guide III:32, the weaning-from-idolatry passage, is not a marginal gloss but the key to the whole work: the sacrificial laws are a divinely ordained transitional form, valuable only insofar as they steer people away from the idea that powers can be fed and appeased and toward knowledge of God, and that this reading is more coherent than the alternative that the offerings have intrinsic atoning value. I sketch it roughly like this: the instrumentalism is not about a fixed historical period but about the human cognitive condition, which the messianic age improves but does not abolish, so the forms persist in their final stage, and besides, as a halakhist writing for the actual world, Maimonides has to legislate the law of sacrifice anyway. I've been trying to seperate the obligation from its reason for weeks and keep failing, and I'm not sure my messianic move is right, but I'll go with it.

What I want is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the thesis. Give me the objections I'm actually likely to get from this particular audience, not the ones I'd get from a secular philosophy department, ordered by how likely and how damaging each is, and phrase each the way that person would phrase it: the observants will talk about ta'amei mitzvot, about God not lying, and about the fact that most of the Talmudic literature treats sacrifice as atonement; the historian will use Maimonides' own legal code against him, including the part where the Messiah rebuilds the Temple and the offerings come back; the philosopher of religion will press me on what grounds the continuing obligation once the instrumental purpose is achieved. For each objection I want a reply of about two minutes, a note on what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and where the reply is weakest. I know my soft spot is somewhere in the gap between the Guide and his own code, but I can't tell who in the room will actually throw it, or in what form, so I'd rather be coherant and wrong than vague and right. And end with the question I'm hoping nobody asks, the one that would genuinely sink the talk.
