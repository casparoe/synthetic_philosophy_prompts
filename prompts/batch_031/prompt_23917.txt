You are advising a small conceptual research group — five researchers, a two-year horizon, and no budget for empirical machine-learning work. The group will work only on the theory and philosophy side of the alignment problem: clarifying what is actually being asked, decomposing the problem space, and identifying which subproblems most of the empirical effort quietly depends on.

Start with the meta-question, because the group cannot prioritize until it has an answer: is "the alignment problem" one problem or many? Give a diagnosis of the structure of the problem space. Is there a single core problem — something like "make the agent's objective track what we actually want" — that the rest are instances or corollaries of, or is it a loose family with no common core? And does the answer to this question actually change how you prioritize, or is it a distinction without a practical difference?

Then, given that diagnosis, pick the two or three subproblems the group should take on over the next two years, and order them. I care less about the list than about the reasoning behind it. For each subproblem you include:

- Why it is upstream: which other subproblems, or which empirical approaches, would be unworkable or misdirected if it were left unsettled.
- What a finished piece of work on it would actually be — a definition, a theorem, a decision procedure, a taxonomy, an impossibility result — and who downstream would consume it.
- How tractable it is for a five-person conceptual team with no ML budget, and what specifically would make it intractable for such a team.
- What evidence or result, over the next year or two, would move it up or down your list.

Use explicit criteria — importance, tractability, neglectedness, and how many other questions depend on the answer — and say how you trade them off against one another. Distinguish subproblems that are load-bearing (many others depend on them) from ones that are leaves (they depend on others but little depends on them), and say why a conceptual team should care about that distinction. End by naming the single finding that, if it came out in the next year, would most change where you put the group's effort.

Deliver a defended agenda, not a survey and not a reading list.
