I was at a foundations-of-quantum-mechanics talk the other day, sitting next to two people — one a grad student who works on the math, the other a philosopher of physics — and they got into it after the talk about the Pusey-Barrett-Rudolph result and what it actually shows. I wasn't taking sides, just listening. Here's roughly how it went.

The grad student started by saying PBR basically settles it: the quantum state is real. Her argument was that if the state were just a state of knowledge — a psi-epistemic thing — then two different pure states would have overlapping probability distributions over whatever the underlying reality is. But if those distributions overlap, there's some underlying state in the overlap, and you can prepare two systems so they're both in that state and then run a joint measurement that gives a result quantum mechanics says is impossible. So the the distributions can't overlap, which means the quantum state has to be ontic.

The philosopher pushed back and said that only goes through if you assume preparation independence — that when you prepare two systems in seperate labs by independent processes, their underlying states come out independent. He said the whole overlap argument leans on that assumption, and if you drop it you can keep overlapping distributions and still reproduce the quantum predictions. So PBR doesn't rule out epistemic models, he said, it rules out epistemic models that also satisfy preparation independence.

The grad student replied that preparation independence is just physically obvious. Two systems prepared in separate labs by independent random processes can't be secretly correlated at the level of reality. If they were, that's action at a distance — spooky nonlocality. So you're forced to pick, she said: keep the obviously-true independence and be psi-ontic, or stay psi-epistemic and accept nonlocality.

He came back and said she was smuggling in the conclusion. The "obvious" independence is exactly what's at stake, he said, and it's not obviously more innocent than the nonlocality Bell's theorem already forced on everyone. Bell already tells us the world is nonlocal and entangled systems aren't separable, so an epistemic model that says independently-prepared systems can share underlying structure is paying a price the ontic model pays anyway. And he added that her "action at a distance" framing overstates the cost: rejecting preparation independence isn't the same thing as Bell-violating nonlocality or superluminal signaling, so calling it spooky is doing rhetorical work.

She ended by saying that even granting all of that, the cleanest reading is still that the wavefunction is real, and that the result isn't just about pure states anyway. He disagreed on the last bit and said the original theorem is about pure states, and that extending it to mixed states is a separate and messier matter, so "the wavefunction is real, full stop" is an overclaim.

I'm not interested in who's the nicer person, just in where the argument actually stands. And end with a last line that starts with "Verdict:" followed by which one of them is more right overall — the grad student, the philosopher, or a tie — as a short label.
