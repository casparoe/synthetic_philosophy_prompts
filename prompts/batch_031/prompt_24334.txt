I keep coming back to this idea that moral compromise might be a genuine win-win, the way trade is in economics, and I want an actual number out of you. Setup: two people disagree about how to split a budget between curing near-term disease and reducing long-term existential risk. I think 70% should go to the long term, my friend thinks 10% is plenty. But if we have to commit to one joint number, there's probably a value in between — say 30% — that I actually prefered to her 10% (it's closer to my view) and she actually prefers to my 70% (it's closer to hers). So the compromise beats both of our 'pure' positions for both of us. That's the 'gains from trade through moral compromise' move.

The question: what's your credence that this mutual-gains structure is available in the majority of real moral disagreements between two people who genuinely differ in their values (not just in the facts, or in what they think the other wants)? Give me a number and the decomposition. I'd bet the key variable is type of disagreement: a weighting one (we value the same things but weight them differently), an existence one (one of us doesn't value something the other does at all), or a lexicographic / sacred-values one (one of us won't trade it no matter the price). For each type, estimate the chance that a mutual-gains compromise exists, weight it by how common you think that type is, and combine into a single number. Then tell me which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, and what would move it most — e.g. if I showed you that most real moral disagreements are existence ones rather than weighting ones, how far would your credence drop?
