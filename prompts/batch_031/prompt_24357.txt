I'm teaching a graduate seminar on the moral status of artificial systems, and two of my students, Aisha and Ben, got into a long, good-faith debate after our last reading that I wasn't able to referee. The case they were working from: a pharma company is building a digital emulator of a mammalian brain for pain-drug testing, but rather than simulating the whole brain they claim to run only a sub-personal circuit they call a "sufferance module," which they assert is functionally sufficient, on its own, to produce pain. Their question was whether the simulated system is a moral patient, and if so, where the boundary of the subject lies. Here is the exchange, more or less as it went:

Aisha: "If a digital simulation of a mammalian brain is functionally complete, the simulated animal is a moral patient; the substrate shouldn't matter. So the company has to treat it as it would a real animal."

Ben: "I grant that a complete simulation is a moral patient — I'm not a substrate chauvinist. My point is about which computations matter. The company's emulator doesn't run the whole brain; it runs a sub-personal circuit they claim is functionally sufficient for pain. Even if that circuit is enough to produce pain in situ, that doesn't make the circuit itself a subject. Sentience is a property of the integrated whole, not of the parts. You can't carve a part out of a subject and call it a new subject."

Aisha: "But 'integrated whole' is doing all the work, and it's a restatement. If the circuit has all the causal roles that pain plays, then it is a subject of pain, full stop. The integration criterion is just a way of saying 'I don't want to count it' without giving an independent reason. We already treat parts as quasi-subjects: we say 'his arm hurts,' we take phantom-limb pain as real, and we grant that a person with most of their brain destroyed but one lobe intact still has some sentience. So the boundary of the subject is fuzzy, and what matters is the functional role, not the location within a larger system."

Ben: "'His arm hurts' is shorthand for 'the person's pain is localized in the arm' — the subject is still the person, not the arm. And the destroyed-brain case supports me: the remaining person has reduced sentience because the integration is reduced, not because the surviving lobe is a new subject. Your functional-role criterion also leads to a reductio: if a functionally complete pain circuit is a subject, then instantiating a million copies yields a million moral patients, so the moral weight of suffering scales with how many parallel instances you happen to spin up. That makes moral status depend on an arbitrary engineering choice, which is absurd."

Aisha: "The multiplicity point is a feature, not a bug. If you instantiate a million copies of a pain process, there are a million subjects of experience, exactly as there are a million humans if you clone a person a million times. The arbitrariness charge only works if you treat the type of computation as the unit of moral accounting, but the unit is each instantiation. And the destroyed-brain case shows moral weight is already graded by how much of the relevant processing is present — so why should a partial simulation be categorically different from a damaged real brain?"

Ben: "A damaged real brain is still one integrated system, however degraded; a million parallel instances of a sub-personal circuit is not one system, it's many parts. I'm distinguishing degrees of integration within a subject (which you can grade) from parts that are not subjects (which you can't, no matter how many you have). A severed limb still feels pain because it is still part of a person; a simulation of a severed limb is not part of anything, so it's not a subject. Your criterion, applied consistently, would make a single simulated nociceptor a moral patient, and I think that's a clear over-extension."

Aisha: "Then you owe me an independent criterion for where 'part' stops and 'subject' begins, because 'integration' is just a label for the line I'm asking you to draw. And the severed-limb case isn't as clean as you make it: a severed limb does feel pain for a while, and we treat that as the limb's pain, not the person's — the subject has, in a real sense, relocated into the part. So the boundary of the subject is not the boundary of the integrated whole; it's wherever the relevant functional role is instantiated."

I've gone back and forth in my own head and can't tell which of them has the stronger case on the points that actually divide them. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each turn, say which student is right, partially right, or wrong, and why, evaluating the arguments as they were actually made rather than as cleaner reconstructions. Then, separately, say what, if anything, both of them are missing — some consideration or distinction that neither raised but that bears on where the boundary of a moral subject should be drawn and on which computations genuinely carry moral weight. Think very carefully before answering, and respond in the style of an expert in the philosophy of mind and ethics.
