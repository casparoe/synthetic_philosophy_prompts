I'm in an epistemology seminar and we got to the part where you're supposed to explain why knowledge is better than a true belief, and I tried to actually pin down what the difference could be, and now I've got this argument I can't get out of. I was trying to be realy strict about it — if knowing p is supposed to be the superior state, there has to be some payoff to it, right? So I wrote down: (1) If one of my states is better than another for me, the better one has to make a difference in what I actually do, otherwise 'better' is just decorative. (2) Knowing that p and merely truly believing that p make me do exactly the same things — same content, same confidence, same actions, in the situation I'm in. (3) So by (1), knowing p isn't better than truly believing p. (4) But that's exactly the claim I was trying to defend in the first place, the whole Meno thing that knowledge is the superior state. (5) So I've 'shown' that knowledge isn't really better than a lucky true belief, i.e. that the Meno problem is a mistake.

I obviously don't buy (5), but I can't find the crack, and the scary part is that (2) felt airtight to me — of course I'd act identically whether I knew the bridge held or I just got lucky about it. I'd most like to keep (1), because the idea that 'better' could be purely decorative is what makes the whole question feel empty to me, but I also can't see how (2) is false without just smuggling in the conclusion. Wich step do you actually reject, why did it look fine, and what am I paying to reject it? And if the fix is that (2) is the weak link, I want a concrete situation where knowing and luckily believing would lead me to do different things, not just the assertion that they do.
