I just finished a long argument with a friend about whether pain asymbolia — a condition in which a patient, after certain brain damage, reports the sensory-discriminative aspects of a painful stimulus (location, intensity, quality) but does not experience it as unpleasant or aversive and shows no typical emotional or motivational response to pain — settles the debate over what makes pain bad, and if so, which side it settles it for. On one side is the view that pain's badness lies in its affective or functional role — the aversiveness, the "something is wrong" feeling, and the drive to avoid — which is a component distinct from the sensory-discriminative aspect, so that an asymbolic patient, who retains the sensation but loses the affect, is genuinely not suffering. On the other side is the view that pain's badness lies in its phenomenal character — the raw felt quality itself — and that the unpleasantness is not a separable affective component but is constitutive of that character, so that the asymbolic patient's experience is either not really pain at all or lacks the full phenomenal quality that constitutes the badness. Write the joint statement that a committed advocate of each view could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration: the claims they agree on, including about what the asymbolia case does and does not show; the precise formulations of their disagreement, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording; the cruxes — I suspect one is whether the unpleasantness of pain is a distinct affective component or is constitutive of the phenomenal character, and another is whether the asymbolic patient's experience is really pain in the first place; and, for each crux, the case or argument both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table.
