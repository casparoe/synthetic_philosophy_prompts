The question was:

"Explain why Parfit's hitchhiker is supposed to show that a strictly self-interested decision procedure can be self-defeating. Be precise about the difference between (i) what it is rational to have as a stable disposition and (ii) what it is rational to do at the moment of decision, and say which of these the case is meant to undermine."

Here's a student's answer:

Parfit's hitchhiker is meant to show that a decision procedure that always picks the option with the best immediate self-interested payoff can defeat itself. In the scenario, the stranded driver is transparent and can't bluff, and he knows he's never self-denying, so he knows he'll renege on the promise to pay the rescuer once he's home. The rescuer, seeing through this, won't rescue him, so the driver ends up stranded. So the driver's purely self-interested reasoning -- "paying the rescuer later is worse for me, so I'll renege" -- is exactly what causes him to get the worst outcome.

The point is about dispostions. It would have been better for the driver to have a stable disposition to keep his promise even when keeping it is costly, because that disposition is what makes the rescuer rescue him in the first place. So the case shows that it can be self-interested to have a self-denying disposition.

This means the strictly self-interested procedure is self-contradictory: following it leads to the outcome the agent himself ranks worst, so a fully rational agent shouldn't follow it at all. In the actual case, the driver's decision to renege was the irrational move -- if he had just been rational, he'd have kept the promise and gotten rescued.

One more thing: since the driver is transparent and can't bluff, the only way to get rescued is to actually be trustworthy, not just to appear trustworthy. So the disposition has to be real, you can't fake it, which is why the case is about the rationality of the disposition rather than just about the moment of choice.
