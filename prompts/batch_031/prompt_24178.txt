I've been chewing on this grue thing (Goodman's, I think — I picked it up off a blog post and I'm going mostly from memory) and I built an argument that now looks to me like it proves too much, and I can't find the crack. Here's the whole thing:

(1) A hypothesis is confirmed by the evidence to exactly the degree it fits the evidence, where "fits" just means every observation so far comes out true under it.
(2) I've looked at a bunch of emralds and they've all been green.
(3) So "all emeralds are green" fits the evidence.
(4) Now define "grue" as "green if observed before noon tomorrow, blue otherwise." Every emerald I've seen so far was seen before noon tomorrow and was green, so each one is grue as well.
(5) So "all emeralds are grue" fits the evidence just as well.
(6) So by (1), the evidence confirms "all emeralds are grue" just as much as "all emeralds are green."
(7) But if I look at a fresh emerald after noon tomorrow, "grue" says it'll be blue and "green" says it'll be green.
(8) So the same body of evidence confirms both "the next emerald is green" and "the next emerald is blue."
(9) The evidence confirms a contradiction.
(10) And evidence that confirms a contradiction confirms nothing.
(11) So inductive evidence confirms nothing — I have no more reason to expect the next emerald to be green than to expect it to be blue.

I obviously cant live with (11), because I do have a reason to expect green. But I also don't want to just wave (1) or (6) away, because that feels like special pleading. So which step do you actually reject, and what does it cost me to reject it? And is the whole thing just a verbal trick that dissolves on inspection, or is there something real lurking in it that I'm not seeing?
