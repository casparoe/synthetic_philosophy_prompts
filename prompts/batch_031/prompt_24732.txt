I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy, and I've spent the last two years working on population ethics at infinite population sizes. Next month I'm presenting a chapter of my dissertation at a workshop on population ethics and infinity, and I want to be ready for the question period.

My thesis, stated precisely: the repugnant conclusion, as usually stated, is a finite artifact. At infinite population sizes, the total-welfare comparison that generates the repugnant conclusion is undefined or indeterminate (the relevant infinite sums do not converge, or are indeterminate), and the dominance principle — which is the engine of the repugnant conclusion — is not well-defined at infinity, because "making a finite improvement without making anyone worse off" is not the right operation in a space of worlds that is disconnected: some worlds cannot be reached from any other world by a finite sequence of finite changes. So the repugnant conclusion is not a genuine problem at infinity. The real problem, I argue, is the problem of these "impossible" or "unreachable" worlds, which shows that the standard axioms of population ethics (dominance, the goodness of finite improvements, the addition principle) are inconsistent or incoherent when extended to the infinite.
