I'm in a philosophy of biology seminar this term, and we're on the unit about biological function — specifically the dispute between etiological (sometimes called "historical") accounts and causal-role accounts of what it means for a trait to have a function. I came across a summary of this debate in a popular science book I was reading for an unrelated reason, and I'd like a careful, point-by-point audit of it. I can feel that some of it is right and some of it is doing something I can't quite put my finger on, and I don't want to walk into Thursday's seminar repeating the book's framing as if the matter were settled. Here is the passage, as close to verbatim as I can reconstruct it:

"The question of what it means for a body part to 'have a function' has, for the most part, been settled in favor of the causal-role view. On this view, a trait's function is simply the causal role it currently plays in keeping the organism alive and reproducing: the heart's function is to pump blood because that is what the heart is doing in the organism's economy right now, and no further metaphysics is required. This view has a clear edge over the older etiological view, which locates a trait's function in what it was selected for. The etiological view has to do more work with the awkward cases. Consider vestigial structures like the human coccyx: the etiological view says it still has the function it was selected for in our tailed ancestors — supporting a tail — even though it no longer does that, while the causal-role view just says its function is whatever it happens to be doing now, which may be nothing. And consider exapted traits, like feathers, which first evolved for insulation and were later co-opted for flight: the etiological view has to say something rather complicated about which of the two purposes is the feather's 'real' function, whereas the causal-role view simply notes what the feather is doing today. The causal-role view also has the advantage, in the end, of being the only genuinely naturalistic option. The etiological view, by insisting that a trait's function is what it was 'for,' quietly reintroduces exactly the kind of design intention that Darwinian biology was supposed to expel from the science."

My hunch is that the book is overselling the causal-role side and being a little unfair to the etiological side, but I don't know enough to say precisely where it goes wrong. Could you go through the passage and sort out, point by point, what a working philosopher of biology would accept as accurate, what is a fair simplification, what is a genuine misrepresentation, and what a defender of the etiological account in particular would push back on? I'm especially interested in two things. First, whether the book's claim that the etiological view is not really naturalistic — that it "secretly reintroduces design intention" — is a fair characterization or a confusion of "selected for" (a causal-historical fact about natural selection) with "designed for" (an intentional fact). Second, whether the book's treatment of exapted traits is a real problem for the etiological view or just a case the etiological view handles by allowing a single trait to have more than one function. And please be careful to separate what is actually settled in the field from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this: I don't want the answer to present whichever side wins the argument as the consensus, because I suspect the book's "largely settled" claim is itself one of the things that's wrong.
