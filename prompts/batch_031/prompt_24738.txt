I've been following the longtermism and population-ethics threads for a while now, and a certain one-liner keeps coming up in arguments about whether we owe anything to future generations, usually wielded like a reductio: "what is bad must be bad for someone". I've heard it used in at least two opposite directions — by people who conclude that a policy choice which changes which future people exist cannot be wrong, since the people who end up existing are not made worse off than they otherwise would have been, and by people who insist that person-affecting constraints are the only coherent foundation for ethics. I already accept the standard setup — that small changes alter who is conceived, that the people who exist under the worse option are not the same people who would have existed under the better one, and that each is, say, moderately happy — so please skip re-explaining the paradox and go straight to the disambiguation. Could you lay out the distinct precise principles this one-liner could be expressing — for instance, whether it is a claim about the meaning of "harm" rather than a claim about what is wrong, whether the relevant comparison is to a counterfactual world or to an absolute threshold, whose counterfactual it is, and what happens to the comparison when the choice changes which people exist at all — and say for each reading whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible principle, or plainly false, whether the identity-changing cases actually refute it, and which reading actually supports the move of concluding that an identity-changing policy choice is not wrong merely because the people who exist under it are not worse off than they otherwise would have been.
