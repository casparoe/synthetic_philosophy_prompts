I am a retired physicist — I spent my career in condensed-matter theory — and I have only recently begun to read philosophy, so please forgive any imprecision in my use of the technical vocabulary. I would like you to write a careful, expert-level essay on a puzzle that sits at the intersection of the philosophy of mind and the philosophy of time, and I will try to set it out as precisely as I can.

The puzzle concerns the color-phi effect in motion perception. If a red dot flashes on the left and, after a short interval — on the order of a tenth of a second — a blue dot flashes on the right, many observers do not see two separate flashes. They see a single object moving from left to right. The striking feature is where the color change appears to occur. It is not seen at the right-hand location, where the blue stimulus actually appeared. Instead, the moving object is seen to be red as it leaves the left, to switch to blue somewhere in the middle of its path, and then to continue as blue all the way to the right. The color change is, in effect, mislocated: it is experienced at a place where no blue stimulus ever was. And because the perceived color change is placed at a location that no physical stimulus occupied, it also seems to be misdated — it does not line up with the moment at which the actual blue stimulus was presented.

What I find most troubling about this is that it puts pressure on the naive assumption that there is a single, privileged "time of experience": a "now" at which the experience simply happens, in some central theater of the mind. The experienced color change seems to be dated to a time and a place that correspond to no physical event at all.

Here is the contrast I have been chewing on, and I would like you to take it seriously as the organizing framework of the essay. I have been thinking about two different ways a system could misrepresent or rewrite an experience, and I have borrowed the labels from political history:

- An "Orwellian" revision is one in which there is a fixed, true original — a real past, a genuine experience — and what changes is the record of it. The original is overwritten by a false but stable account, and the subject is made to accept the false account in place of the true one. There is a genuine state of affairs that has been rewritten.

- A "Stalinesque" revision is one in which there is no fixed original at all. The "facts" are not a rewrite of anything; they are manufactured in the moment to fit whatever narrative is currently required. There is no underlying true state of affairs that has been overwritten — the "fact" is a performance, generated on demand.

My working hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than simply endorse, is that the color-phi effect, and the timing-of-experience problem it raises, is best understood as a Stalinesque rather than an Orwellian case: that is, that the brain does not possess a true, fixed experience — a genuine "time of experience" — that it then rewrites, but that the "time of experience" is itself a fabrication, with no true original behind it.
