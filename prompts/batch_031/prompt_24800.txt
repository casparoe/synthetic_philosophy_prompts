I teach an upper-level undergraduate ethics seminar — about 16 students, one 90-minute session. I want an in-class activity that makes them feel the force of the particularist challenge to general moral principles, and I want that force to come from what happens to them during the activity rather than from my explanation. The specific idea I want them to run into is this: the moral significance of a feature of a case is not fixed. The very same feature can count as a pro tanto reason in favor of an act in one situation, a reason against it in another, and be morally neutral in a third. I do not want the words "particularism," "generalism," or any philosopher's name to surface until the debrief; by the end of the activity they should have discovered the problem inside their own rulebook before I name it.

Please design a structured game with the following parts:

1. A commitment phase in which the group, together, assigns a fixed moral valence to each of five candidate features — keeping a promise, telling the truth, following the posted rule, helping a friend, and standing by your own group — tagging each as a reason for, a reason against, or neutral, and writing the results down as a shared, visible "rulebook."
2. A sequence of staged cases, dealt one at a time in an order you (or a co-facilitator) control, in which at least one rulebook feature must do the decisive work. Engineer the cases so that a feature the group tagged "reason for" (or "reason against") demands the opposite verdict in the case, and include at least one case in which the right move is to treat a feature as neutral. The students should not be able to tell in advance which cases will flip a feature.
3. A visible-cost rule: every time the group patches the rulebook to handle a counter-case, it must add a written proviso or exception, appended where everyone can watch the rulebook grow.
4. A payoff scheme that needs no real money — points for a correct verdict on each case, or a group "credibility" score that drops a fixed amount each time the rulebook as written gives the wrong answer.
5. A minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes, with a buffer for the rulebook-keeping.
6. A debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the theses at stake — the valence-flips as the holism claim, the ballooning provisos as the claim that there are no exceptionless general principles, and the case-by-case judgment as the claim that moral reasoning is creative rather than rule-applying — and then states the strongest generalist reply: that defeasible, ceteris-paribus principles are still genuine principles, so a rulebook that keeps needing provisos is not a refutation of generalism but perhaps just a portrait of a complex domain.
7. An honest note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — for instance, students pre-empting the flips by writing in exceptions during the commitment phase, or one dominant student monopolizing the rulebook — and how to handle each.
8. A frank note on which feature of the dispute the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply in the debrief: the activity can make the particularist view feel plausible, but it cannot itself decide whether the proliferation of exceptions is a refutation of generalism or merely evidence that the moral domain is intricate.
