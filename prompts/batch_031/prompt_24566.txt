I retired last year after a long career in theoretical ecology, and over the past couple of years I've been reading philosophy of action and decision theory, which is where I ran into Herbert Simon's notion of satisficing. I have no trouble with the basic idea — that agents operating under real cognitive and informational constraints do not maximize but stop once an option crosses some acceptance threshold — but I keep hitting a debate in the literature that I cannot get a handle on, and I would like a diagnosis of it rather than a survey.

As I read it, the dispute is over the status of satisficing as a rival to maximization. One camp, roughly the expected-utility and decision-theory side, treats satisficing as at best a descriptive caveat: it records how people in fact fall short of rational choice, but it is not a genuine theory of what rationality requires, because it never pins down where the threshold lies or how it is set, and so it cannot deliver the kind of determinate predictions that expected utility does. The other camp, the bounded-rationality tradition descending from Simon, treats satisficing as a positive account of rational decision-making: given the agent's real limits, a rational agent ought to search and stop in just the way satisficing describes, and the looseness of the threshold is a virtue, because it tracks the adaptive, context-sensitive way real agents actually calibrate their standards.

In my own field I live with this tension every day. We build optimality models of foraging and reproduction that are useful only "as if" the animal were solving a clean optimization problem, and everyone knows the animal is not doing that. So I have a working hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis: I suspect the impasse is largely about what the word "rational" is doing. One side means "normatively ideal," in which case satisficing cannot compete, and the other means "adaptively adequate given the constraints," in which case unconstrained maximization is the straw man. If so, the two camps are answering different questions under a single name, and "Is satisficing a real theory?" is ill-posed because it inherits the ambiguity.

Could you diagnose what kind of disagreement this really is — verbal, substantive, empirical, or a hidden question about what a decision theory is for — using what each side actually argues rather than the labels they attach to themselves, and then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a distinction that separates the two senses of "rational," a formal result, an empirical test, or a reformulation of the question? And if you believe no progress is to be expected, I would like you to say why.
