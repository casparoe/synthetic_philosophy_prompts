I'm preparing a set of teaching materials on the normative force of promises, and I keep coming back to a distinction I find harder to pin down than it should be: whether a promise obliges the promisor to *perform the promised act* or only to *make the promisee whole for whatever the promisee lost by relying on the promise*. I'd like you to construct a thought experiment, to the specifications below, that isolates exactly this distinction.

Here is what I need. Build a single, realistic scenario — an everyday personal or workplace situation, not a trolley and not science fiction, and no longer than one paragraph — in which a promisor has made a promise that the promisee accepts, the promisee then relies on the promise by forgoing a cheaper alternative (so the reliance loss is a definite, quantifiable sum), and after the reliance an unforeseen event for which the promisor is not at fault makes performing the promised act possible but more costly for the promisor than the promisee's reliance loss. The promised act must remain something the promisor *could* still do — costly, not impossible — and the promisee must genuinely prefer the promisor's performing the act to simply taking the cheaper alternative, so that the choice between performance and compensation is live for the promisee. Do not stipulate the verdicts inside the scenario; the scenario should be the bare facts, and the verdicts should fall out of applying the theories to those facts.

After the scenario, walk through what each of four standard accounts of why promises bind says the promisor owes, and why: the reliance-based account, the contract-based account, the will- or autonomy-based account, and the reason-based account (the view that a promise generates a new reason for the promisor to perform). I expect the reliance-based and reason-based accounts to come out on one side (no duty to perform, at most a duty to compensate) and the contract-based and will-based accounts to come out on the other (a duty to perform); I want you to say which structural feature of the case is doing the work in producing that split, what happens to the case if you remove that feature (does it collapse into a case where all four accounts agree?), and how the most plausible defender of the account that comes out worst would try to attack the case. Think very carefully before answering, and respond in the style of an expert who has actually worked through the literature. If the specifications as I've posed them are jointly unsatisfiable, or if the request rests on a confused premise about what these theories say, say so plainly and tell me where the problem is rather than forcing a scenario that can't do the job.
