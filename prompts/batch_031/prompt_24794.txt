I am a novelist constructing a scene in which a philosophy professor is forced to defend Kierkegaard’s treatment of Abraham and the teleological suspension of the ethical against the charge that it is only a paradoxical literary gesture. I want the professor’s position to be as defensible as it can be, but I do not want it to be straw-manned. What is your credence that there exists a coherent normative framework in which a particular individual can be subject to an absolute obligation that suspends the universal ethical without the framework collapsing into incoherence, arbitrary exception-making, or a hidden re-description of ordinary moral justification? Please give a number from 0 to 1 and decompose it into the main sub-questions: whether the ethical can be understood as a domain that admits of absolute exception; whether a duty to God can be conceptually distinct from a duty to human beings rather than merely a stronger instance of the same moral system; whether the Abraham story can be read as presenting a possible normative situation rather than only a symbolic test of faith; whether the suspension is a logical possibility, a practical possibility, or both; and whatever other factors you think matter. Assign a rough probability to each sub-question, explain how they combine into the final number, and identify the sub-question to which your estimate is most sensitive. Also say what argument, if pressed in the scene, would most plausibly lower your credence, and what argument would most plausibly raise it.
