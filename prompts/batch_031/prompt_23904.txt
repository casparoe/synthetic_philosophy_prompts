A working group on the philosophy of mind is running an adversarial collaboration between two camps: one defends an Orwellian account of perceptual timing—experiences are formed and then later overwritten, so that the report one gives is not the experience one had—and the other defends a Stalinesque account—what was registered at the time was already the revised version, so the later report is faithful to what was actually experienced. The anchor case for the collaboration is the color phi phenomenon, in which two dots, one red and one green, presented in rapid alternation at different locations are seen as a single colored dot moving smoothly between the locations, with no moment of seeing a stationary dot in the middle; the two camps also argue about what this case can and cannot establish, about the problem of dating experiences, and about whether the dispute is substantive or merely verbal.

Think very carefully before answering, and respond in the style of a senior philosopher of mind who has been asked to arbitrate the drafting. Draft the joint statement these two camps could both sign after a good-faith collaboration. It must contain: (1) a numbered section of agreed claims, including shared claims about how the color phi stimulus works, what the standard report is, and what either account must explain; (2) the precise statement of their disagreement, with each side's position phrased so that its own side would accept the wording; (3) the cruxes—the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side, for example whether there is a fact of the matter about the time-ordering of experiences that is independent of report, and whether a later overwrite is a change in the mind or a change in the record of the mind; and (4) for each crux, a case, argument, or probe both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. If you conclude that part of the dispute is verbal, say which part and why. Format it as the numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table. The final document must be at most 900 words.
