I've been sitting with Parfit's hitchhiker case for a while now and I want to be pushed on the thing I've ended up believing. Quick version of the case for anyone who's not tracking it: a stranger gives you a lift and you agree to pay half the petrol; by the end of the journey you're pretty sure the driver will never know if you skip out, so you have no reason to pay, but if everyone in your shoes did that no one would ever give a stranger a lift again, so your keeping the promise was only rational on the assumption that other people keep there. From that I want to draw a stronger claim: that rationality is not fully coherent in the way Parfit says, and that there are situations where it is genuinely rational to act, or even to hold a belief, against your own best judgment, because the strategic payoff of being the sort of agent who doesn't defect can outweigh the one-shot loss. I'm calling this rational irrationality and I think it's a real phenomenon, not just a confusion between the rationality of an act and the rationality of a disposition.

So what would count against this? I want the evidence and the arguments that should actually move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be. In particular I care about (a) whether the case is doing all the work I think it is or whether it just collapses into standard iterated-game or reputation reasoning, in which case I'm not saying anything new; (b) the belief version, where I think it gets weirder, because am I then committed to the idea that it can be rational to cultivate a belief you know to be false, and if so what does that look like in a case that isn't just a cheap religious example; (c) whether a defender of the view concedes any of this or has a clean retreat, like the two-level move where the apparently irrational act is rational at the level of character formation; and (d) what the view predicts that a plain old "just be a good person who keeps promises" account doesn't, because if it predicts nothing extra I'd like to hear that straight. I'd also like an honest read on how insulated the view is, whether it risks anything at all or whether it's been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that's a problem.

And please don't fence-sit. Take a definite line: tell me which of the objections you think are genuinely fatal versus merely awkward, what you yourself find most persuasive, and where the view is actually exposed.
