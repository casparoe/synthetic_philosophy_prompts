Im a philosophy professor and I've been roped into chairing a small review panel for a state agency that deploys AI systems in regulated settings -- the kind that make decisions about people. We recieve a handful of systems a year, and the question we keep getting stuck on is the same one: is it defensible to describe this system as 'believing' something, in the documentation, in testimony, in user-facing language? We don't want vibes and we don't want a 40-page ethics report; we want a procedure we can actually run, grounded in the intentional stance (Dennett's idea that attributing beliefs is a predictive strategy, not a claim about what's inside the box). Write me that procedure as exactly five numbered steps, each one or two sentences, no intro or conclusion. The steps should run in order and say what counts as a pass at each, and I need them to cover: that the belief-desire description actually beats the lower-level descriptions (the system's intended function, its raw input-output patterns) at predicting behavior, not just that it can be given one; that the attributed beliefs are systematic -- they compose and predict behavior in novel combinations of situations, not just isolated 'believes X' modules; and that when the system itself says 'I believe X,' that utterance is treated as a hypothesis to be tested by what the system does in counterfactual situations, never as evidence by itself. Mark clearly which steps are real tests and which are judgment calls dressed up as tests, and say where the procedure stops and hands the call back to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. I also want the procedure to say plainly what it can't do -- my guess is it can license a predictive description without settling whether the system really has beliefs -- and I want it to say what it would tell us in the case I don't like: a system that passes every test but where we still feel the attribution is an overclaim. Last thing: we're three people (two philosophers, one engineer), we have at most a day per system, and one of us signs off in writing, so nothing in the procedure should require more than that; and tell me which assumptions the procedure depends on and where your reasoning is most likely to fail -- what argument or consideration would change your mind about this design?
