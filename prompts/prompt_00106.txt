I've been reading about evidential cooperation in large worlds (ECL) for a seminar on decision theory under uncertainty, and I keep getting stuck on whether the whole framework actually delivers what its proponents claim. The basic idea seems to be: if the universe (or multiverse) is large enough that many causally disconnected agents face structurally similar decision problems, then evidential decision theory recommends that agents cooperate with each other's values even absent any causal interaction, because updating on "what kind of agent I am" gives evidence about what sufficiently similar agents elsewhere will do. Proponents like Oesterheld argue this generates real reasons for altruistic compromise across value systems; critics worry the whole thing rests on question-begging reference classes, illicit priors about the distribution of agents in a large or infinite universe, and a conflation of evidential correlation with anything action-guiding once you take causal decision theory seriously.

Write an essay that lays out the case for ECL as a genuine source of practical reasons, then subjects it to the strongest objections you can muster — particularly worries about (a) whether "similarity" between agents can be made precise enough to ground correlation without smuggling in the conclusion, (b) whether the relevant probability distributions over agents in a large/infinite world are even well-defined, and (c) whether a committed causal decision theorist has any reason to care about the evidential correlations ECL relies on. Don't just present both sides and shrug — reach a verdict on whether ECL survives these objections as stated, or whether it needs to be substantially weakened (e.g., restricted to a narrower class of cases) to be defensible, and defend that verdict.
