So I spent about 35 years as a physicist before retiring, and now I've fallen down the philosophy rabbit hole in my old age (my wife says its worse than when I got into birdwatching). Lately I've been reading Leibniz's Monadology alongside some effective altruism / rationalist blog posts about something called "evidential cooperation in large worlds" (ECL) -- the idea that if the universe or multiverse is vast enough, causally disconnected agents who can't ever interact might still benefit from acting as if they'd cooperate with distant others who reason similarly, purely on evidential/decision-theoretic grounds rather than causal ones. It struck me how weirdly close this is to Leibniz's monads, which also don't causally interact with each other but are nonetheless coordinated (via pre-established harmony, in his telling, rather than acausal correlation).

Given that Leibniz clearly had the conceptual machinery to think about non-interacting agents whose behavior is nonetheless correlated across a vast plurality of possible/actual worlds, what do you think he would have made of ECL if you explained the modern argument to him in his own terms? Would he have endorsed the idea that rational agents "cooperate" acausally across a large or infinite universe, or would his commitment to a divine guarantor of harmony have made him reject the whole framework as unnecessary (since God already ensures compossibility and coordination, so why would individual monads need to reason their way into cooperation)? I'd love a sense of how his metaphysics of possible worlds and compossibility would mesh with, or clash with, the game-theoretic/decision-theoretic assumptions underlying ECL.
