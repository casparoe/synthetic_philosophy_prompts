Please describe the most important implications of Wittgenstein's private language argument for how we should interpret the output of brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) that decode attempted or 'inner' speech from neural activity in patients with complete locked-in syndrome—a state of total paralysis, including the eye muscles, in which the patient has no voluntary channel left for confirming or correcting what the device produces. Central to Wittgenstein's argument is the claim that a sign whose correct use cannot be checked against anything but what seems right to the user at the time cannot sustain a real distinction between being right and merely seeming right. What follows for how much epistemic trust we should place in BCI-decoded utterances in such cases, given that the only anchor for the meaning of a patient's words is a decoder calibrated on their past, public use of language, with no ongoing way to confirm that it is still tracking that usage correctly?
