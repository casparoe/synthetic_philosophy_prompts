A few months ago I fell down a rabbit hole trying to understand why serious gamblers and quantitative traders keep invoking something called the "Kelly criterion" instead of simply taking any bet with positive expected value, which is what I'd always assumed a rational person should do. I have no formal background in math or economics, so rather than take anyone's word for it, I worked through the standard illustration myself with a spreadsheet: imagine a fair coin flipped repeatedly on the same pot of money, where heads adds 50% to your current wealth and tails takes away 40%. The arithmetic average of those two outcomes is a genuine 5% gain per flip, and yet when I simulated someone staking their whole bankroll on this coin, flip after flip, they were almost always wiped out within a few dozen rounds, because the geometric average of the two multipliers comes out below one. I mentioned this to a friend who trades for a living, expecting him to find it as strange as I did, but he just shrugged and said that's simply what the Kelly criterion is for. That answer didn't satisfy me, because it seemed to skip past the genuinely strange part: that "always take the better-than-fair bet" can be almost certain ruin for the one person actually living through the sequence of flips, even though it looks unambiguously correct if you instead average across a large crowd of people who each play the game just once. I've since gathered that this split — between what some people now call arithmetic versus geometric rationality, or between averaging over an ensemble of possible people and averaging over one person's own timeline — is treated as a genuine, still-contested fault line in thinking about rational choice, tied to the idea that wealth built up through repeated multiplicative bets is not "ergodic."

I'd like you to write me a short story that makes this dilemma felt rather than explained. Two characters who trust each other and are each offered the same repeated, favorable-on-average bet would probably work well: one who keeps sizing their stake by the textbook expected-value calculation, and one who insists on holding back and staking only some fraction of what they have each round, the way Kelly-style position-sizing would recommend, even though it looks needlessly timid by comparison round after round. I don't want a story that pauses to explain the math to the reader; I would rather the diverging fortunes of the two characters, and the growing unease of whichever one is watching the other seemingly "irrationally" hold back, do that work instead. It needn't be set among gamblers or traders at all — I could just as easily picture two subsistence farmers deciding how much seed grain to risk on a chancy but promising new crop every season — so long as the bet genuinely compounds and repeats, and the ending doesn't resolve too neatly into a stated moral.
