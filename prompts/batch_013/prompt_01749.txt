ok so I had a whole argument with my friend last night about Hobbes and his thing with geometry — the guy genuinely believed he'd squared the circle and spent decades fighting John Wallis about it, never backed down once. what gets me is his actual argument for why geometry is the one totally certain, demonstrable science: because WE draw the lines and figures ourselves, so unlike natural bodies (which we didn't make, so we can only guess at their causes), we know the causes of geometric objects directly, since we generated them ourselves by motion/construction. he even runs the exact same logic to say civil philosophy is just as certain, since we make the commonwealth too.

my friend thinks this argument just holds up fine on its own terms. I think it secretly depends on there being one true geometry (Euclid's) that obviously matches how bodies actually move through space, and that it would've been in real trouble the moment non-Euclidean geometry showed up, since now theres more than one internally consistent "geometry" and its not obvious which one, if any, is the thing we're actually constructing.

so genuinely curious what you think: given what you know about Hobbes's actual philosophy of geometry (the maker's-knowledge argument, the materialism, the Wallis feud) — what would he have said if confronted with non-Euclidean geometry? does his certainty argument survive, or was it always quietly riding on Euclid being the only option?
