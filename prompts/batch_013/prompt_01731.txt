I'm a retired physicist - spent about thirty-five years doing optics work before I finally cleared out my office - and since retiring I've been teaching myself philosophy of science, nights and weekends, no formal background in it so bear with me. A friend from my old department pointed me towards Poincare's "Science and Hypothesis," and I got stuck on a passage from the chapter called "The Theories of Modern Physics." Its from the old 1905 English translation so the prose is a bit creaky, but I'd rather give you the whole passage than paraphrase it and loose whatever it is he's actually saying. Here it is:

"The ephemeral nature of scientific theories takes by surprise the man of the world. Their brief period of prosperity ended, he sees them abandoned one after another; he sees ruins piled upon ruins; he predicts that the theories in fashion to-day will in a short time succumb in their turn, and he concludes that they are absolutely in vain. This is what he calls the bankruptcy of science.

His scepticism is superficial; he does not take into account the object of scientific theories and the part they play, or he would understand that the ruins may be still good for something. No theory seemed established on firmer ground than Fresnel's, which attributed light to the movements of the ether. Then if Maxwell's theory is to-day preferred, does that mean that Fresnel's work was in vain? No; for Fresnel's object was not to know whether there really is an ether, if it is or is not formed of atoms, if these atoms really move in this way or that; his object was to predict optical phenomena. This Fresnel's theory enables us to do to-day as well as it did before Maxwell's time. The differential equations are always true, they may be always integrated by the same methods, and the results of this integration still preserve their value. It cannot be said that this is reducing physical theories to simple practical recipes; these equations express relations, and if the equations remain true, it is because the relations preserve their reality. They teach us now, as they did then, that there is such and such a relation between this thing and that; only, the something which we then called motion, we now call electric current. But these are merely names of the images we substituted for the real objects which Nature will hide for ever from our eyes. The true relations between these real objects are the only reality we can attain, and the sole condition is that the same relations shall exist between these objects as between the images we are forced to put in their place."

I understand the physics fine, I've ground through Fresnel's equations more times than I care to remember, and I know roughly how the ether got dropped in favor of Maxwell's field theory. What I can't work out is what Poincare thinks actually carries over from the old theory to the new one, concretely, and whether pointing at "the relations" is a real answer to the worry he opens with, or just a fancier way of saying "well, it was a decent approximation" and leaving it there. It also seems related to something I only found out about recently, the pessimistic meta-induction - that since more or less every past scientific theory eventually got overturned, we've got no good reason to think our current ones (particle physics, whatever) won't go the same way. Is Poincare's passage here basically an early attempt at answering that argument, and if so does it actually work, or does it just push the problem back a level? I skimmed a couple of encyclopedia-type summaries on scientific realism but they mostly stay at the "no miracles argument vs. pessimistic induction" level and don't really dig into what's going on in this specific bit of text, so I'd appreciate a proper close reading if you're willing.
