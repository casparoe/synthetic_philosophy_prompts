In my computer science class, we'd just wrapped up a unit on algorithmic complexity when our teacher — who likes to go on relevant tangents — mentioned that Kurt Gödel actually anticipated the P versus NP question decades early, in a March 1956 letter to John von Neumann. Apparently Gödel asked whether one could always decide if a given formula has a proof of length n using a number of steps that grows only linearly or quadratically in n, rather than by exhaustively searching through every possible proof, and remarked that if such a fast procedure existed, the mental work of mathematicians on yes-or-no questions could essentially be handed over to a machine.

That same week, my school's philosophy club had been discussing an excerpt from Roger Penrose defending what I've since learned is called the Lucas-Penrose argument: the idea, based on Gödel's incompleteness theorems, that the human mind cannot be equivalent to any single formal system or algorithm, since we can always recognize the truth of that system's own Gödel sentence even though the system itself can never prove it. A few of us left club convinced this was basically the same worry as the one in Gödel's letter, just dressed up in different vocabulary, since both seemed to be about whether machines could ever fully replace human mathematicians. Our sponsor told us we were conflating two genuinely separate arguments, but had to run to her next class before she could explain why.

I would like to actually understand this rather than just take her word for it. Could you lay out precisely what each argument needs to assume in order to succeed — for instance, what the Lucas-Penrose argument requires regarding the consistency of human reasoning, versus what Gödel's remark to von Neumann requires regarding brute-force search and the P versus NP question — and then explain why settling one of these issues would have no automatic bearing on the other? What would help most is if you could sketch two concrete scenarios: one in which P turns out to equal NP while the Lucas-Penrose argument against mechanism still succeeds, and another in which P is not equal to NP while Lucas-Penrose fails, so that I can actually see how the two questions come apart rather than just being told that they do.
