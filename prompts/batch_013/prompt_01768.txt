A while back I went down a rabbit hole after stumbling on an old forum thread about researchers who had formalized Kurt Gödel's modal ontological argument for the existence of God in Isabelle/HOL, a proof assistant I've used for a small side project of my own. What struck me wasn't the headline ("computer confirms proof of God") but the fine print: once the axioms were made fully precise, they turned out to entail that literally every true proposition is necessarily true — that is, they collapse the whole distinction between the contingent and the necessary. That felt like a far more interesting result than the nominal conclusion about God. It also sent me down a side path into Gödel's primitive notion of "positive properties" and how his proof relates to Alvin Plantinga's modal ontological argument, built around "maximal excellence" and "maximal greatness," which as far as I can tell is the version that actually shows up in most intro philosophy courses.

I'd like your help comparing the two arguments at a reasonably technical level, pitched at someone who is comfortable with formal/symbolic notation but has never taken a philosophy class. Concretely: (1) identify, in substance, which premise or premises in Gödel's system are doing the work that generates this "everything true is necessarily true" result, and explain why it follows from them; and (2) tell me whether Plantinga's version relies on anything playing an equivalent role, or whether its leaner apparatus — no axiom closing the set of positive properties under entailment, no detour through a notion of "essence" — simply avoids that particular problem, and if so, what it costs him elsewhere (for instance, in how exposed his possibility premise is to parody-style counter-arguments). Both proofs are usually described as leaning on S5-strength modal logic to bridge "possibly necessary" and "necessary," so I'm most interested in exactly where the two proofs part ways despite that shared machinery.

Since I'll want to form my own opinion too: setting aside whether either argument is theologically persuasive, which of the two would you call the more logically resilient piece of formal philosophy, and why?
