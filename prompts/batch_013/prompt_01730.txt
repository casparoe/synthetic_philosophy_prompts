I've been working through Timothy Williamson's Knowledge and Its Limits for a graduate seminar on self-knowledge, and one move in the book's overall architecture keeps nagging at me. Chapter 4 mounts the anti-luminosity argument proper: by considering a subject who, having started out cold, gradually warms up over a sequence of moments, Williamson uses a safety-based margin-for-error principle to argue that no non-trivial mental condition is luminous, where a condition counts as luminous just in case, whenever it obtains, one is thereby in a position to know that it does. Chapter 5 then presents itself as simply drawing out further constraints on knowledge already implicit in that result, redeploying a structurally similar margin-for-error strategy on cases of inexact perceptual knowledge instead (judging a tree's height, say, or the size of a crowd) to conclude that knowledge itself is not luminous, and hence that the KK principle -- if one knows that p, one is in a position to know that one knows it -- fails quite generally, not just for some restricted class of states.

Several people in my cohort treat these two results as essentially a package deal: accept the feeling-cold argument, and you're already committed to the anti-KK conclusion down the line. I suspect the inference from one chapter to the other is less automatic than Williamson's presentation makes it look, but I haven't been able to pin down exactly where it could be resisted -- whether in the kind of margin-for-error principle each case licenses, in what the relevant safety condition actually demands, or in some disanalogy between introspective and perceptual knowledge that the book glosses over. Could you argue against this generalization: that is, argue that granting Williamson's Chapter 4 verdict about phenomenal conditions like feeling cold does not, by itself, commit one to his further Chapter 5 verdict that KK fails for knowledge in general? I'd like the argument to engage with the actual mechanics of the margin-for-error and safety reasoning in each case, rather than resting on the bare assertion that introspection and perceptual estimation are simply "different domains."
