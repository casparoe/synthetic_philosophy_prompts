I spent my whole career as an experimental particle physicist, and now that I'm retired I've fallen into reading philosophy and decision theory for the first time in my life (my wife says it's a midlife crisis, just thirty years late). A few weeks back I re-read about Shubik's old "dollar auction" game — the one where an auctioneer sells a dollar bill to the highest bidder, except the second-highest bidder ALSO has to pay whatever they bid and gets nothing for it, so both sides keep escalating way past a dollar just to avoid being the one who's out money for nothing. I brought it up at a reunion dinner with some old colleagues and it turned into a two hour argument, because it immediately reminded several of us of the whole Superconducting Super Collider saga back in the 90s — Congress kept funding that thing for years past the point most people in the field already suspected it was doomed, before finally cancelling it in '93 after about two billion dollars and a lot of tunnel were already in the ground down in Texas.

What's been nagging at me since is that the dollar auction isn't really just "sunk cost fallacy" wearing a different hat. The whole point of Shubik's setup is that it's a competitive bidding war between two (or more) parties who both stand to loose money for nothing, which seems like a meaningfully different beast than a single lab, agency, or company just deciding on its own, with no rival bidder in the picture, whether to keep pouring money into a project that isn't panning out (more like the classic Staw "Knee-Deep in the Big Muddy" R&D-funding studies from the 70s). Big multinational facilities, where several countries have each sunk money into a shared collider or telescope and nobody wants to be the one that walks away and eats the loss while the others reap whatever benefit remains, seem like they might have that genuinely multiplayer dollar-auction structure, whereas a single national funding agency deciding whether to kill its own pet project seems closer to plain vanilla escalation of commitment.

So I'd genuinely like your take: what do you think are the most important implications of the dollar auction specifically — as opposed to just generic sunk-cost reasoning — for how funding agencies, grant review panels, and multinational scientific collaborations ought to structure their go/no-go decisions on big, expensive, long-running projects? I'm especially curious whether the genuinely competitive, multi-party nature of the dollar auction actually changes the practical advice in any real way, or whether it mostly collapses back into the same handful of standard fixes (pre-committed kill criteria, rotating the decision-makers, independent review, and so on) regardless of how many parties are involved.
