A friend and I were originally arguing about multiple realizability, of all things, and somehow ended up spending most of the evening on a much narrower question that neither of us could resolve: whether Sydney Shoemaker's "subset" account of realization actually earns non-reductive physicalists an escape from Kim's causal exclusion argument, or whether it just relocates the problem. My friend's position was that once you define realization so that a mental property's causal powers are, by construction, a proper subset of its physical realizer's causal powers, the exclusion worry simply evaporates: there is no longer a distinct, competing sufficient cause for the physical cause to screen off, since the mental instantiation isn't causally separate from the physical one in the first place, just narrower. She also insisted that this is basically the same move Stephen Yablo made years earlier with his determinable/determinate proposal, just dressed up in the language of causal powers instead of properties, so that anyone who accepts one should accept the other, and any objection to one is an objection to both.

I wasn't so sure, on either count. On the first point, it seems to me that individuating a mental property partly by way of a strict subset relation to its realizer's powers risks either trivializing realization into type-identity wearing a nonreductive costume, or leaving the "included" powers unable to do any explanatory work that isn't already done by the superset, which just looks like exclusion again under a different name. On the second point, I recall that the determinable/determinate relation is supposed to satisfy some fairly specific formal constraints, e.g., that no two determinates of a single determinable can be co-instantiated, and that no property can be a determinate of more than one determinable, and I don't see why the mental/physical case, especially once multiple realizability is in play, is guaranteed to satisfy those constraints just because it satisfies the more permissive subset-of-powers condition. So it seemed to me the two strategies could come apart even though they're often presented as siblings.

Could you help adjudicate this? I'd like you to (1) spell out as precisely as you can why a subset of causal powers is supposed to be immune to exclusion in a way that a merely correlated or supervenient property isn't, (2) assess whether the subset strategy and the determinable/determinate strategy really are notational variants of one another or whether they face distinct objections, and (3) tell me, with reasons, which of the two you find more promising as a response to Kim, or whether you think both ultimately fail for the same underlying reason.
