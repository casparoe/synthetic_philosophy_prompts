So I spent about forty years as an entomologist before I retired, mostly doing taxonomic work on weevils (turns out there are a genuinely absurd number of weevil species), and since retiring I've gotten weirdly into epistemology. Lately I've been trying to branch out from the usual Western reading list, since apparently classical Indian philosophy has a whole tradition of asking "what actually counts as a legitimate way of coming to know something" - the Nyāya school calls these "pramāṇas" and lands on four of them: perception, inference, comparison, and testimony. Felt oddly relevant to how I used to actually do science, so I got curious.

Anyway, I dug up a 1913 English translation of the Nyāya Sūtras by Satis Chandra Vidyabhusana (found the scan on archive.org - OCR's rough in spots, I cleaned up the obvious typos below) and got completely stuck on aphorism 4, which gives the definition of perception, the first of the four pramanas. Here's the passage, translators notes included:

4. Perception is that knowledge which arises from the contact of a sense with its object and which is determinate, unnameable and non-erratic.

Determinate.—This epithet distinguishes perception from indeterminate knowledge; as for instance, a man looking from a distance cannot ascertain whether there is smoke or dust.

Unnameable.—Signifies that the knowledge of a thing derived through perception has no connection with the name which the thing bears.

Non-erratic.—In summer the sun's rays coming in contact with earthly heat quiver and appear to the eyes of men as water. The knowledge of water derived in this way is not perception. To eliminate such cases the epithet non-erratic has been used.

[This aphorism may also be translated as follows:—Perception is knowledge which arises from the contact of a sense with its object and which is non-erratic, being either indeterminate (nirvikalpaka, as "this is something") or determinate (savikalpaka, as "this is a Brahmana").]

Two things are bugging me here, and my philosophy reading group (mostly other retired scientists - we mostly just confuse each other further, honestly) couldn't sort it out either. First, "determinate" and "unnameable" seem to me like they're pulling in opposite directions: if I perceive a weevil *determinately*, surely that means perceiving it AS a weevil, of such-and-such genus, which already involves categorizing or naming it - so how is that same perceptual act supposed to have "no connection with the name"? Second, right after giving the main definition, the translator offers what he calls an alternate rendering of the very same aphorism, and that version says perception can be EITHER indeterminate OR determinate, which sounds like a meaningfully different claim than the first version (which just flatly calls perception "determinate," full stop). Is that second translation jsut a hedge because early Sanskritists weren't fully confident, or is there a genuine, substantive dispute buried in how to parse this one aphorism - something later commentators actually fought over? I'd like an actual walk-through of what's going on here, not just a "well, Sanskrit is ambiguous" shrug. I want to understand the real philosophical content, ideally with the indeterminate/determinate (nirvikalpaka/savikalpaka) distinction properly unpacked and explained in a way that dissolves the apparent contradiction (or tells me why it doesn't actually dissolve).
