In my graduate seminar on decision theory this term, one of my students—a former professional poker player who returned to academia after several years on the circuit—raised an example that has left me dissatisfied with the answer I gave in class, and I would welcome your help thinking it through properly. Consider a coin known with certainty to land heads sixty percent of the time, and suppose you are offered a long sequence of even-money bets on it: on each round you may stake any fraction of your current bankroll, heads doubles the stake and tails loses it, and you may play as many rounds as you like. I told the class that a rational, risk-neutral agent should stake their entire bankroll every round, since this is what maximizes the expected value of their wealth at each stage. My student objected that this policy amounts to near-certain ruin, since a single occurrence of tails wipes the bettor out completely, and across a long enough run the chance of tails coming up at least once approaches one, however favorable the arithmetic looked at the outset. He maintains that the fraction professional gamblers actually stake—on his account, about a fifth of the current bankroll each round, regardless of how many rounds remain—is not a symptom of risk-aversion but simply the fraction that maximizes what an individual bettor's wealth will actually do over a long career, as distinct from what happens on average across a hypothetical population of bettors who each live through a different random sequence of flips.

What I cannot settle, and what I would like your help with, is whether my student has identified a genuine shortcoming in ordinary expected-value reasoning as applied to sequential, compounding wagers, or whether his rule simply redescribes expected-utility maximization under a suitably concave, for instance logarithmic, utility function for wealth—in which case my original answer to the class was correct after all, and the disagreement is merely terminological. He resists this redescription, insisting that even a bettor with no independent aversion to risk should still adopt his rule, once they properly distinguish an average taken across possible worlds from the average that one and the same person actually accumulates across time. I would like to understand, before the seminar reconvenes, whether this distinction marks a substantive difference in what rationality demands under repeated risk, or whether it collapses on reflection into a familiar fact about risk-averse preferences expressed in unfamiliar terms.
