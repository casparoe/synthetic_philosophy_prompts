I'm a PhD student in economics, and this spring our department's graduate student association spent an inordinate amount of time debating how we elect our representative to the faculty council, after a plurality vote the previous year elected a candidate with only about a third of first-place votes, even though a majority of members preferred each of the other two candidates to the winner. At the meeting where the bylaws subcommittee proposed switching to ranked-choice voting, two people got into an extended back-and-forth that the rest of us mostly just watched: Daniel, a friend from the political science program who knew Arrow's theorem mainly from popular treatments of voting theory, and Rachel, who TAs our microeconomic theory sequence and has actually taught the social choice unit.

(1) Daniel opened by objecting that the whole exercise was pointless: Arrow's impossibility theorem, he said, proves that no voting rule can be fair once there are three or more candidates, so switching systems would just trade one undisclosed set of flaws for another, and we might as well keep the plurality rule everyone already understood. (2) Rachel replied that this moved too fast: Arrow's theorem indicts a specific bundle of conditions, most importantly independence of irrelevant alternatives, and plurality violates that condition about as badly as a rule can -- that's exactly what a vote-splitting spoiler is -- so even granting the impossibility result, some rules are clearly worse than others by the theorem's own lights; it doesn't license shrugging and doing nothing. (3) Daniel came back with: fine, but if independence of irrelevant alternatives really is a baseline fairness requirement and literally no ranked system can fully satisfy it, doesn't that mean whichever ranked system we adopt remains illegitimate in a way that's different in kind from an ideal we're merely approximating, rather than just "better" in some vaguer sense? (4) Rachel answered that full independence is a much stronger demand than the intuition people actually care about here -- that a hopeless also-ran shouldn't be able to flip the outcome between the top two contenders -- and that some ranked rules satisfy that narrower property even though none satisfy the full axiom; she added that Arrow's proof only constrains rules that take rankings as their sole input, so a cardinal rule like score voting escapes the theorem altogether. (5) Daniel's last word was that this just relocates the difficulty: once ballots are cardinal, you inherit the problem that a "7" from one voter may not mean what a "7" from another voter means, and nothing stops people from strategically maxing out or zeroing out their scores -- so "outside the scope of Arrow's theorem" is not the same thing as "actually fair," and he still wasn't convinced any change was worth the trouble.

I've had the standard micro theory sequence but never a dedicated course on social choice, and I couldn't cleanly adjudicate this at the time. Could you go through the exchange move by move and, for each numbered claim, say whether it's correct, overstated, or mistaken, and why -- including anything you think both Daniel and Rachel are getting wrong or eliding about what Arrow's theorem does and doesn't establish? Please close with a brief overall verdict on whether the subcommittee's proposal survives the exchange.
