I'm putting together a handout for my epistemology seminar on assertion, and every year when we hit the knowledge-norm-vs-truth-norm debate at least one student says it just sounds like two people shouting "know!" and "true!" at each other without explaining why anyone should care. So before I write my own version, I thought i'd get you to take a crack at it: write me a dialogue between a philosopher who defends the knowledge norm of assertion (the Williamsonian line that you may assert p only if you know p) and one who defends a truth norm instead (the Weiner/Whiting line that the only constitutive requirement on assertion is that p be true, and everything else that looks epistemic is really just a general conversational norm that happens to apply to assertion the way it applies to other speech acts too).

Dont just have them trade position statements though, I want real engagement on two specific bits of data. First, lottery propositions: why does it sound so bad to flatly assert "your ticket lost" before the draw is announced, even when your evidence is about as good as evidence for a contingent claim ever gets? The knowledge theorist shouldn't just say "because you don't know it" and stop there — they need to explain why lack of knowledge specifically is doing the work, rather than mere lack of certainty or lack of sufficiently good grounds. That matters because explaining the oddity the second way, via inadequate grounds rather than a special knowledge requirement, is exactly the move the truth theorist wants to make instead, cashed out as a general conversational norm rather than anything built into assertion itself. Second, Moore-paradoxical assertions like "it's raining, but I don't know that it's raining" — both of them need a story here too, and it should connect back to whatever they each said about the lottery case. If it comes up naturally, let one of them bring up Lackey-style "selfless assertion" cases — someone with excellent evidence, who recognizes it as excellent evidence, but who doesn't believe p for unrelated reasons and asserts p anyway — as a complication for the other side. No need for stage directions or a tidy ending, I just want two people who actually know this literature cold and are genuinely trying to talk each other out of their view, not two monologues stapled together.
