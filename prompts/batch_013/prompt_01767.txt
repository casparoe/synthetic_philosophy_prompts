I'm running a graduate seminar on dynamic choice this term, and last week's session left me a bit stuck on something I'd like an outside opinion on. Everyone's seen the vanilla money-pump argument: if your preferences over A, B and C are cyclic, a bookie cycles you through trades and drains you dry, so transitivity is supposedly rationally required, fine. What's been bugging me is the fancier "forcing" money-pump argument Johan Gustafsson runs for completeness in that Cambridge Elements book of his (and in the papers since) -- the claim that even a mere preferential gap between two options (not A>B, not B>A, and not indifferent either) can be pumped, so long as the gap is the sort that survives small sweetenings, a la Chang's old clarinetist-vs-lawyer case. The twist that makes it bite is that at each step the agent is supposedly rationally *required* to take the trade, not merely permitted to, so you can't just shrug and say "well, I'm allowed to refuse."

I put this to a couple of my students, and one of them, who's been lurking in the AI-safety corner of the internet (something about coherence theorems for AI agents, of all things), threw Elliott Thornley's reply back at me: block the whole scheme by just refusing any trade thats worse than one you already turned down earlier in the sequence. My gut says this move quietly smuggles completeness-like structure back in through the agent's memory of its own past choices, rather than actually vindicating incomplete preferences, but I couldn't get further than a gut feeling, and Rabinowicz's older foresight-based defenses against the cyclic-preference pump are hovering in the background too, muddying things further. So: write me a real essay on this, not a survey. Lay out the strongest version of the forcing money-pump argument for completeness, then the best resolute/foresight-style defense of incompleteness against it, and then actually tell me which one wins -- does the argument succeed in showing that rational preferences must be complete, or not? Don't hand me a "both sides have a point" shrug, I get enough of those from my own department.
