So my philosophy of language class just spent a whole week on Frege's "On Sense and Reference" and the classic puzzle of why "a=a" is trivial but "a=b" can be an actual discovery (the Hesperus/Phosphorus thing, if you know it). Everyone, including the professor, kind of just repeats the textbook line that sense vs. reference is THE fix for this. But I went back and read the earlier Begriffschrift, and it turns out Frege originally had a totally different answer: in section 8 he floats the idea that identity statements are really about the signs themselves — "a=b" just says that the names "a" and "b" happen to pick out the same object, so its basically a claim about our notation rather than about the world out there. Then literally on the first page of "On Sense and Reference" he turns around and rejects that exact idea, and his reason is that the hookup between a sign and whatever it designates is arbitrary (anyone could stipulate any name for anything), so if identity statements were really about that hookup, they wouldn't be expressing any actual knowledge, just reporting on our own conventions.

I want you to argue FOR the earlier signs-based view, against Frege's own objection to it — don't just lay out both positions neutrally, actually make the strongest case you can that the metalinguistic account survives the "it's arbitrary" charge and explains the a=a / a=b contrast at least as well as sense and reference does. One thread you could pull on (or ignore, if you've got a better one): does it actually matter that the sign-to-object hookup is arbitrary, if ordinary speakers have no a priori way of knowing which arbitrary hookups actually obtain? Discovering that two names were stipulated onto the same object seems like it could still count as discovering something, even if what's discovered is a fact about language rather than about the object itself. Bonus points if you can connect this to how modern Millian/direct-reference theorists, who insist names contribute nothing but their referent to a proposition, end up needing some kind of metalinguistic or pragmatic story anyway to explain why "a=b" can be informative — which might suggest Frege gave up on his first instinct too fast.
