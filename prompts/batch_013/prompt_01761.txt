I am at work on a historical novel set in the Dutch Republic in the late 1640s, and one chapter turns on an invented exchange between Descartes and a fictional apprentice, Aeltje, who assists a local anatomist and has just watched him do something to a living animal that troubles her deeply. Before drafting the scene, I put together some notes on Descartes' actual position, since I do not want to hand him either a straw man or an anachronistically enlightened defense. I would value your comments on where this reconstruction holds up and where it does not.

My notes:

Descartes' case, as I understand it, rests on the two arguments he gives in Part V of the Discourse: that no animal, however capable, can join words together to declare a thought on some arbitrary new subject, a feat even the dullest human being manages; and that no animal acts with the general, all-purpose flexibility of reason, since its behavior can always be traced to the particular disposition of its organs, the way a clock's hands move without the clock knowing the hour. From this he concludes that animals have no mind and no thought, thought being for him a state one is immediately aware of having.

I want to avoid the image most readers already carry: an animal nailed down and shrieking while Descartes calmly explains that the sound is only a spring unwinding, usually attached to some story about his wife's dog. That version looks like a later composite — he had no wife, for one thing — that seems to have absorbed accounts, from Cartesian circles at Port-Royal, of animals nailed down for dissection, well after he was gone. What he is actually on record doing is narrower, and I think more useful dramatically: a 1638 letter to Plempius describes opening the chest of a living rabbit to watch its heart at work, in the course of a real dispute with Harvey over what causes the heartbeat. And in a 1649 letter to Henry More, he draws a line I want Aeltje to find and press on: he denies animals thought but not sensation, insofar as sensation depends on a bodily organ, while conceding that the underlying question cannot be settled with certainty either way.

My plan is to have Descartes offer something close to his real reasoning, unhurried and untroubled, and have Aeltje ask what a sensation nobody is aware of having would even be, and how he could tell the difference from outside.

Is this a fair and accurate reading of Descartes' actual view, or have I flattened it into a straw man in one direction, or cleaned it up too generously in the other? And is Aeltje's question one his own texts leave genuinely unanswered, or is there a move available to him that I have not considered?
