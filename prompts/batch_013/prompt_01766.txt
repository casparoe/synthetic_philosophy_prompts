It came about because I got through the Wittgenstein unit in the only philosophy elective my school offers faster than the rest of the class, and she offered to keep giving me things to read on my own. For the past month I've been working through Saul Kripke's book on the rule-following paradox, and over the same stretch I've been teaching myself the basics of neural networks, using "train a tiny network to add two numbers" as my toy project. I ran into the well-known problem that these networks learn addition just fine within whatever range of numbers you train them on, then do something close to arbitrary once you test them outside that range, and it struck me that this looks a lot like Kripke's "quus" example. I turned the idea into a short essay for my independent study, and since nobody around me knows this material well enough to check it, I would really appreciate some outside comments before I hand it in.

Here is the essay:

Kripke's skeptic asks what fact about my past use of "plus" makes it true that I meant ordinary addition rather than a deviant function he calls "quus," defined to agree with addition whenever both numbers are less than 57, and to equal 5 otherwise. Since I have never before added numbers that large, my past behavior fits both functions equally well, yet I feel certain "125" is uniquely correct for "68+57." Kripke argues that nothing can supply the missing fact. A disposition to answer "125" cannot do it, since a disposition only describes what I would actually do, mistakes included, and can never tell me what I ought to do. An idealized machine built to compute "plus" cannot do it either, since any physical machine could always malfunction or be extended in more than one way, and saying it is "supposed to" go on computing addition already assumes a standard of correctness that has to come from somewhere outside the machine. Kripke's own "skeptical solution" gives up on finding such a fact and instead grounds meaning in communal practice: I count as meaning plus not because of some fact in my head or my history, but because my answers keep matching what the people around me are willing to endorse, and would correct me against if I strayed.

A trained neural network seems to land in exactly this position. If I train a small network on addition using only numbers under 57, nothing in its weights decides whether it has learned addition or some "quus"-like function that merely agrees with addition inside that range; infinitely many functions fit the training data equally well, and whatever the network actually outputs for "68+57" is just whatever its architecture and training process happen to produce, not the discovery of some prior, intended function. What machine learning calls successful "generalization," as opposed to "overfitting," is not a hidden fact that the network's weights either match or fail to match; it is a judgment fixed by a held-out test set and by community conventions about what the network was supposed to learn. That looks to me like Kripke's assertability conditions wearing different clothes, and it makes me suspect machine learning quietly assumes something close to the skeptical solution already, rather than assuming there is a deeper semantic fact underneath it.

I would like comments on two things especially. First, whether I have represented Kripke's argument accurately, particularly his reasons for rejecting dispositions and idealized machines, since that is the part I find hardest to paraphrase without flattening it. Second, and more importantly, whether the comparison to neural network generalization is doing real philosophical work or only looks similar on the surface, since a network's weights do fix one perfectly determinate function at every input, which is not obviously true of Kripke's genuinely fact-less picture, and I am not sure whether that difference quietly undermines my whole comparison or just relocates the same problem somewhere else. If the analogy breaks down at some specific point, I would much rather hear that now than after I turn the essay in.
