For the past year or so I've been working through Thomas Schelling's writing on my own — especially the bargaining chapter of The Strategy of Conflict and his later book Arms and Influence — and I keep coming back to his argument that voluntarily narrowing your own options, or making some commitment irrevocable, can be a source of strength in a negotiation rather than a weakness, a logic he pushed all the way out to his discussion of "doomsday machines." More recently I came across something called the "commitment races problem" that comes up in AI safety discussions: the worry that sufficiently capable, consequence-driven AI agents would feel pressure to lock in binding commitments as early as possible, before they've had time to reason things through carefully, since whichever agent commits first can force the others to adapt to it — and that this rush to commit prematurely could produce genuinely disastrous outcomes. Schelling died in 2016, a few years before anyone wrote the idea up in quite that form, but the underlying logic strikes me as an almost direct descendant of what he'd already worked out about bargaining and Cold War deterrence. Based on what he actually argued, what do you think Schelling's own diagnosis of the commitment races problem would have been — would he have treated it as essentially the same phenomenon he already understood, just running faster and among machines, or would he have pointed to some real disanalogy between human and AI bargaining that ought to change the conclusions people draw? I'd like your reasoning laid out in some depth rather than just a short verdict.
