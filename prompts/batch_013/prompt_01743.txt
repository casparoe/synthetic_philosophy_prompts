As a longtime reader of the rationalist blogosphere, I've absorbed the standard treatments of the Sleeping Beauty problem many times over: Adam Elga's original argument for a credence of 1/3 on waking, David Lewis's halfer reply, and Bostrom's Self-Sampling and Self-Indication Assumptions, which can diverge sharply, as the Presumptuous Philosopher illustrates. What none of this literature has given me is any sense of why the disagreement should matter to someone actually living inside it, rather than to philosophers arguing in a seminar room.

Write me a short story, no more than a page or two, built around an original scenario rather than the usual coin flip and memory wipe. Center it on a character who must act under genuine self-locating uncertainty — placing a bet, ordering an evacuation, consenting to some risky procedure — where the reasonable choice differs depending on whether they treat themselves as a random sample among actually existing observers or among all possible ones. Let that divergence show up in what the characters do and argue, not in a paragraph of exposition, and please don't let the ending adjudicate which position is correct; nobody has actually earned the right to that certainty yet.
