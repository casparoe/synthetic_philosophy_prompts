After decades spent designing controlled experiments, in which the control group serves as a stand-in for what would have happened to the treated subjects absent the intervention, I have taken up philosophy of language in retirement, and one issue has left me puzzled. Indicative conditionals ("if the culture was contaminated, the assay failed") and counterfactual conditionals ("if the culture had been contaminated, the assay would have failed") strike me as two grammatical dressings of the same underlying inference -- essentially a Ramsey test, in which one adds the antecedent to what is already known and sees what follows -- differing only in whether we already know which side of the comparison turned out to be actual. Yet philosophers insist on building entirely separate machinery for the two: Adams's probabilistic semantics, keyed to conditional probability, for the indicative case, and Stalnaker and Lewis's possible-worlds semantics, keyed to similarity between worlds, for the counterfactual case. If the two conditionals really are the same inference, one framework ought to reduce to the other, so which do philosophers take to be more fundamental, and why has the other persisted as more than a notational variant?
