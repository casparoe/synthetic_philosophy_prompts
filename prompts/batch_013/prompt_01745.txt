I'm part of a small in-person discussion group that grew out of a local rationalist meetup (the sort of crowd that cut its teeth on Overcoming Bias and early LessWrong), and we've spent the last few sessions working through the classic pragmatic arguments for the axioms of expected utility theory. Two sessions ago we did money pumps, and a disagreement broke out between two of the regulars, Marcus and Elena, that the group never really settled even after arguing about it over drinks afterward, so I'm hoping you can succeed where we didn't.

For background: the standard money-pump argument says that if you strictly prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, someone can turn you into a money pump. Starting from C, you'll pay a small fee to trade for B (since B is preferred to C), then pay again to trade for A (since A is preferred to B), then pay once more to trade back for C (since C is preferred to A) -- and now you're holding exactly what you started with, minus three fees, and the whole cycle can be repeated forever. This is meant to show that cyclic preferences of this kind are irrational. (1) Marcus argued that it only refutes a naive agent who re-evaluates from scratch at every choice point. An agent who instead commits at the outset to a plan for the whole sequence -- something he'd read about under the name "resolute choice" -- could see the trap coming, accept the first trade for B, and then simply refuse the second trade, even though A is preferred to B taken on its own, ending up one fee poorer but never walked all the way around the circle. Since the agent's underlying ranking of A, B, and C never stopped being cyclic, Marcus concluded that the pump shows you need foresight and resolve, not that you need transitive preferences. (2) Elena objected that this is a shell game: staying resolute requires the agent, at that second step, to turn down an option -- A over B -- that they explicitly rank higher, purely because "that was the plan." An agent who can override their own preferences on demand, whenever honoring them would look bad, could be defended against any charge of irrationality whatsoever, which can't be right. (3) Marcus replied that we already accept this kind of override as rational elsewhere: in Kavka's toxin puzzle, a billionaire pays you a million dollars just for sincerely intending, tonight, to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow that will make you painfully ill for a day -- and the money lands in your account before you'd ever have to go through with it, so the reward goes only to someone capable of committing to an act they will have no reason to perform once the payoff is banked. His point: being able to act against your in-the-moment preferences, because you committed to it beforehand, is an asset, and an agent who can't do this is the one at a disadvantage. (4) Elena granted that this kind of commitment can be rational, but said the two cases aren't parallel: in the toxin case, the resoluteness is doing independent work, since it's precisely what makes the intention credible enough to earn the million in the first place. In the money-pump case, she said, resoluteness doesn't buy the agent anything except not looking cyclic, which is rationalizing the ranking rather than defending it. She added that a resolute agent still isn't safe in general: a cleverer pump that offers the whole cycle as a single choice up front, before any "plan" gets a chance to insulate the later steps, would still catch a ranking that is genuinely cyclic.

Could you go through this move by move -- (1) through (4) -- and say who has the better of it at each stage, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also like your own verdict on the substantive question underneath it all: does the availability of resolute choice actually show that the money-pump argument fails to establish that rational preferences must be transitive, or does Elena's rejoinder hold up against it? And if you think Marcus and Elena are each onto something real but talking past each other, I'd be curious what you think the missing piece is -- and since this crowd likes a number attached to a claim, a rough credence for how likely it is that resolute choice really rescues cyclic preferences, as opposed to merely relocating the problem, would be appreciated too.
