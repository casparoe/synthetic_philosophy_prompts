Quick context: I write a science-and-ideas column, and I've been chasing a piece on the "value-free ideal" in science -- the idea that researchers should just report facts and leave value judgments to voters and politicians. Someone in my replies pointed out that this ideal has a wierder backstory than people realize, rooted in the Vienna Circle, and now I can't stop reading about it. Turns out Carnap, Neurath and Hahn were mostly committed socialists writing in "Red Vienna" in the 1920s, and their 1929 manifesto explicitly ties the whole "scientific world-conception" project to the workers' movement, casting metaphysics and "eternal values" talk as the natural ally of clerical and nationalist politics. And yet Carnap spent decades afterward insisting that his actual philosophical apparatus -- verificationism, the attack on metaphysics, his non-cognitivism about ethics, the principle of tolerance -- was strictly neutral among political programs, and that party politics was something Circle members hashed out privately, not something baked into the philosophy; the two were supposed to stay seperate. Historians of the Circle (Uebel, Reisch, and others, from what I can tell) are still arguing about who's right.

Here's the essay I want your help drafting, just for my own background before I start calling people (no need to worry about citations or exact quotes, I'll chase those later): was Carnap basically correct that his logical empiricism was politically neutral, with the manifesto's socialist framing being more Neurath's doing or just movement PR -- or does the neutrality claim fall apart once you notice what banishing metaphysics and treating value-statements as non-cognitive actually *does* in a political climate where the right's rhetoric leaned so heavily on metaphysical, organicist appeals? Lay out the strongest version of both readings, then don't dodge it: tell me which one you actually buy, or stake out a more precise middle position, and defend it like you mean it.
