I've been part of a small reading group for a few years now -- mostly people I first met through the rationalist blogosphere (Less Wrong, Slate Star Codex, that whole cluster) -- and we've been slowly working our way through classic texts in epistemology. A few weeks ago we finally got to Hilary Putnam's brain-in-a-vat argument, from the first chapter of *Reason, Truth and History* (1981). For context, since it matters for what follows: Putnam's central move is that if you were a brain that had been in a vat, wired to a machine feeding you experiences, from the very start of your existence, then on a causal or use-based theory of reference your word "vat" would not pick out real vats at all -- it would refer instead to whatever actually plays the relevant causal role in producing your experience, such as certain recurring features of the simulating program. So when a lifelong vat-brain thinks or says "I am a brain in a vat," the sentence, correctly interpreted according to its own semantics, comes out false, since it is not a brain in a vat in whatever sense its own word "vat" refers to; and obviously, if you are not envatted at all, the sentence is simply false as a plain matter of fact. Either way, Putnam concludes, the sentence "I am a brain in a vat" can never be truly asserted by anyone thinking it -- which is supposed to give us a strange, purely semantic route to knowing we are not such brains.

Two of the regulars, Nadia and Marcus, ended up in a genuine disagreement over whether this actually works, and nobody else in the group could settle it. Reconstructed roughly: (1) Nadia argued that the argument succeeds on its own terms -- since a lifelong vat-brain's word "vat" refers to program features rather than real vats, its utterance of "I am a brain in a vat" is self-undermining in exactly the way Putnam describes, so we can rule out, a priori, ever having been such a brain. (2) Marcus objected that this only touches lifelong envatment: if you lived a normal embodied life for thirty years and were only wired into the vat yesterday, your word "vat" would still refer to real vats via your linguistic history, so you could think "I am a brain in a vat" and have it come out true -- meaning Putnam's argument leaves the recently-envatted scenario completely open. (3) Nadia replied that this is not much of a concession, since nobody found brain-in-a-vat skepticism troubling because of the one-day-old case; what makes it interesting is the possibility of lifelong, undetectable deception, and that is exactly the possibility the argument closes off. (4) Marcus answered that this gives away the game: the reason BIV skepticism is unsettling in the first place is that it threatens your beliefs about the world right now -- whether there is really a table in front of you -- and a recently-envatted brain has exactly as much reason to doubt that as a lifelong one does, so Putnam's semantic maneuver leaves the actually-threatening version of the hypothesis completely untouched.
