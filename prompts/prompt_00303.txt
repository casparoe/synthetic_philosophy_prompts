I'm several drafts into a novel whose protagonist, a reclusive cryptographer, keeps a lifelong diary in a cipher she invented as a child after a traumatic event she could never bring herself to describe in ordinary words. No one else has ever cracked it, and she is the only person alive who can decode it. While reading around in philosophy of language to give the book some intellectual scaffolding, I came across Wittgenstein's private language argument, and it struck me as an almost uncanny match for her situation: a language that, by construction, only one person can understand and use correctly seems to be exactly what he showed to be impossible. I'd like the novel's central metaphysical thread to turn on this insight, so I want to make sure I represent the philosophy faithfully rather than just gesturing at it.

Specifically, I'm considering writing a climactic sequence in which my protagonist, now elderly, tries to reread her own early diary entries and finds that she can no longer be sure she's decoding them correctly — the idea being that, per Wittgenstein, her cipher could never have "really" referred to anything in the first place, since there was no one else around to check whether she was applying its rules correctly or merely believed she was. Is this a sound way to dramatize the private language argument, or am I stretching the concept to cover a case (a self-invented cipher for concealing plaintext) that isn't actually the kind of "private language" Wittgenstein had in mind? I'd rather find out now than build fifty pages on a misreading.
