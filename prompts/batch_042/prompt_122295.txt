Write a dialogue between two philosophers of typography — call them Mara and Theo — who are arguing about whether there's an essence to the letter 'a'. Mara thinks every 'a' is just an instance of a topological family (closed loop, stem, bowl) and that style is just noise on top of that skeleton. Theo thinks that's backwards: he says an 'a' is only recognizble because it sits inside a specific 'letter spirit', a kind of relational style-space shared by the whole alphabet, so if you isolate the form and drop it into a font with totally different proportional rules it stops being an 'a' even though the topology hasn't changed. Make it real back-and-forth — neither should be a strawman. Have them work through a specific hypothetical: the 'orphan glyph', a form with perfect 'a'-topology that gets placed into a constructed alphabet where every other letter is made of only horizontal wavy lines and no closed loops. Mara should say it's still an 'a', just an outlier; Theo should say it's become a foreign object, maybe an 'o' with a tail or just noise. Then have them push on whether this dispute is about cognition (what our brains parse) or about ontology (what the letter actually is). Keep it casual but in the weeds — don't just rehash Plato vs nominalism. And please, no bibliographies or citation lists; a model without wifi shouldn't need to look up Hofstadter to do this.
