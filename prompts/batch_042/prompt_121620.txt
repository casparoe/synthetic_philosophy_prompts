Exam prompt: Coherence-based arguments—Dutch-book arguments and money-pump arguments—are frequently presented as showing that rational agents must maximize expected utility. Critics object that coherence demands only consistency among beliefs or preferences, not conformity to a specific axiomatic framework. Evaluate this dispute. In particular, address: (i) whether the standard Dutch-book argument against non-probabilistic credences tacitly assumes that an agent's betting behavior reveals her all-things-considered beliefs rather than partial beliefs that might be filtered through risk attitudes; (ii) whether a rank-dependent model using cumulative-prospect-theory-style weighting can avoid standard money-pump arguments by permitting context-dependent preference reversals that preserve dynamic consistency; and (iii) whether immunity to a money pump requires the independence axiom, or whether continuity or completeness does the essential work.

Student answer: Coherence arguments do establish expected utility maximization, but only if coherence is interpreted as dynamic consistency across time. The Dutch-book argument shows that non-probabilistic credences allow a bookie to construct a set of bets that guarantee loss, which proves rational agents must have probabilistic beliefs. Once beliefs are probabilistic, the money-pump argument demonstrates that any violation of the independence axiom permits a cycle of trades that leaves the agent with a sure loss. So coherence jointly enforces probabilistic belief and expected-utility preference.

Regarding (i), betting behavior reveals all-things-considered beliefs because a wager is an all-things-considered act. The claim that risk attitudes contaminate belief revelation therefore collapses: there is no separate layer of partial belief in the decision context.

Regarding (ii), rank-dependent models remain vulnerable. Even allowing context-dependent reversals, any deviation from independence generates a cycle exploitable across contexts. Dynamic consistency offers no protection because the pump operates between contexts rather than within a single temporal sequence. Thus cumulative prospect theory cannot escape.

Regarding (iii), independence is the axiom that specifically blocks the pump. Completeness and continuity only secure a representational utility function; they do not prevent cyclic preference structures. Only independence eliminates the patterns that make sure-loss trading possible. Consequently, coherence arguments genuinely support expected utility maximization, not merely weak consistency.
