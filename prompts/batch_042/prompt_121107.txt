As a novelist constructing a narrative about institutional memory, I have been studying the case of a democratic republic—call it the State of Meridian—whose legislature enacted a permanent Civic Repair Statute. The statute directs a portion of all income tax, in perpetuity, to a Restoration Fund for descendants of a historical atrocity committed by prior regimes; yet its preamble explicitly declares that no present citizen bears moral guilt, framing the payments instead as the repair of a broken continuity within the body politic. Concurrently, the state’s official curriculum requires that the event be taught not as the crime of particular individuals, but as a failure of the nation itself considered as an enduring moral agent across generations. What philosophical commitments about collective guilt, the ontology of nations, and moral agency over time does this institutional arrangement presuppose? In particular, is it coherent with Karl Jaspers’s distinction among criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt, or does it smuggle in a substantive theory of the nation as a trans-temporal subject capable of bearing guilt that its citizens do not? And if the nation can be guilty without its members being guilty, what exactly is the locus of that guilt—an institution, a culture, a collective will—and what would have to change in the statute’s framing if we replaced that ontology with a purely individualist or a purely structuralist account?
