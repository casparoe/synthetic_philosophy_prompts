I sit as the doctoral student representative on our university hospital's Neuroethics Review Panel, which convenes next Thursday to evaluate a proposed clinical trial protocol for assessing awareness in patients with disorders of consciousness. The investigators—an interdisciplinary team of neurosurgeons and cognitive scientists—have designed a diagnostic framework they call the Unified Stream Protocol, which operationalizes consciousness by searching for a singular, globally broadcast representation within a specific fronto-parietal convergence zone. They defend this architecture by explicit analogy to a Cartesian display: they hold that genuine phenomenal presence requires one canonical version of a representation available to a central consumer, and they propose using the presence or absence of this unified signal to determine whether minimally responsive patients possess the kind of integrated awareness that should ground family consent and clinical decision-making.

A minority report submitted by two philosophers of science on our panel rejects this framing entirely, arguing that the protocol illegitimately presupposes a Cartesian theater that has been thoroughly undermined by the multiple drafts model of consciousness; they contend that awareness is constituted not by a final unified display but by distributed, temporally staggered drafts across cortical networks, none of which enjoys privileged status, and they warn that adopting the protocol could systematically misclassify patients whose awareness takes a non-convergent form. Our chair has asked me to draft the panel's preliminary opinion memo for circulation before the vote: it should recommend a concrete course of action—approval, rejection, or conditional approval with specific amendments and a six-week timeline for revision—while treating the investigators' unified-display hypothesis and the dissenters' distributed-drafts objection with equal philosophical seriousness, rather than dismissing either side as merely outdated neuroscience or abstract metaphysics. The memo should also specify what practical safeguards or methodological adjustments would be required if we were to permit the trial to proceed under either theoretical framing.
