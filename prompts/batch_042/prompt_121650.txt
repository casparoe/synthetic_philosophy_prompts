I'm putting together a handout for a graduate seminar on self-reference and personal identity, and we've been working through Hofstadter's idea of the "I" as a strange loop—specifically the claim that the self is a self-referential symbol arising when a pattern in a substrate turns back upon itself to represent itself. I want a taxonomy of the metaphysical positions this commits us to, structured as a decision tree in which every branch is a concrete case or claim that separates views without requiring anyone to know the philosopher's label in advance.

The tree should begin with a root question: do you accept that an isomorphic strange loop could exist in a non-biological substrate, such as a digital simulation or a gradually replaced synthetic neural network? From there, split on whether the loop's persistence requires continuous causal history of the original substrate, whether the symbol "I" must be a singular token or can be distributed across nodes, and whether autobiographical narrative continuity is a necessary condition for the loop's survival or merely a descriptive overlay. The leaves must include: (1) the substrate-independent pattern view associated with Hofstadter; (2) a strict biological-continuity view that denies cross-substrate loops; (3) an eliminativist view treating the loop as a useful fiction with no metaphysical weight; (4) a narrative-constructivist view that accepts the loop descriptively but denies it tracks a real entity; (5) a hybrid "bounded loop" position—possibly unexplored—according to which the loop is genuinely self-referential yet can only persist through an unbroken causal chain of pattern maintenance, so gradual replacement is permissible but discontinuous copying is not; and (6) any other logical combination your branching allows, even if currently unoccupied, with a brief diagnosis of whether it is incoherent, unstable, or merely unexplored. Please phrase every node as a scenario a student could answer yes or no to—e.g., "If your neurons were replaced one by one with functionally identical synthetic units over a decade, would the same loop persist?"—and explicitly flag any pair of leaves that your questions cannot genuinely separate. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the aim is for students to navigate by reasoning from the concept alone. Keep the taxonomy formal, with about six to eight leaves, and note empty branches with a sentence on why they might be unattractive or incoherent.
