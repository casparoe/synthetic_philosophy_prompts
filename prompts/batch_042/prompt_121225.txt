I sit on the investment-ethics panel of the Meridian Foundation, a long-horizon grantmaker holding roughly fifty million in capital earmarked for intergenerational risk reduction. For Thursday’s vote we must decide on the Intergenerational Climate Lockbox proposal: the capital is placed in a perpetual, risk-free compound instrument that disburses only in 2225, contingent on atmospheric carbon levels exceeding a defined threshold; if the threshold is not met, the fund rolls forward rather than dissolving. The architects invoke pure-time-preference neutrality, arguing that discounting future welfare solely because it is future is ethically arbitrary, so the pure rate should be zero with only growth-rate and catastrophe-risk adjustments permitted. Opponents counter that locking resources away for two centuries imposes dead-hand control, denies agency to intermediate generations, and relies on an unverifiable counterfactual about their preferences; they also argue that the proposal’s Descendant Veto clause—allowing trustees in 2100 to redirect the fund if climate-risk profiles have materially changed—either violates the neutrality principle by injecting present-biased discretion or renders the lockbox pointless. I need a formal memo recommending adoption, rejection, or adoption with binding conditions, including a concrete implementation timeline and an explicit bottom line. The memo should treat the pure-time-preference argument with philosophical precision rather than reducing it to a slogan, should present the strongest autonomy-based objection to your recommendation honestly, and should analyze whether the Descendant Veto resolves the tension between neutrality and agency or deepens it. Write it as a document the panel could circulate, amend, and vote on.
