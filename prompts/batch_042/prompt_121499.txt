I was reading this old Victorian-era translation of Zhuangzi I picked up at a used bookstore — the one with the butterfly dream. The passage goes something like this: Zhou falls asleep and becomes a butterfly, happy and free, with no idea he's ever been Zhou. Then he wakes up and is Zhou again, with all his human worries and memories. He asks if maybe he's really a butterfly that's now dreaming it's Zhou, since he can't tell the difference from inside the experience. Everyone I talk to about this treats it like a generic "how do you know you're not dreaming right now?" problem, but I keep getting stuck on something the text actually mentions and then drops: the transformation only seems to go one direction. Zhou remembers being the butterfly, but there's no sense that the butterfly, if it woke up, would remember being Zhou. So is the point really about skepticism, or is it about whether identity is just the current form that happens to hold the memory thread? If the "real" self is whichever state has the continous chain of recollection, then Zhou has a claim to being the baseline and the butterfly is just a temporary mode — but that feels like it's sneaking in human exceptionalism, which doesn't seem very Zhuangist to me. I'm confused about whether I'm supposed to read the asymmetry as meaningful or just as a narrative quirk, and whether it changes the usual skeptical reading entirely. Can you help me parse what's actually going on with the directional flow of transformation here?
