ok so for my philsoophy class we talked about assertion norms — like do you need knowledge, belief, or just truth? and i think i built an argument that breaks the knowledge rule but it proves way too much. here goes:

(1) you should only assert p if you actually know p, not just believe it or hope its true.
(2) but tons of what i say is secondhand — like i tell my sister australia's capital is canberra because my friend told me. call this "assertoric inheritance": im putting my word behind p even though my reason is just someone else's word.
(3) if the knowledge norm holds, then in assertoric inheritance i still need to know p myself.
(4) but i dont have direct knowledge, only testimonial knowledge, and that only works if my friend actually knew p and wasnt lying.
(5) so to be justified inheriting, i need to know that my friend knew p.
(6) but my friend probably inherited it too, so i also need to know that she knew her source knew p, and so on all the way back to direct observation.
(7) nobody can trace the whole chain. so if (1) is true, nobody can ever assert anything learned secondhand.
(8) thats almost everything we say in normal life, which is clearly absurd.
(9) therefore (1) is false and assertion only needs belief or maybe just truth.

my study partner says the slip is between (3) and (5) — like i sneak in a second-order requirement that the knowledge norm never asked for. but im not sure why that doesnt just save the norm by making it useless. if i reject (5) instead, am i saying testimony works even when i dont know my source knew? that feels risky. which step do i actually give up, and whats the cost of each? also is there some hidden connection to epistemic closure here that im missing? i really want to keep (1) if i can, but (8) is killing me.
