Construct a realistic, one-paragraph strategic scenario set in a hospital pharmacy-and-therapeutics committee (not a trolley problem, and not a canonical game such as the Prisoner's Dilemma or Battle of the Sexes) in which two committee members—each with three discrete decision options—face payoffs shaped by an institutional rule you embed naturally: a delegated veto right that can be activated only through an internal audit lottery running with a known, non-degenerate probability. Engineer the narrative so that one member’s middle option is strictly dominated only by a mixed strategy of the other member (randomizing over her remaining options with weights matching the audit-lottery probabilities), not by any pure strategy she can adopt. After presenting the scenario, define the payoffs implicitly through described outcomes rather than displaying a matrix, then do the following: (1) list which strategies survive pure-strategy iterated elimination of strict dominance; (2) show that allowing domination by mixed strategies eliminates the middle option and trace the full elimination sequence to its unique outcome; (3) identify the exact structural feature—the interaction between the veto-probability weights and the payoff differentials—that makes pure-strategy elimination stop short while mixed-strategy elimination proceeds; (4) state the verdict delivered by pure-strategy rationalizability versus full (mixed-strategy) rationalizability, and explain why the resulting disadvantage to the first member is not an artifact of elimination ordering; and (5) explain how removing the probabilistic veto feature collapses the divergence back into a standard pure-strategy case, and how a defender of pure-strategy rationalizability could argue that the broader concept imports an unrealistic degree of strategic randomization into committee deliberation.
