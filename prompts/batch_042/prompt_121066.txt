In philosophy of medicine, the standoff between naturalist and normativist accounts of health and disease has persisted for decades: naturalists treat disease as a deviation from species-typical biological function, while normativists insist that the concept is irreducibly value-laden, since labeling a condition a disease requires judging it undesirable or harmful. Every standard objection—about cultural variability in diagnosis, about conditions like pregnancy or aging being statistically normal yet sometimes pathologized, about whether evolutionary dysfunction can be defined without smuggling in norms—has been answered and counter-answered without anyone changing camps. Recently, however, a distinction rarely pressed in the literature has started to seem central: pathology might be an intrinsic property of an organism, or it might be a relational property that only obtains between an organism and a specific ecological or social environment. Once that distinction is on the table, it becomes unclear whether naturalists and normativists are disagreeing about the same kind of thing at all, or whether naturalism captures intrinsic dysfunction well while normativism captures the relational judgment that something is going wrong for this particular being in this particular setting. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not another comparison of the theories. Is the long stall a substantive clash about the ontology of biological dysfunction, a verbal dispute about whether the word 'disease' carries evaluative weight, or are the two sides answering different questions—one about clinical taxonomy, one about the justification of medical intervention—under the single heading 'the concept of health'? Please support your diagnosis by engaging concretely with what naturalists and normativists actually commit to when they talk about function, harm, and statistical normality, and address whether the intrinsic/relational distinction resolves anything or merely relocates the problem. And tell me what, if anything, could break the impasse: a formal distinction, an empirical finding about cross-cultural diagnostic agreement, or a reframing that separates descriptive classification from prescriptive treatment? Or should we conclude that the disagreement is bedrock—resting on incompatible intuitions about whether nature itself can be wrong—and that no further progress should be expected?
