I'm putting together a taxonomy for a philosophy of action seminar on the integrity objection — the idea that saving your spouse because you ran a moral calculus involves having one thought too many. I want a decision tree that sorts positions by what people actually think, not by which label they pick.

Start with whether the problem is really about reasoning, or about how we describe motives after the fact. Then branch: does the objection apply only to close relationships, or also to institutional roles like a doctor triaging patients or a judge? Next split: can a utilitarian fix this by separating levels of reasoning, or does the fix just push the problem upstairs?

Leaves should include: the strict Williamsian view that any substitutable justification corrupts the attachment; the two-level utilitarian who thinks we cultivate direct motives but justify the system indirectly; an institutional separationist who protects private life but allows calculated policy; a particularist who says the mistake is treating reasons as generic; and any unnamed combos. For each leaf, note its most awkward commitment. Flag pairs your tree can't seperate — I think the separationist and two-level view might collapse at the institutional node. Keep every branch as a concrete question someone can answer without knowing the label.
