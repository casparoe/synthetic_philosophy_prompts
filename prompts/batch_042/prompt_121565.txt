I spent much of last evening arguing with a close friend—who is trained in classical philology and tends to treat every historiographical claim as an anachronism waiting to happen—about whether the so-called cyclical theories of history in Polybius and Ibn Khaldun are genuinely cyclical in any metaphysical sense. I am preparing to present this view at a joint seminar next Thursday: the audience will be about fifteen scholars split among ancient historians who read Polybius in Greek, medievalists who work on Ibn Khaldun's Muqaddimah in the original Arabic, and philosophers of history who have spent decades debating Spengler and Toynbee but regard grand narratives with deep suspicion. My thesis is that Polybius's anacyclosis and Ibn Khaldun's model of asabiyya and state decay are not ontological commitments to eternal recurrence, but rather linear, institutional-learning frameworks that borrow seasonal and biological metaphors purely as heuristic devices; I further claim that Spengler and Toynbee projected a nineteenth-century organicist metaphysics onto these texts, and that this misreading has distorted the modern debate about whether history can be said to "repeat." I want a Q&A prep sheet tailored precisely to this audience—not generic philosophy objections, but the ones these particular people will actually raise, ordered by how damaging they are to my argument, each phrased in the vocabulary they would use, with a two-minute reply and an explicit note on where that reply is weakest. Please include the specific objection I am hoping nobody asks, which concerns the textual basis for denying that Polybius intended his constitutional cycle as more than a pedagogical pattern. Also, since my friend kept insisting that I was conflating descriptive patterns with normative predictions, please build into the objections at least one that targets that exact conflation, and another that challenges whether my reading can survive the apparent teleological language in Ibn Khaldun's discussion of civilization's inevitable decline. The prep should assume I have already sketched my argument in the talk—so target the weak joints in that sketch rather than asking me to defend the thesis from scratch.
