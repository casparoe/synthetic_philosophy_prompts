I work as a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, and I recently began reading Darwin's The Descent of Man after a colleague brought up evolutionary ethics in a team discussion. Darwin argues that moral sentiments are extensions of social instincts shaped by natural selection, and he treats religious belief as a byproduct of an overactive tendency to infer agency rather than as an innate adaptation. I want to understand how he might have extended this framework to issues that arose only after his death, reasoning strictly from the internal logic of his actual commitments rather than importing modern positions anachronistically.

First, evolutionary debunking arguments in metaethics claim that because our moral judgments are products of natural selection, they lack objective truth-tracking authority; given Darwin's own naturalistic account of morality, would he have regarded this debunking as undermining his view, or as fully compatible with it? Second, contemporary work on artificial-agent alignment considers systems that can model cooperative behavior but possess no evolutionary history of social instincts; would Darwin have considered such agents capable of genuine moral sentiments, or would he have judged those capacities necessarily hollow without the biological substrate he describes? Please avoid bibliographies, reading lists, or citations—I am interested in testing the coherence of the reasoning itself, not in tracking sources.
