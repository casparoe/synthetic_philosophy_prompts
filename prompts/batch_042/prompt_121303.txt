For decades, the literature on moral responsibility has circled the same impasse around Galen Strawson’s Basic Argument: if an agent cannot be causa sui—cannot ultimately cause herself—then she cannot be truly deserving of praise or blame, and moral responsibility collapses into a fiction sustained by reactive attitudes. Compatibilists respond that this sets a standard no one actually requires, substituting metaphysical fantasy for the practical conditions of accountability; Strawson’s defenders reply that without such “ultimate” authorship we are merely tracking psychological patterns, not assigning desert. Every counter-move—the infinite-regress objection, the “enough-ness” reply, the analogy to physical causation—is anticipated and defused within a page, and discussion groups now treat the exchange as ritual rather than live dispute. I am trying to understand whether this stagnation reflects a substantive disagreement about the causal conditions of agency, a verbal dispute about what “responsibility” must include, or a deeper structural problem: two inquiries—normative ethics asking how we ought to treat one another, and metaphysics asking what kind of origin makes an action genuinely “mine”—running under a single heading.

What makes the stall especially puzzling is that both sides now concede the empirical psychology of choice. Recent work in cognitive neuroscience treats agentive experience not as an all-or-nothing metaphysical property but as a graded, mechanistically explicable representation—intact in ordinary decision-making yet fragmentable in pathologies like alien hand syndrome or certain delusions of control. If authorship is genuinely variable in this way, does it undermine Strawson’s demand for an uncaused cause, or does it merely relocate the debate onto whether graded, constructed authorship can ever ground real desert? I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself: is it about hidden factual claims regarding the nature of agency, about competing conceptual standards for “enough” causation, about bedrock moral intuitions concerning punishment that neither side can argue for, or about a category mistake in framing moral responsibility as requiring a specific causal origin? And given that diagnosis, what—if anything—would count as progress: a conceptual distinction that separates reactive attitudes from desert, an empirical result from cognitive science, a reformulation of compatibilist conditions, or an admission that the question is ill-posed?
