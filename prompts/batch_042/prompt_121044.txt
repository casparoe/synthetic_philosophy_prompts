Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a binding rationalist and a diachronic-integrity rationalist on Parfit's hitchhiker and the rationality of self-binding. Both camps accept the standard scenario—an agent stranded in a remote location who can secure rescue only by promising to pay afterward, though once safe she has no instrumental reason to honor the promise—but they also agree to examine a modified case in which the agent can adopt an irreversible commitment procedure before departure, such as a structural or cognitive restructuring that makes defaulting psychologically impossible without altering her post-rescue beliefs or values.

Present a numbered section of agreed claims: about the formal structure of the decision problem; about the distinction between time-slice and diachronic rationality; about the difference between a binding mechanism and a change in underlying preferences; and about the conditions under which a future self can be said to have been genuinely constrained rather than merely predicted.

Then set out the precise disagreements in wording each side accepts: whether adopting the commitment procedure is an exercise of rational autonomy or a form of self-alienation; whether the post-rescue agent who cannot default remains fully an agent or has become a kind of automaton; and whether the rational assessment of the prior decision depends solely on prior preferences or also on the normative status of the resulting psychological state.

Identify the cruxes. I suggest two: first, whether rational agency requires that one's future reasons remain transparent to one's present deliberative perspective; second, whether a reason that exists solely in virtue of a prior commitment counts as a genuine reason for the later self, or only as a causal residue.

For each crux, specify a concrete case or argument both sides agree is relevant—such as the analogy to democratic precommitment, or the case of an agent who takes an irreversible intervention that eliminates the capacity to reconsider promises—and record each side's advance prediction about what the case shows.

Conclude by stating whether any portion of the dispute is merely verbal, identifying which portion and explaining why, and summarize the minimal conditions each camp says must be met for the other to concede.
