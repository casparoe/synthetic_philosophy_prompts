I am drafting a problem-set for my graduate seminar on just war theory and the moral equality of combatants, and I want a case that requires more than reciting Walzer's symmetry thesis or McMahan's revisionist account. Here is the track record of rulings by a hypothetical International Military Ethics Tribunal, with cases drawn from State Alpha (an objectively unjust aggressor) and State Beta (an objectively just defender).

Ruling 1: The tribunal acquitted a conscripted soldier from Alpha who killed Beta soldiers. He had been raised in an epistemically isolated environment with no access to dissent, sincerely believed Alpha's propaganda, and had no practical option to refuse service.

Ruling 2: The tribunal acquitted a volunteer soldier from Beta who killed Alpha soldiers, despite fighting primarily for pay and expressing private indifference to Beta's cause.

Ruling 3: The tribunal convicted a mercenary from Alpha who fought for high pay, had regular access to international media, expressed private doubts about Alpha's cause, and killed Beta soldiers.

Ruling 4: The tribunal acquitted a mercenary from Beta who fought for high pay, held no personal belief in Beta's cause, and killed Alpha soldiers in defensive positions.

Ruling 5: The tribunal convicted a senior Alpha commander who designed the epistemic isolation system and ordered offensive operations, even though he claimed he was coerced by the regime.

Now consider this new case: A volunteer from Alpha was raised in the same epistemically isolated conditions as in Ruling 1, but later obtained smuggled evidence that Alpha's cause was unjust. He continued fighting because his family was held hostage by the regime—a credible and immediate threat—and in that context killed a Beta soldier. He privately acknowledged the injustice but acted under severe structural duress.

If the tribunal wishes to remain consistent with its own precedents, what should it decide? Identify the closest analog among Rulings 1–5 and explain whether the precedents genuinely determine the outcome. Articulate at least two distinct principles—one closer to the moral equality of combatants and one closer to revisionist asymmetry—that are jointly consistent with all five rulings but diverge on this case. For each principle, state the predicted verdict and the reasoning. Give your best-guess prediction, with explicit justification for preferring one principle, and be candid about whether the record underdetermines the decision. Finally, specify one further hypothetical case that would discriminate between the two principles, explaining precisely how each principle would rule on it and why that ruling exposes the underlying logic.
