I'm preparing a feature on how philosophers of consciousness classify temporal distortions in subjective experience, and I've been tracking the judgments of a cognitive scientist I interviewed whose work spans perceptual psychology and philosophy of mind. The piece turns on a distinction—familiar from discussions of Daniel Dennett's multiple drafts model—between what has been called Orwellian revision, in which an experiential record is retrospectively rewritten after the fact, and Stalinesque revision, in which false details are fabricated into the perceptual stream from the outset, comparable to airbrushed photographs versus altered archives.

Her track record across standard cases is consistent but not uniquely determined. In the classic color phi phenomenon, where a green dot followed by a red dot at a different location is experienced as a single object continuously changing hue mid-flight, she classifies the illusion as Stalinesque: the brain constructs the color transition into the present draft rather than altering a stored memory of the initial dot. For Benjamin Libet's readiness-potential experiments, in which neural preparation precedes the reported moment of conscious decision, she assigns Orwellian status: the subjective timestamp is retrospectively aligned with the earlier neural event, revising the temporal narrative in recollection. She treats the cutaneous rabbit illusion—discrete taps on the arm experienced as a smooth hopping motion—as Stalinesque, arguing that the spatial trajectory is built into the percept itself. By contrast, post-hypnotic confabulation, where a subject invents a reason for an action actually triggered by suggestion, she judges Orwellian, since the explanatory narrative is inserted into memory after the behavior has occurred. In standard change-blindness paradigms she denies either mechanism operates, holding that the missing information was never incorporated into any draft.

The difficulty is that at least two interpretive rules accommodate this pattern equally well. One rule distinguishes by content domain: revisions to perceptual qualities such as color, motion, and form are Stalinesque, whereas revisions to causal, temporal, or narrative attributions are Orwellian. Another rule distinguishes by temporal direction: if a later cognitive signal is used to enrich an earlier percept, the result is Stalinesque; if an earlier event is made to align with a later report, it is Orwellian. A third possibility turns on resistance to correction: Stalinesque revisions are those maintained even under immediate probing, while Orwellian ones only emerge in delayed retrospective reports.

My article needs to address a new case described in a recent predictive-processing seminar but not yet widely discussed in the philosophical literature, which the researchers term anticipatory semantic shadowing. A subject is shown an ambiguous bistable figure—interpretable either as a duck or as a rabbit—followed 250 milliseconds later by a subliminal auditory prime that biases interpretation toward one reading. Neuroimaging indicates that auditory processing occurs after initial visual encoding but before conscious report. Crucially, subjects not only report seeing the biased interpretation from the very onset of the display, but they also resist immediate correction when shown the unprimed image, insisting that the duck or rabbit was present all along. Given her precedents, I need to predict whether she will classify this as Orwellian or Stalinesque, identify which of the candidate rules—content-domain, temporal-direction, or counter-evidence availability—actually governs her verdicts, explain which rule best fits her overall pattern and why, and propose a further hypothetical case—perhaps a purely temporal misalignment without qualitative content, or a qualitative distortion that collapses under immediate questioning—that would decisively adjudicate between them.
