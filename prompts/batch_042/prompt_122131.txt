I'm preparing the capstone session for a seminar of advanced philosophy undergraduates who have spent the term on traditional epistemology—Gettier problems, reliabilism, basic conditioning on evidence—but have never seriously encountered meta-cognitive defeat or the possibility of rationally divided belief. I want a seventy-minute teaching plan, not a lecture script, for introducing how higher-order evidence relates to epistemic akrasia: specifically, whether it can ever be rational to retain a first-order belief (for instance, that a particular logical inference is valid) while simultaneously accepting higher-order evidence that one's present reasoning faculties are unreliable in this domain. The students arrive thinking that "believing you are wrong" automatically cancels the original belief, and that any retention is just stubbornness or confusion.

Please lay out the dependency order: which conceptual distinction must be secured before they can even understand what akrasia is in this context—such as the separation between object-level evaluation and meta-level tracking—and which idea must come only after they see that higher-order evidence is not merely additional first-order evidence. Choose one carrying example that can sustain the entire session; it should not be the familiar peer-disagreement scenario they have seen elsewhere, but rather something involving direct self-monitoring of reasoning quality, and you should specify what that example must possess (the first-order belief must remain traceable step-by-step) and what it must lack (it should not allow the student to dismiss the belief as a vague hunch). Then map the predictable misconceptions: that accepting higher-order defeat necessarily requires abandoning the first-order belief, that epistemic akrasia is simply irrationality by another name, and that being able to "check one's work" step-by-step always dissolves the higher-order worry. For each, indicate precisely where in the sequence to head it off—before introducing the example, during the analysis of the example, or after drawing the formal distinction.

Finally, propose one diagnostic question for the end that reveals whether they have grasped the specific tension rather than just repeating that "sometimes you rationally keep believing despite doubts." Also state clearly what should be left out at this level—perhaps formal modeling of self-locating conditionalization, or specific calibrationist frameworks—and say so explicitly, so I do not accidentally drift into material that requires tools they do not have.
