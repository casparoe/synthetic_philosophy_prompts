before i commit to any view on advance directives and whether the earlier self gets to boss around the later one, i want the full spec. write the desiderata a theory of diachronic medical authorty has to meet: which cases it must get right (gradual dementia vs sudden injury, the patient who said 'no tubes' but now seems happy, the case where future values are genuinely different not just mistaken), which distinctions it can't collapse (normative authority vs predictive accuracy, legal bindingness vs moral weight, partial vs total value change), and what it can't contradict about how people actually revise preferences. flag which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just liberal or conservative intuitions wearing a mask. there's also this symmetry thing people ignore: if past-self authority works because they had epistemic priority about 'what matters,' don't we have to let past selves veto present choices outside medicine? say whether that breaks the list or is just a bullet to bite. briefly score precedent-autonomy vs present-best-interest against it, but don't waste space on a bibliography.
