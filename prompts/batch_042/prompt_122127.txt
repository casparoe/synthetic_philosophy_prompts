A philosophy blog I read recently summarises Bernard Williams's integrity objection, including the case he discusses involving one thought too many, in the following way: Williams holds that utilitarianism alienates agents from their ground projects by treating every personal commitment—whether parenthood, artistic vocation, or friendship—as fungible with any other source of welfare. If saving a stranger at negligible cost to one's own child produces greater aggregate wellbeing, then utilitarianism demands that one save the stranger, which shows that the parental role carries no special normative weight. In the drowning-wife scenario, a husband who rescues his spouse only after confirming that this maximises overall utility has already corrupted his attachment, because genuine love requires acting without that kind of calculation. Williams extends this to argue that utilitarianism imposes an impossible burden of negative responsibility, making agents accountable for every harm they fail to prevent, so that no life organised around specific projects can remain intact. Consequently, he rejects not only act-utilitarianism but any moral theory that permits agent-neutral trade-offs between personal commitments and aggregate outcomes, since all such theories reproduce the same alienation. Is this summary fair? Please examine it claim by claim, distinguishing accurate interpretation from common oversimplification and from direct misrepresentation. In particular, does Williams's appeal to negative responsibility entail that agent-neutral reasons are illegitimate in every domain, or is his target more narrowly the totalising demand that an agent always be ready to substitute her projects? And does the one-thought-too-many example demonstrate that consequential reasoning is always inappropriate within intimate relationships, or does it instead reveal something more restricted about the agent's motivational structure?
