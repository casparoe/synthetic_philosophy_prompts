I am delivering the opening remarks—approximately ten minutes—at an interdisciplinary workshop on state sovereignty and armed conflict, with an audience split between Hegel scholars, international lawyers, and practitioners of conflict resolution. I want the remarks to do genuine philosophical work rather than serve as a mere welcome. Take Hegel's claim that war is not a contingent calamity for the state but carries a specific ethical and structural function: it preserves state sovereignty and prevents civic life from collapsing into mere private interest, which he ties to the state as the actuality of ethical life. Render this accurately for the scholars—without citing texts, giving page references, or providing a bibliography—while translating it clearly for the non-philosophers. The central structural task is to use this framework to pose a question about our present: if that ethical function of war was inseparable from the sovereignty of the individual state, and if global governance structures now dilute that sovereignty, can the function be transformed rather than lost? Specifically, frame the workshop's theme by asking whether modern citizens can perform an analogous ethical act—risking particular interest for a universal that transcends the state—within international institutions, or whether the decline of state-centered sovereignty entails the loss of the very mechanism that, on Hegel's account, kept ethical life vital. Do not let the remarks become an apology for violence; rather, let the philosophical idea shape what the workshop seeks to understand about sovereignty, collective risk, and civic identity today. Write in a formal, polished register appropriate to a philosophy professor addressing a mixed academic and professional audience.
