I don't have any formal training in philosophy, but I've been thinking on my own about a question that keeps bothering me: why can I feel real grief and fear for people I know are entirely invented, while actual tragedies happening far away often leave me strangely untouched? The explanations I have come across seem too neat—something about temporarily setting belief aside—but my own experience doesn't feel like flipping a switch. Instead, I have started to think that the mind might maintain two separate accounting ledgers for reality: one cognitive, which tracks what is actually the case, and one affective, which processes emotional significance according to its own rules. Fiction, on my view, is the rare situation where these two ledgers are deliberately allowed to diverge, so that the emotional ledger can register full-scale responses to events that the cognitive ledger has already marked as non-actual. I think of it as a kind of double-bookkeeping, though I am sure there is a proper philosophical name for it that I do not know.

At first, she understands these recordings exactly as she should: reconstructed data with no present causal power. But gradually she notices that her emotional responses—grief, moral guilt, even romantic attachment—are being processed with the same weight and urgency as responses to her own lived life, even though her cognitive understanding remains intact. The story should trace the precise moment when she realizes her affective ledger is writing commitments that her cognitive ledger refuses to authorize, and it should show her confronting whether to disable the archive's emotional-access protocols or to accept that her emotional self has been operating under a different ontology all along. Please avoid a straightforward moral ending; I want the choice left genuinely ambiguous, and I want the emotional mechanics to stay specific to this decoupling of belief and affect rather than drifting into a standard story about artificial sentience.

I am not looking for an essay disguised as fiction; I want the idea of double-bookkeeping to emerge through the protagonist's actions and her failed attempts to reconcile the two ledgers, not through explicit philosophical dialogue.
