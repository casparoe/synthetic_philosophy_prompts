Question: Some philosophers distinguish "epistemic fidelity" in historical drama—the obligation to get verifiable facts right—from "axiological fidelity," the obligation to represent a dead subject's own value-structure rather than projecting modern values onto them. Is axiological fidelity an independent moral obligation, or does it collapse into epistemic fidelity plus audience sympathy? Assess whether the dead can suffer a distinctive representational harm, and whether a dramatist's ignorance of the subject's values excuses inventing them.

Student answer: Axiological fidelity is genuinely independent. Epistemic fidelity governs only chronology, documented dialogue, and actions; axiological fidelity governs whether we render the practical reasoning—the values that structured those actions—rather than substituting our own. Portraying a principled pacifist as motivated by cowardice violates axiological fidelity even if every date and event is accurate. The dead can suffer a distinctive representational harm: though they lack sentience, the narrative continuity of their commitments can be corrupted, and this corruption affects not merely audience sympathy but the integrity of the historical record itself. Because such harm is essentially about false belief in the audience, axiological fidelity ultimately reduces to a stricter form of epistemic duty about internal states, not a separate moral category. Nevertheless, if a dramatist genuinely cannot access the subject's values—because archives are silent or letters are lost—the obligation is fully suspended, since one cannot be held responsible for what is permanently unknowable.
