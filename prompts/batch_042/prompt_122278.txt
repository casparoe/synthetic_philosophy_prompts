After a long and somewhat heated discussion last night with a close friend in my ethics seminar—she defends a suffering-focused consequentialism that denies Benatar’s asymmetry but assigns suffering a steep, possibly convex moral weight, while I find myself drawn to a stricter axiological negative-utilitarian framing in which suffering is intrinsically bad, non-suffering is neutral, and reducing suffering holds absolute or near-absolute priority—we realized we were talking past each other on whether the suffering-happiness asymmetry is intrinsic or merely instrumental, and on whether any threshold of well-being could ever justify the creation or preservation of severe suffering. Rather than continue informally, I want you to draft the adversarial-collaboration document we should have produced: a joint statement that a committed axiological negative utilitarian and a committed suffering-focused consequentialist could both sign in good faith.

The document should open with a numbered section of agreed claims, phrased precisely enough that neither party feels misrepresented: include agreement on the formal structure of suffering-weighted evaluation, specifically on whether it operates via lexical ordering or through a steep convex weighting function; on the non-identity problem as applied to future-suffering-prevention policies that do not alter who comes to exist; on the moral status of wild-animal suffering and of artificial-suffering risks; and on the conditions under which empirical evidence about welfare measurement could in principle revise either side’s weighting. It should then present a disagreements table stating, in wording each camp would accept for its own view, exactly where they still diverge: whether the asymmetry between suffering and well-being is axiological or deontic; whether there exists any scenario in which happiness could morally offset suffering; and whether suffering-reduction policies are subject to a distinct “moral risk asymmetry” that does not apply symmetrically to well-being-creation policies. Next, identify the cruxes—particularly whether suffering-dominance collapses into lexicality under conditions of moral uncertainty, and whether adaptive preferences formed within suffering-reduction interventions undermine the reliability of welfare comparisons—and for each crux specify a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it (for example, a population-ethics scenario comparing a very large population with minimal but nonzero suffering against a smaller population with high well-being), together with each side’s explicit advance prediction about that case. Finally, include a brief assessment of whether any portion of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive, especially around the distinction between describing non-suffering as “not bad” versus “good,” and note whether settling the formal-weighting question would dissolve the practical disagreement.

In drafting this, please separate what is settled in the philosophy of population ethics and welfare evaluation from what remains genuinely contested among informed practitioners, and do not present either camp’s view—especially the claim that suffering carries lexical priority—as the consensus position. Write formally and polished, as this is intended for a rigorous philosophical dataset. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific authors or papers; if you invoke a formal feature such as the non-identity problem, describe it in the text rather than attributing it by name. Avoid relying on obscure technical results or theorists not part of general philosophical literacy, though you may inject conceptual distinctions—such as the lexical-versus-convex weighting distinction and the notion of moral risk asymmetry in suffering-reduction policy—to keep the prompt substantive and away from over-familiar ground.
