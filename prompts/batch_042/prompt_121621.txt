Consider the track record of a metaphysician of biology who has ruled, case by case, on whether a counterfactual reproductive history would have yielded the same person. Her verdicts are these. First, if the sperm that actually fertilized the egg had been a different sperm from the same father, the result would not have been her. Second, if the egg had been a different egg from the same mother, the result would not have been her. Third, if that exact sperm and egg had been gestated in a different woman's uterus, she would still be the result. Fourth, if her parents had conceived a child a year earlier with different gametes altogether, that child is her sibling, not her. Fifth, if the zygote formed from her actual gametes had been used for somatic-cell nuclear transfer into an enucleated donor egg, producing a genetic clone, that clone would not be her. Sixth, if the same fertilization had occurred in an artificial womb with no change to the gametes, she would still be the result.

The new case is mitochondrial replacement therapy applied to her own conception: her mother's nuclear DNA is transferred into an enucleated donor egg that supplies only cytoplasm and mitochondria, and that reconstructed egg is then fertilized by her father's sperm. The child therefore carries her parents' nuclear genome and results from that specific sperm meeting that reconstructed egg, but every mitochondrion and all cytoplasmic machinery derive from the donor.

Predict her verdict on whether that child is her. Then identify at least two general principles that jointly accommodate verdicts one through six but diverge on this seventh case; for each principle, state what it predicts here. Give your best guess at which principle she actually employs, with the reasoning that makes it the closer fit to her track record, and name the closest analog among her earlier rulings. Finally, propose one further counterfactual—different from the seven above—on which the two principles would disagree, explaining why it serves as a decisive test.
