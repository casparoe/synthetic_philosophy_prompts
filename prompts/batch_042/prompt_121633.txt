I'm a PhD student in philosophy of medicine and I sit on the ethics advisory board for our university's reproductive health partnership — we're a small group of philosophers, clinicians and one legal person who advise on pilot schemes. We've got to circulate a written opinion before our meeting on 12 March, and I need help drafting it. The case is a proposed "welfare-weighted conception counselling" programme: the partnership wants to allocate counselling and screening resources asymetrically, putting heavy funding into support for couples at risk of having a child with severe suffering, but offering essentially nothing to couples whose projected child-welfare scores sit well above the threshold. The justification is the procreation asymmetry — the idea that we have strong moral reasons not to bring about suffering lives, but no corresponding positive reason to bring about extra happy ones. But they've pushed this further into institutional design: they claim that because potential happy lives have no positive moral claim on us, the clinic should be actively neutral (even dismissive) towards requests for "optimisation" support, and they've built a budget cutoff so that if you're above the line you recieve zero access to subsidised screening, even for moderate, non-severe conditions.

Some board members think this is just consistency; others think scaling the asymmetry up to resource allocation turns potential people into entities with only "negative standing" — they can block funding by being at risk, but can't attract it by being possible — which feels like a category mistake. There's also a practical worry about adaptive preferences near the cutoff. I want the memo to recommend a concrete course of action — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with a clear time frame (e.g., halt the pilot for six months pending review, or launch a restricted one-year trial with safeguards). Don't just lay out the pros and cons and leave us hanging; take a definite position and defend it, including an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the other side. The reasoning should be accessible to clinicians, not just philosophers, and please don't treat the asymmetry as a slogan — engage with whether it actually justifies institutional neutrality in this specific way. Also, I'm personally sceptical that the asymmetry scales like this, but I want that worry addressed seriously rather than dismissed. One small thing: please make sure it's reasoning holds together for people who aren't specialists — I keep seeing drafts that assume everyone knows the literature.
