I am revising a chapter I wrote some years ago on the ontology of space, and in sharpening my treatment of Newton's bucket I have constructed an argument that now strikes me as suspicious—its premises seem individually defensible, yet they appear to entail that chemical reaction rates in a sealed laboratory depend on the distribution of distant galaxies. I would like help locating the error and understanding what I must give up to avoid it.

Here is the argument, step by step.

(1) Spatial relationism holds that every spatial fact reduces to actual relations among material bodies; there is no container independent of them.

(2) Newton's rotating bucket produces a concave water surface indicating real rotational motion, even in a hypothetical scenario where no other bodies are nearby.

(3) The relationist can preserve (1) only by appealing to distant matter: the bucket rotates relative to the fixed stars, or to the total cosmic mass distribution.

(4) Consequently, on relationism the local shape of the water is not explained by any feature of the bucket or water themselves, but solely by the global configuration of bodies far away.

(5) By parallel reasoning, the inertial force I feel when a vehicle suddenly decelerates is not produced by any local interaction, but by my change of motion relative to the universal mass distribution.

(6) Therefore relationism entails that every local dynamical effect is globally determined by the state of the entire universe.

(7) Modern physics, however, assumes local autonomy: an experimenter in a sealed laboratory can determine her state of motion with local instruments—accelerometers, gyroscopes—and can formulate and test local laws without knowing the cosmic distribution of matter.

(8) If local autonomy requires causal independence from distant bodies, then relationism violates it.

(9) By parity of reasoning, if mechanical inertia is globally determined, then chemical bond strengths, nuclear decay rates, and biological processes in that same laboratory must also vary with the cosmic arrangement of matter.

(10) That consequence is plainly false.

(11) Hence relationism is false, and space is an absolute substantival container.

My intuition is that something slips between steps (6) and (8)—perhaps an equivocation between ontological determination and causal interaction, or between epistemic accessibility and metaphysical independence—but I cannot precisely identify the mistake. Which step should I reject? Why does it initially seem plausible, and what does giving it up cost the relationist? If I want to preserve both the relationist ontology and the local autonomy of physics, which further case—say, a rotating hollow shell in an otherwise empty universe, or a laboratory isolated in a void—would best reveal whether my rescue works?
