I've been arguing with my freind about whether reading other people's lives actually makes you morally better, or if it's just a fancy kind of entertainment. I'm takeing this course on ancient biography and we got onto Plutarch — she thinks his paired lives are basically hagiograpy with better PR, which made me wonder what actually distinguishes a biography that teaches from one that doesn't.

Here are my gut verdicts. Plutarch pairing a Greek and Roman figure to highlight one virtue like continence: that works, you can see the pattern across both lives and it sticks with you. A modern saint's life where every failure gets rewritten as hidden virtue: useless, maybe harmful. A biography of someone admirable in one domain but a disaster in another — huge political courage but brutal to family — I think that teaches something specific, but only if the biography doesn't smooth over the contradiction. Reading about someone who made the exact mistake you keep making, but in a totally different historical context: sometimes it clicks, sometimes it feels like comparing apples and oranges, and I keep flip-floping on which. Then there's this case I'm stuck on: a deliberately negative portrait where the author clearly wants to tear the subject down, yet the subject still comes across as kind of impressive despite the author's best efforts. Does that teach more or less than straight hagiography?

I'm guessing the principle has something to do with whether the biography preserves enough particularity that you can actually map the virtue onto your own situation, but I can't make it cover the negative-portrait case without making the principle too complicated. There's also this weird format my professor mentioned, a shadow biography where you write about a real person but frame every decision through a virtue they never possessed — what would my account say about that? And is there a difference between reading for imitation versus reading for some other kind of moral orientation? If there's a coherent principle here, I'd like it spelled out with the case that motivates each clause, plus an honest admission of which of my verdicts it has to drop, and what it predicts for an autobiography where the author clearly doesn't understand their own worst motives but describes them in enough detail that you can see them yourself.
