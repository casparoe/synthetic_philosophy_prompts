As a novelist drafting a literary manuscript about the hidden architecture of economic knowledge, I am trying to translate the socialist calculation debate—particularly Mises’s argument that rational allocation requires market prices generated through private exchange, Hayek’s insistence on the dispersion of tacit, contextual knowledge that no central authority can fully gather, and Lange’s attempt to preserve socialist planning through a trial-and-error pricing mechanism—into a fictional world that does not read like a textbook allegory. I have conceived a setting called Oikos, an isolated archipelago society where production is directed by a central bureau that substitutes digital computation with a ritualized acoustic system: citizens gather in resonant halls and vocalize their consumption patterns from the prior cycle, creating collective echo-chambers that generate pseudo-prices through harmonic convergence rather than exchange. In this system, goods whose value depends on embodied, inarticulate skill—such as the tuning of wooden instruments or the curing of specific marine resins—produce no clear vocal signal, so the bureau records them as surplus and reduces their production, even as practitioners know they are becoming scarce.

I want the narrative to center on an instrument-maker who recognizes that her craft knowledge is precisely the kind of dispersed, practical information that cannot survive translation into the echo-chamber without being distorted or flattened. The dramatic tension should turn on whether her awareness of this gap is itself a form of knowledge that can be communicated to the bureau—perhaps through direct speech, or perhaps only through the silent, material persistence of her work—or whether articulating it within the acoustic mechanism necessarily destroys its specificity, much as some philosophers of the debate worried that formalizing knowledge removes its functional role. Could you write a short story, in a tone of restrained literary realism with speculative elements, that stages this dilemma through concrete scenes—showing the hall’s rituals, the instrument-maker’s workshop, and a single moment of decision—without naming Mises, Hayek, or Lange explicitly, but allowing their respective positions to structure the social logic and the characters’ inner conflicts? I am especially interested in whether the story can suggest, through its form rather than its dialogue, that some economic truths might be genuinely incommunicable to the planning apparatus without thereby being reduced to mere subjective preference.
