I'm preparing notes for a graduate seminar on explanation in the social sciences, and I've become dissatisfied with the usual dialectic between methodological individualism and holism. Rather than asking whether social structures have autonomous causal powers or are reducible to individual actions, I want to propose a different framing, but I'm unsure whether it survives scrutiny. The standard individualist position, as I understand it, insists that all social explanations must ultimately bottom out in beliefs, preferences, and interactions of individuals; the holist position responds that macro-level entities—institutions, norms, economic cycles—exhibit patterns that are not predictable from micro-level descriptions alone and therefore require autonomous explanatory treatment. In my draft argument, I replace the reducibility question with a criterion of predictive stability across scale transitions: an explanation succeeds when its predictions remain invariant whether we model the system agent-by-agent, in meso-scale clusters, or through aggregate dynamics, and it fails precisely at the scale where that invariance breaks down, which marks the entry point of a genuinely distinct explanatory level. I apply this to the formation of speculative bubbles: individualist rational-expectations models explain pre-bubble behavior well, holist feedback-loop models explain the collective runaway, but only a scale-transition account explains why identical agents generate price stability under one regulatory architecture and explosive instability under another, because the relevant change is in the predictive mapping between scales, not in either level taken alone. My worry is that this "predictive stability" criterion might collapse into a form of pragmatic instrumentalism that both camps would reject—merely selecting models by convenience rather than identifying real explanatory structures. Additionally, note where my reasoning is most likely to fail if the criterion is applied outside economics, such as to normative or intentional social phenomena.
