You are an expert in moral philosophy and institutional reform strategy. Before constructing the procedure, briefly brainstorm how argument-driven, interest-driven, and honor/status-driven mechanisms might interact in a single norm-collapse event, noting where they reinforce or block one another.

Then design a reusable decision procedure—presented as an ordered checklist with explicit stopping rules and judgment-call nodes—that an NGO strategist could run to assess whether a harmful but entrenched social norm is ripe for rapid moral collapse rather than gradual erosion. The procedure should operationalize the tension between rationalist argument-driven accounts (associated with Appiah's analysis of honor codes and moral revolutions), material-interest explanations, and Bernard Williams-inspired skepticism that moral change is ever primarily rational, instead emphasizing shifts in honor, prestige, and social visibility. For each stage, specify: the question to ask, what counts as sufficient evidence, where the procedure outputs "not ripe—stop," and where it must hand off to human judgment because the issue is evaluative rather than empirical.

In constructing this, inject and operationalize the synthetic concept of "evaluative audience fragmentation": the idea that honor-based norms collapse not when elites change private beliefs, but when the social audience that confers prestige splits into incompatible evaluative communities, making continued adherence costly in status terms rather than in moral ones. Make this a measurable node in your procedure.

Apply the procedure to two cases:
1. The historical decline of dueling among European elites in the nineteenth century.
2. A hypothetical contemporary campaign against a workplace discrimination practice currently protected by local prestige structures—specifically, the expectation that senior managers publicly humiliate junior staff as a display of authority.

Separate what is settled in the historical and philosophical literature from what remains genuinely contested among specialists. In particular, do not present as consensus the claim that one mechanism (argument, interest, or honor) always dominates, or that evaluative audience fragmentation is an established empirical law rather than a theoretical construct. Flag every point where your procedure relies on a contested premise, and think carefully about whether the checklist can genuinely distinguish empirical from normative judgments.
