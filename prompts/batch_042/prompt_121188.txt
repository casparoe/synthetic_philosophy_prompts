I'm preparing notes for a seminar on collective identity, and I want to defend a somewhat unfashionable middle position that avoids both cultural essentialism and the stronger constructivist denial of any stable collective patterns. I think the notion of national character is incoherent if understood as a shared psychological profile distributed across citizens—something like a collective personality trait that explains individual behavior. But I also think it can be salvaged if we reconceive it as what I will call a modal institutional temperament: a stable, counterfactual-sensitive pattern in how a society's formal and informal institutions respond to coordination problems, where the "character" is not inside anyone's head but in the feedback loops between law, custom, and infrastructure. On this view, saying that a particular society is rule-bound or conflict-averse is not a claim about the mean conscientiousness of its citizens, but a claim about the resilience of certain bureaucratic or deliberative norms under institutional perturbation—testable, in principle, by stress-tests of the system rather than by surveys of individuals.

Please argue that generalizing about peoples is ethically and epistemically acceptable precisely when the generalization is cast in this institutional-modal form, and unacceptable when it collapses back into psychological essentialism. Your argument should address the following tension: if institutional temperaments are real but causally depend on millions of individual actions, doesn't any institutional-level claim still license, in practice, predictions about individuals? Resolve this by showing that modal claims have a different logical form—specifically, that they assert dispositions of the collective system rather than statistical distributions over members, and that this distinction blocks the slide from "this society tends toward X" to "this person is likely X." You need not invoke specific named theories or texts; construct the argument from the conceptual machinery sketched above, and make it rigorous enough that someone skeptical of both essentialism and radical constructivism would find the middle ground defensible.
