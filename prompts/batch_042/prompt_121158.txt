I've been reflecting, with no formal philosophical training, on the distinction between knowing how to do something and knowing that something is true—say, knowing how to keep one's balance on ice versus knowing that friction coefficients drop below a certain threshold. The standard framing—practical versus theoretical—feels too neat, and I want to think more clearly about where the real tensions lie. If I had only a few years of serious, unsponsored attention to devote to this, and I wanted my effort to matter for both personal understanding and for how we think about training people or designing systems, which open conceptual questions or lines of implication should I prioritize, and in what order?

I'm especially torn among three directions, though I'm open to others: first, whether knowing-how requires a particular kind of error-correction—failure of the body or the skill itself—rather than contradiction by evidence, and whether that means an entity incapable of physical breakdown could genuinely possess it; second, whether the intellectualist view, that knowing-how is just knowing-that arranged for action, actually dissolves the distinction or merely pushes the mystery into how propositions become embodied; and third, whether resolving this matters practically for how we design education or artificial skill, or whether the practical implications are mostly overrated. 

Please defend a ranking: for each line of work, explain why it is upstream or downstream of the others, how tractable it is for someone reasoning without access to labs or specialized archives, and what kind of conceptual result in the next few years would cause you to reorder the priorities. I am less interested in a survey of every named position than in a defended agenda—what deserves the years, what the criteria are, and where limited attention should actually go.
