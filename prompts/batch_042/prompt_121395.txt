As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I am drafting an essay that treats the phrase "Western values" not as a stable philosophical taxonomy but as what sociologists of science call a boundary object—a concept that holds together different social groups precisely because it means something different to each of them. In diplomatic speeches, it typically signals liberal democratic norms; in domestic culture-war rhetoric, it often denotes a particular constellation of religious, gender, and economic traditions; in academic comparative ethics, it is sometimes reduced to a list of institutional practices. My central claim is that attempts to fix its content—rule of law, individual autonomy, secular governance, market capitalism—inevitably become circular: a value counts as Western when Western political actors invoke it, and those actors invoke it because they wish to present it as Western. I further argue that the category is destabilized by internal contradictions, such as the simultaneous appeal to universal human rights and to culturally specific traditions of hierarchy and order. Before publication, I want to stress-test this thesis. What would genuinely count against it? Specifically: is there a non-circular, historically stable core that survives across diplomatic, domestic, and philosophical usages? Could the boundary-object framing be dismissed as a trivial observation about all political vocabulary? And does my emphasis on circular selection criteria overlook robust functional or institutional explanations for why certain values cluster geographically—explanations that do not depend on rhetorical self-definition? Please rank these potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish those that proponents of the "Western values" category would likely concede from those they would reject as missing the point, and write your response in American English.
