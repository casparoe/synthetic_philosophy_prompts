Read Peter Shaffer's Amadeus—both the play and its well-known film adaptation—through the problem of what moral obligations, if any, we have toward the dead when we dramatize their lives. The work invents Salieri's lethal envy and frames Mozart's death as possible poisoning, fabrications that have largely overwritten popular historical memory. Does this constitute a wrong against Mozart or Salieri themselves—perhaps a form of posthumous representational harm—or is the harm only indirect, residing in the audience's false beliefs or in the erosion of public historical understanding? I'd like a reading that takes both the play's artistic self-awareness and the philosophical debate about wronging subjects who can no longer suffer seriously, asking whether Amadeus dramatizes the conflict between narrative license and legacy, or merely exploits it. Where must an honest critic admit that the work resists the ethical framework rather than illustrating it?
