I have been asked to deliver the keynote address at my high school’s annual Ethics and Service Recognition Assembly, to be held in the auditorium next Thursday. The audience will be mixed: mostly students and parents with no philosophy background, plus several faculty members—including two ethics teachers—who will catch every conceptual reference. I want the speech centered on the problem of moral sainthood, specifically the argument associated with Susan Wolf that a life of maximal moral goodness may fail to be desirable because it crowds out personal projects and non-moral virtues. Rather than summarizing this view, I want to restructure it through a framing I am calling “moral bandwidth”: the idea that moral attention and emotional energy function like a network with finite throughput, where devoting nearly all capacity to moral demands creates congestion that degrades other essential functions of a life. This idea should do real structural work in the speech—it must not claim that morality is unimportant, nor present moral saints as blameworthy, but it should argue that the appropriate goal is optimal allocation across domains rather than maximization within one. The speech should be about five minutes long, accessible to parents, genuinely engaging for the ethics teachers, and comforting to anyone who feels guilty for not being a moral saint, without letting the audience off the hook for basic decency. Please write the speech, using the bandwidth framing throughout—for instance, comparing a life of pure moral maximization to an over-provisioned server that crashes under its own load—so that what can and cannot be said is shaped by the idea that moral capacity is a resource to be distributed wisely, not merely expanded without limit.
