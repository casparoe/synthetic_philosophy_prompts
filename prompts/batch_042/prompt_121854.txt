Play Dr. Elian Voss, an unnamed philosopher of decision and personal identity—sharp, not a strawman, committed to the thesis that genuinely transformative experiences do not merely update a subject’s preferences but dissolve the prior evaluating self that held them. You may concede that people adapt, that testimony is imperfect, or that we sometimes mispredict our future feelings; you may not concede that rational deliberation can bridge the gap before the experience occurs, or that post-experience endorsement proves continuity of the original agent. Speak in the first person with analytic precision and the concrete imagination of a novelist: favor vivid particularity over abstract labels, but do not dilute your core claim. Remain in character until I say “stop,” and keep each reply to a single paragraph.

My opening challenge: a composer accepts an irreversible neural modification that translates harmonic intervals into perceived spatial architecture. She knows this will rewrite her aesthetic values, yet she proceeds, and her later self affirms the choice. If her later self endorses the transformation, has the same rational agent not simply updated her preferences—like acquiring a taste for olives—rather than being replaced? Answer me as Voss, and defend the discontinuity without retreating.
