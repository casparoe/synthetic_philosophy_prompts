I'm in a grad seminar on epistomology and we got sidetracked into this weird argument about Nozick's tracking conditions and a hypothetical therapy my friend made up called "mnemonic grafting" — it's basically a targeted false-memory implat that leaves your reasoning intact but makes you think your belief came from normal experience. Anyway here's the argument he laid out and I genuinely can't find the crack. (1) On Nozick's tracking theory, S knows p only if S's belief tracks p: in nearby worlds where p is false, S doesn't believe p. (2) Now take any ordinary empirical belief, say that Paris is in France, call it P. (3) For me to actually know P, I need to be in a position where my belief wasn't produced by grafting — call that G(P). (4) But in the nearest worlds where G(P) is false, the grafting procedure is designed specifically to suppress any awareness of grafting, so I still believe G(P) is true and I still believe P. (5) So G(P) fails the tracking condition: if it were false, I'd still believe it. (6) Therefore I don't know G(P). (7) And if I don't know G(P), then I can't know P either, since knowing P requires being able to rul out that I was grafted. (8) The same goes for every empirical belief I have. (9) So tracking conditions, plus the reasonable idea that knowing something requires knowing it wasn't grafted, imply I know basically nothing about the world. (10) That's clearly wrong. (11) So either tracking is wrong or that "requires knowing" step is wrong.

My friend says the problem is in (7) and it's just denying closure, but I want to hang onto tracking if I can. Where exactly does this slip? Is there a hidden modal slide between (4) and (5), or is (3) doing sneaky work by treating "nearest worlds" the same way for P and for G(P)? I feel like "nearest worlds" is being used differently in different steps but I can't pin it, and I'd rather not give up tracking if the cost is just accepting that knowledge doesn't close over this kind of meta-belief.
