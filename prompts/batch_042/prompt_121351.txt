I'm a doctoral student in epistemology and philosophy of mind working on first-person certainty, and I'm trying to decide where to concentrate my dissertation efforts on the scope and limits of Descartes's cogito argument. The standard classroom treatment treats the conclusion — that I exist as long as I think — as philosophically settled, but I think there is a live cluster of conceptual questions about exactly what epistemic work the cogito does, and I want to build a research agenda rather than write a historical commentary.

Specifically, I'm weighing four interrelated open lines of inquiry. First, there is the ontological minimalism problem: does the cogito prove only that a particular instance of thinking occurs, or does it also license the attribution of a substantive kind-nature (e.g., being a res cogitans) without smuggling in premises from elsewhere? Second, there is the temporal-extension problem: does the proof establish the existence of only a momentary subject, or does it implicitly depend on, or establish, some minimal continuity conditions across time? Third, there is the generalizability problem: can the cogito's epistemic structure — whatever makes it immune to global doubt — be extended to first-person judgments about non-cognitive states (for instance, affective or volitional states) without collapsing into circularity or losing its privileged status? Fourth, there is the interface problem with contemporary philosophy of mind: does resolving what the cogito proves have any downstream implications for debates about de se content, self-reference without identification, or the transparency of phenomenal consciousness, or are those domains conceptually insulated?

My request is not for a survey of the literature, but for a defended prioritization. Given roughly three years and a background in logic and early modern philosophy rather than empirical cognitive science, which two or three of these lines should I treat as upstream priorities? For each, I want to know: why it is tractable with conceptual and logical tools rather than requiring empirical results; how its resolution would constrain or enable the others; and what kind of conceptual result — not an empirical discovery — would cause you to revise the ranking upward or downward. I am especially interested in whether there is a hidden dependency: for example, whether settling the temporal-extension problem is a precondition for asking the generalizability question in a non-circular way, or whether the interface problem is actually a dead end that absorbs resources without altering the core epistemology.

Please rank them with explicit criteria — importance for first-person epistemology, tractability for a philosopher with my training, and downstream dependency — and tell me which one you would advise deprioritizing entirely, with the honest reason that its answer would not change much elsewhere.
