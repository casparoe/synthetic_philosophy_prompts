Analyze the post-2019 restoration of Notre-Dame de Paris as a live, institutional version of the Ship of Theseus paradox. I want a precise mapping, not a metaphor: identify what counts as the "planks"—the 13th-century oak roof frame, Viollet-le-Duc's 19th-century spire that actually collapsed, the medieval stained glass that survived, the melted lead roofing, and the hidden structural steel and fireproofing that will be inserted during reconstruction. Then apply the standard theories of diachronic identity—material continuity, spatio-temporal continuity, and formal or functional persistence—to determine what kind of object is being "restored." Crucially, note that Notre-Dame was already a palimpsest: the spire that burned was itself a 19th-century replacement, and the cathedral had been repeatedly rebuilt after fires and revolutions. So the Ship analogy already fails at the level of historical fact, because there is no original "ship" to preserve.

Next, examine the political and cultural decision to reconstruct identically (including Macron's brief proposal for a contemporary spire and its rapid abandonment). Does the public insistence on exact material replication support a naive essentialism about the building, or does the quiet acceptance of modern engineering, hidden materials, and non-visible structural changes show that what people actually value is formal and functional continuity—what metaphysicians sometimes call four-dimensional persistence of a design-plan rather than of matter?

Finally, turn the analysis back on the paradox itself: if an institution can survive the replacement of every material part, the insertion of new functional parts, and the loss of its original designer's intentions, does the Ship of Theseus still pose a problem for architectural or institutional identity, or does Notre-Dame reveal that the paradox was always a category mistake—treating a building with legal personality, sacramental function, and collective memory as if it were a mere aggregate of wood and stone? Give me a disciplined two-way account—what the framework explains about the public reaction and where it is silent, and whether the restoration debate confirms, complicates, or dissolves the material-continuity view of identity—without turning this into a history lesson or asking for citations.
