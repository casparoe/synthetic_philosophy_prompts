I'm working on the conditions for successful performative utterances in institutional and technologically mediated contexts, and I want to check whether my case judgments hang together under a single principle. Here are my verdicts.

When a duly appointed judge utters the sentencing formula in the courtroom, the defendant's legal status changes: the performative succeeds. When a child utters the same formula to a playmate, nothing institutional changes: it fails, despite the phonetic form being identical.

Now consider mediated cases. A state-authorized algorithm issues a speeding citation through an automated voice message that literally contains the phrase "You are hereby fined." My intuition is that this succeeds institutionally—the fine is valid—yet something about the performative force feels attenuated, because no human speaker is present to bear responsibility for the declaration.

Compare that to a pre-recorded video will, in which the testator states "I hereby bequeath my property to X," played posthumously and accepted by the probate court. Here I think the performative succeeds fully, even without a living speaker at the moment of institutional uptake, provided the recording was made with the appropriate authority and intent.

But then consider an AI assistant that, at a user's request, generates and sends a message saying "I sincerely apologize for the harm caused," where the user never actually felt remorse and merely wanted to smooth over a conflict. The recipient accepts the apology and the relationship is repaired. I feel that, despite the social effect, no genuine apology has occurred—there is no performative success—because the speaker (the user) lacked the requisite internal state, and the AI is merely a transmission mechanism without institutional authority of its own.

Finally, there is a case I keep revising. A smart contract automatically executes the phrase "Ownership is hereby transferred" upon verification of payment, with no human agent uttering or endorsing the statement at the moment of execution. The blockchain record updates. Is this a performative utterance at all, or just a causal mechanism with linguistic clothing? I lean toward saying it is not a genuine performative, because there is no agent who can be held to account for the declaration, yet I am unsure because the institutional effect is identical to a human-conducted transfer.

Is there a single principle—perhaps involving authorized agency, present accountability, or the alignment of internal state with institutional role—that captures all five verdicts? Or do I need separate accounts for legal, social, and algorithmic contexts? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which of my judgments it forces me to abandon (especially the smart-contract case and the automated citation), and apply it to one further case I have not mentioned: an AI mediator that, detecting mutual consent between disputing parties, formally pronounces "The dispute is hereby settled" and logs the agreement in a binding arbitration database.
