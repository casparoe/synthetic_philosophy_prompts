I am preparing the question sheet for a moderated colloquium Q&A with a philosopher—call her the author of a recent defense of “stabilized-baseline omissionism”—who argues that the moral distinction between killing and letting die tracks not bodily movement but a difference in causal-explanatory structure. On her view, killing introduces a destructive factor that disrupts an already-sustained baseline state (for example, metabolic homeostasis), whereas letting die is the failure to initiate or maintain a counterfactual intervention chain that would have overridden an independent pathological process. She distinguishes “interruption omissions” (ceasing an intervention that has become embedded in the stabilized baseline, which she treats as structurally closer to killing) from “non-initiation omissions” (withholding an intervention never integrated into the baseline, which remains pure omission). She further requires that qualifying baselines be “agent-independent in origin,” though they may be technologically mediated.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that do not ask her to restate this framework. I want the sequence to press where the literature actually presses, with each question anticipating her most likely reply and including a concrete follow-up keyed to it. Specifically, include:

- One question on the metaphysics of “causally sustained baseline,” asking whether the baseline is identified descriptively or normatively, and how the view avoids circularity in deciding which causal loops count—followed by a reply-keyed follow-up on whether a patient’s own physiological compensatory mechanisms ever constitute a “baseline” that makes withdrawal of external support a pure omission.

- One question on life-support withdrawal, taking her likely answer that withdrawal is an interruption omission because the ventilator has become part of the baseline, with a follow-up that makes it harder for her to distinguish withdrawal from killing by lethal injection—specifically asking what she says when both acts produce identical causal pathways (cessation of respiration followed by cardiac arrest) and differ only in the agent’s prior history of intervention.

- One question on triage or resource rationing, where treating patient A might require withdrawing support from patient B, forcing her to classify one omission as killing-adjacent and another as pure omission despite symmetric resource scarcity—followed by a reply that asks whether the moral difference can really turn on which patient was intubated first rather than on the present causal structure.

- One question on the moral relevance of prior commitments: if a physician has contractually promised to maintain ventilation, does that commitment transform the technological loop into an agent-dependent baseline, thereby changing withdrawal’s moral category? The follow-up should press whether this makes the killing/omission distinction depend on contingent institutional history rather than on the causal structure she originally advertised.

- At least one additional question on the “agent-independent in origin” condition, asking how she classifies cases where the baseline is maintained by a device implanted years earlier by the same clinical team—followed by a reply that asks whether temporal distance ever dissolves agent-dependence, or whether she must accept that long-term patients are structurally closer to being killed upon withdrawal than acute patients are.

Finally, conclude your response with a brief reflective note stating where your own reasoning in framing these distinctions is most vulnerable: identify the specific argument or empirical finding—perhaps about clinical attributions of causation, or about the normative loading of “baseline” selection—that would cause you to abandon the stabilized-baseline framework, and say which of the four distinctions above you think is least likely to survive that pressure.
