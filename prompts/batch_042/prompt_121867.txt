Analyze the Corrupted Blood incident in World of Warcraft—between September 13 and October 8, 2005—when a raid-only debuff intended for Hakkar the Soulflayer escaped into the general player population through infected companion pets, spreading virally across Azeroth and altering player behavior and social coordination for weeks, as a philosophical case study in the ontology of virtual harm. Do not treat it merely as a software bug: treat the plague as an emergent virtual object with causal efficacy that reached beyond server architecture into real-world psychological welfare and collective decision-making. Through the lens of virtual-world realism versus anti-realism, map precisely who or what counts as a victim, whether the harm is internal to a fictional ontology or collapses into ordinary empirical psychology, and whether virtual objects in this episode exhibit modal reality—existing in possible worlds that causally interface with ours without being reducible to them. Then turn the case back on the theory: does the incident demonstrate that virtual harms are genuinely autonomous, or does it reveal that virtual-world ontology is silent precisely where harm claims become serious? Where the framework offers no verdict, say so explicitly rather than forcing one.
