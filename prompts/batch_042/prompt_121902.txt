As a voting member of your university's Interdisciplinary Research Governance Committee, you must help decide on the "Accelerated Benchmark Initiative" (ABI) proposed by four internal labs and two industry sponsors. Under the ABI, participating labs compete to develop AI evaluation protocols, with compute credits and publication priority awarded in tranches on a first-to-publish basis. Proponents argue that competitive release sharpens innovation; critics contend the structure creates a multipolar trap: each lab individually benefits from accelerating publication, yet collectively they degrade evaluation rigor, producing benchmarks that appear authoritative but are systematically shallow.

The committee must vote by next Thursday. The options are: approve the ABI without structural changes; approve it only if bound by a mandatory "asymmetric escrow" protocol (all raw evaluation data and intermediate protocols are deposited with an independent trustee and released only after every participating lab has completed independent verification, with contractual penalties for premature disclosure or selective reporting); or reject it outright on the grounds that competitive architectures of this kind are structurally prone to races to the bottom that escrow mechanisms cannot reliably contain.

Draft the committee's formal opinion — approximately 700–900 words — recommending one option with a concrete implementation or rejection timeline. The memo should:

- Frame the decision through the lens of multipolar traps and races to the bottom, not through generic risk-benefit analysis.
- Examine whether the escrow condition genuinely resolves the coordination failure, or instead relocates it: specifically, consider whether participants might race to influence the trustee's release criteria, to game verification timelines, or to shift competitive pressure onto opaque dimensions of evaluation design.
- Treat the strongest opposing argument with intellectual honesty. If you recommend approval (with or without conditions), engage seriously with the claim that competitive markets in science correct errors faster than coordinated protocols, and that escrow governance is too brittle to survive industry pressure to dissolve it. If you recommend rejection, engage seriously with the claim that well-designed escrow mechanisms can align individual and collective incentives, and that rejecting competitive structures forecloses epistemic gains.
- State a clear bottom line — approve, approve with conditions, or reject — specifying the conditions, deadlines, and review checkpoints if conditional, and explaining what evidence or outcome would trigger a reconsideration.

Write in the direct, precise voice of a committee document suitable for circulation among members who disagree.
