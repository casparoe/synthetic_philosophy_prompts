Construct a thought experiment in a fully deterministic, classical physical system—say, a closed biosphere of moderate scale governed by continuous dynamics with every Lyapunov exponent non-positive—where an embedded agent, composed of the same matter and subject to the same laws, attempts to predict the system's macro-state. Confine the scenario to one paragraph, keep it realistic (no exotic physics, no demons, no perfect predictors), and engineer it so that the agent can reliably compute the state at time t + 1 but is structurally unable to compute the state at time t + k for any k beyond a specific bound. The failure must not come from measurement error, noise, or chaotic amplification; it must arise from the fact that executing the predictive computation for horizon H requires the agent to occupy physical configurations and expend energy that are themselves variables in the deterministic dynamics, so that at horizon H + 1 the computation's own footprint alters the very trajectory it seeks to model, rendering the prediction self-undermining rather than merely inaccurate.

After presenting the scenario, demonstrate that determinism remains intact and chaos is genuinely absent. Identify the isolating feature—predictive substrate interference, or the saturation of embedded computation—showing exactly why it is neither an epistemic limitation nor a dynamical instability. Then state the verdict each of three positions should give about in-principle predictability: the Laplacian determinist who equates determinism with universal forecastability; the chaos theorist for whom unpredictability requires positive Lyapunov exponents; and the embedded-computation view holding that prediction is always bounded by the predictor's physical footprint within the system. Finally, specify what feature of the case does the work, what happens if that feature is removed—for instance, if the predictor is externalized or its footprint reduced to zero—and whether removing it collapses the scenario back into a trivial case of Laplacian predictability or into a standard observer-effect problem.
