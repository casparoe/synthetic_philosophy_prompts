I serve on my university’s Institutional Partnerships Review Board, and we are meeting next Thursday to vote on a proposed three-year collaboration between our School of Public Health and the Eastern Regional Health Consortium. The consortium operates under a communitarian-paternalist ethical framework that treats collective social stability and expert judgment as primary goods, whereas our institutional code is built on liberal-procedural commitments to individual autonomy, transparent disclosure, and voluntary withdrawal. The proposal asks us to co-design a community mental-health intervention using the consortium’s unique longitudinal datasets and field infrastructure; in exchange, our faculty would provide methodological training and assist the consortium in publishing in peer-reviewed Western journals. The central conflict is that the consortium’s standard protocol relies on family-level authorization rather than individual informed consent, and it restricts data access to protect community harmony—practices our code treats as serious violations, yet ones that are legally normative in the consortium’s jurisdiction.

In a course I am taking on value pluralism and institutional ethics, I have been considering whether partnerships across incommensurable value systems can generate what might be called moral gains from trade—not merely pragmatic compromises, but structured arrangements in which each system retains sovereignty over its core commitments while exchanging goods it values but lacks. Applied here, the idea would be an institutional moral division of labor: our university governs consent, transparency, and withdrawal rights within the study’s Western-facing research arm, while the consortium governs community engagement and cultural adaptation within its own arm, with a shared governance layer that allocates authority by domain rather than blending the two frameworks. The strongest objection I want taken seriously is that this division is cosmetic: by lending our institutional credibility to a study that ultimately relies on family-level authorization, we are not trading goods but corrupting our own value system for access and funding, and no structural firewall can prevent moral contamination.

Please draft the short formal memo I will circulate before Thursday’s meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action—approve with conditions, reject, or approve with a pilot-phase structure that includes specific review milestones and a clear time frame. The reasoning should address whether moral values across incommensurable systems can be exchanged without corruption, apply the division-of-labor framework honestly, and treat the contamination objection as a serious philosophical challenge rather than dismissing it. The memo should be accessible to board members who are not philosophers, but it should not flatten the conceptual problem into a simple risk-benefit calculation.
