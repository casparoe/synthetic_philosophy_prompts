As a retired physical scientist who has recently begun reading philosophy, I have noticed a recurring move in arguments about whether an object is the same thing as the material it is made of, and I wonder whether philosophers have given it a specific name.

Consider two situations. First, a sculptor shapes a statue from a lump of clay. If the statue is crushed, people usually say the statue is gone, but the clay remains. That suggests that what makes the statue what it is has to do with its shape or form, not with the particular bits of matter, since those bits survive exactly when the statue does not.

Second, imagine an aircraft that is maintained for fifty years: every beam, panel, and wire is replaced in turn, yet the plane keeps its registration number and its continuous operational history. Most people say it is still the same aircraft. Now suppose all the old parts that were removed are gathered in a hangar and assembled into a second plane that is physically identical to the original design. Most people then say this second plane is not the original aircraft, even though it contains every piece of matter that was in the original plane at the start, while the continuously maintained first plane now contains none of those original pieces.

What puzzles me is not just that form and matter can come apart. It is that the reasoning seems to switch directions depending on the case. In the statue case, people treat the survival of the original material as belonging to the clay, not to the statue, so the statue can disappear while its material lives on. In the aircraft case, they treat the survival of the original material as irrelevant to the first plane's identity, allowing it to survive complete material replacement, yet they treat that same material continuity as decisive for denying identity to the second plane. There does not seem to be any stated general rule that tells us when the organizational history of a thing counts more than the continuity of its stuff, and when the opposite holds. The criterion appears to shift so that the intuitive answer for each particular case is preserved.

Is there an established term for exactly this pattern—this selective, case-driven shifting between reasoning about form and reasoning about material continuity, without an explicit governing principle? I am not asking for the general problem of whether a statue is identical to its clay; I am asking about this specific argumentative habit. How is it different from simple equivocation, or from saying that different kinds of things simply have different rules for staying the same over time? And if philosophers have not named it, what should it be called, and what are its precise boundaries—particularly the boundary that separates it from a legitimate view that different categories genuinely do have different conditions of persistence?
