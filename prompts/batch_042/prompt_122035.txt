Hey — I've been sketching an argument for my grad seminar on standing and hypocrisy, and I think it collapses into something absurd but I can't locate the leak. Here it is, step by step.

(1) If A blames B for wrong W, then A must have standing; and standing requires that A is not herself guilty of W, or of any wrong that is morally on a par with W.

(2) Now suppose B failed to save a stranger at trivial cost. That's wrong. But A also fails every day to save strangers at trivial cost (not donating enough, ignoring appeals, etc.). So A is guilty of the very same type of wrong: omission of rescue at low cost.

(3) Therefore, whenever A blames B for an omission, A lacks standing, because she commits structrual identical omissions.

(4) But this reasoning doesn't depend on omissions alone. If B lies, and A has ever told a white lie to avoid awkwardness, then lying is wrong, and A has done a wrong of the same kind — just with lower stakes. So by (1), A lacks standing.

(5) In fact, for any wrong W that B does, there's some paralel failure in A's life: she's never perfectly just, never perfectly honest, never perfectly generous. So for every possible act of blame, the blamer is hypocritical in this structural sense.

(6) So nobody ever has standing to blame anyone for moral failings, except maybe angels or people with perfect moral records.

(7) That's clearly wrong — we want to preserve that parents can blame children, that victims can blame wrongdoers, and so on. So one of the steps has to go.

The part I want to save is (1), or at least the core idea that direct hypocrisy undermines standing. I also like the parity move in (2)-(4). Where's the slip? Is it that (1) demands too strong a consistency? Or is there an equivocation between same kind and same moral weight? Or maybe the argument is actually valid and the real cost is giving up (1) entirely?

Also — I threw in this thing I'm calling the bookkeeping model: the idea that moral standing is like an account that gets debited every time you commit a paralel wrong, no matter the scale. Maybe that's what's driving the absurdity. Does rejecting the bookkeeping model fix it without ditching (1)?

Anyway, which premise do you reject, and what's the price? I'd rather keep (1) and the parity intuition than swallow (6), but I want to know exactly what I'm paying.
