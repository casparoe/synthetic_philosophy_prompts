I've come to accept a sharpened version of Keynes's view on the good life. The standard reading of his abundance argument is that once material scarcity is solved, humanity's central task shifts from production to wise leisure—cultivating art, friendship, and direct experience rather than accumulating goods. I think the view is stronger and more pessimistic: abundance doesn't merely free up time; it removes the external structure that gives work meaning for most people, so the "good life" of cultivated leisure is not a universal human endpoint but a temperamentally specific achievement requiring pre-existing cultural capital. Without that capital, abundance produces not flourishing but passive consumption, status games, and a free-floating anxiety that economic necessity had previously masked. This isn't a failure of education but a feature of cognition shaped by evolutionary scarcity. What would count against this synthesis?
