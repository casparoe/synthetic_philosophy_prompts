I'm trying to work out when its okay for a scientific model to lean on assumptions that are flat-out false, going by my gut on cases. Climate scientists treat earth as a perfect sphere with uniform reflectivity to study long-term warming -- that seems fine, the bumps cancel out. But macroeconomists assume every agent is a rational calculator with perfect foresight, then use that to explain panic-buying toilet paper -- not fine, you've thrown out the very mechanism that causes the panic. Physicists treat planets as point masses to derive orbital periods -- fine. But apply the exact same point-mass model to tidal locking in close binaries and it feels wrong, even though the equations dont change, because tides need actual surfaces to grab onto. Epidemiologists assume random mixing to forecast total infections -- okay. Use that same homogeneous model to design a targeted vaccine rollout for hospital staff -- not okay, the heterogeneity is exactly what you're trying to exploit. Biologists use smooth differential equations for predator-prey dynamics with millions of individuals -- fine. Sociologists use smooth diffusion for opinion polarization in large networks, ignoring that people are discrete and have hard thresholds, and that feels wrong too, even at scale. Heres the one I keep flipping on: an economist treats firms as black boxes with no internal structure, just profit maximization, and uses it to explain why some companies adapt fast to new tech and others dont. Is there a single principle that separates the okay idealizations from the bad ones, and if so what does it say about my economist case? Also, which pair of my verdicts has to go if we want the principle to stay simple?
