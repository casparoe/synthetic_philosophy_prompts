I'm designing a diagnostic taxonomy for an upper-level metaphysics workshop on the special composition question, and I want it built as a branching decision tree rather than a survey of named theories. The audience is advanced undergraduates who understand part-whole relations but shouldn't need to have memorized any particular literature to navigate the instrument. Each internal node should be a concrete commitment or case judgment—phrased as a yes/no or either/or question—so that a student can work through the tree and land on a position without recognizing philosophical labels in advance. Start with the root split over whether any composite object exists beyond elementary parts, but quickly move to finer-grained distinctions that separate restricted views from one another and from universalism and nihilism.

The tree must include at least these diagnostic nodes, presented in order: (1) whether a scattered object composed of a wooden table in one room and the Moon in another counts as one thing; (2) whether a biological organism remains the same object as its cells are entirely replaced over time; (3) whether an artifact like a ship with gradually replaced planks is genuinely one persisting object; (4) whether a mere heap of sand with no fixed boundary is a composite object; (5) whether a gerrymandered geographic region defined only by political convention has parts that genuinely compose it; and (6) whether a temporal fusion—where parts at t1 and t2 share no spatial overlap but are linked by a continuous causal process—constitutes one persisting whole. The leaves must include unrestricted universalism; mereological nihilism; organicism (composition restricted to living organisms); artifact-functionalism (composition restricted to objects with intended or functional unity); a spatial-contiguity view requiring direct contact among parts; and at least one unnamed hybrid or unexplored branch—such as a view allowing temporal but not spatial scattering, or one allowing both organisms and artifacts but excluding conventional regions. For every leaf, provide one sentence describing its most awkward commitment: the specific case it is forced to accept or deny that seems intuitively wrong. Also flag any pair of leaves your branching questions cannot genuinely separate, and mark every unoccupied combination of answers with a brief diagnosis of whether the gap is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Build the tree from the logical space of answers first, then locate standard positions in the leaves; do not use philosopher names at the nodes.
