After a long argument with a friend about whether there is genuinely an asymmetry between our reasons not to create suffering and our reasons to create happiness, I am drafting a proposal for a one-semester independent study in philosophy. Rather than rehearse the standard Benatar-Parfit framing, I want to investigate whether the asymmetry survives if we ground it purely in conditions for attributing harm to a specific moral patient, without granting any standing—even potential—to beneficiaries. The precise question: if we accept that harm requires an actual or determinately identifiable victim, but reject that benefit requires any corresponding beneficiary-subject, can we consistently maintain that collective or identity-affecting procreative choices generate obligations not to harm without generating parallel obligations to benefit? Write a proposal—roughly two pages, suitable for a small research grant or workshop pitch—that states this question precisely, explains why the standard person-affecting versus impersonal framing obscures rather than resolves it, outlines the method (first clarifying patienthood conditions, then mapping them onto individual and collective procreative scenarios, then testing consistency), describes what a successful result and an honest failure would each look like, and gives a realistic assessment of whether this is tractable in one semester without collapsing into a general survey of population ethics. I will supply my own citations; do not include a bibliography or reading list. Keep the framing sharp and avoid generic introductory material.
