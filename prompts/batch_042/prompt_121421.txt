In both the philosophical literature and in discussion groups I have followed, the dispute between conciliationists and steadfastists has settled into a frustrating stall. The conciliationist holds that when two epistemic peers—agents with equal evidence and equal reasoning competence—disagree about the same proposition, each should reduce confidence, often by meeting somewhere near the midpoint or by suspending judgment altogether. The steadfastist responds that an agent may rationally hold her ground if she possesses first-person access to the strength and structure of her own reasoning, even when she fully recognizes an equally competent peer who reaches the opposite conclusion. Over recent years the two sides have refined their accounts of peerhood, agreed that evidential equality is necessary but insufficient, and mapped out various conditions under which an asymmetry might be legitimate. Despite this, the positions have not moved.

The conciliationist seems to treat such autonomy as descriptively real but normatively inert, while the steadfastist treats it as a source of justification capable of breaking the symmetry imposed by peer disagreement. If this is right, then the parties are not arguing over shared norms applied differently; they are defending incompatible bedrock commitments about what makes belief rational, commitments that neither can defend without circularity.
