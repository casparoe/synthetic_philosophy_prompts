I'm preparing my position paper for a philosophy of population ethics seminar and want a consistency audit before I present it; I have been discussing these issues with friends and find myself defending a particular package, but I am unsure whether the pieces actually cohere. Here is where I have landed. First, I endorse the procreation asymmetry in its strong form: there is a decisive moral reason not to bring into existence a person whose life would be dominated by suffering, yet there is no corresponding moral reason—let alone an obligation—to bring into existence a person whose life would be predominantly happy. Second, I take the correct explanation of this asymmetry to be strictly person-affecting: harm and benefit must be anchored to the actual, identifiable interests of particular individuals, and because there is no one who is deprived when a happy life is not created, no moral reason is generated. Third, on that same basis I reject impersonal axiological accounts—those that treat total welfare across possible worlds as generating direct obligations—because I think they entail the Repugnant Conclusion and violate a kind of moral individualism that I am committed to. Fourth, I accept that the non-identity problem undermines harm-based objections in many policy domains: if a choice determines which specific people come to exist, and every person who actually exists has a life worth living, then no particular individual can claim to have been harmed by the choice that led to their existence. Fifth, despite this, I maintain that we have stringent, time-neutral obligations to mitigate climate change, partly in virtue of the severe suffering that will befall people living in the twenty-second century, and I do not think the temporal distance of that suffering weakens our reasons against causing it. Sixth, I hold that it is permissible, and sometimes prudentially admirable, for individuals to have children when those children are likely to have good lives, even though I deny there is any positive moral duty to do so. Seventh, I think moral reasoning should not depend on whether we frame a decision as affecting "possible people" or "actual future people," but should instead track whether there is a concrete subject who bears the cost or enjoys the benefit.

Please go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and how severe each tension is. I am particularly worried that my climate-obligation claim pulls against my acceptance of the non-identity problem and my rejection of impersonal goods, but I do not want to prejudge that. Rank the tensions by severity, and for each one indicate the least costly revision—whether giving up the climate-obligation framing, weakening the person-affecting constraint, modifying the asymmetry, or something else. Also say which single revision, if I had to make only one, would clean up the most inconsistencies without abandoning the core of my view. I should add that I am not asking for a bibliography or reading list; I simply want an audit of the logical architecture.
