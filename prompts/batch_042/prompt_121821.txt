I am a high school senior preparing an interview for my school's philosophy review, and my guest is a philosopher who defends a specific account of incommensurable values. She argues that hard choices involve not merely epistemic uncertainty but a structural feature she calls comparative paralysis: when two options are supported by genuinely incommensurable values, rational choice does not eliminate the standing reason for the unchosen option, leaving the agent with a persistent, non-emotional form of rational regret that is not reducible to disappointment. I want eight to ten rigorous interview questions that go well beyond having her restate the view and avoid any generic biographical material. At minimum, I need one question on how comparative paralysis differs from ordinary indecision or incomplete information, with a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that the persistence of reasons is objective rather than psychological; one on whether introducing a second-order meta-value—such as a principle of coherence or a hierarchical ordering—would dissolve the paralysis or merely relocate it to a higher level; and one on the implications for collective decision-making, specifically whether democratic deliberation can function if citizens systematically experience comparative paralysis about competing public goods like liberty and equality. For each of these three, include the anticipated follow-up. I also want a question pressing whether the denial of comparability risks collapsing into value subjectivism, with a follow-up anticipating her appeal to irreducible reason-giving relations rather than subjective preference. The tone should be precise and respectful, appropriate for an informed high-school interviewer, and the questions should turn on the actual content of her position rather than on external literature.
