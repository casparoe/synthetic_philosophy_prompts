Having spent the better part of four decades in experimental physics before turning, in retirement, to philosophy of science, I have been trying to articulate a consistent Bayesian picture of scientific reasoning and would welcome a conceptual audit before presenting it to a reading group. I do not want bibliographies, citation lists, or references to literature I could not check without access to a library—only a direct diagnosis of whether my commitments hang together.

My position comprises the following seven theses:

(1) I adopt subjective Bayesianism as the normative framework for scientific belief: rational agents hold degrees of belief that obey the probability calculus and update by strict conditionalization on their total available evidence.

(2) I deny logical omniscience. It strikes me as rationally permissible for a scientist to fail to derive, or even to be unaware of, logical consequences of her theoretical commitments, and such failures should be reflected in her credence assignments rather than treated as mere performance errors.

(3) I regard the problem of old evidence as genuine and not fully dissolved by Garber’s distinction alone. In particular, I think that when a scientist first recognizes that her theory H deductively implies a long-known datum E—exemplified by Einstein’s 1915 derivation of Mercury’s perihelion anomaly—she should rationally update her credence in H upward, and that this update is required, not optional.

(4) I further propose that the correct object of this update is not merely the bare logical relation H → E, but a structured proposition that couples H with the specific derivational pathway D that yields E—something like the conjunction (H ∧ correct-derivation(D,E)). I take this to introduce a form of path-dependent confirmation: the same empirical record can support H differently depending on which derivational route the agent actually grasps. I concede that standard Bayesian sample spaces do not naturally represent this structure.

(5) I maintain that accommodating already-known data should never carry the same confirmatory weight as predicting genuinely novel observations; nevertheless, it should not be assigned zero weight, and under the mechanism described in (4) it can be substantial.

(6) I hold that scientific rationality demands intersubjective convergence: if two researchers share identical observational records, identical prior probabilities, and have the same theoretical derivations available to them, then they ought to converge on identical credences in competing theories.

(7) I also think that explanatory grasp—the actual cognitive achievement of seeing why H entails E via D—is a legitimate variable that can differ between equally informed, equally rational agents, and that this difference should be reflected in their respective credences rather than dismissed as irrational divergence.

Please examine this set for internal tension. Specifically:

- Which pairs are genuinely incompatible, which are only apparently in tension, and which conflicts are masked by superficial agreement?
- Rank the genuine tensions by severity, indicating for each what the least costly revision would be and what philosophical price that revision exacts.
- Assess whether (4) is compatible with (1) and (2) taken together, or whether it smuggles in an implicit norm of logical omniscience despite my explicit denial in (2).
- Determine whether (6) can survive intact alongside (4) and (7), or whether intersubjective convergence must be weakened; if the latter, specify the minimal weakening that restores consistency.
- Finally, identify whether there is a single revision—perhaps to (4), (6), or (7)—that would eliminate the largest number of conflicts without abandoning the core Bayesian orientation expressed in (1).

Be direct and specific; I am seeking an audit of my own position, not a survey of the literature.
