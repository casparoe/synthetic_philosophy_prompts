I have been reading rationalist and longtermist forums for years, and I now work as a senior analyst for a climate-adaptation grant fund that makes awards for infrastructure with fifty- to one-hundred-year impact windows. Every quarter my board reviews four to six proposals—seawalls, managed-retreat incentives, carbon-capture subsidies, migration-corridor planning—each of which will alter settlement patterns, birth rates, and family formation enough to change exactly which people exist in the affected regions by 2120. We have no reusable framework for deciding when that identity-shifting effect blocks an otherwise beneficial grant, or when it is ethically irrelevant. I need a decision procedure I can run in about twenty minutes per proposal, without requiring board members to have studied population ethics, that turns the nonidentity problem and its standard distinctions into ordered tests with clear stopping conditions.

The deliverable should be a reusable flowchart in words: a sequence of questions applied in fixed order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, explicit “no” stopping points where the proposal is rejected or sent back for redesign, explicit handoff points where the remaining issue is one of values rather than facts and must be signed off by the board, and honest flags for steps that look procedural but are actually judgment calls dressed up as rules.

To prevent the framework from collapsing into either “all future people are interchangeable” or “identity-change makes evaluation impossible,” build in two niche operational criteria that you define in place. First, the Counterfactual Structural Continuity Test: ask whether every person who could occupy the relevant social and environmental role in the future scenario is at least as well off as every occupant of the corresponding role in the best feasible alternative scenario, regardless of specific identity. Second, the Modal Complicity Boundary: stop the analysis and flag for human judgment whenever the only harm identified is to present people whose autonomy is being overridden for speculative future benefit, or when the causal chain to future structural conditions is too indirect to assess.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals:
1. A seawall and coastal-development package that prevents present displacement but locks in high-density settlement patterns that will shape family formation for generations.
2. A carbon-reduction subsidy expected to lower regional birth rates through economic restructuring, improving climate outcomes but ensuring that the people who live in the improved climate are not the ones who would have lived under the status quo.
3. A managed-retreat grant that relocates present communities, destroying specific social networks but opening land-use patterns that will determine future population composition.

In at least one case the procedure should yield an answer the board will resist—probably an approval where intuition wants a block, or a block where intuition wants approval. State clearly what the procedure cannot resolve: for example, whether impersonal value should dominate person-affecting considerations, or whether the board owes duties to specific future individuals rather than to structural conditions. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not ask anyone to look up sources. Keep the language precise but accessible to a technically literate non-specialist, and mark every point where the analyst must still exercise irreducible judgment.
