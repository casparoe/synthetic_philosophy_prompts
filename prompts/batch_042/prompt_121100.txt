Write the full set of desiderata that any adequate philosophical and clinical theory of human death must satisfy in order to count as complete, given that the concept is required to do distinct work in at least three registers: determining eligibility for organ procurement, fixing the legal moment at which inheritance and marital status shift, and marking the metaphysical boundary at which a particular human organism ceases to exist. For each desideratum, state the specific case or distinction that motivates it—including the tension between brain-death criteria (irreversible loss of all brain function including brainstem) and circulatory-death criteria (irreversible cessation of integrated cardiopulmonary function)—and indicate whether you take it to be non-negotiable or a preference of one camp. Explicitly flag any pair of desiderata that you suspect are jointly unsatisfiable, with the reason. In particular, address how the theory must classify the following hypothetical but clinically adjacent scenario: a body maintained on full extracorporeal support exhibits no detectable brainstem reflexes, yet retains intact hypothalamic-pituitary hormonal cycles and reproducible spinal reflex arcs; determine whether this state should count as death of the organism, death of the person, both, or neither, and which desideratum forces that answer. Then, briefly score three mainstream candidates—the whole-brain formulation, the higher-brain formulation, and the circulatory-respiratory formulation—against your list, identifying for each which single desideratum does the most to exclude it if any does. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating conflicts rather than on the scorecard.
