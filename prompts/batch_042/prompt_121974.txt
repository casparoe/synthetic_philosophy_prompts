I spent thirty years as a field ornithologist studying avian speciation across Pacific archipelagos, and since retiring I have been auditing graduate seminars in philosophy of science. I am planning a single 90-minute workshop for approximately ten philosophy graduate students who have encountered the standard literature on the species problem but have never faced live classification ambiguity with practical stakes. I want an in-class activity—not a lecture or debate—where participants must apply competing species concepts to a deliberately constructed set of cases: specifically, a ring species exhibiting asymmetric gene flow across an island chain, paired with a cryptic species pair that are genetically distant but ecologically identical in every observable behavior and habitat. The rules and roles should be arranged so that the group divides into three functional positions—taxonomist, conservation lawyer, and evolutionary theorist—each forced to render a binding classification decision on whether the organisms in question constitute one species or several, with that decision then triggering a predetermined downstream consequence (for example, a conservation funding allocation or an explanatory framework for a subsequent evolutionary prediction). The point is not for them to reach consensus but for them to feel the pull of lumping versus splitting as a practical commitment rather than an abstract preference. After the exercise, provide a debrief that maps specific moments of disagreement onto the metaphysical commitments of the biological, phylogenetic, and morphological species concepts, and include an honest note on which feature of the problem—perhaps the temporal boundary problem or the normative dimension of classification—this staged scenario cannot reproduce and I must supply in discussion myself. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, particularly given that the participants will be philosophy students with no field experience, and how I should handle it. Write this in a formal register appropriate for a workshop proposal, and avoid asking for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, since I want the design to stand on its own logic.
