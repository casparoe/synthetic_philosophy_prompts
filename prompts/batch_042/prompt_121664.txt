I have been working through Adam Smith’s framework of sympathy and the impartial spectator in a moral psychology seminar, and I am trying to determine what principle actually governs when sympathetic feeling legitimately guides judgment versus when it must be corrected or overridden. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases, and they do not sit together easily. First, a factory owner who genuinely feels the suffering of workers injured by unsafe machinery, and who—after consulting the internal spectator—concludes that reform is required despite the cost: I judge this correct, and I take the spectator’s approval to be what transforms mere sentiment into moral authority. Second, a mother who feels intense sympathy for her hungry child who stole bread, yet whose spectator condemns the theft: I think sympathy and judgment can conflict here, that the spectator should prevail in the verdict on the act, but that the sympathy itself remains virtuous rather than defective. Third, a bureaucrat who approves a policy causing severe but dispersed harm because statistical benefits are large, feeling no direct sympathy for the particular victims because they remain abstract: I judge this a corruption of moral judgment, not a triumph of impartiality, since the spectator’s view seems distorted by the absence of particular fellow-feeling. Fourth, an eighteenth-century reformer who extends sympathetic identification across racial and social boundaries that his society deems improper, with the spectator ratifying that expanded sympathy: I think this is precisely how moral progress occurs, with the spectator correcting parochial prejudice rather than endorsing it. But here is the case I keep reversing: a modern practitioner of what I will call “sympathetic calibration,” who reaches judgments about tax policy or institutional design not through spontaneous emotional response but by deliberately and sequentially imagining herself in each affected position, constructing an artificial equilibrium of exchanged perspectives without ever feeling actual warmth or distress. One part of me says Smith should reject this as cold mechanism lacking the moral affect that makes sympathy valuable; another part says it is the purest realization of the spectator, since sympathy just is this imaginative exchange of situations. I also want to know what the principle predicts for a case I have not listed—specifically, an algorithmic system that aggregates reported emotional responses from thousands of people to generate an “impartial” recommendation on a moral dispute, with no single human experiencing the integrated perspective.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving the necessity of particularized feeling, the priority of the spectator, or the conditions under which imaginative exchange counts as genuine sympathy—that captures the first four verdicts, resolves my uncertainty about calibrated imagination, and predicts the algorithmic case? If not, which of my judgments should be abandoned, and what does the repaired principle say about whether sympathy must always be spontaneous and embodied to do moral work?
