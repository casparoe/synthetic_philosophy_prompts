I've been turning over a conversation I had with my sister, and the more I examine it, the more convinced I am that it touches on something fundamental about how language works—though I don't have the vocabulary for it.

We were deciding whether to invite our parents to a reunion planning session. I asked her directly whether she thought they should be included. She replied: "Dad's been very busy with his garden club lately, and Mom always brings that lemon cake everyone likes." Both statements are true. Yet she later told me she meant them as a refusal—she believes parents complicate logistics—and that she was being indirect only to avoid an open argument.

This leaves me with a puzzle I cannot resolve on my own. The words she spoke were factually correct, yet they seemed to carry a decision that was never actually stated. Was she being dishonest in some sense? Or is there a legitimate gap between what a sentence literally conveys and what a speaker uses it to accomplish?

I have tried to work out a principle for myself: perhaps ordinary conversation operates under an implicit agreement that participants should be direct and supply exactly the information needed for the purpose at hand, and that departing from this is a kind of failure or deception. But that principle cannot be right, because then polite hints, soft refusals, and leaving things unsaid would all count as violations. So cooperation in talk might not be about maximizing directness at all, but about recognizing that a speaker can intend one thing while her words, taken literally, point in another direction.

My question is this. In a case like my sister's, where the literal content is true but apparently serves as a vehicle for an unstated answer, should I understand the "real" meaning as something extra that the speaker attaches to her words, or as something already recoverable from the words themselves once the shared context is taken into account? And if indirectness is not a breach of conversational cooperation but actually part of how that cooperation functions, does that change whether we ought to regard her reply as deceptive rather than as a legitimate way of saying no?
