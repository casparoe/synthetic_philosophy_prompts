For a graduate seminar in medieval philosophy and religion, I want a full specification—written as a formal desiderata document—for any adequate philosophical reconstruction of Maimonides' position on animal sacrifice. Before we score particular readings, lay out exactly what such a reconstruction must deliver.

Frame your answer around what I will call the sacrificial persistence problem: Maimonides offers an anthropological-genetic explanation of sacrifice (it was instituted as a pedagogical accommodation to an Israelite population habituated to idolatrous animal veneration) yet, in his legal codification, he treats sacrificial law as permanently binding, classifies its rationales among the opaque commandments, and implies that sacrifices could be restored. Specify the criteria an interpretation must meet to resolve, or at least properly articulate, this tension rather than dissolve it by textual dismissal.

Write the desiderata: the phenomena and texts the reconstruction must accommodate, the distinctions it must preserve, the results from Maimonides' broader metaphysics and jurisprudence with which it must remain consistent, and the cases in which it must yield determinate judgments. For each requirement, state the motivating consideration, and mark whether you regard it as non-negotiable or as the preference of a particular interpretive camp (concessive-pedagogical, esoteric-symbolic, legal-formalist, or other).

Your list must explicitly address the following three layers, treating them as structural demands rather than optional themes:

1. The anthropological-genetic layer: the reconstruction must account for why sacrifice was commanded given human psychological history, without reducing the command to a merely provisional institution that reason now renders obsolete.

2. The legal-permanence layer: it must explain how sacrificial law retains binding force and potential future actualization, without treating the legal texts as rhetorical cover for an essentially abolished practice.

3. The metaphysical-symbolic layer: it must determine whether the specific conditions of the sacrificial animal—domestic status, physical perfection, species specification—carry symbolic weight relating to the subordination of human appetite to intellect, or whether they are purely conventional legal markers. Crucially, it must do so without attributing to Maimonides a concern for animal suffering or individual animal welfare that conflicts with his Aristotelian hierarchy, in which particular providence extends only to rational beings.

In addition, the reconstruction must preserve the distinction between exoteric pedagogical function (taʿlīm) and esoteric ontological or legal status, showing how the two relate—whether as surface and depth, as complementary descriptions, or as genuinely unresolved commitments—without collapsing them into one.

Further consistency requirements: compatibility with his view that divine law does not change essentially; with his claim that prayer substitutes for sacrifice only in the absence of the Temple, not in principle; with his insistence that the reasons for commandments are in principle accessible to human reason even when unknown; and with his denial that non-rational animals are subjects of particular divine concern.

Then identify which pairs of desiderata you suspect are jointly unsatisfiable—especially whether an exhaustive concession thesis (that sacrifice exists solely to cure a now-cured psychological defect) can coexist with the permanence thesis, and whether a robust symbolic reading can avoid importing modern animal-ethics concerns foreign to his metaphysics. Flag which single requirement does the most damage to the currently dominant readings.

Briefly indicate how the three main camps fare against your list, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves: which demands are genuinely non-negotiable, which reflect camp prejudice, and where the list itself reveals that no reconstruction can satisfy every legitimate constraint simultaneously.

Write this formally, as a specification for evaluation rather than as an essay defending one view. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation requests; I will supply references. Avoid invented references to obscure manuscript variants or precise responsa citations—rely on the well-known conceptual tension between the Guide and the Mishneh Torah. A table is acceptable if it clarifies the scoring, but the conceptual work should remain in the specification of the desiderata.
