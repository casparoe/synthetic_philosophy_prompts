A recent policy essay summarizes Robin Hanson's proposal for futarchic governance in the following way: In this system, citizens vote democratically on a single cardinal welfare metric—say, a composite of GDP growth and environmental quality—while separate conditional prediction markets continuously price every proposed policy against that metric. The policy with the highest expected market value is then enacted automatically, without further legislative deliberation on the means. The essay claims that this separates values from beliefs: democracy supplies the ends, and traders with financial stakes supply the factual predictions, which eliminates the problem of voter ignorance about complex policy effects because belief-formation is effectively outsourced to informed specialists. It also asserts that futarchy resolves Arrow's impossibility theorem by replacing ordinal preference aggregation with cardinal market pricing, and that government becomes more transparent because market prices are public. Finally, the essay concludes that the only serious barrier to implementation is legal—specifically, prohibitions on political betting—so that adoption requires either a special jurisdictional exemption or synthetic play-money markets with carefully calibrated incentives.
