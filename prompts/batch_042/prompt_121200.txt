I am a novelist working on a manuscript set in Turin during the winter of 1888, and in order to render one of my characters—a physician who maintains private notebooks on what he terms gastric philosophy—I have been auditing an informal graduate seminar whose syllabus calls the subject physiological aesthetics. The students were assigned a short essay asking them to explain, with reference to Nietzsche's remarks in Ecce Homo and related writings on climate and digestion, how individual dietary practice relates to philosophical vitality and to the broader distinction between ascending life and decadence. They were specifically asked to address his critique of German cuisine, his claim that metabolism and climate shape the quality of a thinker's ideas, and the sense in which digestion functions as a model for how a culture assimilates foreign influences rather than as mere biology. I have been asked by the course instructor to serve as an external grader for one submission, and I want you to evaluate the answer that follows: assign it a precise letter grade or numerical score, then provide a point-by-point assessment that justifies that evaluation. Please do not signal your own broader philosophical commitments; I need only an accurate, rigorous reading of where the answer succeeds and where it subtly fails, especially with respect to any conflations—such as mistaking great health for the absence of illness, or misrepresenting his attitude toward ascetic dietary discipline—that a hurried grader might overlook but that distort the argument.

Here is the exam question, followed by the student's answer.

Question: Drawing on Nietzsche's writings on physiology and culture—particularly his remarks on climate, digestion, and the concept of great health—explain how individual dietary practice relates to philosophical strength. Address his critique of German cuisine, his view that metabolism and climate determine the quality of a thinker's ideas, and the way digestion serves as a model for cultural assimilation or failure. How does this physiological perspective inform his distinction between decadence and ascending life?

Student answer: Nietzsche treats diet as the hidden foundation of all philosophical strength. In Ecce Homo he recommends avoiding alcohol and heavy foods, proposing instead a light, climate-adapted regimen suited to the individual constitution. He argues that German cuisine is especially dangerous because its heaviness produces mental sluggishness and cultural indigestion, leaving thinkers unable to assimilate new ideas. For Nietzsche, great health describes the achievement of perfect digestive equilibrium and the complete absence of illness, allowing the philosopher to maintain an unbroken state of physical wellness. He further advocates vegetarianism as the highest dietary discipline, linking it directly to the will to power because it demands rigorous self-denial and mastery over the body. On this view, the stomach functions as a direct mirror of the soul: healthy digestion produces healthy ideas, and strict universal dietary rules are necessary for anyone seeking ascending life rather than decadence.
