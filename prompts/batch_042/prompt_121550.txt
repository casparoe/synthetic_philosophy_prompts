Read Frank Capra’s It’s a Wonderful Life (1946) through the lens of contingency, chaos, and the philosophy of historical explanation—specifically the “for want of a nail” problem of whether small perturbations cascade into large-scale divergence, and whether such divergence reveals genuine chaos or merely hidden structural fragility. The film’s central counterfactual—George Bailey never having been born—produces Pottersville, a dystopian inversion of Bedford Falls. Treat this not merely as a narrative device but as a philosophical thought experiment about historical causation.

In your reading, distinguish between “trigger causes” (individual actions or omissions that initiate change) and “structural amplifiers” (institutional and social conditions that determine whether a perturbation dies out or scales up). Apply this to the film: is George’s absence the sole trigger, or does Pottersville emerge because Bedford Falls lacked resilient institutions—banks, civic associations, moral norms—that could have absorbed the loss of one man? Which scenes (the run on the Building and Loan, Potter’s monopoly, Mary’s spinsterhood, the bridge) illustrate fragility, and which suggest that the town’s decency was always structurally dependent on George?

Next, consider “counterfactual stability”: which macro-features of Bedford Falls remain constant across the two worlds (class hierarchy, economic dependence on the Building and Loan, gender roles) and which are radically unstable (the town’s moral character, Mary’s life trajectory, the existence of the Bailey Park development)? Does the film implicitly endorse a “great man” theory of local history, in which one virtuous individual holds chaos at bay? Or does it accidentally show that George’s presence masked a system already vulnerable to monopoly and despair?

Finally, say where the philosophical lens illuminates the film—perhaps George’s decision not to jump, or Clarence’s intervention as a “butterfly” perturbation that restores the timeline—and where the film resists it: for instance, the sentimental resolution may suppress the darker implication that if one man’s absence can destroy a community, then that community was never genuinely just or resilient. What does the film add to the philosophical debate about contingency that a purely abstract argument—say, about sensitive dependence versus structural causation—misses?

Take both the film and the conceptual framework seriously; do not reduce the work to an illustration.
