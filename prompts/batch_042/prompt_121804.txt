Two members of my philosophy of mind reading group have been arguing about whether dreams are experiences, drawing on Norman Malcolm's claim that dreaming is not an experience at all and Daniel Dennett's objection that this depends on an unjustified Cartesian theatre model of consciousness. I was present and did not take sides; please go through their exchange and say who is right at each step, not merely who wins overall, writing in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one might write in a letter, and please use British English throughout.

The first speaker, Adrian, began by defending Malcolm. He said that the grammar of first-person dream reports reveals the problem: we say "I dreamt that I was flying" upon waking, but we never genuinely say "I was experiencing flight during my sleep" in the same present-tense way we report waking perceptions. For Malcolm, this grammatical asymmetry shows that "experience" applies only to waking consciousness, and that attributing experiences to sleep is a category mistake. Adrian then added his own point, which he called the retrospective binding problem: even if we reject the idea of a central observer in the brain, as Dennett urges, we still face a problem of phenomenal accessibility. If there is no mechanism by which a sleeping subject can access representational content at the time it occurs, then there is simply no subject for whom the dream is present, regardless of whether consciousness is distributed or centralised. Moreover, he noted that dream reports depend on memory consolidation that happens primarily during the transition to waking, so even if representational content exists in REM sleep, it only becomes phenomenally available during that consolidation window upon awakening.

The second speaker, Beatrice, replied with Dennett's anti-theatre argument. She said that requiring a central audience for experience is precisely the error Malcolm makes; consciousness can be constituted by distributed functional and representational states without any inner spectator. During REM sleep, she argued, the brain generates complex representational sequences that do not need to be watched in order to be experiences. They need only be organised in the right functional way, and Dennett's rejection of the Cartesian theatre shows that Malcolm's demand for a present subject is an unnecessary relic of an outdated model of mind.

Adrian countered by pressing the temporal implication of retrospective binding. He granted that distributed states might occur during REM, but insisted that this means either there was no phenomenal experience during REM itself, which vindicates Malcolm, or that the experience is temporally displaced to the moment of waking, which seems incoherent because experience requires some form of contemporaneity between the subject and the content. He asked how Beatrice could claim he experienced flying over Paris at three in the morning during REM when, on her own account, the representational content was only activated and bound into consciousness at seven in the morning upon waking.

Beatrice responded that this actually supports Dennett rather than Malcolm. On her reading, a dream is not a private film played during REM and later recalled; it is a narrative constructed in the very process of awakening and consolidation. The experience of the dream is therefore constituted at that moment of reconstruction, not replayed from an earlier screening. So there is a genuine experience, but its temporal location is the awakening transition, not the REM period itself. Malcolm is wrong to assume that if dreaming involves experience, that experience must have occurred during sleep. She added that remembering a childhood event involves an experience now about a past event, and nobody finds that incoherent; similarly, the dream experience is an experience now, upon waking, of a narrative that refers back to REM-state traces.

Adrian then pressed what he saw as the decisive objection. If Beatrice is correct, then when he reports at seven in the morning that he experienced flying over Paris at three in the morning during REM, he is mislocating his own experience. The experience actually occurred at seven, as part of a waking reconstruction of neural traces. But then he is not reporting an experience that happened in sleep; he is reporting a waking experience of reconstructed narrative. That collapses Beatrice's position back into something very close to Malcolm's, because it denies that there is any sleep-bound experience of flying at all. The only difference, he said, is that Beatrice allows a waking experience of narrative activation, whereas Malcolm denies even that by insisting on strict grammatical usage.

Beatrice's final move was to distinguish between the temporal location of the neural substrate and the temporal location of the experience. She said that the narrative is genuinely generated by those REM traces, so it is not a false memory or pure fabrication; but it is also not a replay of a prior experience, because there was no prior experience to replay. Thus Malcolm is wrong to deny that dreams are experiences, and Adrian is wrong to think that temporal displacement requires contemporaneity with the neural event. The experience is real, present upon awakening, and causally tied to sleep without being spatially or temporally confined within it.

Please adjudicate move by move: assess Adrian's opening grammatical and accessibility argument, Beatrice's anti-theatre reply, Adrian's retrospective binding and temporal incoherence challenge, Beatrice's consolidation-as-constitution response with the memory analogy, and Adrian's collapse objection together with Beatrice's final distinction. Rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify anything important that both speakers have missed in their exchange about the nature of dream phenomenology or the relationship between report, memory, and experience.
