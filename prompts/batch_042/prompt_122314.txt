I've been trying to understand why the word genocide feels like it belongs in two incompatible worlds at once. Raphael Lemkin originally meant something broader than just killing—he included destroying a group's culture, language, and institutions—but the legal definition adopted in international law is much narrower: it requires proof that someone specifically intended to physically destroy a protected group. Meanwhile, activists and journalists apply the word to situations where a group is being slowly destroyed through forced assimilation, land dispossession, or ecological collapse, cases where there may be no explicit order to kill anyone but the conditions for collective life are being eliminated. Here is the conceptual knot I can't untie: the legal category is designed to catch deliberate, identifiable intent, which means the more a state hides behind bureaucracy, environmental policy, or economic pressure rather than direct violence, the further the situation falls from the legal definition—even though the moral harm may be more total. So is the tension just that lawyers and activists are using the same word for different jobs? Or is there a deeper conceptual reason why genocide must remain tied to specific intent, such that broadening it to cover structural and ecological destruction would collapse the distinction between genocide and other forms of mass oppression? I am not asking for references or a history lesson; I want to know whether there is a coherent conceptual boundary here. And if my framing is confused—if I am treating a legal technicality as if it were a moral problem, or missing something obvious about how the category works—please say so plainly rather than going along with the premise.
