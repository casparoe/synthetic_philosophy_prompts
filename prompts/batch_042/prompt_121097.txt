I'm constructing a course module on moral responsibility that moves from classical compatibilism to contemporary abolitionism, and I've been focusing on the sustained exchange between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso—specifically Dennett's defense that determinism leaves intact the varieties of control and reason-responsiveness that make desert-based practices socially indispensable, and Caruso's counter that neuroscience undermines the folk-psychological assumption of uncaused agency and that criminal institutions should be redesigned around a model of social defense analogous to public-health quarantine rather than retributive blame. My students can articulate both positions with precision, agree that determinism is at least highly plausible, agree that unnecessary suffering is bad, and yet split cleanly into two camps that seem immune to mutual persuasion.

What I want is not another adjudication but a structural diagnosis of why this particular disagreement has proven so intractable. Is the stall primarily metaphysical—hinging on whether the control conditions Dennett identifies genuinely satisfy the normative demands that ground just deserts—or is it normative in disguise, with Dennett defending an institutional architecture that relies on reactive emotional attitudes for social stability and Caruso insisting that any architecture retaining retributive coloring is unjust regardless of its efficacy? Could there also be a hidden empirical disagreement about what data on recidivism, deterrence, and social cohesion actually show, masked behind philosophical vocabulary? Furthermore, I want you to consider whether the recent proliferation of algorithmic risk-assessment tools in criminal sentencing—systems that claim to predict future behavior and allocate sanctions without invoking human agency or blame at all—exposes a gap in both accounts. Does Dennett's defense of desert have resources to address whether a statistical model can justly hold anyone responsible, and does Caruso's abolitionism apply with the same force when the decision-maker is not a blaming human but an opaque predictive system?

Please diagnose the disagreement using what each thinker actually says about control, reactive attitudes, and institutional design. Identify whether this is a verbal dispute about the definition of free will, a substantive normative clash about the legitimacy of reactive emotions, or a case of two philosophers answering different questions—one about the metaphysics of agency, one about the ethics of punishment—under a single heading. And tell me, given your diagnosis, whether any conceptual distinction, empirical finding, or institutional innovation could genuinely resolve the impasse, or whether this is precisely the kind of philosophical deadlock where the best outcome is not resolution but a clear map of where and why the paths diverge.
