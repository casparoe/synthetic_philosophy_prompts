The exam question was:

Some contemporary democratic theorists defend "lottocracy"—governance by bodies selected through sortition (random lot). In your answer, (a) distinguish allocative sortition (random assignment to specific governing offices or juries) from deliberative sortition (random selection to constitute a mini-public for collective deliberation), and explain why defenders think random selection improves democratic legitimacy relative to election; (b) identify one serious objection that applies specifically to deliberative sortition and not, or not in the same way, to allocative sortition; (c) evaluate whether that objection undermines the case for lottocracy altogether, or only for one form of it. In part (c), explicitly consider the role of statistical representativeness and the possibility of "epistemic drift"—the tendency for group deliberation to shift participants' views away from the distribution of opinions in the original randomly selected sample.

The student wrote:

"Sortition divides into two types. Allocative sortition assigns citizens randomly to offices, like jury duty or legislative seats, so that the government becomes a microcosm of the population's values and preferences. Because every citizen has an equal chance of holding office, it eliminates elite capture and corruption that come with elections. Deliberative sortition, by contrast, brings a random sample together to discuss an issue and reach a recommendation; the idea is that the sample is descriptively representative, so its conclusions should reflect what the broader public would think if they had the same information.

One objection specific to deliberative sortition is epistemic drift. Once people start talking, they influence each other, so the group's final position no longer matches the statistical distribution of views in the initial sample. This doesn't happen in allocative sortition because there the officials just carry out their assigned roles without collective deliberation altering their individual judgments; each office-holder acts independently. So epistemic drift undermines deliberative mini-publics but leaves allocative systems intact.

Moreover, this objection doesn't really distinguish lottocracy from electoral democracy, since elected legislatures also deliberate and experience the same drift. Therefore it can't be used to reject sortition in favor of elections. In fact, because allocative sortition avoids deliberation altogether, it is immune to drift, which actually gives pure allocative lottocracy an advantage over both deliberative sortition and elected government. The statistical basis of representativeness is preserved in allocative systems precisely because no collective discussion takes place."
