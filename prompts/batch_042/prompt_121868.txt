I was arguing about this with a colleague last week — she said moral conflicts are usually zero-sum, but I think sometimes they're more like trade: each side gives up something they value less to gain something they value more, so both come out ahead. The catch is that people often pretend a core commitment is just instrumental so they can trade it away, which is corruption wearing a compromise costume. There's also this problem of whether values even have exchange rates, or if we're just fooling ourselves.

Anyway, I need a reusable decision proceedure — an ordered checklist with specific questions, what counts as a sufficient answer, where it stops with a hard no, where it hands the call to a human because it's pure values not facts, and honest flags for where it'll misfire or disguise a judgment call as a rule. It should be something staff with no philosophy background can run in about thirty minutes. Then apply it to three cases: first, letting a local council require gender-segregated community meetings in exchange for guaranteed girls school access; second, accepting a local restorative justice practice that uses material compensation but skips any verbal apology for accidental harm; and third, allowing a religious leader to restrict family-planning counseling to married couples only in exchange for free clinic space — I want this to be the case where the proceedure spits out an answer I'll probably hate. Write it in American English, don't include any bibliography or citation list since our field staff won't have library access, and put a one-sentence summary as the very last line.
