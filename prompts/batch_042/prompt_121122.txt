I've noticed a recurring asymmetry in how historians and science writers treat non-human forces—pathogens, climate shifts, volcanic eruptions—as agents within historical narratives. When a society collapses, suffers famine, or undergoes rapid social transformation after an epidemiological or environmental shock, the shock itself is often cast as an autonomous historical actor: the plague "reshapes" labor markets, a volcanic winter "triggers" political unrest, a warming period "drives" migration. Human societies appear largely reactive, as if the germ, the climate pattern, or the eruption possessed causal priority. Yet when a society faces a comparable stress but remains stable, adapts without crisis, or recovers quickly, the same kind of force is typically downgraded to a background condition, and the explanation shifts to human institutions, cultural practices, or political choices that "absorbed" or "managed" the stress. The non-human entity does not merely share explanatory space with human agency; its assigned agency seems to expand or contract depending on whether the outcome is crisis or continuity, and this shift rarely comes with an explicit principle stating when the environment gets causal priority and when it is treated as inert substrate.

This is not standard environmental determinism, which assigns a consistent, principled causal weight to physical forces, nor is it a symmetrical interactionist model that holds human and non-human factors in constant joint tension. Rather, it is an outcome-dependent attribution: germs, climate, and volcanoes appear to possess historical agency precisely when human societies fare poorly, and to lose it precisely when they fare well or hold steady. I am looking for the established term, if one exists, for this specific pattern—the conditional, narrative-driven assignment of autonomous causal power to non-human entities based on the valence of the historical result. If there is such a term, how exactly does it differ from determinism, and what are its boundary conditions? If there is no established concept, can you name and define the phenomenon precisely, specify how to distinguish genuine cases of it from cases where human adaptation was genuinely sufficient or where the environmental stress was genuinely absent, and propose clear criteria for when the attribution is warranted versus when it is a post-hoc explanatory convenience?
