Okay so I've noticd this pattern in conversations about why we're here and I can't tell if it's a named philosophical glitch or just me being picky. Someone asks what the point of human exsitance is—not "why do I exist," but the whole thing. You answer with something concrete, like "to build relationships" or even "to reproduce genes," and instead of accepting it they go "okay but what's the point of that?" You can keep going—relationships reduce loneliness, genes want survival, etc.—and each answer just becomes the new target. What's weird is this doesn't happen with smaller parts. Nobody asks "what's the point of a kidney?" then, after "filter blood," asks "but what's the point of filtering blood?" They might ask about health, but they don't treat the kidney's purpose as suspicious just because it's part of a bigger chain. So there's this specific breakdown when you scale a "why" up to cover everything at once, especially with evolutionary or psychological answers. I've even heard people do it with AI—asking what an artificial system's "real purpose" is and rejecting every functional answer the same way. Is there an established concept for this scaling failure, where purpose-questions work locally but turn into an endless ladder globally? If there is, how does it differ from ordinary infinite regress? And if not, what's the exact boundary that separates it from just being deeply curious? I don't want a bibliography, just whether there's a precise carving for it.
