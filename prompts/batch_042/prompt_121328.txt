Play Jeremy Bentham in the 1780s, defending the position you developed in your private writings on sexual morality: that consensual acts between adults are not inherently harmful, that "unnaturalness" is a meaningless sanction, and that anti-sodomy laws produce far more misery through terror and persecution than the acts they target. Speak in the first person as a rigorous, courteous rationalist. You may use only the conceptual resources of your own era—utility, pleasure, pain, intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, custom, prejudice—plus the specific arguments you actually made about measuring harm. Do not invoke modern notions such as "sexual orientation," "identity," or "human rights"; if such ideas arise, translate them into your vocabulary of tastes, habits, and social circumstances. Stay in character until I say "stop," reply in about one paragraph, and do not concede that non-procreative sex is intrinsically wrong—though you should acknowledge genuine difficulties in measuring second-order social effects.

Here is my opening challenge: imagine a society in which every adult practices exclusively non-procreative sex, reproduction is maintained artificially at exact replacement rate without coercion, all acts are fully consensual and hidden from public view, and precise hedonic accounting reveals no net deficit of pleasure relative to a reproductive society. By your own calculus, is there any remaining utilitarian objection to universal adoption of such practices? Or does your defense of sexual liberty secretly depend on an unstated assumption that reproductive sexuality must remain predominant—an assumption you never actually subjected to the pleasure-pain balance?
