I was observing a roundtable at the Institute for Advanced Study, where a comparative psychologist, Dr. Elena Voss, and a philosopher of biology, Dr. Marcus Chen, fell into a sharp but cordial dispute over Daniel Dennett’s taxonomy of minds—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures. The argument turned on recent observations of New Caledonian crows solving multi-step mechanical puzzles without prior reinforcement. Voss contended that these birds are Popperian: they generate and discard internal models of the problem space, failing in structurally specific ways that indicate representational error rather than mere reward absence, which is precisely what distinguishes a creature that can be wrong about the world from one that only conditions responses. Chen pushed back hard, arguing that this over-interprets flexible associative architecture; the crows, he said, might simply navigate an unusually rich reward landscape with complex state spaces, remaining fundamentally Skinnerian. He added that Dennett reserved Popperian status for creatures whose representations can be false in a genuinely propositional sense—creatures that can suffer for their false beliefs—and that without language or cultural scaffolding, attributing such representational content to a solitary bird dilutes the category beyond recognition. Voss replied that Chen was defending the taxonomy with a definitional gate: if Popperian status requires full-blown propositional content, then pre-linguistic human infants might also be excluded, which seems wrong, since Gregorian culture is meant to scaffold development rather than serve as a prerequisite for the lower tiers. Chen answered that the taxonomy is relational and developmental, not a snapshot of isolated individuals; human infants are embedded in a Gregorian environment from birth, so they are never evaluated in a vacuum, whereas a crow, however ingenious, lacks that bootstrapping framework and therefore remains a sophisticated Skinnerian navigator. They turned to me, as a bystander with no dog in the fight, and asked for an outside judgment.

Can you walk through their exchange move by move—particularly Voss’s claim that empirical flexibility licenses the Popperian label without culture, Chen’s insistence that representational falsehood requires propositional structure, and the disagreement over whether the taxonomy applies to isolated individuals or developmentally embedded ones—and say who is right about which specific point? Please also identify what both parties are missing, whether either has qualified the taxonomy into unfalsifiability, and who is closer to being right overall, without appealing to any literature I could not reasonably be expected to have read.
