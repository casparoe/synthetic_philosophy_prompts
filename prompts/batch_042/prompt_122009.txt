I'm working through a conceptual problem that came up in a seminar on the philosophy of design cognition, specifically in connection with the Letter Spirit project and the broader question of what makes every 'a' an 'a' despite the enormous stylistic variation across roman, italic, cursive, and blackletter forms. I have walked myself into an argument that now strikes me as proving far too much, and I need help locating the precise point of failure. Here is the chain exactly as I presented it to my study group. (1) Take the full range of 'a' forms—the double-story roman, the single-story italic, the cursive loop with its ascender, the blackletter double-decker. There is no invariant geometric feature—no shared contour, topological relation, or set of strokes—present in every instance. (2) So whatever makes each of these an 'a' cannot be an essential geometric form; it must be something relational, such as family resemblance within a writing system, functional position in an alphabet, or historical lineage within a typographic tradition. (3) But if membership in the category 'letter' depends entirely on such relational or conventional properties, then there are no intrinsic boundary conditions for the category: any mark could be admitted as an 'a' if the community treats it as occupying the right slot, and there is no perceptual-cognitive difference between recognizing an 'a' and recognizing an arbitrarily licensed mark. (4) This means literacy is not a cognitive achievement of identifying objective categories but merely a socially trained conformity to arbitrary conventions. (5) Yet we treat certain misrecognitions—for instance, reading a 'q' as an 'a' in a particular font—not as legitimate alternative conventions but as straightforward errors, which implies there is a normative, non-arbitrary standard of correct recognition. (6) Therefore, the relational account in (2) must be false, and there must be an essential geometric kernel shared by all 'a's after all, despite the obvious diversity in (1).

I feel the slip is somewhere between (3) and (5), but I cannot name it precisely. The conclusion in (6) seems absurd—no serious typographer or cognitive scientist believes in a hidden geometric essence of 'a'-ness—yet I want to preserve both (1), which looks undeniable, and the intuition behind (5) that misreading is genuinely erroneous rather than merely unconventional. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I must choose, I would rather keep (1) and (5) together and absorb the price elsewhere.
