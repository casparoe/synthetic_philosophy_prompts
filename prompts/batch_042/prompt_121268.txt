I'm trying to work out whether Buddhist ethical evaluation is best read as a mental-state consequentialism—where the moral quality of an act is fixed by the root volition (cetana) and its karmic fruit is a separate, non-aggregable consequence—or as a view that genuinely cares about suffering-reduction but restricts permissible means. Here are my concrete verdicts. A surgeon performs a painful but life-saving operation with genuine compassion: excellent, even though she caused pain. A commander orders a retreat that saves a thousand soldiers but leaves fifty behind to be executed; he feels only sorrow, no hatred: the decision was necessary, yet he still bears a heavy moral stain that the saved thousand do not cancel. A teacher lies to a struggling student, saying she has talent, and the student eventually masters the craft: wrong, because false speech corrupts the mental continuum regardless of the beneficial result. A monk uses funds he knows were stolen to build a hospital that saves hundreds; he feels no greed, only concern for suffering: the hospital should stand, but his acceptance of tainted resources remains ethically defective in a way that the lives saved do not wash away. A father strikes his child in anger to stop him from running into traffic, and succeeds: blameworthy, because the presence of aversion in the volition makes the act unwholesome despite the child's survival.

These point to a principle in which the ethical quality of an act is set by the presence or absence of greed, hatred, and delusion in the root mental state, while outcomes determine only the scope of karmic ripening, not the agent's moral evaluation. But I keep hesitating about the commander: if outcomes never cancel stains, why does his sorrow matter at all? And what about the ruler who orders the execution of one innocent person to prevent a riot killing hundreds, feeling only sorrow and no hatred—does he bear the same stain as if he had killed in rage?

State the best principle or pair of candidates that captures my pattern, predict its verdict on the ruler case, and be explicit about which of my five verdicts it forces me to drop.
