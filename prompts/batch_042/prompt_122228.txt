Ive been thinking about collapse as a concept and I think Ive spotted an inconsistency nobody really addresses. There's this quantitative framework — I think it might come from Diamond but I cant place it exactly — where a society only genuinely collapses if it sheds over 70% of its large-scale institutions within a century and then fails to recover them for at least five hundred years. Apply that to Easter Island: the moai-building system and the hierarchical organisation behind it vanish permanently, population drops sharply, so thats clearly a collapse. The Bronze Age loses palaces and linear writing for a bit, but city-states and writing come back within a few centuries, so technically thats not a collapse by this rule, just a rough interlude. And the Maya never actually lose writing or agriculture; they just stop building massive ceremonial centres in the southern lowlands and reorganise politically further north. So if we take this metric seriously, Easter Island is the only true collapse of the three, and calling the others collapses is just journalists being dramatic. But I feel like there must be something wrong with using institutional recovery time as the deciding factor — maybe it ignores demographic trauma? Can someone point out the flaw? Am I missing why the Bronze Age still counts despite eventually bouncing back?
