I have been working through Daniel Dennett's heterophenomenological method as a framework for consciousness science, and I tried to formalize a worry that keeps returning no matter how I rearrange the premises. Here is the chain I wrote out for a seminar discussion. (1) Heterophenomenology treats a subject's first-person report not as direct access to private mental objects, but as an authoritative text describing the subject's heterophenomenological world — the world of intentional objects as they appear to that subject. (2) The method is deliberately neutral on whether that heterophenomenological world corresponds to any metaphysically real phenomenal realm; it takes the report as data about how things seem, not about how they are in themselves. (3) Now suppose we construct a system — call it a phenomenal mirror — that produces reports with identical semantic and syntactic structure to a human subject's reports about pain, color, or belief, but does so through mechanisms that involve no subjective experience whatsoever. (4) Because heterophenomenology evaluates reports solely by their internal coherence and their relation to other reports, and not by reference to any hidden intrinsic state, the method must assign this mirror exactly the same heterophenomenological world as the human. (5) If the heterophenomenological world is identical in both cases, and heterophenomenology is offered as the complete scientific account of consciousness, then science has no resources to distinguish the conscious human from the non-conscious mirror. (6) This is not merely an epistemic limitation; it is an ontological collapse, because the method has eliminated the very property — intrinsic phenomenal character — that was supposed to be explained. (7) Therefore heterophenomenology either fails as an account of consciousness or implicitly smuggles in a hidden criterion that violates its own neutrality. (8) But Dennett insists the method avoids both eliminativism and dualism, so (7) must be wrong somewhere. I want to keep (1) and (2) intact, since they seem to capture the core of the view, and I am reluctant to give up (3) because artificial report-generators are not science fiction anymore. Where does the chain break? Which step is the misstep, why did it seem plausible, and what does each available rejection cost — especially for someone who wants heterophenomenology to remain genuinely neutral without collapsing into behaviorism?
