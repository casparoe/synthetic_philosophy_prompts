Compare guilt and shame as candidates for the better moral emotion, but do so through a specific conceptual lens: treat each not merely as an individual psychological state, but as a normative institution that a culture can calibrate. First, distinguish guilt's structure—centered on a discrete act, internal judgment, and reparative action directed at a specific other—from shame's structure—centered on global self-evaluation, exposure before an audience, and either concealment or reintegration. Second, evaluate which structure is more conducive to moral repair without collapsing into either perpetual self-abasement or cheap transactional forgiveness. Third, address the constructivist challenge: if the normative force of these emotions depends partly on the social practices that regulate them, can a culture rationally choose to privilege one over the other, or does such a choice presuppose a pre-institutional standard of emotional appropriateness? In your answer, avoid treating either emotion as a universal constant; instead, examine whether the very distinction between them is stable across different normative architectures.
