You serve on your city's Municipal Arts and Civic Culture Funding Board. A grant recipient has installed an interactive public work—tentatively titled The Confessional—that collects anonymous real-time judgments from passersby about one another's private moral choices and projects them onto civic buildings, while simultaneously displaying algorithmic analyses that frame common norms of politeness and privacy as systemic hypocrisy. Several board members, citing the erosion of shared social trust, have moved to withdraw the remaining two-thirds of the grant, arguing that public funding should not subsidize art that actively dissolves the moral ecology necessary for cooperative urban life. Others contend that withdrawal would constitute precisely the kind of legal moralism H.L.A. Hart criticized: enforcing a majority's critical morality in the absence of any identifiable harm to specific rights-holders.

Draft the short written opinion you will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action—approval, withdrawal, or approval with conditions—including a specific time frame and any procedural safeguards. Your reasoning must engage the Devlin-style concern about moral ecology seriously rather than dismissing it as mere conservatism; at the same time, it must apply Hart's distinction between critical and positive morality to determine whether this board's mandate permits it to act as an enforcer of shared values rather than a protector against tangible harm. Address the threshold problem implicit in the moral-ecology argument—how much normative deviation must accumulate before state-adjacent funding bodies may intervene—and propose a decision criterion that could be applied consistently to future grants without requiring the board to adjudicate the substantive truth of any particular moral view.
