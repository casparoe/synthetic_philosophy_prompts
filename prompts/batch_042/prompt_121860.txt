As someone who spent three decades in experimental physics before turning seriously to philosophy of mind, I find the standard treatment of the absent-minded driver problem—the case in which a motorist determined to reach Barcelona drives past the correct highway exit because, though he knows the route in general, he fails to register that he is presently on that highway—unsatisfying when analyzed purely in terms of flat propositional belief. Please argue that this case is better understood not as a contradiction in the driver’s doxastic states, but as a structural failure within hierarchical predictive processing: specifically, defend the claim that the driver’s high-level generative model correctly encodes his navigational goal and the overall route, while lower-level sensory predictions suppress the egocentric update that should trigger the exit maneuver, producing what we might call an “epistemic decoupling” between situated awareness and intentional action. In making your argument, explain why diagnosing the driver as simultaneously believing and not believing he is on the highway confuses representational levels that neuroscience treats as functionally distinct, and address whether this layered account preserves, dissolves, or transforms the intuition that the driver is genuinely irrational. I want a conceptual argument evaluated on its own terms, not an exegetical review; please do not provide a bibliography, citation list, or survey of the literature.
