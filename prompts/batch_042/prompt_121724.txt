I am writing a paper for a graduate seminar on comparative political ethics, and my professor has asked each student to defend a specific normative claim rather than survey existing literature. Over the past weeks we have been examining how Western anti-corruption frameworks—particularly those embedded in global transparency metrics and international legal conventions—tend to classify clientelism and ritual gift-giving as corruption by default, treating any exchange of material goods for political access as a deviation from an ideal of impersonal bureaucratic rationality.

I want to argue that this classification rests on a hidden ontological commitment: it assumes that the legitimate state is composed of atomized, pre-social individuals who enter contractual relations only by choice, so that any durable reciprocal obligation between official and constituent appears as a contamination of public reason by private interest. Against this, I want to claim that in many political traditions authority is not derived from an abstract mandate but is continuously constituted through networks of mutual obligation—gift-giving, patronage, and clientelism included—and that labeling these practices as corruption therefore does not describe an objective dysfunction but rather imposes a culturally specific model of personhood onto societies where it does not apply. The argument should work through the conceptual distinction between transactional corruption, in which a specific official act is exchanged for a specific private reward, and constitutive reciprocity, in which the gift creates the very social bond that makes authority recognizable as authority in the first place. I also want the argument to address the obvious objection: if we abandon the Western standard, do we lose all normative purchase, or can we still distinguish between healthy reciprocal obligation and genuinely predatory extraction? Please construct the argument in a way that shows why the Western framework is not merely one local standard among others but is actively misleading when applied to contexts where political legitimacy is relationally generated, and do so without relying on empirical case studies, bibliographic references, or citations.
