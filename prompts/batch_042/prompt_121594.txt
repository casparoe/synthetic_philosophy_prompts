I'm preparing the final examination for a graduate seminar on autonomy and consent, and I want an essay prompt that forces students well past the standard four-part taxonomy—disclosure, understanding, voluntariness, competence—into territory that cannot be resolved by reciting those labels. Last week, in conversation with a colleague in medical ethics, I kept returning to a scenario that doesn't map cleanly onto deception or coercion as they are usually taught: a patient with intact decision-making capacity is told every technically true fact about a high-risk neurosurgical intervention, yet the disclosure is organized within what we might call a "survival-framed salience architecture." In this framing, long-term cognitive and identity-related risks are mentioned with statistical precision but embedded in language and sequencing that directs emotional and deliberative attention almost exclusively toward immediate mortality reduction. The patient consents, experiences the long-term outcome, and later claims her consent was invalid—not because any proposition was false, nor because she was threatened, but because the informational architecture rendered certain risks practically invisible to her reasoning despite being technically present.

Please write a single exam question designed for a 90-minute response of about 800 words. The question should ask students to determine whether this scenario represents a failure of disclosure, a failure of competence, a disguised form of coercion, or a distinct category—say, "salience-mediated vitiation"—and to defend their taxonomy by appealing to the underlying normative purpose of consent rather than to checklist compliance. It should also require them to evaluate whether a purely procedural model of consent (all formal conditions satisfied) can accommodate the case, and whether remedying it demands reform to disclosure standards, to competence assessments, or to both simultaneously. Along with the question, provide a model answer of approximately 700 words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit analytical moves, assigning specific marks to each (total 25), with a one-line note on how candidates typically miss or botch each move, so that the scheme clearly separates an A-level response from a B-level one. Do not include a bibliography or citation list; I will supply references myself.
