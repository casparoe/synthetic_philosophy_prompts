I am drafting a philosophical novel in which an eighteenth-century consciousness is gradually reconstructed within a distributed computing architecture—an anachronistic premise, but one that allows me to probe whether historical theories of personal identity can withstand technological scenarios never imagined by their authors. In particular, I want to understand how John Locke might have responded to the logic of Derek Parfit's teletransporter, not in the familiar instantaneous replication form, but in a variant I have devised for the narrative: a subject undergoes a decades-long, neuron-by-neuron replacement by synthetic substrates, maintaining continuous first-person awareness throughout, yet periodically generating temporary "branch" instances that possess full self-consciousness and memory continuity for brief intervals before being merged back into the primary stream.

Given Locke's well-known rejection of substance-based identity in favor of consciousness and memory continuity, I wonder how he would adjudicate whether the final reconstructed individual is identical to the original, and—more subtly—whether those ephemeral branch copies, which experienced subjective existence but were ultimately dissolved, possessed a moral claim to survival that the primary stream somehow violated. I ask not for bibliographic references or reading lists, but for a disciplined reconstruction of Locke's likely reasoning, applied carefully to this hybrid scenario that blends Parfit's replication problem with the incremental replacement problem, and for an honest account of where Locke's framework illuminates the case, where it falls silent, and whether the very existence of those transient branches reveals a tension in his account that only becomes visible once continuity is made granular rather than binary.
