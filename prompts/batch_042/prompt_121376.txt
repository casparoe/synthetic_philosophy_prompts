I work as a software engineer building multilingual document-processing pipelines, and I have been evaluating statistical and neural translation systems on extended texts that contain structured relationships across sentences—social hierarchies, logical dependencies, and argumentative polarities. I have noticed a recurring failure mode that is not captured by the usual vocabulary of mistranslation or grammatical error, and I am trying to determine whether philosophy of language or translation studies already has a precise name for it.

The pattern works like this. At the local level—individual clauses or sentences—the output is fluent, grammatically correct, and lexically appropriate. The failure is global and relational: across a sequence, the system preserves surface coherence but systematically inverts or dissolves the very structure that organizes the sequence.

Two concrete instances I have observed:

First, in translated multi-turn Japanese business correspondence, honorific registers are assigned correctly within each utterance according to grammatical role. Yet across the exchange, the hierarchical relation is flipped: the subordinate’s request is rendered with language appropriate to a superior, and the superior’s reply employs humble forms appropriate to a subordinate. Each sentence is defensible in isolation; only the full dialogue reveals that the social architecture has been inverted.

Second, in translated philosophical argumentation, an anaphoric chain linking a thesis to its counter-arguments is preserved grammatically—pronouns agree in gender and number—but the logical relation is dissolved. A pronoun that should refer back to the original thesis is resolved instead to the opposing position, not because of source ambiguity, but because the system tracks lexical proximity rather than argumentative role. The result reads as a fluent monologue about a single view, when the source was explicitly dialectical.

Defenders of the technology treat these as context-window limitations or rare coherence errors, noting that per-sentence accuracy remains high. Critics treat them as evidence that the system lacks understanding of the domain. I am not asking for that broad debate—something in the vein of Hofstadter’s concern about shallow, symbol-manipulating processing—but rather whether there is an established, narrower concept that names exactly this configuration: local syntactic fidelity coupled with systematic cross-local inversion of relational structure.

If such a concept exists, how does it differ from ordinary discourse-coherence failure, from context-truncation artifacts, and from simple equivocation? If it does not exist, can you propose a term with clear boundary conditions that separate it from those neighboring failures, specifying what must be present—fluent local output, a preserved but inverted global relation, and the absence of random noise—to count as an instance?
