Design a classroom simulation for an upper-level philosophy seminar of approximately 14 students, running 80 minutes, that lets participants experience the tension in moral revolutions—specifically whether a norm collapses through rational argument, through shifting material interests, or through the reconfiguration of honor and shame dynamics—drawing on Bernard Williams's suggestion that moral change often tracks the erosion of honor structures rather than the victory of arguments, and on Kwame Anthony Appiah's account of honor worlds sustained by interlocking status rewards that can be redirected. Rather than using slavery or dueling directly, construct the exercise around an invented institutional practice—call it "binding challenge," a formalized dispute-resolution ritual in an imagined mercantile republic governed by a tripartite honor-credit ledger that ties reputation, kinship obligation, and commercial surety together. The rules should assign roles (arbitrators, challengers, creditors, chroniclers), include hidden payoff interdependencies that make honor credits non-fungible with material gain, and build in an exogenous shock at roughly the 35-minute mark that silently rewrites the status rewards without announcing a normative change. Include a precise timeline (setup, two deliberation rounds, shock, resolution), a debrief that maps each participant move onto the argument/interest/honor distinction, and an honest note about what the activity cannot reproduce—likely the long temporal lag between elite status-shifting and widespread normative consciousness that both thinkers describe, which a single session must artificially compress and the facilitator must supply. Also specify what will predictably fail on the first run—such as students treating the honor ledger as pure currency and missing the shame component—and how to correct it in the moment. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the deliverable should be runnable without any external sources.
