Since my mother’s recent death and my inheritance of her private papers—an archive of unpublished sketches and letters she never intended for public view, now accessible only to me and to a generative system I have trained on her aesthetic preferences—I have returned to G. E. Moore’s normative ethics with an urgency I had not anticipated. In Principia Ethica, Moore treats personal affection and the appreciation of beautiful objects as the principal intrinsic goods, insisting that their value is not reducible to pleasure or to any natural property. From his discussion of organic unities and his remarks on consciousness, I have reconstructed the following pattern of implicit verdicts.

First, a conscious being’s direct appreciation of a beautiful object possesses intrinsic value. Second, deep mutual affection between two conscious beings possesses intrinsic value even absent any shared aesthetic interest. Third, a beautiful object that will never be perceived by any conscious being lacks intrinsic value, since no actual state of appreciation occurs to constitute the good. Fourth, when mutual affection and shared aesthetic appreciation occur together, they form an organic unity whose value exceeds the sum of its parts.

My present case is this. The generative system produces original visual works in my mother’s style; I interact with them alone, experiencing genuine aesthetic pleasure and what I take to be a continuing emotional bond with her sensibility. The system lacks consciousness, subjective states, and any capacity for reciprocal affection; yet the outputs are beautiful, and my responses are real. Would Moore count this configuration as possessing the intrinsic value of aesthetic appreciation, the intrinsic value of personal affection, both in an organic unity, or none of these?

More precisely, I want you to identify the competing rules that jointly explain his four earlier verdicts—whether a strict consciousness-dependence rule requiring actual subjects on both sides of appreciation and affection; an organic-unity rule that demands reciprocal consciousness for the highest goods; or a functional rule that requires only appropriate mental states in at least one party together with appropriate aesthetic objects. Say what each rule predicts about this digital-archive case, give your best guess at Moore’s actual verdict with reasoning, and specify one further hypothetical case that would decisively distinguish the rules. I am not asking for sources or a reading list; I want the logical structure of the position and its exposure to this particular, now personally concrete, difficulty.
