As a novelist working on a cycle about artificial forms of disclosure, I have been reading Bernard Williams’s philosophical reflections on opera—particularly his claim that the genre’s inherent stylization, in which characters sing rather than speak and music bears the emotional architecture, does not obscure reality but strips away the social mediation and self-consciousness of ordinary dialogue to reveal feeling with an almost unsettling directness. I want a short story that makes this claim palpable without explaining it, and specifically not in an opera house. Imagine a protagonist in an ordinary contemporary crisis—perhaps delivering a mandatory public address, recording a message for someone they cannot face, or being compelled into a ritualized choral performance—who finds that the prescribed artifice of the form (repetition, melodic constraint, rhythmic rigidity) suddenly allows them to articulate an emotional truth they had been unable to speak in conversational prose. The narrative should treat this as a lived phenomenological event rather than a metaphor: the moment when stylization becomes transparency. Keep the philosophical undertone implicit; I am interested in whether fiction can render Williams’s idea that operatic artificiality achieves a kind of emotional honesty more immediate than naturalistic speech. Write it with the precision and compression of literary fiction, not exposition.
