I have been developing a framework on moral emotions for an independent philosophy project, and I want a consistency audit before I present it. Here is where I stand. First, guilt is morally preferable to shame because it targets specific actions rather than global self-worth, which preserves the agent’s capacity for repair rather than paralysis. Second, a culture can collectively choose to institutionalize guilt over shame through education, ritual, and social norms; this is not biologically fixed but is part of a culture’s moral emotional architecture. Third, moral emotions are partly constituted by the practices that frame them, so guilt’s superiority is not a culture-independent fact but is relative to values such as autonomy, accountability, and repair. Fourth, despite this constructivist element, a culture that institutionalizes shame can still be criticized for moral error, especially when it suppresses individual dignity. Fifth, the guilt-shame distinction is not binary but a spectrum, with hybrid emotional states being common in lived experience. Sixth, individuals within a shame-based culture can rationally cultivate guilt-oriented responses without waiting for collective change, and doing so is morally progressive rather than disloyal.

Please audit these six commitments for joint tenability. Which pairs pull against each other in ways that are not obvious? Which tensions are merely apparent and dissolve once the commitments are interpreted charitably? Rank the conflicts by severity, and for each genuine tension specify which commitment is the weakest link and what giving it up would cost the overall framework. I am especially unsure whether my claim about collective institutional choice survives the spectrum view, and whether my universal preference for guilt can coexist with the idea that moral emotions are practice-constituted, but please treat all pairs systematically rather than focusing only on those. I want to know whether the package is salvageable without abandoning the core idea that a culture can deliberately shape its dominant moral emotion.
