In democratic theory, the demos is frequently analogized to a private members' club: it drafts its own charter, elects its own officers, and sets admission rules without answering to outsiders, which is then used to argue that boundary-drawing is purely self-determining and owes no justification to the excluded. Audit this analogy explicitly: build the correspondence table (charter, membership vote, club assets, excluded applicant, and so on), then isolate the disanalogies that bear specifically on democratic legitimacy rather than on private association—especially the fact that a polity’s economic and institutional infrastructure is often built substantially through the labor and extraction of people now residing outside its territorial boundary with no path to membership. Does the club analogy still license the conclusion that there is no independent standard of inclusion, or does this feature force a revision? Identify what normative assumptions the analogy quietly imports about voluntary entry, shared purpose, and the separability of goods, and propose a better analogy—perhaps an inherited estate, a corporation with non-voting stakeholders, or something else—saying whether switching analogies changes the verdict on who belongs to the demos.
