Following a rather exhausting debate with a close friend last night over whether institutional history is genuinely contingent or merely appears so from our present vantage point, I have found myself unable to stop replaying an exchange I witnessed earlier this week between two colleagues, Rowan and Sam, during a seminar on historical ontology. Their disagreement centred on the evolution of peer-review norms in the natural sciences, and specifically on whether the current configuration — double-blind evaluation by two or three anonymous referees, high rejection rates, and substantial editorial gatekeeping — represents a genuinely path-dependent outcome that might easily have failed to emerge, or rather a functional convergence that any mature scientific community would eventually approximate. Rowan opened by arguing that the present system is locked in by contingent mid-twentieth-century developments: the particular post-war expansion of state funding in specific disciplines, the accidental dominance of a handful of journals that adopted anonymised review for administrative convenience rather than epistemic principle, and the subsequent network effects that made deviation costly for individual researchers. She insisted that if one reran the history of scientific publishing without these particular contingencies — say, in a counterfactual world where open, signed evaluation had gained institutional traction first — the community would have arrived at a radically different equilibrium, closer to post-publication commentary or transparent collaborative assessment, and that this demonstrates history is genuinely locked in by its specific trajectory rather than pulled inexorably toward a unique functional endpoint.

Sam responded that Rowan was conflating historical origin with historical destiny, and that her counterfactual relied on an implausible suppression of persistent functional pressures. He granted that the precise institutional packaging was contingent — the number of reviewers, the particular forms used — but maintained that the underlying problem structure, namely the need to filter claims in conditions of specialised expertise, limited reader attention, and potential conflicts of interest, would have generated functionally equivalent mechanisms regardless of the initial path. He observed that even open-review experiments eventually reintroduce anonymity or hierarchical filtering in practice, suggesting convergence rather than divergence. Rowan countered that this appeal to functional equivalence smuggled in the very outcome it sought to explain: the problems Sam identified, such as reviewer overload or bias in evaluation, are themselves artefacts of the locked-in system, not pre-institutional constants that would exist independently of it. Sam then claimed that this objection merely demonstrated feedback dynamics within a stable equilibrium, not path dependence in the philosophically interesting sense, since a system can be both self-reinforcing and functionally optimal. Rowan concluded that Sam's position reduced path dependence to a trivial claim about institutional inertia, stripping it of any real explanatory power regarding why history unfolds as it does rather than otherwise. Could you adjudicate their exchange move by move — assessing Rowan's initial contingency claim, Sam's convergence reply, Rowan's artefactual objection, and Sam's equilibrium defence — indicating who is right about each specific point and what, if anything, both parties are failing to address about the nature of historical lock-in?
