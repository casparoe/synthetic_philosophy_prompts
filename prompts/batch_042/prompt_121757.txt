I've been thinking about Good's theorem on the value of information—the result that a rational agent's expected utility cannot fall by acquiring free information and then acting optimally. The standard rationalist takeaway is simple: never refuse free data. The usual objections are "curiosity killed the cat" scenarios, where knowing something triggers a harmful action, but defenders reply that an optimal decision rule simply conditions on the knowledge. I want to push on a less-discussed angle that I haven't seen worked out in the forums: call it reflexive drift. Suppose an agent knows that receiving information of a specific kind—say, a precise forecast about her own future emotional state—will not only update her world-model but will, with predictable probability, corrupt the very preference ordering she currently uses to evaluate outcomes. Write an essay weighing whether this undermines Good's result. On one side, defend the view that ex ante preferences govern and any post-update shift is just another state to be conditioned upon, so the theorem holds if the state space is expanded. On the other, argue that if the agent can anticipate the corruption, the information is not value-neutral and Good's non-negativity fails for the preferences she actually cares about preserving. Reach and justify a concrete conclusion: does reflexive drift carve out a principled exception to "always take free information," or can the rationalist framework absorb it without loss?
