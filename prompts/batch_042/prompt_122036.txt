Here are notes for an essay on whether the Great Divergence framing—specifically the contrastive question “why Britain and not China?”—is well posed. The notes argue that the formulation implicitly reifies Britain and China as homogeneous units with singular developmental trajectories, thereby obscuring substantial internal variation, such as the proto-industrial dynamics of the Yangtze Delta versus other Chinese macro-regions, or pronounced regional disparities within Britain itself. They propose replacing the default nation-state comparison with a “modular” approach that pairs sub-national regions with comparable ecological endowments and institutional configurations. The notes further claim that Joseph Needham originally framed the problem in epistemic and scientific-cultural terms rather than in the competitive economic-industrial terms favored by modern economic historians, and that conflating these two registers has distorted contemporary debate. Please comment on these notes: first, on whether the modular-comparison proposal genuinely resolves the reification problem or merely displaces it onto smaller geographic units; second, on whether the distinction drawn between Needham’s epistemic concern and modern economic-history usage is defensible or artificially sharp; and third, on whether the notes overlook a significant counter-argument that the contrastive question remains fruitful precisely because nation-state-level institutions (fiscal-military capacity, colonial access) were the decisive causal variables.
