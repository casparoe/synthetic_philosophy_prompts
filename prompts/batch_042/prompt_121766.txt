I am preparing material for a graduate seminar on hinge epistemology, and I want to test whether Wittgenstein's framework in On Certainty can illuminate a concrete historical clash of world-pictures rather than only abstract skeptical puzzles. Consider the 1925 Scopes Trial in Dayton, Tennessee: treat it not as a culture-war anecdote or a straightforward legal contest over the Butler Act, but as an encounter between two competing hinge structures. On one side stood a framework in which biblical revelation operates as an unmoving hinge—propositions such as the literal creation of the Earth in six days and the special creation of human beings function not as falsifiable empirical hypotheses but as the bedrock against which particular scientific claims are evaluated and, if necessary, rejected. On the other side stood a scientific hinge in which methodological naturalism and deep-time cosmology form the background certainties, rendering literalist creationism not merely false but largely unintelligible as a serious contender within that framework.

Please analyze the trial through this lens with a disciplined two-directional mapping. Identify the actual players—Darrow, Bryan, the jury, the scientific expert witnesses who were prepared but never permitted to testify—and specify for each side what counted as movable empirical claim versus unmoving hinge certainty. Examine what Darrow's cross-examination of Bryan actually accomplished: did it expose an internal contradiction within Bryan's hinge framework, or did it merely demonstrate that Bryan's hinges were incommensurable with Darrow's? Then explain what the hinge-epistemology account captures that standard sociology-of-science narratives miss—particularly the phenomenon that neither side experienced the dispute as a disagreement about evidence that further data could settle. Finally, be explicit about where the framework breaks down or falls silent: does it have anything to say about the pedagogical and legal stakes of the trial, or about why one hinge framework eventually achieved institutional dominance in American public education despite the trial's immediate outcome?

To push the analysis beyond a standard application, I ask you to introduce and apply a distinction I am currently testing—between vertical hinges, which are world-structuring certainties about what exists and how it behaves, and horizontal hinges, which are methodological and social certainties about who possesses authority to speak, what counts as legitimate expertise, and how democratic majorities may properly set curricular boundaries. Assess whether the Scopes Trial was primarily a conflict of vertical hinges, or whether its deepest incommensurability lay at the horizontal level, with each side operating under different unmoving certainties about the proper relationship between popular sovereignty and scientific authority. Where the distinction clarifies the trial's dynamics, use it; where it obscures them, say so, and indicate whether the failure reveals a limitation in the distinction itself or in Wittgenstein's broader account of how hinge conflicts are resolved.
