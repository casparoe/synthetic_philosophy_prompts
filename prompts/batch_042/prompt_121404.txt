okay so i've been digging into famine explanations and there's a recurring move i don't have a word for. in bengal '43, the irish famine, and the great leap forward you always see the same structure: there's an obvious natural trigger -- monsoon failure, blight, drought -- and people grab that as the explanation. but then you check the food availability numbers and they're not catastrophic; what actually kills people is entitlement collapse or deliberate distribution failure, often political. sen's framework handles the mechanism, but that's not my point.

the pattern is specifically this rhetorical/conceptual slide: using the precipitating event (weather, crop disease) to *close* the causal story, so that asking about political responsibility feels like adding an unnecessary layer. people do it across completely different regimes and centuries. it's not just ignorance of entitlement theory; it's treating a meteorological fact as if it were an explanitory endpoint.

is there an established name for that slide? i don't mean ordinary causal overdetermination, and i don't mean the general 'disasters are socially mediated' point. i mean the specific error of letting a natural trigger absorb all the explanatory weight, blocking the political analysis. if there's no term, can we coin one and map its boundaries? specifically: how is it different from (1) a legitimate partial cause, (2) a motte-and-bailey where 'famine was caused by drought' is the defensible core, and (3) just not knowing sen's work? i want the carving, not a bare label.
