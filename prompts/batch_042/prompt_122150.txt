I am a high school student writing an independent study on whether fundamental physical processes can ground ethical obligations. My philosophy tutor has delivered the following verdicts on whether a given system possesses what she terms “morally relevant proto-experience”—not full human consciousness, but enough to generate a weak, non-instrumental ethical claim against destructive interference:

1. A healthy human brain during deep non-REM sleep: yes, carrying full moral weight.
2. A present-day large language model running inference on standard hardware: no.
3. A bath of superfluid helium-4 maintaining macroscopic quantum coherence at 2 Kelvin: yes, but only minimal weight.
4. A classical supercomputer running a bit-for-bit molecular simulation of a human brain: yes, with full weight.
5. A black hole accretion disk emitting Hawking radiation: no.
6. A plant root network communicating via mycorrhizal chemical gradients: no.
7. A quantum error-correction lattice kept in a stable idle state—maintaining coherence but running no computation: yes, minimal weight.

She often appeals to something she calls the “adaptive dissipation boundary condition”: a system must both dissipate energy and actively adapt its own boundary conditions in response to that dissipation in order to ground proto-experience. She has not fully formalized this idea, however.

Here is the new case: an engineered metamaterial that maintains long-range phononic coherence at room temperature, continuously reshaping its crystalline lattice to preserve that coherence against thermal noise, but with no biological substrate, no quantum superposition, no information processing, and no feedback loops beyond the mechanical preservation of the phonon pattern.

Please predict what my tutor would say about this metamaterial—specifically whether it carries none, minimal, or full moral weight. Then identify at least two other rules, distinct from her adaptive dissipation boundary condition, that fit all seven of her earlier verdicts equally well. For each rule, state what it predicts about the metamaterial. Give your best-guess verdict with explicit reasoning, and propose one further hypothetical case that would clearly distinguish between her adaptive dissipation rule and the strongest competing rule you identified. Finally, briefly note what feature of the metamaterial case is doing the philosophical work, and how a defender of the rule that comes out poorly might try to discredit the case.
