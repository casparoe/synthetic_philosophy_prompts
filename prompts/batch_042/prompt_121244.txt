Please audit the garden analogy that is commonly deployed to explain heritability in popular and policy discourse: genetic differences are likened to different seed varieties, environmental differences to soil and weather conditions, and the heritability coefficient is taken to indicate what proportion of the variation in harvest size is attributable to the seeds rather than to the growing environment. In debates about education and social mobility, this analogy is sometimes extended to claim that if heritability for academic attainment is high within a given society, then expansive redistributive investment in schooling or social conditions is largely futile, since the 'soil' can only alter outcomes so far. Lay out the correspondence table precisely—what corresponds to the seeds, the soil, the weather, the harvest, and the coefficient—then analyse where the mapping collapses, with particular attention to gene-environment interaction, the population-specific nature of heritability estimates, and the illicit normative leap from a descriptive partition of variance to a prescriptive conclusion about resource allocation. Conclude by judging whether the anti-interventionist policy claim survives once these disanalogies are made fully explicit, or whether the analogy was shouldering the argumentative burden all along.
