People explaining Grice’s distinction between what is said and what is implicated often reach for a two-stage rocket analogy: the first stage—literal saying—gets the utterance off the ground by fixing conventional content through disambiguation and reference assignment, while the second stage—conversational implicature—ignites once the Cooperative Principle and its maxims come online and carries the message to the speaker’s actual intended meaning. The analogy is used to defend the view that literal content is always genuinely present and computationally prior, but never sufficient on its own; understanding requires the second-stage inference. Audit this analogy carefully. Give me the explicit correspondence table—what plays the role of the first-stage fuel, the second-stage ignition, the trajectory correction, the payload, and ground control. Then identify where the mapping breaks down: consider cases of direct metaphor or irony, where some philosophers argue there is no genuinely asserted literal proposition serving as a fired ‘first stage’; consider generalized conversational implicatures that seem to arise without specific contextual premises acting as ‘ignition’; and consider whether the analogy smuggles in a modular architecture—encoding, then decoding, then inferring—that Grice’s own framework may not require. Does the conclusion that literal meaning is always present but always incomplete survive these disanalogies, or does the rocket picture collapse once we allow that in some utterances the first stage is never actually launched?
