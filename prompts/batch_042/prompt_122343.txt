My study group is trying to apply Ramsey's dissolution of "the meaning of life" to smaller-scale cases and we've ended up with an overreach. I cant see the exit. (1) In the Epilogue to The Foundations of Mathematics and Other Logical Essays, Ramsey treats "what is the meaning of life?" as a pseudo-problem: "meaning" gets pulled between its linguistic sense and a cosmic-importance sense, and "life" works as a singular noun that can stand either for a biological process or for the totality of everything. (2) If this dissolution works, it works structurally: any singular noun that can denote both a concrete practice and a totality will generate the same confusion when paired with "purpose" or "meaning". (3) So "science" should be just as vulnerable — it denotes both specific methods and the totality of human inquiry — and asking "what is the purpose of science?" ought to dissolve in exactly the same way. (4) But Ramsey clearly doesnt think scientific inquiry is conceptually confused; he takes aims and methods in science to be perfectly legitimate. (5) So either the dissolution is restricted by an unstated boundary condition, or it overreaches and kills all teleological questions. (6) We think the hidden boundary is scale combined with infinity: Ramsey mentions that where eternity and infinity are concerned we shouldnt feel heroic emotions, so maybe the pseudo-problem only triggers when the object is literally universal or infinite. (7) But "my life" isnt infinite, and "humanity" probably isnt either, yet both seem grammatically exposed to the same slide between local and total reference. (8) So if we keep (1) and reject (6), we need a principled reason why cosmic life is uniquely confused; if we keep (6), we need to explain why "science" escapes. Which step should we actually drop, and what does giving it up cost? I'd rather keep (1) than (5), but I want to know exactly what I'm buying. Please answer in British English.
