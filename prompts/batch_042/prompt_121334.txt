I am a postgraduate student reading for an MPhil in Historical Theory, and I also work part-time as a research assistant for a municipal heritage trust that is curating a small exhibition on the contested closure of the Meridian Textile Mill in 1928. Our board receives frequent submissions—oral history transcripts collected in the 1970s, digitised fragments of local newspapers with probable OCR errors, corporate minute books held in private hands, and retrospective affidavits prepared for a 1960s compensation claim—and we lack any consistent method for deciding which materials should shape the public narrative and which should be relegated to a footnote or excluded entirely.

Drawing on the principles of source criticism that run from Herodotus’s distinction between direct observation and hearsay through Thucydides’s insistence on contemporary verification and onward to modern archival concepts such as provenance, original order, and the distinction between external and internal criticism, please construct a reusable decision procedure that a non-specialist researcher could apply in under thirty minutes per source. The procedure should take the form of ordered questions or tests: first for authenticity and provenance (external criticism), then for credibility, bias, and internal consistency (internal criticism), with explicit stopping points where a source is rejected, explicit hand-off points where the decision becomes a matter of curatorial values rather than epistemic fact, and explicit flags for cases the procedure cannot settle. It must handle at least three hard cases: an oral testimony recorded in 1975 that describes workplace conditions in 1928 but conflicts with the corporate ledger; a digitised newspaper clipping in which OCR errors have introduced a phantom manager’s name that alters the causal story; and a retrospective affidavit drafted in 1965 by a former foreman seeking compensation, where institutional incentives and the passage of time intersect. Apply the procedure to each case, including one where it yields an answer the heritage board is likely to dislike—perhaps the exclusion of a vivid oral account that fails internal criticism, or the inclusion of a dry but reliable ledger that undermines the preferred narrative of worker solidarity. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: which judgments it deliberately leaves to curators, where it is likely to misfire with digitised or mediated sources, and why no procedural substitute exists for the historian’s ultimate responsibility for interpretation.
