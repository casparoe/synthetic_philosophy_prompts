I serve as a senior analyst for a regional infrastructure board that makes allocation-mechanism decisions roughly twice a month—specifically, whether a given resource sector should be governed by central price-setting and quota assignment or left to local district managers with decentralized adjustment authority. I am currently working through the socialist calculation debate in an informal study group, and I want to move past abstract summaries of Hayek’s dispersed-knowledge objection, Mises’s argument about the impossibility of rational economic calculation without market prices, and Lange’s competitive-socialist reply that simulated markets can recover price signals within public ownership. What I need is a reusable, two-hour decision procedure—call it a knowledge-topology audit—that my non-economist staff can run on any proposal without consulting outside literature. The audit should classify the decision-relevant information across four dimensions I want treated as decisive: spatial dispersal (how localized the data is), tacitness (how much can be fully articulated in standardized reports versus remaining embodied in practice), temporal volatility (how quickly conditions shift relative to reporting cycles), and interdependence (how strongly local variables interact with neighboring districts). For each dimension, specify the threshold questions, what evidence counts as sufficient, and how the combined profile maps onto one of three institutional forms—full centralization, full decentralization, or a hybrid with bounded local discretion—with explicit stop-rules: a clear “do not centralize” verdict, a clear “centralization is epistemically permissible” verdict, and the precise points where the audit must halt and return the question to the board as a value judgment rather than a knowledge assessment. The procedure should be honest about its failure modes, especially when political pressure to centralize conflicts with the knowledge profile, and it should require no bibliographies or citation checks.

Please run the audit on three concrete cases. First, real-time load-balancing tariffs for a municipal grid during heat waves, where local managers hold granular usage patterns but the board favors uniform carbon pricing—treat this as the case where the procedure likely contradicts my strong prior that central pricing is always preferable, and explain exactly which dimension forces that result. Second, emergency drought-water rations across agricultural districts, where soil-moisture sensors provide partial quantitative data but local irrigation practices and informal water-sharing agreements remain largely unrecorded. Third, preventive maintenance scheduling for aging bridges, where wear depends on unreported local traffic overloads and micro-climatic exposure. For each, show the step-by-step classification, the institutional verdict, and the specific feature the procedure cannot resolve without a normative call. Finally, state clearly what would change your assessment of the audit’s reliability: which empirical discovery, conceptual refinement, or counter-argument about the aggregation of tacit knowledge would lead you to revise the mapping between knowledge topology and institutional form, and identify the weakest link in the reasoning chain—the point where a skeptical colleague could most plausibly drive the procedure to collapse.
