I've been stuck in this arguement with my study partner about Parfit's hitchhiker for months now and I'm genuinely unsure whether we're talking past each other or if there's actually something deep going on. You know the case: the driver won't pick you up unless you promise to pay once you arrive, but after you're dropped off there's no further incentive to pay, yet it seems rational to want to be the kind of agent who follows through. In our little seminar, half the people say this proves rationality needs some diachronic commitment mechanism built into practical reason—like you have to be able to bind your future self—while the other half say it's just an agent with strange preferences, and once you're safe there's genuinely no reason to pay, end of story. We've run through causal decision theory, evidential reasoning, reputation effects, precommitment devices, and nobody moves an inch.

My working theory is that the stall isn't about rationality at all, but about a hidden disagreement over whether practical reasons can be time-indexed in a way that binds a future self that might not share your current values—basically a metaphysical question about personal identity sneaking in under the label rationality. But I could be wrong. Is this actually a verbal dispute about what rational means? A substantive disagreement about the structure of practical reason? Or are the two sides answering different questions—one normative, one descriptive—while using the same example? And given whatever diagnosis you land on, what would count as actual progress? A new formal result, a conceptual distinction we haven't drawn, or is this one of those debates where the intuitions are just bedrock and no argument will shift them?
