Having retired from a career in biophysics and only recently begun working seriously through philosophy of medicine and metaphysics, I find the standard clinical framing of death unsatisfying: it presents the issue as a binary contest between brain-death and circulatory-death criteria without first specifying what any adequate philosophical theory of human death must actually deliver. Before adjudicating between those two clinical standards, I want the prior conceptual labor done. Write the complete desiderata—what phenomena it must explain, what distinctions it must preserve, what boundary cases it must handle correctly, and what biological results it must not contradict, with the reasoning behind each requirement. In particular, address how the theory treats prolonged somatic integration in brain-dead bodies that nevertheless sustain gestation or homeostatic function; how it distinguishes the irreversible loss of consciousness or personhood from the cessation of the organism as a unified biological whole; and how it accommodates, without collapsing, boundary conditions involving split-brain architecture, anencephalic persistence, or partial neural preservation in which some integrative capacities remain while others are permanently abolished. Specify which of these desiderata are genuinely non-negotiable and which are merely preferences of particular medical-legal camps—for instance, a demand for deterministic physiological markers versus tolerance for probabilistic ones—and flag any pair or subset you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable given current neuroscience or the metaphysics of organisms. I want a committed response, not a neutral taxonomy: take a definite position on which two or three requirements do the heaviest philosophical work, defend that ordering, and indicate precisely which popular clinical criteria fail because they neglect those core demands. Please do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I am working without access to external sources and need the conceptual architecture to stand entirely on its own reasoning.
