My reliabilism-obsessed friend has these takes on knowledge and circularity: if you use your eyesight to verifiy your eyesight is working, that's circular and you don't learn anything; same if you use a thermometer calibrated against itself. But she allows cross-checking — using touch to confirm sight, or a second thermometer of a different design. On the "easy knowledge" side, she says knowing you have hands lets you know you're not a brain in a vat, but only if your hand-knowledge wasn't justified by first ruling out BIV. She draws a hard line at "no shared premises" in the justification chain.

Here's a weird case I ran into: there's an old practice in analog computing where you build a circuit that simulates a physical system, then use measurements of the actual system to tune the circuit's resistors, and then claim the circuit "independently predicts" the system's future behavior because it matched past data. But the past data was used to set the resistors! Would she call this epistemic bootstrapping? Would she say an engineer who trusts the circuit's 2030 predicition "knows" it? I'm less interested in yes/no than in what rule she's actually running — is it about shared premises, shared methods, temporal ordering, or something about causal independence? Which of her earlier verdicts is the closest analog? And what new case would actually split her rules apart?
