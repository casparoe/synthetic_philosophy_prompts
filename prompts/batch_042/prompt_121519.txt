In a graduate seminar on environmental historiography, a colleague kept invoking the phrase "nature is not a backdrop" to defend the claim that we ought to list the 1815 Tambora eruption and the bacterial spread of plague alongside Napoleon and the Committee of Public Safety as genuine historical actors, rather than treating them as mere background conditions. Before I challenge her in the next session, I want to understand exactly what that slogan could be committing her to. Could you lay out, entirely in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, boldface, or tables, the distinct precise theses that "nature is not a backdrop" might express: beginning with the modest claim that physical and biological conditions constrain human choice; then the stronger explanatory thesis that non-human processes must appear in the causal narrative with independent explanatory weight; then the metaphysical claim that entities like volcanoes or pathogens possess a form of agency analogous to human intentionality; and finally the radical historiographical position that explanatory credit should be distributed across a network of human and non-human nodes without privileging consciousness or intention? For each reading, please say whether it is defensible, whether it genuinely escapes the charge of environmental determinism, and whether it actually licenses treating a volcanic eruption or an infectious agent as an actor in the same sense as a statesman. I want to be able to read your response straight through like a long letter and then use the distinctions to frame my questions for office hours.
