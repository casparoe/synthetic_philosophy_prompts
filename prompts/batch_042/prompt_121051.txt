I'm drafting a novel about a neuroscientist-poet who insists she has purged all private-sensation talk from her research, yet her journals describe colors and pains with such specificity that readers feel them viscerally. A philosopher-character challenges her with an analogy: to invoke her own qualia in writing is like trying to quote a word without using it—if the sensation enters language, it becomes public and forfeits its supposed ineffability; if it never enters language, she describes nothing at all. I want the dispute to turn not on whether she "really" has the sensations, but on whether the very idea of quoting a private state without publicizing it collapses into contradiction, and whether a consistent middle position—treating the descriptions as purely functional predictions rather than phenomenal reports—can survive without sliding into either full-blooded realism or outright eliminativism. Is the incoherence specific to phenomenal experience, or does it apply to any theoretical posit accessible only by indirect report? And if a middle ground exists, what hidden premise would it have to deny about the relation between language and experience?
