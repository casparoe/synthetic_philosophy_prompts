Since receiving a provisional diagnosis that required me to evaluate how clinical measurements validate themselves—when the same instrument both produces and certifies its own readings—I have become personally preoccupied with the bootstrapping problem in epistemology, particularly the problem of easy knowledge raised against reliabilist accounts of basic knowledge. For a graduate seminar I am organizing on self-certifying belief systems, I need a taxonomy—not a prose comparison—of the positions on whether basic empirical knowledge can be used to justify the reliability of the cognitive process that produced it.

The deliverable should be a branching tree in which every internal node is a concrete diagnostic claim or scenario, phrased so that a student can answer it without knowing which philosophical label it leads to, and every leaf is either a named position or an explicitly unnamed combination, accompanied by one sentence on its most awkward commitment. The root should divide on whether one accepts basic knowledge: beliefs about particular objects (a red table, a clinical reading, a sensor output) that are known without being inferred from other beliefs. From there, the branches should separate positions on three axes that genuinely come apart: whether such basic knowledge can be deployed in reasoning about the reliability of its source without vicious circularity; whether the legitimacy of that reasoning depends on the contextual salience of skeptical alternatives (instrument malfunction, brain-in-a-vat scenarios); and whether successful bootstrapping yields genuine knowledge that the skeptical hypothesis is false, or merely practical entitlement to dismiss it.

Please include as leaves at least: an unrestricted reliabilist position that accepts the bootstrapping; a hard-line anti-bootstrapping view that denies the legitimacy of such reasoning entirely; a contextualist position that makes the reasoning's legitimacy depend on whether the skeptical scenario is a live option; an epistemic disjunctivist position that avoids the problem by treating skeptical alternatives as rationally excluded in the good case; and one unoccupied combination—explicitly noted—with a brief diagnosis of why it is unexplored or incoherent. Use concrete cases rather than abstract labels at the nodes: for instance, a self-calibrating sensor that checks its own outputs, a cross-verified clinical measurement in which the same protocol validates itself, or a testimonial chain in which a speaker vouches for her own reliability. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.

The tree should be built from the logical space of answers first, with named positions then located within that space. Flag any pair of leaves that the tree cannot genuinely separate. The audience is graduate students in philosophy familiar with externalism and reliabilism, but they should navigate the tree by assessing cases, not by recognizing position names.
