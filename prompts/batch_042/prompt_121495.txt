I am designing a small interdisciplinary seminar—ten sessions over a single semester—bringing together philosophers, historians of modern Europe, and sociologists of bureaucracy. Our point of departure is the conceptual terrain mapped by the German Historikerstreit over uniqueness versus comparability, together with Zygmunt Bauman's thesis that modern bureaucratic rationality was not an accidental instrument but a constitutive condition of the genocide. Rather than rehearsing these familiar positions, I want the seminar to identify and settle the open conceptual questions that actually determine whether those older debates still matter for how we think about modernity, collective responsibility, and administrative ethics.

To that end, I am asking for a prioritized agenda: which three or four unresolved conceptual problems should occupy most of our time? One candidate concerns the logic of comparison itself—specifically, whether one can consistently distinguish structural analogies (shared institutional mechanisms across different regimes) from moral analogies (the equation of victim experiences), and whether maintaining that boundary advances or obstructs historical understanding. Another involves what I will call temporal asymmetry: does living in a world permanently reshaped by the Holocaust's memorial culture make retrospective comparisons with contemporaneous atrocities conceptually different from prospective ones? A third asks about the downstream implications for theories of technological and administrative progress—if rational bureaucracy can be constitutive of mass violence rather than merely neutral, what conceptual adjustments are required for how we evaluate institutional innovation elsewhere?

Given that the participants have roughly three months, work without archival access, and are trained in conceptual rather than empirical historical methods, I want you to rank these clusters and defend the ordering. For each line you prioritize, explain why it is upstream of the others—whether resolving it renders subsequent debates moot or transforms their terms—how tractable it is for philosophers rather than professional historians, and what kind of conceptual outcome within a year would justify reordering the list. I am also interested in which practical implications—whether for genocide-prevention frameworks, for the design of public memory, or for theories of collective moral agency—genuinely depend on resolving these questions, and which are commonly asserted but do not actually follow.
