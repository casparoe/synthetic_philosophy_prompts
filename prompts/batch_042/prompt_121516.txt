I've been working through the arguments about postdictive consciousness and the color phi illusion, and I'm tired of comparing theories without knowing what victory conditions actually look like. Before I weigh predictive-coding accounts, Dennett-style multiple-drafts models, and Stalinesque memory-revision views against one another, I want the specification written out first.

Write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of the temporal dynamics of conscious visual experience—specifically one that treats the color phi phenomenon as a central case and distinguishes an Orwellian revision (no original intermediate phenomenal state exists; the experienced past is rewritten) from a Stalinesque revision (an original intermediate state exists but is overwritten in report or memory)—must actually deliver. For each requirement, state the motivating case or conceptual distinction behind it, and flag whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp.

In particular, cover the following. Which perceptual cases must be explained? Include the standard phi illusion (a dot that appears to travel and change color in mid-trajectory), the ultra-short-ISI variant in which no motion is perceived at all, the case where the second-colored stimulus is masked or absent, and backward-masking conditions that disrupt registration of the second color. Which distinctions must be preserved? At minimum: Orwellian versus Stalinesque mechanisms; phenomenal consciousness versus access or reportability; retrospective revision of past experience versus revision only of present judgment; and diachronic phenomenal binding (integration across a brief interval) versus discrete snapshot construction. Which empirical and logical constraints apply? The theory should be consistent with the known latency of early visual processing and with physical causal ordering in neural dynamics, even if experience itself is postdictive; it should also be empirically distinguishable, so that an Orwellian and a Stalinesque process do not collapse into the same observational predictions.

Then identify whether any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable. For instance, can a theory maintain both that there is a determinate fact about what was phenomenally present at time t and that all intermediate experience is constructed retroactively? Or does the demand for empirical distinguishability conflict with the claim that phenomenal states are inherently private? Flag the tension.

Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on a scorecard, but briefly—without citations—indicate how predictive-processing models, a strict Stalinesque overwriting account, and a pure Orwellian/no-original-state account fare against the list. Finally, inject one precise, somewhat niche constraint: specify whether the theory must treat diachronic binding as requiring a continuous neural substrate across the temporal interval, or whether discrete, reconstructed snapshots can genuinely satisfy the phenomenology of smooth motion. I want that point sharpened, since it determines whether postdictive construction can replicate experienced continuity or whether it necessarily introduces micro-gaps that should be detectable.
