I have been following debates about whether dreams are genuine experiences — the kind of dispute that runs through Malcolm's scepticism about dream reports and Dennett's defence of dream experience — and I keep encountering a specific argumentative manoeuvre I do not have a name for.

Here is what it looks like in practice. A philosopher argues that, because the sleeper's sensory pathways are blocked and no external stimuli reach the cortex, there cannot be any occurrent perceptual experience during the dream itself; at best there is only a representational trace laid down in memory. Yet when the same philosopher is pressed on how dreamers can, upon waking, describe elaborate visual scenes with such confidence, they invoke exactly those traces and speak of them as if they preserved the feel or phenomenal character of an experience, without conceding that any experience actually occurred. Conversely, a defender of dream experience will cite the vividness of waking reports as proof that something was genuinely experienced in sleep; but when confronted with confabulated or impossible dream contents, they retreat to saying that the experience was merely representational and therefore need not correspond to anything that actually happened. In both directions, the distinction between an occurrent experiential state and a representational memory-trace is deployed selectively: it is emphasised when it protects one's own position and quietly dropped when it would undermine it.

This is not quite the motte-and-bailey, because the two positions are not a defensible core and an indefensible outgrowth — they are two equally contested interpretations of the same distinction, applied asymmetrically. Nor is it simple equivocation, since the terms (experience, representation, phenomenal character) keep their technical meanings; what shifts is the burden of proof assigned to them.

Is there an established term for this specific pattern — the strategic, context-dependent toggling between an occurrent-experience reading and a representational-trace reading within the philosophy of dreams? If so, how does it differ from more general failures of dialectical consistency? If not, can you coin and define a term for it, specify its boundary cases (for instance, distinguishing it from ordinary auxiliary-hypothesis adjustments), and indicate whether it is unique to the dream debate or appears elsewhere in philosophy of mind?
