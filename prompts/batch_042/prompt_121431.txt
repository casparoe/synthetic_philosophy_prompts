Exam question: Graham Priest defends dialetheism—the view that some contradictions are literally true—against the charge that it makes rational belief impossible. He distinguishes logical contradiction (formal inconsistency within a system) from rational incoherence (an epistemic failure of belief). His paraconsistent logic LP is designed to block ex contradictione quodlibet, so that from A and not-A one cannot derive arbitrary conclusions. Evaluate whether blocking formal explosion genuinely answers the critic who claims that believing contradictory propositions is epistemically irrational, or whether the critic's objection targets something other than formal derivability—such as the normative status of belief, the possibility of rational revision, or the regulative role of consistency. In your answer, clarify what LP achieves technically and what, if anything, it leaves unresolved about rational belief.

Student answer: Priest is right that logical contradiction and rational incoherence come apart. In LP, if you accept that A and not-A are both true, you still cannot derive arbitrary B because the logic restricts inference rules and blocks explosion. This means your belief system remains formally coherent. The critic who calls dialetheism irrational is confusing logical form with psychology: believing contradictory things does not cause mental collapse. Since rational belief only requires that your beliefs not entail falsehoods arbitrarily, and LP ensures that they do not, holding a dialetheia is no worse epistemically than holding an ordinary false belief. Once you reject the classical explosion principle and adopt paraconsistent logic, there is no remaining rational obstacle to dialetheism. The only people who think otherwise are still operating inside classical logic, which Priest has already shown to be inadequate for paradoxes like the Liar.
