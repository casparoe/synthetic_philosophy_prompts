You are an expert in formal epistemology and decision theory, with deep familiarity with belief-updating rules under ambiguous or partial evidence. Before giving your final diagnosis, briefly outline the logical structure of the argument below, flag any hidden scope shifts or equivocations between evidential correlation and causal dependence, and consider whether the flaw is a subtle misapplication rather than a false premise. Then answer precisely: name the step that fails, explain why it initially looked plausible, and state what abandoning it costs—especially with respect to premises (1), (2), and (5), which I would prefer to preserve.

Here is an argument I built for a seminar on uncertain evidence, and I now worry it proves that Jeffrey conditioning undermines rational choice in predictor-style cases.

(1) Jeffrey conditioning is the correct update rule for uncertain evidence: if an experience shifts one's credences over a partition {E_i} to new values q_i without resolving which E_i obtains, then for any hypothesis H the rational posterior is P_new(H) = Σ P(H | E_i) q_i.

(2) The rule is fully general: it applies to any partition over which credences shift, regardless of whether the partition concerns physical states, mental states, or counterfactual outcomes, provided only that the shift is evidentially driven.

(3) Consider the Partitioned Wager: you must choose between Action A (guaranteed $1,000) and Action B ($3,000,000 if a hidden predictor matched your choice, $0 otherwise). Before choosing, you observe a noisy diagnostic that shifts your credence over {Predictor matched A, Predictor matched B} to 60/40, without revealing which state holds.

(4) By (1) and (2), you should apply Jeffrey conditioning with these 60/40 weights to update all beliefs, including beliefs about the predictor's actual state and thus about each action's payoff.

(5) The conditional probabilities P(Payoff | E_i) are fixed by the predictor's prior, causally determined choice; they do not change depending on which action you now take, since the matching already occurred.

(6) To evaluate Action A, you combine the updated belief that the predictor matched A (weight 0.6) with A's payoff in that state; to evaluate Action B, you combine the updated belief that the predictor matched B (weight 0.6) with B's payoff in that state. Thus the Jeffrey weights enter the evaluation of each action relative to its own corresponding partition member.

(7) Consequently, Jeffrey conditioning yields two different rational assessments: evaluated relative to A, the expected value favors A; evaluated relative to B, it favors B. The rule does not deliver a unique recommendation independent of the choice being assessed.

(8) But a decision rule should determine what to do without circular dependence on the option under evaluation. So applying Jeffrey conditioning consistently makes rational choice in the Partitioned Wager impossible.

(9) One might block this by restricting Jeffrey conditioning to partitions causally independent of the present choice. But (2) asserts generality, and there is no non-ad hoc epistemic boundary that excludes decision-relevant partitions—the mathematics makes no such distinction.

(10) Therefore either Jeffrey conditioning is not general (giving up (2)), or it renders rational decision impossible in cases like this (giving up its practical applicability). Which step should I reject? Is the error in (6), where the weights are applied action-relatively rather than to a single updated belief state? And if so, what does fixing it cost—does it require abandoning the idea that Jeffrey conditioning guides choice at all?
