Below is a final-exam question from an undergraduate philosophy of history course, followed by a student's answer. I am grading it as the TA. Please assign a grade from 1 to 10 (with 7 as competent but flawed) and justify it with point-by-point comments. Identify the parts the student handles well—definitions, distinctions, and references—then isolate the specific errors: any mischaracterization of Collingwood's re-enactment, any confusion or misattribution regarding Skinner's position on present concerns versus moral presentism, and any logical gap in the final conclusion about normative vocabulary entailing moral judgment. Do not indicate your own agreement with the student; assess only the accuracy and coherence of the answer. End with the numerical grade and a one-sentence summary of the central weakness.

Exam question: Consider a historian writing about 19th-century institutionalization practices for mental illness. She asks whether she can describe commitment procedures without retroactive moral categorization—that is, without applying modern labels such as "human-rights violation" that carry present normative weight—and whether she should evaluate those practices by present ethical standards. In your answer, distinguish explanatory anachronism from moral presentism; discuss Collingwood's idea of re-enacting past thought as a potential method for avoiding presentist judgment; and explain why philosophers such as Skinner think that avoiding all present concerns in historical inquiry is neither possible nor desirable. Conclude with your own position on whether historians can ever fully escape the standards of their own time.

Student answer: Presentism is the error of judging past societies by present-day norms. When writing about 19th-century asylums, the historian risks two distinct mistakes. Explanatory anachronism occurs if she explains commitment practices through modern psychiatric categories that did not then exist. Moral presentism occurs if she condemns those practices for violating autonomy rights that the actors themselves did not recognize. These risks should be separated, even though both involve applying modern frameworks.

R.G. Collingwood held that the historian must re-enact the past agent's thought within her own mind. By doing so, she can suspend her own ethical commitments and inhabit the internal rationality of the past, describing institutionalization without importing modern human-rights language. Collingwood therefore provides a method for escaping present moral standards entirely, at least in evaluation.

Quentin Skinner, by contrast, argued that every historical question is shaped by contemporary interests, so we can never fully escape our present concerns. For Skinner, this implies that moral presentism is unavoidable: because we possess only our current normative vocabulary, any evaluation of past practices necessarily applies modern ethics. What Skinner actually warns against is not evaluation itself, but the anachronistic attribution of modern intentions to historical agents.

Consequently, while translating past practices into modern explanatory language is inevitable, moral judgment is not only unavoidable but also appropriate, provided we avoid confusing past intentions with present ones. The historian cannot fully escape her own time, but she can prevent misrepresentation by recognizing that her ethical standards are genuinely her own.
