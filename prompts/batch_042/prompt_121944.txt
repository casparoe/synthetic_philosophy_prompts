I am working through a Schlick-inspired account of life's meaning and want a consistency audit before I present it. My commitments are as follows: (1) I accept Schlick's claim that meaning lies in play—activities pursued for their own sake with absorbed, self-forgetting presence—rather than in teleological purpose or lasting achievement; (2) I regard transformative experiences as valuable precisely because they disrupt settled preferences and open up forms of play one could not have anticipated; (3) I hold that moral desert requires diachronic authorship, so that praising or blaming someone presupposes a stable agent who genuinely owns the action across time; (4) I follow Schlick's empiricist line that personal identity is a constructed narrative of psychological continuity, lacking any metaphysical substrate; (5) I maintain that the distinction between genuine play and compulsive behaviour is drawn purely phenomenologically—there is no third-person or behavioural criterion that could settle the matter; (6) I want to resist the hedonist reading of Schlick by insisting that play is defined by skillful, engaged attention rather than by pleasure or satisfaction.

Please go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and how badly. Rank the real tensions by severity, and for each say what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs. Be specific about which commitment has to give, or whether both can survive with a narrower reading. Identify at least one apparent tension that dissolves on closer inspection. Treat my belief-set as the object: do not lecture me on Schlick in general.
