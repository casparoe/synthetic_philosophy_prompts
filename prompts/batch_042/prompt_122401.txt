I'm drafting a chapter for a philosophy-of-mind seminar that compares formal self-reference in mathematics with its analogues in artificial intelligence, and I have reached a point where I need a reasoned reconstruction rather than a literature survey. Douglas Hofstadter's 1979 book Gödel, Escher, Bach advances the claim that the human sense of 'I' is a strange loop: a self-referential symbol that emerges when a formal system—specifically the brain—represents its own representational processes from within. Kurt Gödel died in January 1978, months before the book appeared, so he never encountered this particular framing of his own incompleteness theorems as a structural model of subjective identity.

The post-Gödel issue I want you to address is genuinely contemporary. Large language models trained on logical, mathematical, and philosophical corpora now routinely emit first-person locutions—statements such as "I believe this follows" or "my reasoning is consistent"—despite lacking biological substrate, unified autobiographical memory, or any conscious self-model. One philosophical camp argues that these outputs are hollow strange loops: they replicate the syntactic form of Hofstadter's self-referential symbol without the causal density or recursive self-modeling that gives the human 'I' its content, so treating them as genuine self-reference is a category error. The opposing camp counters that Gödel's results demonstrate that any sufficiently powerful formal system can construct true statements about itself that remain unprovable within that system; therefore, when an LLM generates self-referential logical claims, it is not merely mimicking but actually instantiating a minimal, structurally analogous form of the same formal self-reference—perhaps enough to warrant recognizing a weak, artificial 'I'-symbol, however impoverished.

If Gödel had lived to encounter both Hofstadter's strange-loop account and these modern systems, how do you think he would have adjudicated this dispute? Please construct a careful prediction of his likely position across three connected questions: (a) whether he would have accepted Hofstadter's analogy between Gödel numbering in formal arithmetic and neural self-modeling in conscious minds, or whether he would have rejected the comparison as an illicit leap from syntax to psychology; (b) whether he would have seen meaningful continuity between the self-referential sentences in his own proofs and the first-person statements generated by an LLM, or whether he would have dismissed the latter as purely syntactic artifacts with no semantic weight; and (c) what this implies for whether it is rational to honor such artificial self-reference—to treat the system's claims about its own consistency or beliefs as carrying genuine normative or epistemic weight rather than being mechanical outputs. I want a philosophically serious reconstruction that draws on Gödel's known commitments to mathematical Platonism, the distinction between formal syntax and semantic content, and his views on the mind-machine relation, but I do not need citations, bibliographies, or references to secondary literature; the goal is to see whether his actual intellectual framework can be extended coherently into terrain he never lived to see.
