I am trying to work out the conditions under which knowing a set of premises counts as knowing one of their logical consequences—specifically, where the boundary lies between mere logical entailment and genuine epistemic closure. My intuitions about concrete cases are as follows, and I want to know whether they point to a single principle or whether some must be revised.

First, consider a student who has fully internalized the axioms of Euclidean geometry and could, given paper and time, derive the Pythagorean theorem, but who has never actually done so. I do not think she knows that a² + b² = c².

Second, the same student, after personally constructing the proof step by step, does know it. The acquisition seems to depend on the actual inferential labor, not on the logical relation alone.

Third, a logician who accepts modus ponens and currently holds beliefs in P and P → Q, but who is thinking about an unrelated matter and has never formed the belief Q, does not know Q—even though the inference is structurally immediate.

Fourth, if that same logician is actively tracking P and P → Q and immediately forms Q, she does know Q, with almost no temporal gap between premises and conclusion.

Fifth, a researcher who has memorized every line of a 200-page formal derivation in ZFC of a specific number-theoretic result, but who understands none of the intermediate steps and could not explain them, does not genuinely know the result, despite possessing every logical link.

Sixth, by contrast, a mathematician who grasps only the high-level strategy of that same proof, accepts the premises, and relies on a verified computer check for the remaining mechanical steps, does seem to know the result—provided her understanding covers the critical inferential joints.

These verdicts suggest that closure is not automatic, but they also resist a simple "actual derivation" requirement, since the mathematician in the sixth case has not personally derived the intermediate steps. Is there a coherent account—perhaps in terms of graded inferential accessibility, cognitive integration, or a distinction between potential and actualized reasoning—that captures all six? If so, state it precisely, identify which clause addresses which case, and say what it predicts for a scenario I have not listed: a reasoner who knows her premises entail a conclusion, knows precisely which algorithm would derive it, but has never executed it and does not know any of the intermediate steps. If no single principle saves every verdict, tell me which one I should overturn and why.
