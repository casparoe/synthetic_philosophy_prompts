Here is a pattern I keep encountering, and I want to know whether it already has a name. In the familiar single-shot Pascal-style mugging, an agent is offered an enormous payoff with astronomically low probability, and expected-utility reasoning seems to compel acceptance despite near-certain loss. Now imagine a chained variant: the agent faces an ordered sequence of such offers, each available only if the previous one was accepted. Offer 1 promises utility U₁ with probability p₁; Offer 2, unlocked only after accepting Offer 1, promises U₂ > U₁ with p₂ far smaller than p₁; Offer 3 continues the pattern, with probabilities falling exponentially and payoffs growing only linearly. The total expected value of the entire chain converges to a modest, bounded sum—much smaller than the resources the agent must hold in reserve—yet at every individual step the agent can justify accepting, because rejecting breaks the chain and forfeits the remaining cumulative expectation.

The result is that the agent becomes locked into an indefinite series of commitments, each defensible in isolation, but collectively consuming all available decision-capacity on outcomes that are collectively almost certain never to occur. The failure mode is not exactly the original mugging, since no single step dominates; it is not the St. Petersburg paradox, since the expectation does not diverge; and it is not a generic slippery slope, since the rational pressure at each link is reconstructed from first principles rather than borrowed from precedent.

Does this chained or sequential structure have an established name in decision theory or philosophy of rationality? If so, what precisely distinguishes it from the single-shot case and from related paradoxes? If not, please coin a precise term, define its necessary and sufficient conditions, and map its boundary cases—especially finite but very long chains, and cases where probabilities do not fall exponentially but payoffs still escalate. I am not asking for a bibliography or citation list; I want the conceptual carving: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
