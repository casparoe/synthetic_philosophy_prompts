As a novelist constructing a historical narrative set in a culture with radically different ethical categories, I find myself wondering whether the very act of describing a past society's judgments in modern moral vocabulary inevitably distorts them. Please give me your actual credence—on a scale from 0 to 1—that a trained historian can produce a sustained account of, say, a pre-modern legal system's internal reasoning without presentist contamination, where presentist contamination means not merely applying modern labels but structurally reorganizing the past actors' reasons to fit contemporary ethical grammar. Decompose this: first, your probability that normative frameworks are genuinely incommensurable across centuries rather than merely differently weighted; second, your probability that historical method—source criticism, contextual reconstruction—can bridge such incommensurability if it exists; third, your probability that the act of narration itself—selecting which reasons to render intelligible—introduces an irreducible presentist filter regardless of method; then combine them. Tell me which term moves your final number most, and what kind of evidence—perhaps a concrete historiographical example you can reason about without citation—would shift that term significantly.
