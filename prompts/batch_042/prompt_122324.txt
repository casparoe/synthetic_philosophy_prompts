As a retired physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of science and decision theory, I have been trying to understand the result—often associated with I.J. Good—showing that freely available information should generally be acquired before making a decision, since the expected utility of acting with more evidence is never lower than acting without it. I find that I can apply this reasoning comfortably to some cases, yet I resist it in others, and I am unsure whether my resistance is consistent or merely a failure of rationality on my part.

Here are my verdicts. First, before selecting between two surgical procedures with different risk profiles, I should certainly take a free, instantaneous genetic compatibility test that might change my choice; acquiring it is plainly rational. Second, before committing retirement savings, I should read an independent risk assessment even though I could commit now and read afterward; delaying the decision until I have the assessment still seems correct. Third, however, before deciding whether to forgive a colleague for a serious breach of trust, I should not seek out a mutual friend’s full account of what was said in private, even though that account is freely available and might alter my decision; knowing every particular seems likely to harden my judgment in a way that tracks resentment rather than forgiveness, and I think I ought to decide on the basis of what I already know. Fourth, as a juror I should not seek the defendant’s prior convictions before rendering a verdict, not because the information is costly, but because possessing it corrupts the fairness of my reasoning, even if it might technically shift my assessment of guilt.

There is a fifth case on which I keep reversing myself. A climate scientist has preliminary data suggesting an imminent tipping point. She can run one more high-resolution model that costs nothing computationally, takes six months, and will either confirm or weaken the finding. During those six months, however, policy action is stalled. Sometimes I think she should run the model—Good’s reasoning applies directly—yet at other times I think she should publish immediately, because the harm done by delay is real and the theorem seems to treat time as costless.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps a qualified version of the value-of-information result—that captures all five verdicts? If so, please state it precisely, identify which clause is motivated by the medical case, which by the forgiveness case, which by the jury case, and what amendment, if any, accommodates the climate scientist’s dilemma. If no single principle saves all five, then which verdict should I overturn in favor of simplicity, and why? Finally, apply the resulting account to a case I have not listed: should an ethics committee seek free, anonymized records of how its members voted on past cases, knowing that seeing patterns might make future deliberation more consistent but might also make members defensive about being monitored?
