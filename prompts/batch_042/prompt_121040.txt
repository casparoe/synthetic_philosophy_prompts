I'm trying to determine what principle, if any, governs when reactive attitudes in Strawson's sense—resentment, gratitude, indignation, and their analogues—are genuinely fitting rather than merely felt. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases. When a colleague knowingly plagiarizes my research and presents it as her own, resentment directed squarely at her feels appropriate. When a corporation deploys an algorithm that systematically denies loans to qualified applicants from a specific neighborhood, I feel strong moral anger, but I'm unsure whether its proper object is the corporation as such, the executives who approved the model, or both together; if the corporation is genuinely the target, then reactive attitudes can attach to collective entities, but if not, they seem to fragment into interpersonal ones. When my closest friend, acting under credible blackmail, reveals a secret I entrusted to him, my resentment dissolves into sympathy for him and anger only at the blackmailer—so the attitude tracks something about the quality of will behind the act, not the harm alone. Yet I feel sustained, undissolved resentment toward my own past self for a reckless financial decision made a decade ago, despite that self being absent and unresponsive, whereas I feel nothing of the kind toward a stranger who made an identical reckless choice—suggesting that temporal identity or ownership of the act matters, not just the quality of will. When an autonomous vehicle with no human oversight malfunctionally injures a pedestrian, I feel no reactive attitude toward the vehicle itself, only concern for the victim; but if the vehicle incorporated an adaptive learning mechanism that specifically evolved to prioritize speed over pedestrian safety after observing human preferences, I do feel something structurally like resentment directed at the system, not merely at its designers. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps centered on reciprocal moral address, or on the presence of a responsive quality of will that is both free and accountable—that delivers all these verdicts, including the split between collective and individual targets and between present and past selves? Or must I abandon the idea of a unified account and treat these as family-resemblance cases? Please state any principle precisely, indicate which case motivates each clause, predict its verdict on a case I haven't mentioned—say, feeling indignation on behalf of an animal wronged by a human—and be explicit about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, particularly the one I keep reversing: whether democratic citizens who vote, with full information and without coercion, to deprive a minority of basic rights are proper targets of the same interpersonal resentment one feels toward an individual wrongdoer, or whether that anger is necessarily a different kind of moral response.
