Consider the institutional practice of strict parliamentary party discipline in modern representative democracies: legislators are formally elected by geographic constituencies to exercise independent judgment, yet they are routinely required to vote according to a centralized party line enforced through sanctions including expulsion, loss of committee assignments, or denial of renomination, while cabinet ministers must publicly defend collective decisions they privately opposed, on pain of resignation. Reconstruct the implicit theory of collective agency, moral responsibility, and political representation that this practice presupposes. Does it treat the party as a corporate agent with unified intentions that legitimately override the judgments of individual members, and is it consistent with viewing representatives as delegates of their constituents rather than trustees of a party will? Now contrast that implicit theory with Simone Weil’s critique that political parties function as mechanisms for manufacturing collective passions that suppress individual attention and destroy the capacity for independent moral judgment about justice. Specifically, examine whether parliamentary discipline can be reconciled with Weil’s claim that legitimate politics requires dissolving mediated party structures in favor of direct, unmediated deliberation among citizens, and identify which element of her critique—the substitution of collective emotion for reason, the confusion of organizational loyalty with fidelity to truth, or the elimination of personal responsibility for judgment—the practice of whipping and collective cabinet responsibility most directly contradicts. Be explicit about whether Weil’s framework demands the complete abolition of parties or allows for transformed organizational forms, and state frankly which feature of modern legislative governance her view cannot accommodate without fundamental revision.
