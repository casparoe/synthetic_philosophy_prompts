I'm drafting an essay on the conditions for valid consent. The standard account holds that consent is invalidated by deception, coercion, or incompetence. I want to defend a further, distinct condition: what I'll call "normative framing asymmetry." On this view, consent can fail even when all disclosed facts are accurate and no direct threat is present, if the consent-seeker controls the interpretive framework—say, presenting a risky clinical trial exclusively in therapeutic language—such that the consenter's practical reasoning is guided by evaluative categories she does not actually share with the seeker. I argue this is not reducible to deception (no false belief is induced about the act itself) or coercion (no sanction is threatened), but is a separate failure of autonomy because it undermines the consenter's authority over the meaning of her own agreement, particularly in bureaucratic healthcare settings where framing is standardized rather than personally tailored.

Please comment on three things: (1) whether "normative framing asymmetry" is genuinely distinct from deception by omission or misleading emphasis, or whether it collapses into one of them; (2) whether my claim depends on an implausibly thick conception of autonomy that would invalidate too much ordinary consent; and (3) whether there are concrete institutional remedies—beyond simply adding more disclosure—that could address this kind of failure without requiring impossible levels of individualized framing.
