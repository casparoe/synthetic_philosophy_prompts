I've been thinking about Sen's liberal paradox in a slightly weird context and I dont think I'm getting it right. The standard story is that if you grant two people minimal rights over personal choices—like whether they read some controversial book—those rights can clash with Pareto efficiency when preferences are interdependent. But I keep wondering: isnt the paradox really just a special case of distributed veto rights? Like, imagine a research collective where each scientist has a liberal right to control whether their own dataset is shared, but the value of every dataset depends on which others are shared too. If A wants full openness and B wants full privacy, and both have rights over their own nodes, we get the same deadlock pattern. So does Sen's result actually depend on the choices being 'personal' in some deep sense, or does it apply to any network of interdependent decentralized rights? And if its the latter, why do we treat it as a paradox about liberalism rather than just a coordination failure in multi-agent systems? I feel like I'm missing a distinction that makes the original book case special.
