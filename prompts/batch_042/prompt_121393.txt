I'm working through Spinoza's political writings for a seminar on early modern theories of popular sovereignty, and I have come to accept an interpretive position that runs sharply against the standard liberal-democratic reading. In both the Tractatus Theologico-Politicus and the unfinished Tractatus Politicus, Spinoza does not treat democracy as a procedural mechanism for aggregating individual wills or protecting rights, but rather as the natural expression of a specific affective configuration—namely, a collective state in which the majority of citizens are guided by active joy and common notions rather than by passive fear, hope, or superstition. On this view, democratic sovereignty—what Spinoza calls the power of the multitude—is not conferred by a social contract, constitutional design, or voting procedures, but is literally the summed rational capacity of bodies that have achieved a shared mode of understanding. A culture therefore does not choose democracy in the manner it chooses a constitution; it can only become democratic to the extent that its institutions, education, and economic organization successfully expand the ratio of rational to passive citizens.

What would have to be true for this interpretation to be wrong? I want an honest map of its exposure, sorted from decisive to merely awkward. Which arguments or passages in Spinoza's texts—particularly regarding the state of nature, the comparative evaluation of monarchy, aristocracy, and democracy, and the sovereign's right—would genuinely undermine the claim that democracy is an affective achievement rather than an institutional form? Are there textual moments where Spinoza treats democratic decision-making as purely procedural, independent of the citizens' internal affective states? Beyond exegesis, what conceptual costs does the view bear? For example, does it collapse democracy into an unrealizable ideal that no historical state has approached, and if so, is that a fatal flaw for an interpretation or a feature that distinguishes Spinoza from liberal theory? I also want to know whether the view risks unfalsifiability: if every empirical failure of democracy is explained away as an insufficient ratio of rational joy, has the interpretation been insulated into uselessness? Please distinguish what a committed defender of this affective-democracy reading would concede as genuinely counting against them from what they would dismiss as a category mistake, and assess whether that exposure is a problem for an interpretive thesis of this kind.
