I am currently preparing a presentation for an advanced bioethics module on reproductive autonomy, and I find myself caught in a conceptual knot that I hope you can help me untangle. We have been examining Julian Savulescu's principle of procreative beneficence, according to which prospective parents have a moral reason to select, from among the possible children they might conceive, the one expected to enjoy the highest quality of life. Alongside this, we have discussed Nicholas Agar's account of liberal eugenics, which maintains that parents ought to be free to employ genetic and reproductive technologies to enhance their offspring, provided that such interventions do not violate the future person's right to an open future and are not coercively imposed by the state. Our seminar also touched briefly on Derek Parfit's non-identity problem, which suggests that altering reproductive choices changes the identity of the future person, thereby complicating the ascription of harm.

During a discussion with colleagues, I was challenged with what seemed to me a rather subtle objection. The objection runs as follows: if procreative beneficence genuinely imposes a moral obligation to maximise expected well-being, and if liberal eugenics makes available technologies that can substantially increase that well-being, then the combination of the two positions does not yield a genuinely liberal framework at all. Instead, it produces a disguised form of compulsory eugenics, because any parent who refuses to enhance—despite having the means—would be failing in their duty of beneficence, and because the non-identity problem shows that there is no specific victim who can claim to have been wronged by the refusal. I attempted to reply that liberal eugenics concerns the scope of permissible state interference rather than the content of parental duty, but my interlocutor countered that this distinction is unstable: once a moral obligation is recognised, the liberal state's neutrality becomes complicity in harm, particularly when the alternative is not harming an existing child but failing to bring about a better one. I now wonder whether this tension is real, or whether it depends on a false presupposition about the relationship between moral obligation and political permission. Specifically, is it coherent to hold that parents are obliged to select the best available embryo without being obliged to alter the genetic constitution of the embryo they have selected? Or does the logic of beneficence necessarily extend across the boundary between reproductive selection and genetic enhancement, rendering the two frameworks mutually undermining rather than complementary?
