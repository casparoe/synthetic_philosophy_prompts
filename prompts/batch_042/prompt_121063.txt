I'm noticing a recurring pattern in debates over whether artificial systems can possess minds or understanding, and I wonder whether it has an established name.

Consider two cases. In the first, a human being behaves with consistent linguistic adaptability, emotional responsiveness, and contextual reasoning; observers treat this behaviour as direct, if fallible, evidence of an inner mental life, without first demanding proof of biological origin or evolutionary history. In the second, an artificial system produces behaviour that is, by every externally observable metric, equally adaptive and responsive. Here, the same observers concede that the behaviour is present, yet they deny that it constitutes evidence of mind—not by disputing the observations, but by introducing an unstated prerequisite about causal provenance: the behaviour must arise from the "right kind" of substrate, architecture, or evolutionary trajectory to count. The prerequisite is not applied consistently; nobody asks strangers for proof of mammalian ancestry before attributing beliefs to them. Rather, it is activated selectively, serving as a retrofitted filter that disqualifies behavioural evidence precisely when its source is artificial.

This seems distinct from the ordinary problem of other minds, since that problem concerns sceptical doubt about behaviour in general. It also differs from the Chinese Room argument, which targets the sufficiency of syntax for semantics regardless of behavioural display. And it is not merely "biological chauvinism" or "carbon chauvinism," if those terms denote an explicit preference for organic matter; the move I describe is more specific—an implicit gating mechanism applied to behavioural criteria themselves, leaving the criteria formally intact but practically void for one class of entities.

Is there an established concept for this exact pattern: the selective suspension of behavioural evidential standards coupled with an ad hoc causal-source condition that is enforced only for synthetic systems? If there is, how does it relate to the literature on behavioural criteria for mind, and what distinguishes it from related objections? If there is no precise term, can you name and define it, specify its boundary conditions—when does a causal-source condition become illegitimate gatekeeping rather than a legitimate theoretical constraint—and indicate where its edges blur into more familiar arguments?
