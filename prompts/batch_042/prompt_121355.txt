For my seminar on consciousness and ethics, I am examining the moral implications of panpsychist accounts in fundamental physics—specifically, whether widespread phenomenal properties in basic physical entities generate obligations or suffering at a cosmic scale. I would like to compare two ethical frameworks that might follow from such a view. The first holds that moral patienthood and the capacity to suffer require a substantial degree of informational or organizational integration; on this account, only complex, unified systems like organisms are genuine subjects of suffering, and the phenomenal properties of isolated quantum fields or particles carry no independent moral weight. The second holds that any entity with even minimal phenomenal properties—no matter how disunified—counts as a locus of suffering, so that decoherence events, thermal fluctuations, or individual field excitations might all contribute to an enormous, diffuse aggregate of harm. Please compare these two positions with respect to three issues: how each addresses the scaling problem (whether the universe contains an implausibly vast amount of suffering if minimal phenomenal subjects are counted), whether the integration requirement in the first view can be justified without circularly assuming that only brain-like structures matter, and how each framework relates to a naturalistic commitment that takes the distribution of consciousness in physics seriously rather than treating it as an afterthought. Which of the two offers a more defensible ethical stance for a panpsychist who wants to avoid both human-centered bias and moral paralysis in the face of cosmic-scale suffering?
