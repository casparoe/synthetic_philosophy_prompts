In a recent workshop on moral luck, a speaker dismissed the relevance of causal luck to assessments of praiseworthiness by invoking the line that moral worth requires acting for the right reasons, treating it as if it alone settled whether an agent whose correct motivation arose through fortuitous causation deserved full credit. I want to bring a precise disambiguation to my next meeting rather than another round of intuition-mongering. Could you separate out the distinct precise theses that slogan might be expressing? Then indicate which readings are tautological, which are substantive and defensible, and which are false—paying special attention to cases where an agent's motivation is correct but epistemically fragmented, or where collective action renders individual reasons ambiguous in ways that obscure whether anyone acted for the right reason at all.
