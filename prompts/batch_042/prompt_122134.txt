Please take the role of a professional political philosopher with deep familiarity in comparative normative theory and distributive justice. Before writing, spend a brief moment brainstorming the conceptual architecture: what exactly makes an attitude toward inequality defensible—present social relations or intergenerational prospects—and where each view is weakest. Then write a continuous-prose dialogue, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or other formatting, between two equally competent interlocutors. One, call her the advocate of prospective equality, should defend the American attitude toward inequality on the ground that large present disparities are morally tolerable only when institutional design makes social positions genuinely open to talent and effort across generations, so inequality functions as a temporary sorting mechanism rather than a caste. The other, call him the advocate of synchronic dignity, should defend the European attitude by arguing that democratic citizenship requires approximate equality of social standing in the present regardless of mobility statistics, since visible hierarchy corrupts deliberation and self-respect even for those who might eventually ascend. They must engage in real back-and-forth: each should answer the other’s actual objections rather than talk past them, and neither should be a strawman. Have them specifically address whether mobility data can ever justify present suffering, whether capping dynastic transmission violates familial continuity, and whether the American view secretly socializes risk while privatizing reward. The dialogue should feel genuinely in the weeds, not a standard textbook exchange. At the end, in the same continuous prose, state how confident you are in the arguments presented and single out the one point about which you are least certain. Do not include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; rely only on reasoning and the invented framing of prospective equality versus synchronic dignity.
