I am designing a handout for a philosophy-of-science seminar on the Anthropocene as a historical-geological category. I want a branching taxonomy—built as a decision tree with labeled split-claims at each node—that sorts positions on two linked questions: whether there is a fact of the matter about when the Anthropocene began, and who, if anyone, has the authority to decide that it is a formal geological epoch rather than an informal historical concept.

The tree should be constructed from logical space first, then populated with actual positions. Each branch node must pose a concrete claim or case that someone can answer without already knowing the label it leads to—for example, whether epoch-status requires a globally synchronous stratigraphic marker, whether a diffuse centuries-long transition can ground a discrete boundary, or whether predictive power over future stratigraphy is necessary.

The leaves must include at least the following positions, or unnamed combinations if nobody occupies them:
- the mid-20th-century “Great Acceleration” view tied to nuclear isotopes and synthetic materials;
- the early-modern “Orbis” view tied to transoceanic contact, biotic exchange, and an atmospheric CO₂ dip around 1610;
- the deep-time view tied to early agriculture, methane release, and Holocene termination millennia ago;
- the anti-formalist view that the term is politically and scientifically useful but should not be ratified by stratigraphers;
- the democratic-geoscience view that the International Commission on Stratigraphy should not hold monopoly authority and that affected communities or interdisciplinary bodies should share in the decision;
- at least one currently unoccupied combination, with a brief diagnosis of whether it is empty because incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Inject a niche angle that moves beyond standard encyclopedia summaries: include a branch on diachronic authority—whether the decision must be made by scientists contemporaneous with the transition, or whether retrospective classification by future observers is epistemically legitimate—and a branch on whether epoch-designation requires descriptive coherence alone or also predictive power about future geological layers. Flag any pair of leaves that your tree genuinely cannot separate, explaining whether they collapse because they agree on all diagnostic cases or differ only in rhetorical framing.

Keep the instrument to about six branch nodes and no more than ten leaves. For each leaf, provide a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests; the audience is working without web access. Write in a formal, polished tone suitable for a graduate handout, and specify the audience size as a seminar of fifteen students.
