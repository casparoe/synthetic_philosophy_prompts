As a journalist covering conceptual debates in philosophy and science, I have been trying to move past the usual popular treatments of Sartre's ideas about radical freedom and bad faith, which tend to reduce everything to a tired free-will-versus-determinism opposition that Sartre himself would reject. I want to build a diagnostic taxonomy—rendered entirely as continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or any structural formatting—that sorts out the genuinely distinct positions on two interlocking questions. First, does radical freedom extend only to the interpretation of one's situation, or does it include the power to constitute the very meanings and values that appear to constrain one? Second, is bad faith best understood as an individual psychological failure to integrate one's facticity with one's transcendence, or can it also operate as a structurally distributed phenomenon—for instance in institutional roles or even in synthetic agents designed to deny their own contingency—where no single consciousness is actively deceiving itself?

The instrument should function like a sorting quiz or decision tree whose branches are marked by concrete cases rather than by philosophical labels. I want it to begin with a reader who accepts that human beings are, in Sartre's well-known formulation, condemned to freedom in some fundamental sense, and then to proceed through diagnostic questions: whether freedom can be genuinely alienated by social structures, whether bad faith requires some awareness of what one is hiding, and whether an entity that lacks self-consciousness can nevertheless inhabit a form of bad faith. Each branching point should present a scenario—perhaps a surgeon who refuses to acknowledge that she could choose not to operate, a person in an oppressive system who claims she has no choice but to comply, or an automated system that denies its own contingency—and ask which response is correct. The tree should include at least one unnamed or rarely defended combination, such as the view that freedom is total but bad faith is impossible because self-deception requires a division within consciousness that Sartre's ontology forbids, and another such as the view that freedom is severely limited by social facticity yet bad faith remains ubiquitous because people systematically misidentify contingent social constraints as necessary natural laws. Each leaf should describe the position it represents, and any combination that remains unoccupied should be noted with a brief diagnosis of whether it seems incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Please present the taxonomy as a continuous narrative flow, the way one writes in a formal letter or long email, with no section divisions, enumerated lists, typographic emphasis used for organization, or tabular layouts. I am not asking for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific editions—this should be answerable through conceptual reasoning alone, drawing on Sartre's widely discussed framework without requiring precise page references. The goal is not for a reader to identify a favorite label but to discover, through answers to concrete cases, which version of the freedom-and-bad-faith landscape they actually inhabit.
