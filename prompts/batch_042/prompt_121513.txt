After a long and rather exhausting argument with a friend about whether Athenian democracy contained any genuine institutional mechanism for self-correction, I want to press a conceptual knot that neither of us managed to untangle. We kept returning to three episodes—the Mytilene debate, the Sicilian expedition, and the trial of Socrates—and my friend insisted they all illustrate the same underlying flaw: a democratic assembly that can change its mind but never learn. I think that collapses structurally distinct failure modes. Could you explain the conceptual difference between a democratic body that reverses a prior punitive decision without revising the epistemic framework that generated it—the kind of reversal we see when the ekklesia overturns Cleon’s original decree regarding Mytilene—and a democratic body that escalates commitment to a failing strategic course precisely because reversal would now damage collective civic honor, which seems closer to the logic of the Sicilian disaster? Then there is the trial of Socrates, which fits neither pattern: there is no strategic reversal or escalation, but rather what looks like performative boundary-maintenance directed inward at the polis itself. Please clarify whether these three cases can be unified under a single concept of democratic failure, or whether forcing them together obscures more than it reveals. In particular, I want to understand how the logical relationship between the assembly and its own prior judgments differs in each case, and whether any feature present across all three could have interrupted the specific failure mode without altering the broader democratic structure itself.
