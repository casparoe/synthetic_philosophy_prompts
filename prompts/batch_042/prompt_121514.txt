hey, i'm prepping discussion prompts for my grad seminar on infinite ethics — Levinas-adjacent stuff, not the usual recap. i want to toss this argument at them and see if they can spot the sleight of hand. one of my students sketchd it out like this:

(1) If responsibility for the Other is literally infinite, then facing one Other gives me one infinite obligation.
(2) Facing ten Others gives me ten infinite obligations.
(3) In cardinal arithmetic, ℵ₀ multiplied by ten is still ℵ₀ — all countable infinities are identical in size.
(4) So my total responsibility is the same whether i face one starving person or ten.
(5) But if the total is identical, there's no quantitative basis for distributing aid differently; helping ten isn't more urgent than helping one.
(6) Yet that feels wrong — more suffering should matter.
(7) If i try to save the scaling intuition by saying obligations do accumulate, then i'm treating the infinite as measurable and comparable, which is exactly the calcuable logic Levinas rejects.
(8) So either infinite ethics can't guide triage at all, or it secretly relies on the kind of quantification it forbids.

where's the exit? is the mistake in applying transfinite math to normativity (step 3–4), or is there a hidden equivocation between 'infinite' as unbounded and 'infinite' as a cardinal number? or should i just accept that infinite ethics really does collapse triage into indiference? i want the students to see that if they reject (3) they pay a price, and if they reject (5) they pay another. also i keep calling this the "crowd problem" but that sounds like sociology — if there's a real name for the paradox let me know, but don't just dump a bibliography on me.
