Hey, I'm drafting a short essay for a philosophy of mind class and I'm not totally sure my central move isn't just confused. The gist is that Clark and Chalmers's parity principle usually gets illustrated with stable external tools—Otto's notebook, smartphones, that kind of thing—but I want to argue it should also cover what I'm calling 'transient cognitive scaffolds.' These are temporary physical setups people build for a single reasoning task and then tear down: arranging pebbles in a pattern to solve a logic puzzle, then scattering them; or temporarily moving furniture around to map out a spatial argument and then putting it back. If parity only cares about functional role during the process, these setups should be part of the extended mind while they're in use. But they clearly fail the usual 'accessability and endorsement' conditions, since there's no lasting possession or retrieval after the fact. So either we weaken those conditions and accept that parts of your mind can literally disappear five minutes later, or we admit the conditions were secretly doing normative work—excluding cases where extension feels weird. I also try to extend this to groups: a team briefly reorganzing a shared workspace to think through a problem together, then leaving—does that make the workspace part of a collective mind for twenty minutes? I'm not convinced that part works.

Could you comment on these notes? I'm especially unsure whether Clark requires persistent accessability or just momentary availability, because that seems to make or break my point. Also, is the collective version just a distraction? And honestly, is this just a disguised version of the coupling-constitution objection that everyone already discusses? I want to know if it's genuinely interesting or if I'm reinventing the wheel. No need for reading lists—just whether the conceptual machinery feels right and where the weak spots are.
