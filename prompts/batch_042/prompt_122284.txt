Below are notes for a short essay arguing that global workspace theory accounts adequately for access consciousness — the availability of information for report, reasoning, and voluntary action — but fails to explain phenomenal consciousness, the subjective quality of experience. I propose an amendment: alongside the familiar global workspace there operates a rapid, non-reportable phenomenal workspace that processes certain sensory representations locally before, or without, global broadcast. On this view, masked or subliminal stimuli may enter the phenomenal layer and carry a faint glow of experience even though they never reach access, which explains intuitions that such stimuli are not entirely unconscious. Conversely, some globally broadcast conceptual states — for example, high-level representations in working memory — may entirely lack phenomenal character. The essay then asks whether this dual-layer architecture genuinely addresses the hard problem or merely relocates it: if the phenomenal workspace is defined precisely as the layer that produces subjective quality, we appear to have named the mystery rather than dissolved it. I also ask whether the model can be empirically distinguished from standard global workspace accounts, since any failure of report might be explained by access blockage rather than absence of phenomenal broadcasting.

Please comment on the coherence of the distinction between access and phenomenal consciousness as drawn here; on whether the dual-layer proposal offers a real explanatory gain or only a redescription; and on whether well-known clinical or experimental dissociations support or undermine this specific architecture. I am especially interested in whether the argument implicitly assumes that phenomenal consciousness must be local, and whether that assumption can be defended without circularity.
