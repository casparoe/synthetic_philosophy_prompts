So I've been thinking about that old sphex wasp example - you know, the one where the wasp drags prey to a burrow and if you move the prey she just restarts the whole routine like a broken loop. Hofstadter used it to talk about mechanical rigidity versus real flexibility, and now people apply it to LLMs and free will debates. But I'm sick of the same surface-level take that 'AI is just sphexish so it can't have agency.'

I want to dig into something more specific. Here's whta I'm actually asking: if you had to advise a philosophy grad student with about three years and no lab or coding skills on open questions in this space, which ones actually matter? I'm especially playing with this framing that sphexishness isn't binary but a gradient of 'interruptibility' - can an agent be knocked out of its loop and genuinely form a new plan, or does it just reboot the same subroutine? LLMs sit in a weird middle: they're interruptible at every token but don't have persistent goals that can be derailed and rebuilt. So the questions seem to be: (1) is interruptibility even the right axis, or should we care more about counterfactual stability of goals? (2) does sphexishness tell us anything real about moral status, or is the analogy just broken? (3) is there a meaningful distinction between evolved rigidity in insects and deterministic behavior in artificial agents?

But I don't want a survey. I want a defended agenda: which of these (or ones I'm missing) are upstream of the others, which are tractable for a philosopher without running experiments, and which are dead ends that just sound cool? Rank them with reasoning.
