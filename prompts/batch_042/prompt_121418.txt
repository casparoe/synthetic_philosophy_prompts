Two colleagues in a safety-engineering seminar, Jordan and Riley, got into a sustained disagreement about whether an impact measure for low-impact AI can be formulated without smuggling in a full specification of human values. Jordan opened by proposing that impact can be captured structurally: compute the divergence between the actual world-trajectory and a counterfactual trajectory in which the agent takes no action, using a maximally inclusive state-space of physically observable macroscopic variables with uniform weighting. Because this only registers change, not good or bad change, Jordan argued it avoids value specification entirely. Riley replied that the construction is value-laden at every joint: selecting which variables count as observable and macroscopic, fixing the temporal horizon, deciding what causal boundary separates the agent's action from background dynamics, and especially adopting uniform weighting—which treats a relocated pebble and human extinction as comparable if they involve equal particle rearrangement—are all normative choices disguised as neutrality. Jordan countered that these are practical or methodological choices, not value specifications; the metric itself remains purely descriptive, and one can apply it for value-laden reasons without the measure containing those values. Riley then pressed that the very notion of low impact is only intelligible as a design goal if we value preserving the status quo, and that without a value-laden filter on divergence, the measure either collapses into absurd uniformity or requires exactly the kind of outcome-weighting Jordan wants to avoid. Jordan's final move was to distinguish sharply between the content of the metric (divergence) and the motivation for deploying it (avoiding harm), insisting that this separation is what makes low-impact design feasible even when we do not know the full value function.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of Jordan's and Riley's main interventions, rate whether it is clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right, and justify your rating. Then say what, if anything, both are missing—particularly whether there is a coherent middle position between a purely structural divergence metric and a fully specified utility function, and whether Jordan's distinction between metric-content and deployment-motivation actually holds up under scrutiny. Do not simply declare a winner; identify exactly where each argument succeeds and where it overreaches.
