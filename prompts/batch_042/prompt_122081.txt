Rank the following three explanatory frameworks for the behavior of perpetrators in the mass shootings of the Holocaust—Christopher Browning’s situational and peer-culture account centered on Reserve Police Battalion 101, Daniel Goldhagen’s dispositional and ideological account centered on eliminationist antisemitism, and a synthetic “bureaucratic ritualization” framework that treats sustained participation not as psychological conformity or ideological conviction but as the performative enactment of institutional identity under conditions of role ambiguity—according to their explanatory power across three criteria: (a) accounting for high voluntary participation in the absence of direct coercion, (b) explaining persistence of killing when practical exit routes existed, and (c) accommodating the observed absence of widespread long-term psychological trauma among perpetrators. For each framework, assign a tier—high, moderate, or low—for each criterion, and provide a concise justification of one to two sentences per tier. Then, in a brief concluding assessment, state whether any single framework dominates across all three criteria or whether the ranking forces a genuinely pluralistic explanation, and identify which feature of the weakest-performing framework most undermines its generalizability.
