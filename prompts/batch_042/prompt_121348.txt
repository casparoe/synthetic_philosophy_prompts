I have been trying to determine what norm governs proper assertion—whether it requires knowledge, mere belief, truth, or something structurally more specific—and I find that my intuitions across cases do not align neatly with any standard view I have encountered. Here are my verdicts, each with the reasoning that drives it. A physician tells a patient, "this drug will cure you," on the basis of strong clinical hope but without adequate evidential support; she happens to be wrong, though she sincerely believes the claim. I judge this improper, and the deficiency seems to lie not merely in falsehood or in lack of knowledge but in the mismatch between her evidential position and the categorical force of the assertion. By contrast, a historian writes in a survey text, "the treaty was signed in 1815," relying on a reputable secondary source that is in fact erroneous; she believes the claim and has done reasonable due diligence, yet she does not know it is false. Here I feel the assertion is permissible, which suggests that knowledge is not strictly necessary if belief is well-grounded in an appropriate institutional context.

A defense attorney states in closing argument, "my client did not commit the crime," fully aware that he did; I do not treat this as a failed assertion governed by an epistemic norm, but rather as a role-bound performative utterance within an adversarial legal frame that suspends the usual requirements. An engineer declares in a post-mortem review, "if we had used titanium, the bracket would not have failed," supported by strong simulation data but with no actual counterfactual verification and without full-blooded belief in the ordinary sense—she treats it as a design heuristic. This feels acceptable, yet it lacks both knowledge and perhaps standard belief, which makes me wonder whether counterfactual planning assertions answer to a different norm altogether, perhaps one of practical justification rather than epistemic warrant. A friend writes, "the check is in the mail," knowing it is false but intending only to defer a social tension without causing harm; I regard this as improper, though its wrongness seems less grave than lying in a sworn affidavit, suggesting that the context of assertion modulates severity without changing the underlying norm. Finally, a mathematician publicly conjectures a result she considers highly plausible but for which she has no proof; this strikes me as legitimate within mathematical practice, even if her confidence falls below what I would call belief, again indicating that disciplinary framing may relax the usual conditions.

Given this pattern, is there a unified principle that distinguishes evidential assertions, institutional role-utterances, counterfactual planning claims, and disciplinary conjectures—applying a knowledge norm to the first, a role-legitimacy condition to the second, a practical-justification standard to the third, and a plausibility threshold to the fourth? Or is there a simpler account—perhaps centered on belief plus contextual appropriateness—that can accommodate all six verdicts without multiplying norms? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon, and apply it to a case I have not mentioned: an automated diagnostic system outputs, "this scan indicates malignancy," calibrated to be ninety-nine percent accurate across populations but lacking any belief or understanding; does it make an assertion, and under what norm, if any?
