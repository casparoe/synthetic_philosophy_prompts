I'm writing on metaphysics and the sciences, and I need a referee for a dispute that broke out during a recent workshop on ontology and cognition. Two scholars—call them Dr. Lena Voss and Dr. Marcus Chen—spent an hour arguing over whether Bradley's regress actually undermines relational unity, or whether Russell's classic dissolution merely papers over it. Voss opened by insisting that Russell's distinction between relating relations and other relations is purely taxonomic: it sorts relations into boxes without explaining how a relation can hold between terms without itself being anchored by something further. Chen replied that this misses Russell's central point—relating is not an act that requires a substrate, just as the fact that a knife cuts bread doesn't demand a further cutting relation linking the knife to its cutting. Voss then pressed a specific case: consider the adverbial structure the molecule vibrates rapidly. If we treat rapidly as a relation R between the vibrating event and the property of rapidity, we seem to need a further relation R' binding R to the event, then R'', and so on. Chen answered that adverbial modification is precisely where Russell's move succeeds—vibrates rapidly expresses one complex property rather than a relation between two entities—so the regress never gets started. Voss then introduced an empirical wrinkle drawn from perceptual neuroscience: in cross-modal binding, the brain integrates color and shape into unified percepts, and some researchers describe this integration as a binding relation that itself requires temporal synchronization signals; if metaphysics treats such binding as genuinely relational, Bradley's regress threatens not just abstract ontology but cognitive science itself. Chen objected that this conflates causal synchronization with metaphysical constitution—the brain's binding mechanisms are causal processes, not ontological relations—so importing Bradley into neuroscience commits a category error. Voss countered that Chen hasn't shown where the line between causal and ontological binding is drawn: if causal processes can unite properties without infinite further relations, why should ontological relations be forbidden from doing the same? Chen conceded that Russell's answer handles logical form, but insisted that Bradley's deeper worry concerns the intelligibility of relational unity itself, which Russell ultimately takes as primitive rather than explained.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move—Voss's opening, Chen's reply, Voss's adverbial case, Chen's response, Voss's neuroscience move, Chen's category-error objection, Voss's final challenge, and Chen's concession—rating each step from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying each rating. Then deliver an overall verdict on whether Bradley's regress is genuinely dissolved or merely relocated, and identify what both parties overlook about the unity of the proposition—particularly whether a non-relational account of unity can remain coherent without collapsing into Bradley's absolute.
