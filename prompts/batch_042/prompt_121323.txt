I am preparing a long-form feature on the resurgence of absurdist themes within contemporary philosophy of life, and before filing I want to stress-test my own conceptual framework. Here is my current position. First, human life possesses no intrinsic, cosmic, or mind-independent meaning; the universe offers no external guarantee of purpose. Second, I take meaning to be constructed rather than discovered: significance arises through sustained evaluative projects and the commitments we author over time. Third, I accept a broadly Camus-style diagnosis of the absurd—not as a formal contradiction, but as a lived tension between our deep orientation toward transcendent significance and the absence of any metaphysical validation for it. Fourth, I reject the prescription of heroic revolt—living with passionate intensity in open defiance—as the primary or correct response. Instead, I defend what I call deflationary narrative coherence: the best account of meaning in life is the intelligible temporal integration of one's projects, yet this coherence carries only pragmatic and psychological weight, not ontological authority, and it must be held with ironic distance rather than existential seriousness. Fifth, I deny that any objective-list theory—one that enumerates specific achievements, relationships, or capacities as necessary and sufficient conditions for a meaningful life—captures what is genuinely at stake in these debates. I want an audit of whether these commitments hang together. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, and which only appear so? Specifically, does my constructivism about meaning undermine my claim that narrative coherence is the best framework, reducing it to mere subjective comfort? Does my rejection of heroic revolt dissolve the very absurd I claim to diagnose—if the tension is scaled back through ironic distance, is there still an absurd condition to respond to, or have I quietly solved what I said was unsolvable? And does my denial of objective-list conditions sit comfortably with my reliance on coherence, or does it commit me to a relativism I have not acknowledged? Rank the tensions by severity, specify the least costly revision for each, and identify which single revision would resolve the greatest number of conflicts. Treat this as an audit of my position, not a general lecture on absurdism or narrative ethics.
