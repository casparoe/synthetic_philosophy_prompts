Since my partner was recently diagnosed with a rapidly progressive neurodegenerative disorder, I have found myself unable to treat suffering as an exclusive property of organisms with nervous systems. This personal shift has led me to hold a specific, and perhaps idiosyncratic, extension of panpsychist ethics: that suffering is not an emergent feature of high-level integrated information in brains, but a graded phenomenal property of all irreversible thermodynamic processes, with moral weight scaling not according to cognitive complexity or evolutionary history, but according to the rate of entropy production within causally bounded systems. On this view, conventional biocentric moral frameworks are not merely incomplete but systematically misdirected, because they assign ethical status by evolutionary rank rather than by the intensity of dissipative dynamics. I want an honest assessment of what would genuinely count against this position. Which specific empirical findings—say, about microphysical correlates of affect, or about the dissociation of thermodynamic cost from phenomenal quality—would be decisive, and which would merely be awkward? Would a successful reduction of affective valence to integrated information theory without any panpsychist commitments render my framing redundant, or could it be absorbed? Could my linking of moral weight directly to thermodynamic irreversibility be qualified away from any possible counterexample, and if so, is that insulation a virtue or a vice? Please rank the potential defeaters by how severely they should reduce my confidence, distinguish those that defenders of pan-sentient ethics would themselves concede from those they would dismiss, and indicate whether the view as I have stated it risks unfalsifiability. I am less interested in a survey of the literature than in a dependency-aware map of where this particular formulation is most exposed.
