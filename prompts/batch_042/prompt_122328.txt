I'm a junior interning at the regional heritage museum's public-history division, and my supervisor has asked me to draft evaluation protocols for new commemorative exhibits. For a course I'm taking on modern state formation, I've been working with the idea that national and local traditions are not simply discovered but often constructed—what historians of nationalism have described as the invention of tradition and the formation of imagined communities—yet I need to translate those frameworks into something a non-specialist curator can actually apply. Could you write a reusable decision procedure, in ordered steps with clear stopping rules, that my team could run on any proposal claiming a practice is an ancient, continuous national or communal tradition? The procedure should audit continuity across three axes—functional purpose, symbolic content, and the social body of participants—while also testing for the mediated, anonymous co-participation characteristic of imagined communities; it should flag where a claim shifts from continuous tradition to revived practice, deliberate modern invention, or retroactive misattribution; and it should explicitly mark the judgment calls—especially about whether symbolic change counts as rupture—where the process must hand off to a person rather than yielding a mechanical answer. Please apply the procedure to three proposals: first, a local "medieval winter solstice procession" that our archives show was organized by a merchant guild in 1923 and revived in 1988 for tourism; second, a proposed national "Day of Unity" flag-raising ceremony presented as descending from thirteenth-century communal oaths but standardized by a government ministry in 1892; and third, the induction ritual at my own school, which students and alumni treat as a century-old founder's tradition but which the procedure will likely reveal was created in 1994—an outcome I will not like, since I value the ritual, but which I want included precisely to show where the procedure conflicts with attachment rather than evidence. In each case, show where the steps give a clear classification, where they stall for lack of documentation, and where values about authenticity must take over from historical analysis.
