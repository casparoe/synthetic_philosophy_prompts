Here is an argument I have been working through about the Knobe effect, and I cannot locate the mistake, though the conclusion strikes me as intolerable.

(1) In the canonical vignettes, subjects judge that a CEO who implements a program knowing it will harm the environment intends that harm, while a CEO who implements a program knowing it will help the environment does not intend that help, despite the mental states being identical except for the outcome's valence.

(2) One prominent explanation holds that this asymmetry is driven not by morality per se but by statistical abnormality: harmful outcomes deviate from an implicit baseline, so they are attributed to the agent's plan, whereas beneficial outcomes match the baseline and require no special explanatory attribution.

(3) If abnormality rather than moral evaluation drives the attribution, then the asymmetry should reverse whenever the statistical baseline reverses, regardless of moral content.

(4) Consider crisis triage: without intervention, patient death is the statistical baseline, while saving a life is the abnormal deviation. By the abnormality account, a physician who foresees a death but saves another patient should be judged as not intending the death (it is baseline), but should be judged as intending the save (it is abnormal).

(5) Yet the folk concept of intention does not appear to invert this way; harm retains its privileged status for intentionality attribution even in contexts where it is the expected, baseline outcome.

(6) Therefore, statistical abnormality cannot be the driver, and moral evaluation must be constitutive of the folk concept of intentional action.

(7) If moral evaluation is constitutive, then whether an agent intended a foreseen side effect depends on whether observers evaluate that side effect negatively, not on any further psychological fact about the agent.

(8) But different observers—operating under different normative frameworks—can disagree about whether a given outcome is bad: one observer might see carbon emissions as harmful, another might see them as a necessary byproduct of prosperity.

(9) Consequently, there is no observer-independent fact about whether the CEO intended the environmental effect; intentionality becomes radically relative to the observer's values.

(10) This dissolves the distinction between intended and merely foreseen consequences that underpins legal doctrines of mens rea and the moral principle of double effect, since those doctrines presuppose that intention is a stable feature of the agent's mental economy rather than a projection of the audience's morality.

(11) So either the abnormality explanation is correct and the Knobe effect is an unstable artifact that should disappear in baseline-reversed contexts (which it does not seem to do), or the moral-constitution view is correct and intentionality collapses into observer-relativity. Both outcomes seem intolerable.

I would most like to preserve (1) and (6), and I am especially unwilling to give up the idea that there is some objective fact about what an agent intends. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, is there a hidden slide between treating moral evaluation as a cognitive trigger for attribution and treating it as metaphysically constitutive of intentionality itself?
