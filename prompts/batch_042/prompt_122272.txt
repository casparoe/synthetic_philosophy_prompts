Construct a one-paragraph thought experiment situated in an ordinary, non-scientific decision context—specifically, a juror weighing ambiguous testimony about whether a contract was signed—engineered so that an accuracy-first epistemologist and an epistemic-utility theorist deliver opposing credence recommendations, and so that the divergence is generated by the theories themselves rather than by ignorance, computational limits, or extreme stakes. The scenario must involve exactly two competing hypotheses about a single event, with an asymmetric but non-catastrophic practical cost structure: a false belief in hypothesis H carries a moderate, concrete penalty, while a false belief in not-H carries none, yet both errors remain epistemically undesirable. Inject the following feature—call it a reflective calibration trap: the juror possesses reliable higher-order knowledge that her first-order evidential method systematically overestimates the probability of H by a small, quantifiable margin, and an accuracy-first norm, which directs her to calibrate her credence solely to maximize expected Brier-score improvement given this self-knowledge, forces her to revise her credence downward below what her first-order evidence alone warrants. By contrast, the epistemic-utility theorist, who allows non-accuracy values such as explanatory coherence and the avoidance of self-undermining doubt, permits her to hold the first-order credence. After presenting the scenario, specify the belief, the method, the relevant nearby worlds, and show precisely why each theory gives the verdict it does. Then identify the structural feature—the interaction between second-order method-awareness and an exclusively accuracy-weighted value function—that makes the case work; explain whether eliminating the practical cost asymmetry collapses the disagreement back into convergence or merely relocates it; and state how an accuracy-first defender could attack the case without denying the juror's higher-order knowledge.
