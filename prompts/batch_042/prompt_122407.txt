As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks—about ten minutes—at an interdisciplinary workshop held in the archives of a science museum. The audience is deliberately split: professional historians of science, philosophers of science, and practicing researchers who rarely think about how their disciplines are narrated. I want the remarks to avoid generic inspiration; instead, the problem of Whig history—our persistent temptation to write the past as an inevitable progress toward the present—should do real structural work in shaping what I can and cannot say. I am particularly interested in using an idea I have been calling “retrospective closure,” which I take to be the habit of treating past conceptual disputes as already settled by the categories we now hold, so that rival frameworks appear not as live alternatives but as primitive approximations of modern consensus.

Please write these remarks in American English, without citations, bibliographies, or reading lists, and without putting any specific author’s words in quotation marks. The speech should open by acknowledging that every narrative needs a through-line, then press on the danger of structural silence: for instance, when we tell the story of nineteenth-century thermodynamics as an inevitable march toward entropy and statistical mechanics, we erase the fact that the “energetics” tradition—treating energy transformation as primary and mechanical explanation as derivative—was not merely wrong but conceptually organized around distinctions that do not map onto ours. The point should land for the experts without losing the scientists, and it should avoid false consolation or sentimental framing. Rather than ending with a simple exhortation to “remember the losers,” the remarks should argue that avoiding Whig history requires recognizing that even our present distinction between “pure theory” and “engineering practice” was itself a contested historical outcome, not a neutral lens through which to view the past. Make the philosophical idea shape the examples, the tone, and the refusal of an easy moral.
