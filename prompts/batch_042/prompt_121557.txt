I'm on this local council commitee for heritage and green spaces — don't ask me how I ended up there, probably my neighbour nominated me after a few pints. We've got to draft a short opinion memo for Thursday's meeting about the old Whitmore gate at Millbrook Park. It's a rusted cast-iron thing from the 1920s sitting on a footpath the council rerouted in the 80s, so technically it serves no traffic purpose. The parks dept wants it demolished: it costs money to maintain, the rust is getting worse, and they say clearing it improves sightlines and lets them replant native grasses. The local history society is furious and keeps talking about Chesterton's fence and Burkean conservatism — don't tear something down just because you can't see its purpose, institutions accumulate wisdom that outlives their makers, etc. I think there's weight to that, but I don't want us to just romanticise old metal.

What makes it genuinely hard is something nobody's documented officially: for the last fifteen years or so, a loose group of urban foragers and amateur ecologists have used the gate as an accidental meeting point. They swap seeds, track seasonal changes, and apparently the gate acts like a threshold that makes people pause and talk — take the gate away and this whole unrecorded social practice probably dissolves without anyone knowing why. So the Burkean point applies, but not in the usual way: the 'hidden wisdom' isn't the original 1920s purpose, it's an emergent social ecology that only exists because the gate stayed put long enough.

Can you draft the memo? Recommend either preservation with a concrete maintenance plan and timeline, or removal with conditions, and explain the reasoning clearly. Take the Burkean conservative view seriously — address why tearing down inherited structures is risky even when the original function is gone — but also give the strongest argument for the other side: if we preserve every rusty gate we'll never adapt, and the maintenance budget is real. Write it in British English, keep it formal enough to circulate but not like an academic paper. And please, no bibliography or reading list — half the committee wouldn't read it anyway, and the rest of us don't need one.
