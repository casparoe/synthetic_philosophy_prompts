hey, retired chemist here, been poking around philosophy of action and ethics since I retired. I keep seeing this move and I dont know if it has a proper name. The idea is indirect normativity -- you figure out what a fully rational, fully informed version of you would want, and that generates your real-world obligations. But I notice people deploy it selectively. Example one: someone asks why they should save for retirement when the future is unpredictable, and the reply is "your idealized self knows the probabilities and wants you to save, so thats your real duty." Example two: in medical ethics, "what would a rational patient want?" is used to justify continuing treatment, but if you point out that a rational patient might prioritize quality of life over length and want to stop, suddenly they say "we cant actually run that computation, so we default to preserving life." So the idealized deliberation is treated as authoritative when it supports the speaker's conclusion, and as an unrealistic heuristic when it undermines it. Ive seen similar patterns in environmental policy debates. Is there an established concept for this specific failure mode of indirect normativity -- not just hypocrisy or inconsistency, but this particular double-use of the ideal agent? If not, can you coin a term with clear boundaries that seperate it from ordinary equivocation and from the legitimate Kantian use of ideals as regulative ideas? I want to know exactly what the phenomenon is, what it isnt, and where the edges are, not just a bare label.
