I am drafting an arbitration clause for two siblings who inherited their father's woodworking studio and disagree over a specific workbench. During his forty-year career, he replaced every board, leg, and surface—some twice—using lumber from the same regional forest and replicating the original joinery exactly. Even the cast-iron vise, the only untouched exterior piece, had its internal gears rebuilt twice. One sibling claims that because no continuous material substrate links the present bench to the original construction, the inherited object is technically a replica; it should be sold as a reproduction, not distributed as family heirloom property. The other sibling insists that continuous functional role, an unbroken causal chain of maintenance, and their father's consistent reference to it as "the same bench" preserve its identity regardless of material turnover. I need the clause to depend on the correct account of how objects persist through change: is identity anchored in an enduring core present at every stage, or is it a relation among temporal parts of a four-dimensional process? More precisely, if an artifact can survive complete part-replacement while preserving structural relations and intentional history, is there still a principled boundary between restoration and replacement, or is that distinction conventional? And if we reject the idea that the bench is fully present at each moment in favor of viewing it as extended in time, does that solve the dispute or merely rename the intuition that something essential was lost? I am not asking for legal advice; I want to know what metaphysical framework should govern the contract, and whether the disagreement reveals a real feature of artifacts or a confusion about tracking conventions.
