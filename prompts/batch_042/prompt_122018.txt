next teusday I'm giving a 20-min talk at an interdisclipinary workshop on moral emotion and social structure. The thesis is that guilt and shame aren't just interchangeable feelings a culture can vote on; they're anchored in different "accountability architectures"—guilt needs confessional, individualizing institutions (courts, therapy, private apology) while shame needs ritual, exposure-based ones (public shunning, honor codes, communal repair). So asking which is "better" misses the point unless you also ask which architecture you're living inside. I do want to argue that guilt is normatively preferable in pluralistic societies because it localizes blame rather than dissolving the self into the group, but I admit that's contingent on already having liberal legal institutions, not a universal moral fact.

The room will have about 15 people: cultural anthropologists who think the guilt/shame binary is mostly a Western taxonomy imposed on everyone else, clinical psych folks who treat shame as toxic and guilt as adaptive, and a few moral philosophers who think this is all empirical sociology that can't ground normative claims. There's also one historian of japanese ethics who might bring up seppuku analogies.
