Plan a fifty-minute undergraduate session in philosophy of mind on whether dreams are experiences, taking Norman Malcolm's denial—grounded in the absence of any criterion for correct dream-report—against Daniel Dennett's view that dreams are narratives composed upon awakening rather than experiences occurring during sleep. Do not summarize the texts; design the teaching sequence: the conceptual dependency order (why Malcolm's criterion-objection must be in place before Dennett's temporal-reconstruction response can be evaluated), the single concrete scenario that should carry the session and why the standard falling-dream example is pedagogically wrong, the misconceptions students will form—especially conflating Dennett's claim with generic skepticism about dream-memory rather than about the temporal location of experience—and the precise point in the sequence to defuse each, one end-of-session question that distinguishes understanding the disagreement from merely parroting positions, and what must be explicitly excluded (e.g., empirical sleep-stage data) with a brief justification for the omission. Keep the focus on the conceptual architecture of first-person authority versus retrospective construction.
