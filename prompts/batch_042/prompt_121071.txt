I serve on the allocation board of the Meridian Literary Foundation, a body that awards annual fellowships to writers at early career stages. At our March session, the chair introduced a restructuring proposal that applies principles of distributive justice—specifically, the idea of a procedural veil of ignorance combined with a difference principle directing resources to the least advantaged—to our selection mechanism. Under her model, applicants would submit work stripped of biographical and generic markers; jurors would evaluate portfolios without knowing whether they read poetry, narrative nonfiction, or experimental fiction, and only after funding commitments are made would assignments be revealed. The allocation formula itself would weight awards toward candidates whose preliminary economic review places them in the lowest resource tier, on the argument that artistic patronage should correct structural inequality rather than reward already-visible excellence.

The board is divided: several members, including our legal advisor, view this as a necessary institutional correction; others, led by our senior poet, contend that applying a framework designed for the basic structure of society to aesthetic judgment collapses the distinction between justice and merit, rendering the fellowship meaningless. I am tasked with drafting the board's decision memo for circulation before Thursday's vote. I need it to recommend a concrete course—approve the model, approve it with a defined pilot period and evaluation criteria, or reject it outright—together with a specific implementation timeline or sunset clause. The reasoning must engage the Rawlsian argument in its strongest form, including the claim that unknown position in the literary economy functions analogously to unknown social position behind a veil, and it must also treat the opposition's strongest point with intellectual honesty rather than dismissing it as elitism. As a novelist who has sat on juries that operated both blindly and with full disclosure, I am particularly interested in whether the memo can articulate why artistic recognition requires a different kind of knowledge than the procedural fairness this model demands.
