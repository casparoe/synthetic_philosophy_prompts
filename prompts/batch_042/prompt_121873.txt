There's this pattern in professional certification thats always bugged me. To become a surgeon or electrician you do thousands of hours supervised practice then perform live — nobody cares if you can write an essay about cutting. But to become a philosopher or historian it's all essays and written exams, and nobody tests whether you can actually think well in real time. What's weirder is that some vocational programmes now make apprentices keep written reflection journals about what their hands are learning, as if knowing-how needs knowing-that to prop it up, while medical residencies don't ask surgeons to write about their gut feelings.

What view of knowing how versus knowing that does that split presuppose? Does it treat them as genuinely seperate domains, or assume practical skill is always opaque without propositional backing? And why demand the translation for tradespeople but not surgeons — is it consistency, or just status? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology here: what's strictly required by the practice, what's just a nice fit, and is the whole thing philosophically underdetermined? Then tell me what'd have to change if we certified everyone the same way, purely practical or purely propositional.
