You are delivering the dedication address for a new archival reading room at a university-affiliated psychiatric hospital. The room honors Thomas Szasz, and the audience is deliberately mixed: clinical psychiatrists who largely reject his thesis, family members of patients who have found his critique of the medical model liberating, and institutional donors who want the hospital to appear intellectually open without undermining its clinical mission. Write the address—approximately eight minutes when spoken—taking Szasz's specific claim that mental illness is a myth not in the sense of being fictitious, but in the sense that human problems in living have been systematically translated into the language and institutional practices of medicine. Use that distinction as structural scaffolding: it should govern which terms you can employ without qualification, which terms require explicit qualification, and which rhetorical moves are forbidden. Specifically, distinguish vocabulary appropriate to suffering, choice, and social context from vocabulary that implies an unexamined biological reductionism, and avoid both equating rejection of medicalization with denial of distress and equating clinical care with moral neutrality. The speech must comfort family members without insulting clinicians, and it must articulate a clear purpose for the room that neither collapses into anti-psychiatric celebration nor retreats into bland institutional pluralism. Do not include citations, bibliographies, or references to specific works, editions, or page numbers.
