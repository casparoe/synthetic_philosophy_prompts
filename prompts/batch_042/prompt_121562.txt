Please compare Aristotle's analysis of weakness of will (akrasia) in the Nicomachean Ethics with the behavioral-economic model of hyperbolic discounting, particularly the quasi-hyperbolic (β-δ) formulation. For Aristotle, akrasia is not a failure to know the universal principle—say, that overindulgence in bodily pleasures is harmful—but rather a failure in the particular: appetite or passion pulls the agent's particular judgment away from what the universal prescribes, leaving the universal knowledge present but unexercised, comparable to the understanding of someone asleep or distracted. The agent thus acts against his own best judgment, not from ignorance of it.

In the β-δ model, by contrast, an agent applies a discount factor that declines hyperbolically with delay. The result is dynamic inconsistency: the same reward is valued differently depending solely on its temporal distance from the present, producing preference reversals without any change in underlying tastes or in the agent's knowledge of long-run welfare.

Your task is to assess whether these two frameworks describe the same phenomenon in different idioms, or whether they track structurally different failures. Address the following in particular.

First, normative status. Aristotle treats akrasia as a rational failure: the agent ought to have governed appetite by practical reason, and the fact that he did not is blameworthy (or at least defective). The β-δ model, however, describes preference reversal as the consistent output of a single evaluation function with a present-bias parameter. Does framing weakness of will as hyperbolic discounting therefore eliminate the normative failure and replace it with a descriptive feature of intertemporal choice?

Second, the locus of failure. For Aristotle, the corruption is in the particular judgment, not the universal: the agent knows that excess is bad but is overcome in the moment by the appearance of pleasure here and now. In hyperbolic discounting, the agent's evaluation of the very same future outcome shifts as it becomes present. Is this shift analogous to Aristotle's particular being pulled away from the universal, or is it closer to what he distinguishes as akolasia—a corruption even of the universal judgment? Or is it something else entirely?

Third, domain clustering and bodily appetite. Aristotelian akrasia is paradigmatically about food, drink, and sexual pleasure—domains in which bodily appetite exerts direct force. Hyperbolic discounting applies with equal formal structure to money, labor, and health behaviors, with no intrinsic reference to bodily passion. Can the Aristotelian framework account for the domain specificity of weakness without importing an empirical discount function? Conversely, can the economic model capture the specifically appetitive, bodily character of Aristotelian failure, or does it necessarily abstract away from it?

Finally, consider whether a synthesis is coherent: might hyperbolic discounting supply the mechanism by which particular judgments are pulled away from universal principles, while Aristotelian practical reason supplies the normative framework that makes that pull a failure rather than a preference? Or are the two frameworks incommensurable—one normative and reason-based, the other descriptive and preference-based?
