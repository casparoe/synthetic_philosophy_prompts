I have been following debates about distributive justice and public policy, and I keep encountering a specific argumentative pattern that I do not have a precise name for. The move runs like this. Someone objects to a collective decision—say, a vaccination mandate, a progressive tax scheme, or a risk-pooling insurance system—by saying that it treats certain individuals as if their welfare were subordinate to the welfare of the group, or as if they existed mainly to serve a larger purpose. The objection sounds absolute: it is not merely that the burden is unfairly distributed, but that being placed in that position is itself wrong, even if one ends up benefiting on balance. Then, when critics point out that ordinary social cooperation involves similar trade-offs—that taxation, jury duty, or traffic laws also ask individuals to bear costs for collective ends—the original objector typically draws a distinction. They say those cases are different because they are universal, or reciprocal, or consented to, or because the benefit is direct rather than statistical. The result is that the initial, stark claim—that it is wrong to be made to serve others’ benefit—is enforced vigorously against some policies and some philosophical examples, such as the surgeon who harvests organs from one healthy patient to save five, but it is quietly suspended for structurally similar institutional practices. What I am trying to identify is whether this specific configuration—a moral boundary that is sharp in hypothetical or targeted cases and porous in systemic ones, defended not by a consistent criterion but by a shifting set of contextual exemptions—has an established name in moral philosophy. Is it an application of the separateness-of-persons objection, a form of selective intuition-pumping, or something else entirely? If there is no precise term, how should one characterize its edges, and what distinguishes it from ordinary contextual reasoning? In your answer, please state how confident you are in your identification, and explicitly single out the point about which you are least certain.
