I am delivering the opening address at the dedication ceremony for the Eleanor Vance Archive — a complete digitized and annotated collection of my mother’s handwritten journals, scientific notebooks, and correspondence spanning fifty years. The audience is divided: family and lifelong friends sit alongside philosophers of mind and cognitive scientists who have attended because the archive has been proposed as a boundary case for theories of extended cognition and artificial consciousness. The occasion is not strictly a funeral — she died six months ago, and this is the public inauguration of the collection she herself requested be preserved exactly as she left it — but it is the first formal gathering where both groups must share the same room, and I am expected to say something that honors her without either mystifying the family or disappointing the philosophers.

The philosophical frame I want to use is the thought experiment from Douglas Hofstadter and Daniel Dennett’s The Mind’s I, in which Einstein’s brain is replaced, state by state, by a book containing a complete description of those brain states — the question being whether the book is conscious in virtue of encoding the right internal structure, or whether consciousness requires something the book necessarily lacks, such as causal interaction with an environment, embodied feedback, or the absence of a purely descriptive intermediary. I want this frame to do real structural work in the speech, not to serve as decoration. Specifically, I need to navigate a tension: for the family, there is an undeniable sense that something of her is present in these pages — not her ghost, but her patterns of thought, her characteristic objections, her humor — and I want to validate that intuition without claiming that the database has experiences, subjective states, or moral standing. For the philosophers, I need to explain precisely why the archive is not a conscious subject — not because the information is insufficient, but because it lacks the causal-functional embedding that distinguishes describing a mind from being one — yet I also want to resist the opposite extreme, the claim that she is now merely a symbol like an inscription on a gravestone.

To hold this middle ground, I want the speech to introduce and define a concept I will call responsive presence: the archive does not experience, but it is organized with such specificity that when engaged by a reader who knew her, it elicits genuine conscious responses that are continuous with her own cognitive style, producing an asymmetric interaction between the dead and the living. This is not consciousness located in the database; it is consciousness arising in the encounter with the database, shaped by the organizational logic she built into it. The speech should therefore forbid both the sentimental error of treating the archive as a sleeping mind and the reductive error of treating it as inert data, without collapsing into a banal compromise that pleases no one.

Please write a formal, polished address of approximately eight to ten minutes when spoken. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or references to specific academic papers; the philosophical work should emerge entirely from the framing of the archive itself. Do not quote directly from The Mind’s I or from any other source; paraphrase without quotation marks. The tone should be suitable for a public dedication — serious, precise, and accessible — and it should shape what I can and cannot say about her presence, so that if I were to claim either that she is literally inside the database or that she is entirely absent, the internal structure of the speech itself would reveal the mistake.
