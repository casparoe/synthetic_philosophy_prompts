I am a novelist revising a manuscript of interlinked stories set in institutional care settings, and I wish to operationalise Tolstoy's theory of art—particularly his distinction between genuine art, which transmits a feeling of universal brotherhood by direct emotional infection, and counterfeit art, which substitutes decorative technique, obscurity, or calculated effect for sincere transmission—into a practical evaluation procedure for individual scenes. Please construct a sequential decision procedure I can run during revision: list the ordered tests (for sincerity of feeling, clarity of transmission, absence of substitutive technique, and accessibility regardless of the reader's prior education), specify what counts as a pass or failure at each stage, mark the precise points where judgment is irreducible and the procedure must halt and defer to my decision as author, and state explicitly what the procedure cannot adjudicate. Apply it to three draft scenes: first, a carer recognising a patient's distress but being blocked by protocol, rendered in free indirect discourse that deliberately withholds emotional labels; second, the same situation rendered through an elaborate extended metaphor comparing the ward to a cathedral; and third, a brief dialogue in which patient and carer exchange only practical instructions, yet the subtext conveys mutual recognition. Ensure that one result contradicts my likely preference as the author—specifically, where the procedure condemns a technique I am inclined to preserve. Write in British English. Finally, indicate what specific argument or consideration about modern narrative form would cause you to revise your verdict on whether Tolstoy's framework can genuinely accommodate techniques such as free indirect discourse, and identify the point in your reasoning where failure is most probable.
