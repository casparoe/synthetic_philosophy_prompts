i've been re-reading The Picture of Dorian Gray and got stuck on something that feels like a philosophy problem but isn't quite the standard art-forgery debate. There's this notion i'm calling 'moral provenance tracking' — basically, the idea that corruption or value has to attach to the right causal origin, not just to whatever looks like the source. In Wilde's novel the portrait is the original artwork and Dorian is, in a sense, the living copy: he keeps the youthful appearance while the painting accumulates every sin. So is Wilde showing that the original bears the moral weight and the copy stays pure, or is he actually undermining that — suggesting Dorian is the real corrupted thing and the canvas is only a record?

I'd like you to read the novel through this lens, especially the locked-room scenes where Basil finally sees the changed portrait and where Dorian stabs it at the end. Does the painting's seeming agency — the way it changes on its own — support the idea that moral damage needs an authentic bearer, or does it break the causal chain that 'provenance tracking' requires?
