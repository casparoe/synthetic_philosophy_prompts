As a novelist drafting a long work about a sculptor who, over decades, replaces every particle of clay in her masterpiece without altering its visible form, I have become preoccupied with the metaphysical problem of material constitution—specifically whether the statue and the arranged clay are one object or two, and what temporary-parts theories imply for persistence through gradual replacement. For the novel's closing chapter, I require a poem that renders this problem structurally rather than discursively. Compose a villanelle in which the first refrain asserts the constitution view—that the statue is nothing over and above the clay arranged in precisely this form—while the second refrain asserts the non-identity view, that the statue survives precisely by being other than any particular arrangement of clay. Let the form's compulsory repetition enact mereological change: each recurrence of the refrains should correspond to a successive replacement of material parts, so that by the time the two lines converge in the final couplet, the reader experiences the logical tension of both being true and both being false without resolution. In the middle stanzas, interweave the novelist's parallel concern—whether a fictional character persists when every sentence that first constituted her is rewritten—without resolving either tension. The poem should be accurate enough that a metaphysician recognizes the distinction between enduring three-dimensional objects and four-dimensional processes, yet it should remain poetry: let rhyme, refrain, and turn do the philosophical work that argument cannot. Do not provide explanatory prose or any bibliography; the verse itself must carry the contradiction open.
