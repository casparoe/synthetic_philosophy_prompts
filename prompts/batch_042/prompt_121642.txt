As a novelist accustomed to nested authorship and contradictory canons, I keep encountering the simulation argument presented through a tidy literary analogy: we are characters in a novel being composed in real time, with the simulator cast as a single omniscient author, and the analogy is used to argue that internal science remains valid because narrative logic holds regardless of an external writer. I think that image smuggles in more coherence than the hypothesis deserves, so I want to audit it under a messier model of creation — a collaborative writers' room with rotating showrunners, partly procedural plot generation, and unresolved inconsistencies that characters can discover but never fully explain. Lay out the correspondence table: simulator, physical law, scientific method, and discovered anomaly mapped onto author, narrative logic, internal critique, and plot hole. Then show where the mapping fractures, especially whether a discovered inconsistency gives a character evidence of an external author or only of production dysfunction, and whether causal closure survives when authorship is distributed and partly automated. Finally, does the conclusion that being simulated is epistemically harmless still stand once the single-author analogy is replaced by this industrial, fragmented model of world-generation?
