Two people in my study group got stuck arguing about whether death is bad for you in the Epicurean sense. One said death can't harm the dead because harm needs a present subject, and once you're dead there's no you to suffer anything — complaining about being dead is like complaining there's no you in 1800, which is truely senseless. The other said thats only true for experiential harm, but death also frustrates interests you held while alive — like wanting your kids to do well or your project finished — and those are genuinely yours even if you're not around to feel the frustration. The first replied that Epicurus covered exactly this: if you're not present to experience the frustration, it's not bad for you personally, maybe bad for the world but not a harm to you. The second said hes treating 'harm to you' too narrowly, like it's only physical pain. Then she threw out this idea she called 'narrative truncation harm' — that a life is like a story with an intended arc, and cutting it short is a structural flaw in that specific life, not just a neutral ending. So death is bad not because you feel it, but because it makes your life an incomplete object. The first guy said thats just aesthetics disguised as ethics — a short novel isn't harmed, it's just shorter. She said its not about length, its about whether the structure you were actually building gets realized; if you were in the middle of something that defined your identity, truncation damages the coherence of that identity-pattern. He said shes smuggling in value judgments about 'complete lives' that Epicurus would reject. Can you go through each move and adjudicate? Specifically rate: (1) the subject-requirement point, (2) the posthumous-interest reply, (3) the Epicurean 'no present subject' counter, (4) the narrative-truncation idea, (5) the aesthetics-objection, (6) the identity-pattern defense. Also, is narrative truncation actually different from the standard deprivation account, or is it just relabeled?
