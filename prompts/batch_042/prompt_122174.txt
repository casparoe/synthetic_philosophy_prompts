I'm organizing a two-hour session for a philosophy-of-mind reading group—mostly second-year graduate students, plus two cognitive-science undergraduates who haven't taken metaphysics—on whether a single self, an "I," can be distributed across more than one biological brain. I'm especially drawn to Douglas Hofstadter's idea that the self is essentially a high-level pattern rather than a property locked to one substrate, and that under the right conditions another brain could continue or genuinely share that pattern. Rather than using standard teleportation or brain-upload scenarios, I want to build the session around a modified hypothetical I sketched for the group: two individuals, call them A and B, linked by a direct neural bridge that transmits only high-level narrative memory and affective valence in real time, but not sensory input or motor control; over years, A begins to experience B's autobiographical memories with the same first-person indexical as her own, yet she retains a separate body and can distinguish "my hand" from "B's hand" through intact proprioceptive feedback.

I need a teaching plan rather than the explanation itself. Specify the order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced and why—what dependency must hold so that the bridge example doesn't collapse into mere empathy or brain-computer-interface hype. Identify exactly what properties this carrying case must have and what it must lack—particularly which features preserve a distributed self and which block it—so that the example does the philosophical work I intend. Map the misconceptions the audience will predictably form, such as the belief that I'm smuggling in a Cartesian ego capable of jumping substrates, or that shared memory automatically equals the loss of individual identity, or that the scenario is just science-fiction speculation irrelevant to actual personhood; tell me precisely where in the sequence each misconception should be headed off. Design a final check—perhaps a targeted question or a subtle tweak to the scenario—that reveals whether they have grasped the idea that pattern-continuity can cross biological boundaries without collapsing into either dualism or mere psychological similarity. Finally, tell me what standard objections, competing theories, or technical literature I should deliberately leave out at this level and openly say so, since two hours is not enough to cover everything, and I want the session to stay focused on whether an "I" can genuinely be distributed rather than on every adjacent debate in personal identity.
