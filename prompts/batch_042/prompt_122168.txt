I serve on the ethics oversight committee for a regional health authority that recently adopted automated risk-scoring tools to allocate scarce specialist appointments, and I have just been appointed to sign off on every proposed rule. Our committee meets monthly, each proposal receives roughly forty-five minutes of review, none of my fellow members have formal philosophical training, and I have become convinced that justifying allocation rules by forecasting direct welfare outcomes—who survives, who suffers—is both epistemically fragile and politically unstable. I want a reusable decision procedure, grounded in indirect normativity and idealized deliberation, that translates justification through principles rather than results: a rule is acceptable only if no affected party could reasonably reject the principle underlying it under conditions in which direct outcome predictions are bracketed but informational and positional knowledge is not arbitrarily suppressed.

To prevent the procedure from collapsing into familiar Rawlsian original-position reasoning or standard Scanlonian contractualism—frameworks my colleagues have already encountered—I want two specific refinements built in. First, a “deliberative residue” step: after bracketing all direct outcome-based reasons, we must audit whether the remaining reasons for or against the rule depend on hidden indexical commitments—implicit assumptions about which social role a party occupies—that survive the bracketing only because suppressing outcome data also obscured positional information. Second, a “second-order exclusion” audit: we must list every consideration excluded from deliberation not because it is irrelevant but because it is contested, then ask whether that very act of exclusion could itself be reasonably rejected by someone holding only generic and legitimate positional reasons.

Please construct the procedure as an ordered checklist with explicit stopping rules: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates with a clear “no,” where it terminates with a conditional “yes, subject to revision,” and where it explicitly hands the decision to a human judgment call because the issue is one of values rather than procedural validity. Include a small, reusable set of test sentences or framing questions that a non-specialist can apply consistently within the time constraint.

Then run the procedure on three proposals: (1) a rule that ranks referred patients by predicted recovery rate and allocates specialist slots downward; (2) a pure lottery that assigns every referred patient an equal chance regardless of prognosis; and (3) a rule reserving fifteen percent of slots for healthcare workers, defended on the grounds that maintaining workforce capacity benefits all patients. For case (2), report the result that the procedure likely endorses—equal non-rejectability—despite its direct conflict with the authority’s welfare-maximizing goals, and explain precisely what the procedure can and cannot say about that conflict.

Please also specify the practical constraints—time per case, required sign-off, absence of philosophical expertise—and flag the points where the procedure is most likely to misfire or generate false positives, particularly where the bracketing of outcome knowledge might illegitimately conceal structural disadvantage. Finally, state how confident you are in this translation from theory to operational checklist, and single out the one step—the bracketing of outcome predictions, the treatment of positional reasons, or the second-order exclusion audit—about which you are least certain.
