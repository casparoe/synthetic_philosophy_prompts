I've been working through modern Stoic books and therapy exercises on my own, with no formal background in philosophy, and the sentence that keeps surfacing is that some things are within our power and others are not, usually boiled down to advice to focus only on what you can control. In forums and self-help contexts it gets used to dismiss anxiety about politics, other people's judgments, and past mistakes that seem irreversible. Before I treat it as a rule for living, I want to understand exactly what claim is being made. Can you lay out the distinct precise theses that sentence could express — from the tautological to the metaphysically bold — and for each specify whether it is defensible, whether it genuinely supports dismissing those particular worries, and what it implies about where the self ends and the world begins? I am especially interested in readings that separate causal influence, predictive success, and moral responsibility as different senses of being in control, and in whether indirect influence — shaping the conditions for an outcome rather than the outcome itself — counts as being within our power. Present the options as a menu rather than picking one, indicate which readings can coexist and which conflict, and say which version people invoking the slogan in modern revival contexts are most likely actually using.
