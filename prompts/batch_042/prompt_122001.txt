I'm on this municipal risk board -- nothing fancy, just local infrastructure and emergency-planning grants -- and I'm trying to work out our boards implicit logic before we vote on something big next tuesday. Over the last year we've made four calls: we killed a high-speed rail plan that had great expected commute savings but a non-negligible tail risk of catastrophic derailment near schools, and picked the bus-lane upgrade instead. We okayed a decentralized water-filteration network even though its average efficiency was lower than the central plant, because the central one had a single point of failure that could contaminate the whole district at once. We turned down a clinical-trial partnership with huge expected health gains because it required waiving standard liability caps for adverse events, leaving risk essentially unquantifable. And we funded a seismic-retrofit grant that had negative net present value on pure cost-benefit terms, but it capped the worst-case death toll in a big quake.

Now there's a proposal for an AI-driven traffic and emergency-routing system. The consultants claim it has the highest risk-weighted expected utility they've ever calculated -- they literally used that phrase, with nonlinear risk weights applied to outcomes -- but the failure mode isn't a single crash. It's a slow drift in routing priorities that compounds over years, and the probability distribution is basically unknown because nothing like it has run at city scale. There's also a hybrid version that keeps human dispatchers in the loop, giving up about thirty percent of the expected-utility gain but with fully quantifable risks and clear manual overrides. My co-chair thinks we're running pure risk-weighted expected utility and should approve the full AI version since the consultants did the weighting for us. Another member says we're actually running a two-step rule: first reject anything with an unbounded or undefined worst case, then apply risk weights only to what's left, which means we reject the AI but approve the hybrid. I'm not sure who's right about our own pattern. Can you lay out the two rules that actually fit our four past verdicts, say what each predicts for both the full AI and the hybrid, identify the closest analog case, and tell me what minimal further case would split the rules if they happen to agree here? I want to be consistent, but I also don't want to just dress up a gut feeling in fancy language.
