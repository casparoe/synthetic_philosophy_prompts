I work as a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I have been attempting to think systematically about the ethics of meat consumption, with particular attention to wild-animal suffering arising from crop cultivation. I have reached a position that strikes me as counter-intuitive, and I wish to understand precisely what would count against it.

My view is as follows. Once one accounts for the indirect death and suffering of wild animals—small mammals, insects, and ground-nesting birds—caused by mechanised planting, harvesting, and chemical application in arable feed-crop agriculture, beef becomes morally preferable to chicken on a per-unit-of-nutrition basis, and potentially preferable in absolute terms if the cattle are pasture-finished. The argument is that cattle can be raised largely on perennial grassland requiring little or no annual tillage, whereas poultry production is almost entirely dependent on cultivated feed (soya and maize). Consequently, each calorie or gram of protein from chicken carries a larger embedded footprint of field-crop mortality. I do not deny that intensive beef production involves substantial direct suffering; rather, I am weighing that direct harm against the diffuse but numerically vast wild-animal suffering embedded in the crop supply chain.

What would have to be true for this conclusion to fail? I am not seeking a bibliography or a reading list. Instead, I want an honest map of its exposure. Which empirical findings—concerning actual pasture yields, wild-animal population densities in grassland versus arable fields, or the sentience-weighting of invertebrates relative to mammals—would be decisive? Which ethical premises are load-bearing: for example, that indirect harm should be aggregated in dietary choice, or that direct harm to farmed animals outweighs distributed wild-animal harm? What arguments or analogies—perhaps drawn from optimisation under uncertainty, or from scaling effects in distributed systems—might reveal hidden assumptions in this reasoning? I also want to know whether proponents of this position have qualified it into unfalsifiability, for instance by retreating to the claim that the comparison depends entirely on specific farm-system variables, and whether such a retreat is intellectually costly rather than prudent. Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish what a committed defender ought to concede from what only a critic would press, and indicate whether the position is genuinely exposed or has been insulated to the point where that insulation itself becomes a weakness.
