I'm trying to wrap my head round a weird cluster of practices that seem to sit next to each other without anyone noticing. In the UK and plenty of other places, you've got wrongful birth suits — parents can claim damages if a doctor fails to spot a serious disability and they end up raising a child they wouldn't have chosen to have. At the same time, courts reject wrongful life suits brought by the child, saying you can't compare an existant disabled life with never having existed, because non-existence isn't a state that can be harmed or benefited. Fair enough, but then look at the funding side: the NHS will cover genetic screening and termination for serious conditions, and there's child benefit and tax breaks for people who reproduce, but there's no parallel state support — not even proper counselling — for people who want to avoid having children altogether on the grounds that they might cause suffering. Meanwhile, if you apply for IVF you get risk counselling about disability, but if you ask your GP about sterilisation because you're worried about the ethics of procreation, you mostly just get a leaflet.

What philosophical commitments about value and existence does that whole pattern presuppose? Does it quietly depend on Benatar's assymetry — absence of pain is good even with no subject, absence of pleasure isn't bad — or could a negative utilitarian or a standard welfare economist sign off on it just as easily? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether the legal and funding sides actually presuppose the same thing or are philosophically underdetermined, and tell me what a policy built consistently on the asymmetry would actually have to change — in fertility funding, genetic screening, or tax — rather than just giving me a bibliography. Write it in British English, and don't worry if I've garbled some legal detail — I'm not a lawyer, just puzzling over the inconsistency.
