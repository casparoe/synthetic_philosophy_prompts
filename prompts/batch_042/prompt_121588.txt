I've been reading some pop psych stuff about WEIRD people and moral differences across cultures -- like how Americans tend to care about harm and fairness while other groups weigh loyalty or purity more. That part makes sense. But I've noticed a specific pattern that keeps bugging me and I can't tell if there's already a name for it.

Here's the thing: people seem to treat whatever moral "factory settings" they grew up with as if they were hardcoded universal axioms, not like local config files you could have set differently. For instance, a US colleague will say "of course you should report a teammate's mistake immediately, that's just honesty" -- treating a culture-specific norm about individual accountability as if it were a logical requirement. Meanwhile someone from a more collectivist background might say "of course you cover for the team first, that's just respect" with the exact same tone of self-evidence. Its not just that they disagree; its that neither can see their own package as a package. They think the other side is running buggy logic or missing an obvious axiom, rather than running different but valid weights.

I've caught myself doing it too. I'll think "any reasonable person would agree" about something thats clearly just my local firmware.

So my question is: is there an established concept for this exact cognitive glitch -- conflating culturally installed moral defaults with objective, culture-independent truths? I know about moral relativism and objectivism and the WEIRD bias, but those feel like big-picture positions, not this specific failure mode. If there's no term, would something like "moral hardcoding illusion" work, and what would its edges be? Also, does this glitch tell us anything real about whether morality is actually objective, or is it just a bias we should correct for?
