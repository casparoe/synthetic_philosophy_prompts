Two colleagues of mine, neither of whom is posing this question, got into a sustained disagreement at a seminar about causation by omission. They were working through a hypothetical case: a municipal pool lifeguard is off-duty but still wearing her uniform near the deck. She notices a swimmer showing mild fatigue. She does not blow her whistle or alert anyone. Moments later, the swimmer suffers a sudden cramp and drowns. The lifeguard had no contractual duty to monitor that particular pool area at that time, though her uniform made her appear responsible to onlookers. Here is the exchange as reported to me.

Colleague One: "An omission is just an absence. Absences do not possess causal powers—there is no event, no energy transfer, no change in the world produced by her failure to blow the whistle. To say the omission caused the death is to confuse metaphysics with morality; we are merely blaming her because we wish she had acted."

Colleague Two: "That confuses causation with physical mechanism. Causation is often a matter of counterfactual dependence: if she had blown the whistle, rescue would have followed and death been averted. Not watering a plant kills it, and there is no physical event in the 'not-watering'—yet it is plainly causal. The whistle case functions the same way."

Colleague One: "Not watering works only because there is a standing expectation or dependency relation—the plant needs water. But here there is no such dependency built into the situation. She was not assigned to watch that swimmer. So you need a background norm to turn a mere absence into a causal omission, which means omission-causation is norm-relative, not metaphysically basic."

Colleague Two: "If you allow norms to do all the work, then causation becomes conventional. But counterfactual dependence is an objective relation: blow whistle leads to rescue; do not blow leads to death. That holds regardless of her contract. To deny it is causal, you would have to say that the bystander across the street—who also did not blow a whistle—caused the death too, since the same counterfactual applies to her. So you do need a restriction principle, but it should concern proximity or capacity, not social roles."

Colleague One: "Exactly—without a restriction principle, omission-causation explodes into absurdity. But 'proximity or capacity' merely smuggles norms back in: why does her capacity matter unless she is expected to use it? And once you admit that past investment or assigned role can generate reasons—like finishing a long project because one has already invested effort—there is no reason roles cannot generate causal relevance for omissions. So your objective counterfactual test fails to restrict causes without circular appeal to the very norms you wanted to avoid."

Colleague Two: "You are conflating two things: the reason we single out one omission as 'the' cause is pragmatic and explanatory, not metaphysical. Many omissions may be causally relevant; we highlight the lifeguard's because it is salient. That does not mean the bystander's omission is not also a cause—only that it is not the one we care about explaining. Thus norms govern explanation and blame, not causation itself."

Colleague One: "If norms govern only explanation and blame, then you have conceded that omission-causation possesses no independent metaphysical status. It is just counterfactual dependence applied everywhere, with norms painting the highlights. But then 'causation by omission' is not a distinctive kind of causation—it is ordinary counterfactual causation filtered by our interests. That dissolves the very category you set out to defend."
