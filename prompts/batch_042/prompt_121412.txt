I'm designing a writing exercise for an advanced philosophy of science workshop focused on diachronic rationality, and I want a sustained satire that makes participants feel the internal pressure of a coherence norm taken literally. Write a deadpan institutional policy memorandum—approximately 700 words, formatted with numbered bureaucratic sections and an appendix—issued by the fictional Office of Temporal Credence Alignment (OTCA) to all reasoning agents. The memo should enforce a strict interpretation of Van Fraassen's Reflection principle: every agent must currently assign to any proposition P exactly the credence they anticipate their own future, better-informed self will assign, with formal penalties for "diachronic divergence." The satire should exaggerate real philosophical tensions—how reflection conflicts with learning from old evidence, how conditionalization seems to undermine itself when an agent predicts she will change her mind, and the ambiguity over which future self counts (the one who receives testimony, the one who makes an emotional error, the one who corrects a logical mistake)—rather than inventing fake objections. To prevent the piece from becoming a generic parody of bureaucracy, inject this niche framing: OTCA also mandates a "Double-Reflection Dilemma" in which agents must align their present moral credences about hypothetical suffering with the credences of a counterfactual "shadow self" who never receives the new evidence, so that apologizing for a past wrong requires currently believing, at the strength of one's future regret, that one was guilty all along. The humor should arise from applying these norms deadpan to ordinary life—checking weather forecasts, trusting a partner, deciding whether to re-read an email—without ever winking at the reader. Include an appendix formatted as an internal case study describing an agent paralyzed because she knows she will later assign low credence to her partner's fidelity, and must therefore now assign that same low credence, yet also must act on it in the present. The piece should be readable without prior formal training—explain "credence" and "conditionalization" briefly in the bureaucratic voice—but precise enough that a philosopher recognizes the satire as fair to the actual debates on reflection, self-defeating updates, and the specification of the relevant future epistemic state. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not name living philosophers; avoid specific publication years or texts, referring only generally to "the Reflection principle"; and do not quote verbatim from any real source.
