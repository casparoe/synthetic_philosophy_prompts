I write literary fiction, and in drafting a novel about scientists and philosophers arguing over the nature of consciousness, I have become fixated on a philosophical impasse that seems genuinely stuck: the debate over whether Daniel Dennett’s distinction between cranes and skyhooks actually does explanatory work, or whether accusations of “greedy reductionism” in cognitive science are themselves confusions about the architecture of explanation.

The stalemate runs as follows. One camp—call them the mechanists—holds that consciousness, aesthetic judgment, and moral reasoning are continuous with evolutionary and computational processes, and that explaining their stepwise assembly eliminates any need for non-reductive scaffolding. The opposing camp, whom I have come to think of as the inheritance critics, accepts every empirical mechanism but insists that explaining how consciousness operates is not equivalent to accounting for its first-person presence; they charge that reducing subjective experience to predictive processing or global workspace dynamics is “greedy” because it treats an explanatory framework as an ontological disappearance, leaving behind what I have been calling a scaffold-inheritance problem—the observation that every successful crane-built explanation of a high-level phenomenon temporarily borrows the very conceptual vocabulary of the phenomenon it explains, then claims to have dissolved it.

Both sides reject Cartesian dualism. Both cite evolutionary continuity. Both agree that explanations should be stepwise rather than miraculous. Yet for decades the debate has not moved. Each new neuroscientific finding is absorbed by the mechanists as another crane, and dismissed by the critics as another instance of explanatory theft. The concrete cases that both sides claim support their view—blindsight, artificial consciousness, aesthetic judgment, moral reasoning—are interpreted in diametrically opposite directions without either side conceding a point.

My working hypothesis, formed not as a philosopher but as someone who constructs narratives, is that this is not primarily an empirical disagreement—no brain scan will settle it—nor a simple verbal dispute about the word “explanation.” Rather, I suspect the sides are answering different questions under a single heading: the mechanists ask whether consciousness has a crane-built genealogy, while the critics ask whether any such genealogy can inherit the normative and phenomenological weight that consciousness carries for us. But I am unsure whether this diagnosis captures the real structure, or whether I am merely redescribing the stalemate.

Please diagnose this disagreement concretely, using what each side actually says about the cases mentioned above. Is it substantive, verbal, or a case of divergent questions masked by shared vocabulary? Is there a hidden disagreement about standards of explanatory success—perhaps about whether an explanation must preserve the phenomenon’s first-person authority, or whether that preservation is itself an illicit skyhook? Are they disputing which cases count as data, or are they locked in bedrock intuitions about whether reduction can ever be “greedy” without being false? And tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a conceptual distinction that separates genealogy from inheritance, a formal result about the scalability of mechanistic explanation, a thought experiment that forces one side to concede, or an argument that the question is ill-posed and should be abandoned. I want an honest assessment of whether anything could move either camp, including whether my own “scaffold inheritance” framing points to a real crux or merely renames the deadlock.
