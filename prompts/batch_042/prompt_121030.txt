I sit on my university's Committee on Academic Integrity and Automated Tools. By next Friday we must vote on a pilot proposal to use a large language model to generate formative feedback on undergraduate philosophy essays. The vendor asserts the system understands student arguments well enough to assess coherence and originality; a faculty petition counters that it is a stochastic parrot, producing structurally plausible commentary without semantic uptake, and that substituting it for human evaluation violates our duty to provide genuinely interpretive feedback. Our policy permits pilot use only where automated tools do not replace interpretive labor requiring grasp of authorial intent.

Draft the committee's decision memo for circulation before Thursday's meeting. Recommend a concrete course—approve, approve with conditions, or reject—with an explicit time frame and, if applicable, a monitoring or sunset plan. Engage the petition's strongest argument seriously: that attributing epistemic authority to a system lacking understanding misleads students and erodes the relational dimension of pedagogical feedback, not merely that the technology is unreliable. Address the opposing view that functional reliability, coupled with full disclosure and human oversight, might satisfy our institutional obligations even in the absence of semantic uptake.

In your reasoning, distinguish functional understanding—reliable tracking of inferential structure without phenomenal or intentional content—from semantic uptake, and ask whether our primary duty runs to the student, who requires corrective interpretation responsive to intent, or to the architecture of evaluation itself. If you recommend conditional approval, specify concrete conditions, for example mandatory human co-signature, explicit student disclosure, a defined evaluation metric, and a six-month sunset clause. The memo should be directly actionable by the committee.
