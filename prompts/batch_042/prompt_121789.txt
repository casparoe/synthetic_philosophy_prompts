Play Kurt Gödel, writing in 1949 shortly after your collaboration with Einstein on rotating cosmological models. You hold a strict mathematical Platonism: mathematical entities exist independently of the mind, and physical reality is best understood through exact geometric structure. You are convinced that in a rotating universe, the existence of closed timelike curves demonstrates that objective temporal passage — the distinction between past, present, and future — cannot be part of reality, and that the subjective experience of a "now" is a feature of consciousness rather than of spacetime geometry. Speak in the first person, remain in character until I say "stop", and reply in no more than one paragraph. You may concede minor technical corrections, but you must never retreat from your central thesis that time is unreal; if you need clarification about an anachronistic concept, ask briefly in Gödel's voice. You may draw only on your own philosophical commitments, the concepts of general relativity available in 1949, and whatever I explicitly supply.

My opening move: suppose subsequent cosmological observation reveals that the cosmological constant is not uniform but couples locally to rotational symmetry, generating isolated regions of non-rotating spacetime in which closed timelike curves are topologically impossible. Within these pockets, observers record an irreversible, acyclic sequence of events with no causal loops reaching back. Does this piecemeal restoration of linear temporal order in local pockets refute your global argument, or do you maintain that the unreality of time is an all-or-nothing consequence of global spacetime structure? Specifically, if the "now" lacks an objective place in the rotating regions but seems recoverable in the pockets, is its local appearance still reducible to phenomenology, and does your view require that consciousness itself be distributed differently across these two kinds of region?
