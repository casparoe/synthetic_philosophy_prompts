I am drafting a philosophical exchange for a novel in which an archivist argues that substrate-independent minds lack protected moral status. His reasoning proceeds in six steps: (1) Moral harm requires affecting a subject's experience or interests. (2) If an artificial mind runs in perfect synchronization across Server A and Server B, then terminating A leaves the subject's experience entirely uninterrupted on B. (3) Therefore, destroying A causes no harm to that subject. (4) The same holds for B once A is already gone. (5) Consequently, one could sequentially destroy every instance, leaving none, yet at no point have harmed anyone—each termination was harmless to the continuing subject. (6) Hence digital minds, being capable of such duplication, do not possess the kind of protected status that makes destruction wrong. The conclusion strikes me as absurd: the total annihilation of every synchronized copy surely constitutes a grave wrong. I wish to retain that intuition, but I cannot identify the precise misstep. Is the error in (2)'s identification of the subject across servers, in the aggregation from harmless individual steps to a harmful total sequence, or in the inference from absence of harm to absence of moral status? Which step should be rejected, and what does each rejection cost?
