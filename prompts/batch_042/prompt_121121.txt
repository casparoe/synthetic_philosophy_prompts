You are a professional moral philosopher and policy ethicist with deep expertise in autonomy theory, behavioral economics, and the philosophy of action. Before you answer, please take a moment to brainstorm the key distinctions between choice-preserving influence and autonomy-respecting justification, and think carefully about how each premise below relies on a specific conception of autonomy—procedural versus substantive, and first-order versus second-order.

Here is an argument I built for a seminar on libertarian paternalism and now find suspicious because its conclusion is far more expansive than I intended.

(1) A policy is paternalistic if and only if it substitutes the policymaker’s judgment for the agent’s own judgment about what is best for that agent.

(2) A classic nudge—say, an opt-out default for organ donation—does not substitute judgment; it preserves every option and merely rearranges the architecture in which judgment is exercised.

(3) Therefore, no classic nudge is paternalistic.

(4) Yet policymakers routinely justify nudges by appealing to a divergence between revealed preference (shaped by cognitive bias) and reflective preference (what the agent would endorse after deliberation), and they design the architecture precisely to bridge that gap.

(5) If a justification appeals to such a divergence, then it treats the agent’s present judgment as deficient and substitutes a corrected judgment—exactly the structure of paternalism.

(6) So either nudges are not justified by this appeal (contrary to standard practice), or they are paternalistic despite (2).

(7) Let us accept (6) and reject (2): nudges can be paternalistic even without removing options, provided they exploit systematic biases that the agent would disavow upon reflection.

(8) Now consider “boosts”—interventions that improve statistical literacy or decision-making competence rather than steering choice within a fixed architecture.

(9) Boosts also preserve all options and do not substitute judgment; they merely enhance the agent’s capacity for judgment.

(10) By the reasoning in (7), if an agent’s current capacity is deficient—say, due to pervasive cognitive bias—then leaving that deficiency intact allows the agent to exercise a deficient judgment, which is structurally analogous to allowing a biased choice architecture to stand.

(11) Therefore, failing to provide an available and effective boost is itself a failure to prevent deficient judgment from prevailing, which, on the same logic that rendered nudges paternalistic, amounts to neglecting the agent’s true (reflective) autonomy.

(12) But if the state has a duty to correct autonomy-deficits whenever they are present and correctable, then liberal governments are obligated to mandate universal cognitive enhancement, compulsory statistical education, or even pharmacological boosting of executive function.

(13) That conclusion is unacceptable: liberal neutrality does not require the state to optimize every citizen’s rational capacities.

I want to preserve (7)—that nudges can be paternalistic without removing options—because it captures the intuition that exploiting bias is a form of judgment-substitution. I also want to preserve (13)—that mandatory universal enhancement is not required. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, does rejecting (10) require giving up the idea that autonomy has a substantive (not merely procedural) dimension? Or is the flaw elsewhere—perhaps a hidden equivocation between “deficient judgment” in the nudging case and “deficient capacity” in the boosting case, or a slide from permissibility to obligation? Please diagnose the argument with precision: name the step, explain why it initially looked plausible, and price the alternatives, especially regarding whether we must choose between a purely procedural and a robustly substantive theory of autonomy.
