I am designing the final examination for an advanced undergraduate seminar titled Democracy as Inquiry: Dewey and After, and I need one essay question that cannot be answered by summarizing Dewey's well-known texts but instead requires students to work through the internal logic of his experimentalism about democracy and social inquiry. The question should present a concrete and somewhat unfamiliar scenario: a municipal democratic body must adjudicate water allocation among three stakeholder groups—industrial engineers who treat scarcity as an optimization problem for predictive modeling, ecological scientists who frame it in terms of carrying capacity and systems dynamics, and a local community that understands the watershed as a relational entity embedded in cultural practices that resist quantification and instrumental framing. Students should be asked to analyze how Dewey's conception of democracy as cooperative social inquiry applies to this case, specifically addressing whether his experimental method requires a shared metric of evaluation that would implicitly privilege the engineers and scientists, or whether his claim that democratic ends are reconstructed through shared experience can genuinely accommodate the community's non-quantifiable framework without reducing it to a mere preference to be weighed against technical data. They should also be required to evaluate whether Dewey's distinction between democratic ends and democratic means holds up under these conditions, or whether the case demonstrates that the distinction collapses when the very framing of the problem—what counts as water, as scarcity, and as a solution—is itself contested. Please provide the exam question itself, followed by a model answer of approximately 650 to 750 words written entirely in continuous prose with no headings, no bullet points, no boldface, no tables, and no quotation marks enclosing passages from real texts, and then provide a marking scheme also rendered entirely in continuous prose that specifies the conceptual moves an excellent answer must make, distinguishes an A-level response from a B-level response, assigns conceptual weight to particular achievements, and identifies the common errors—such as treating Dewey as a simple majoritarian, confusing his experimental logic with technocratic scientific management, or assuming that democratic ends exist prior to the inquiry process rather than being formed through it. The entire response should read like a formal letter or email, should avoid asking students for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and should not depend on knowledge of obscure secondary literature.
