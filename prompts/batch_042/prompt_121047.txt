I've been working through a seminar on collective moral responsibility, and I've become fixated on the case judgments of a particular political philosopher—call her the Reviewer—whose rulings I have gathered from essays she published over the last fifteen years. Her track record is specific: she holds that a corporation whose governing board unanimously authorizes harm is collectively guilty even when ordinary employees are ignorant; that a democratic municipality voting to enforce a harmful segregation policy bears collective guilt rather than mere political liability; that descendants of perpetrators who inherit stolen resources, never acknowledge the wrong, and actively obstruct restitution share in the original guilt; that a nation-state whose present citizens played no part in a past genocide, have formally apologized, and have dismantled the oppressive institutions bears no collective moral guilt though it retains duties of repair; that a university which knowingly preserves honors for a documented-atrocity founder, with current administrators fully aware, is guilty for actively perpetuating that legacy; and that a family inheriting slave-labor wealth, expressing genuine remorse and donating equivalent reparative value, is not guilty despite retaining obligations of justice. A friend insists all six flow from one principle, yet I see at least two coherent rules accommodating every verdict.

The new case involves a post-colonial nation where the original expropriation occurred three generations ago, the formal legal structures enabling it have been abolished and replaced democratically, individual citizens often express private regret, and the present government never endorsed the wrong—yet the economic advantages remain structurally embedded in land and wealth distribution, and the government consistently refuses to recognize any special obligation to the descendants, treating the matter as purely historical. Predict what the Reviewer would say about whether this nation is collectively guilty, identify the competing rules—perhaps one centered on collective authorization and active institutional perpetuation, another on structural continuity coupled with present refusal of acknowledgment and repair—say which prediction you find best supported and why, and propose one further hypothetical case that would cleanly separate those rules.
