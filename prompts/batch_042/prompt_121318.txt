A philosopher of science I collaborate with has expressed the following judgments about belief revision under uncertain evidence.

When she learns with certainty that a well-calibrated instrument recorded a value, she conditions her belief about the measured parameter directly on that observation.

When she learns that a single, trusted predictive model assigns exactly seventy percent to a parameter falling in a specific range, she updates her own credence in that range to seventy percent, holding fixed all of her prior conditional beliefs about other parameters given that range—applying what she takes to be the averaging rule over the induced partition.

When she receives a perceptual impression she describes as probably a raven, perhaps a crow, she revises her beliefs about associated features—plumage color, beak curvature—by taking the weighted average of her prior conditionals on ravenhood and crowhood, using her revised sixty-forty split as weights. She insists, however, that her beliefs about the taxonomy that distinguishes the two species remain untouched.

When she is presented with two independent models that assign eighty percent and thirty percent, respectively, to the same hypothesis, she refuses to apply any direct averaging rule over the hypothesis and its negation. Instead, she treats the conflict as meta-evidence, revising her beliefs about each model's calibration and reliability, and then derives her belief in the hypothesis from those revised reliabilities.

Consider now a new case. She encounters a single study reporting not a point estimate but a ninety percent credible interval for a parameter, where the interval is wide and straddles a theoretically critical threshold—values below the threshold support one explanatory mechanism, values above support a competing one. The study's methods are transparent and its data are public, but the interval spans both regimes.

Predict her verdict: will she apply the averaging rule over a partition induced by the credible interval, treating the interval report as direct uncertain evidence about the parameter? Will she instead treat the report as meta-evidence about the study's precision or the authors' modeling choices, updating her beliefs about study quality first? Or will she adopt a third course—perhaps conditioning on the derived proposition that the parameter lies within the interval, then distributing her belief within that range according to some prior?

State which implicit rule best explains her four prior judgments, identify at least one alternative rule that accommodates those judgments equally well, and specify a further hypothetical case—ideally one involving uncertain evidence about a logical or structural relation rather than an empirical parameter—that would discriminate between your proposed rule and its competitor.
