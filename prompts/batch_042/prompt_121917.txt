A friend who just read some Hofstadter summarized his view on distributed self like this: Hofstadter thinks the 'I' is nothing but a strange loop, which is basically a self-referential pattern in neural wiring. Because it's pure information, the loop can be copied onto other substrates or even split across linked brains. So if you had two people with brain-computer interfaces that shared enough feedback, they wouldn't just be communicating — they'd literally be one distributed 'I' feeling both bodies. He also says this is why split-brain patients already have two selves, we just don't notice because the corpus callosum usually hides the separation. And he concludes that death isn't a big deal for this view, since destroying one brain just deletes one instace of the loop; if the pattern lives on elsewhere, the self continues. Something about this feels like it's sliding from pattern-talk into pure funtionalism, and I'm not sure Hofstadter actually buys the BCI scenario. Is this summary fair? Which parts capture his actual arguments about strange loops and substrate independence, which parts are overstatements, and does his view on split-brain patients really commit him to the idea that two linked people could merge into one consciousness?
