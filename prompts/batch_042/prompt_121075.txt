I recently came across a family archive in which my great-uncle’s death during the March 1943 liquidation of the Kraków Ghetto was classified, in one national record, as a casualty of “genocidal violence” and, in another, as a victim of “war-related population displacement”—the same life, two incompatible moral-legal designations that determine whether descendants may seek reparative recognition. The discrepancy has made the politics of naming atrocities feel urgently personal rather than merely taxonomic, and I am trying to understand whether the gap between Raphael Lemkin’s original, expansive conception of genocide—which included the deliberate destruction of a group’s cultural, linguistic, and social institutions—and the narrower, intent-focused definition adopted by the 1948 UN Convention is not just a legal technicality but a form of epistemic violence that reshapes collective memory itself.

With that in mind, I would like a philosophical reading of Roman Polanski’s The Pianist (2002) that takes this categorical politics seriously without reducing the film to an illustration. The work depicts the annihilation of Warsaw’s Jewish community through mass murder, but also through the liquidation of the ghetto, the erasure of personal names, and the reduction of individuals to anonymous numbers—processes that sit uneasily between physical destruction and cultural erasure. Does Polanski’s framing implicitly invoke Lemkin’s broader vision, particularly in scenes that show the destruction of communal life and the archives of identity, or does it confine the horror to the narrower UN category of intentional physical destruction, perhaps by centering individual survival rather than collective annihilation? I want an analysis that asks where the debate about categorical naming illuminates a specific cinematic choice—say, the representation of the ghetto’s liquidation or the stripping of names—and where the film resists being read through either definition alone. Most importantly, what does The Pianist add to this philosophical debate that legal argument by itself cannot capture: does its aesthetic of witnessing force us to reconsider whether cultural destruction should be treated as constitutive of genocide, even when specific intent to destroy a group physically is the legally decisive criterion?
