After a long, somewhat heated discussion with a friend about what we actually owe to animals—both farmed and wild—I tried to formalize my own view to check whether I am reasoning consistently or just rationalizing the diet I already have. I want an audit of the package below, not a general lecture on animal ethics.

Here is where I have landed:

(1) I am a suffering-focused anti-speciesist: the only intrinsic moral bad is negative phenomenal experience; species membership carries no direct weight, and painless death is not itself a harm.

(2) I think wild-animal suffering is a problem of enormous scale, but I reject direct, large-scale ecological intervention—such as predator culling or engineered trophic restructuring—on strong epistemic grounds: we understand ecosystem dynamics poorly enough that such interventions risk catastrophic backfire.

(3) When evaluating food choices, I think we should count suffering caused to all affected sentient beings, including wild animals killed or displaced by production, not only the farmed animals themselves.

(4) For that reason, I think conventional beef is generally worse than conventional chicken: beef requires far more land per calorie, which means more habitat fragmentation, more wild animals killed by machinery and pesticides, and greater displacement of wild populations.

(5) At the same time, I think there is a serious, under-discussed possibility that beef raised entirely on non-arable marginal pasture—without feed crops—might actually cause less total wild suffering per calorie than a plant-based diet reliant on grains, legumes, and oils, since row-crop agriculture kills substantial numbers of small sentient field animals and occupies land that could otherwise support wild life.

(6) I accept that if a farmed animal lives a net-positive life and is replaced by another with an equally good life, its painless death is not an additional harm. But I deny that this replacement logic extends to wild populations, because wild births and deaths are not part of a managed system designed to preserve net welfare.

(7) My practical obligations are primarily negative: I should avoid choices that cause suffering, but I do not have a positive duty to restructure ecosystems to eliminate wild suffering, particularly when restructuring carries high risk of greater harm.

Do these hang together? Specifically:

- Which pairs are in genuine tension, not merely surface friction? For each real tension, identify the cheapest revision and what it costs me—whether that means giving up (4), weakening (6), or revising my anti-speciesism in (1).
- Which apparent tensions dissolve once the motivations behind the commitments are made explicit?
- Order the tensions by severity.
- If I must make exactly one revision to maximize coherence, which commitment should I alter, and does the remaining package hold together?
