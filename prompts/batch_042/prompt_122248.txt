I am working through section 341 of Nietzsche’s The Gay Science for an independent study on the ethics of affirmation. In that passage, a demon appears and announces that one’s life—down to the smallest spider and beam of moonlight—will recur eternally, and asks whether one would curse him or fall upon one’s knees and bless him for this knowledge. I grasp the standard interpretive line: that eternal recurrence operates as a selective thought-experiment testing whether a person can affirm existence without metaphysical consolation or redemptive teleology. But my instructor has pressed a more specific framing that she calls “evaluative recurrence without cosmological commitment,” paired with what she terms a “horizon of narrative closure without teleology.” On her reading, Nietzsche is not advancing a physical theory of cyclical time, but constructing a normative standard by which a life can be judged complete and worthy of repetition precisely because it lacks an external endpoint or redemptive arc—what she describes as closure achieved through internal coherence rather than through arrival at a final purpose.

I am struggling to see how that framing interacts with the textual details. The demon’s language sounds ontologically weighty—he says this life, “as you live it and have lived it,” will have to return—yet the evaluative reading seems to drain the announcement of cosmological content. Could you help me understand whether the passage genuinely supports a strictly non-ontological interpretation, or whether the demon’s claim forces us to treat recurrence as at least a hypothetical cosmology? And if the latter is required, how does that hypothetical status relate to the idea of narrative closure without teleology? I am particularly interested in whether there is a coherent middle position—perhaps one in which the cosmological claim functions as a regulative fiction that makes the evaluative test psychologically possible—without collapsing either into literal physics or into mere metaphor. What in the imagery or rhetorical structure of the passage supports or undermines such a middle reading?
