I've been out of the lab for about six years now—used to do protein folding, of all things—and lately I've fallen into this philosophy of labor stuff, probably because my granddaughter just started her first real job and we argued about it over thanksgiving. There's this discussion circle at the community center where we've been judging cases about leisure rights and work ethics, bouncing around Weber's Protestant work ethic, that Japanese karoshi phenomenon, the French 35-hour week, and so on, but trying to apply them to actual proposals instead of just history.

So far our rulings: we said factories should be forced to cap shifts at 35 hours if fatigue data shows accident risk spiking, but we rejected the claim that leisure is a social entitlement like healthcare. We said karoshi should count as an occupational safety failure, not personal moral weakness, but we also rejected compensation for "lost leisure years" in a wrongful-death suit—said the harm was the death itself, not missing vacation. We turned down the argument that unemployment benefits should be cut to preserve work discipline, that's just moralizing. We approved letting workers refuse weekend texts from bosses without retaliation—we called it temporal autonomy—but we shot down a universal "rest voucher" proposal for unemployed people funded by overtime taxes. And we agreed that if you quit with accrued vacation, the employer owes you that pay, but only because it's deferred wages, not because the state guarantees relaxation.

Now here's the case we're definately stuck on: a city proposal that gig platforms must pay into a disconnect-fund that gives every registered driver—whether they actually drove that week or not—48 hours of paid "offline time" per month, funded by a surcharge on deliveries made after 10pm. What's our call if we want to stay consistent? Which past ruling is the closest analog, and are we running one coherent rule or just two that happen to overlap? If our precedents genuinely underdetermine it, what principle are we smuggling in with each choice? And what further case—maybe involving unpaid family care work or academic post-docs—would actually split the rules apart?
