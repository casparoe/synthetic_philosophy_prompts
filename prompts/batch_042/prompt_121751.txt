Consider an adversarial collaboration between two camps on paternalism and the ethics of nudging. Camp One comprises defenders of libertarian paternalism who maintain that well-designed choice architecture—such as default enrollment in retirement savings or opt-out organ donation—preserves freedom of choice while correcting predictable cognitive biases, and who hold that the state may legitimately employ such architecture provided it is transparent and easily reversible. Camp Two comprises critics who argue that any intentional shaping of choice environments to steer behavior without explicit rational persuasion violates a robust condition of autonomy—what they term the reflective endorsement condition—and who distinguish sharply between education, which engages deliberative capacities, and nudging, which operates beneath them. The collaboration should address not only familiar cases but also a distinction both camps acknowledge needs clarification: that between surface nudges, where the decision-maker is aware of being in a choice situation but is influenced by framing or ordering, and deep architecture nudges, where institutional design removes the phenomenology of choice altogether (for instance, automatic withholding structures that present savings not as an option but as a pre-constituted budgetary reality).

Draft their joint statement in the following form. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: about the empirical reality of cognitive bias in decision-making; about the normative importance of autonomy; about the formal properties that distinguish coercion from influence; about the existence and structural features of deep architecture nudges; and about the shared observation that standard defenses of nudging focus almost exclusively on surface cases. Second, a disagreements table with columns for Camp One, Camp Two, and the precise crux. The disagreements should include: whether deep architecture nudges preserve freedom of choice in any meaningful sense; whether transparency can be achieved for deep architecture given that awareness of being in a choice situation is precisely what is absent; whether the moral evaluation of nudging depends on the decision-maker's subjective experience of choice or only on the objective availability of alternatives; and whether there exists a principled line between legitimate rational persuasion and illegitimate sub-rational influence, or whether this distinction collapses upon inspection. Third, identify at least two cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one camp. For each crux, specify a concrete case, institutional design, or empirical finding that both sides agree would bear on it, and record each camp's predicted outcome in advance. One crux should concern whether individuals who are later informed that they were subject to deep architecture nudges report a distinctive form of retrospective alienation that differs from mere regret; the other should concern whether societies with extensive deep architecture in welfare provision exhibit measurably different patterns of civic engagement or political efficacy compared with societies relying on explicit opt-in structures.

The statement should be phrased so that each camp accepts the wording of its own position, should avoid bibliographic references or citation requests, and should not ask for reading lists. Write in a formal, polished register appropriate for a philosophy seminar, from the perspective of a professor setting the exercise.
