I'm currently drafting a dissertation chapter on the metaphysics of biological function, and a disagreement with my advisor has left me unsure how to adjudicate between the two dominant frameworks when they are pushed beyond textbook cases. Could you compare the etiological account—according to which a trait's function is the effect for which it was selected in evolutionary history, as articulated in the tradition of Wright, Millikan, and Neander—with the causal-role account, which identifies function with the contribution a trait makes to a containing system's capacity, in the line of Cummins and Craver? I grasp the standard distinction, but I want the comparison focused on how each framework handles the normativity of malfunction and the problem of over-generation in two specific edge cases that seem to pull them in opposite directions.

First, consider a synthetic gene drive engineered into a mosquito population to suppress malaria transmission. The drive possesses no evolutionary history within that species, yet it clearly performs a designed task within both the organism and the broader population dynamics. Does the etiological account have to deny that the drive has any biological function whatsoever, or can it accommodate a design-derived selection history—perhaps at the level of human intentional selection—without collapsing biological function into mere intended purpose? Conversely, does the causal-role account risk over-generating functions by assigning functional status to every molecular interaction that causally contributes to the drive's propagation, thereby eroding the normative distinction between functioning properly and malfunctioning?

Second, take the case of a horizontally transferred antibiotic-resistance plasmid within a gut microbiome that was never part of the host organism's evolutionary lineage but now causally sustains the microbial community's stability under pharmaceutical stress. Here there is genuine selection pressure, but not the "right" kind of evolutionary history relative to the host genome. How does each account determine whether resistance counts as a function of the plasmid, and what does this reveal about whether etiological theories must privilege organism-level selection over gene-level or community-level selection? In particular, I want you to examine whether either account can genuinely distinguish "function" from "accidental benefit" without implicitly borrowing conceptual machinery from the other.

Please do not merely summarize the literature; I need an argument about which framework handles these engineered and horizontally-transferred cases more coherently, what each has to pay conceptually if it tries to absorb the other, and whether the normativity of malfunction ultimately derives from a failure relative to a selected effect or from a deviation relative to an analytically specified system capacity. I'm especially interested in whether the etiological account's reliance on historical selection can be salvaged by pluralizing the levels at which selection operates, or whether that move undermines the very specificity that makes the account attractive in the first place.
