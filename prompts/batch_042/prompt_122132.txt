You are a senior political philosopher with particular expertise in constitutional theory, rational-choice institutional design, and the philosophy of intertemporal agency. You are advising me, the host of a podcast series on conceptual problems in political philosophy. Before you begin drafting, please take a moment to think carefully about where refined Ulysses-contract accounts are most vulnerable in the literature, and start with brief brainstorming on the three standard pressure points: the strategic-versus-normative ambiguity, democratic equality across generations, and the risk of coercion disguised as self-rule.

I am preparing for a colloquium-style interview with an unnamed but well-defined guest: a constitutional theorist who defends a refined version of the Ulysses-contract view applied to constitutional precommitment. She accepts that constitutional precommitment — for example, entrenchment clauses, supermajoritarian amendment rules, or sunset provisions in emergency powers — functions analogously to Odysseus binding himself to the mast. However, she insists that not every strategic self-binding counts as a legitimate Ulysses contract. She introduces a diachronic legitimacy condition: the precommitment is normatively valid only if there is recognisable continuity of normative authority between the agent who binds and the agent who is bound. Where preference transformation is so radical that the bound agent is effectively a different normative subject — or where an external power imposes the binding — the contract collapses into mere coercion or strategic manipulation. She applies this to defend sunset clauses in emergency constitutions, arguing that expiry does not undermine legitimacy but rather confirms that the binding was always conditional on the persistence of the original normative agent.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that press well beyond having her restate this view. The sequence should build: early questions establish the architecture of her account; middle questions press the sceptical pressure points; and the final question should ask what institutional evidence or failure would cause her to abandon the diachronic legitimacy condition altogether. Present the questions in numbered order. For each question, include a brief follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give.

In particular, ensure you cover the following angles, though not necessarily in this order:

1. The distinction between purely strategic self-binding (the familiar rational-choice story associated with Jon Elster) and genuinely normative precommitment. The follow-up should anticipate her likely reply that all apparent normative binding reduces to strategic preference-satisfaction across time.

2. The diachronic legitimacy condition itself: what precisely counts as recognisable continuity of normative authority, and how she applies it to a constituent assembly that binds successors after demographic and cultural replacement. The follow-up should press the boundary case in which almost nothing recognisable remains except formal legal continuity.

3. Constitutional sunset clauses for emergency powers: are these best understood as Ulysses contracts that expire when the crisis passes, or merely as temporary delegations that never involved genuine self-binding? The follow-up should challenge her on whether the expiry of the clause restores the pre-commitment's legitimacy or instead reveals that the binding was never more than a convenient fiction.

4. The tension with democratic sovereignty and equality across generations: if today's people can bind tomorrow's, does this not violate the principle that each generation should be sovereign? The follow-up should anticipate her likely trusteeship or intergenerational-covenant reply and press whether trusteeship avoids the problem or merely renames it.

5. Comparative institutional design: why prefer rigid constitutional entrenchment over ordinary legislative revision protected only by a high supermajority? The follow-up should ask whether the distinction collapses once we recognise that both mechanisms depend on the same diachronic continuity condition.

6. The risk of coercion disguised as self-rule: can a majority within a polity legitimately impose a Ulysses contract on a dissenting minority, claiming that the minority is part of the same continuous normative agent? The follow-up should probe whether her condition has any critical bite here, or whether it always allows the majority to define continuity in its own favour.

7. The scope of the view: does it apply only to formal constitutional structures, or also to informal social norms and private contracts? The follow-up should ask what happens when an informal norm — for example, a community's shared rule about land use — is maintained not by law but by repeated practice, and the practitioners are gradually replaced.

Please ensure the questions are concrete, avoid generic biographical prompts, and do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not include verbatim quotations from real texts; if you reference a specific argument, paraphrase it without quotation marks. Write in polished British English.
