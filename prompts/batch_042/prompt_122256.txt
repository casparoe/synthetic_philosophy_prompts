I run a philosophy of history seminar for 14 graduate students, one 90-minute session. Design an in-class simulation—complete with rules, assigned roles, an aggregation mechanism, a non-monetary payoff scheme, and a structured debrief—that makes participants feel the force of the tension between counterfactual contingency and historical inevitability, specifically through the lens of what I will call counterfactual fragility thresholds: the idea that macro-historical outcomes vary in how much local perturbation they can absorb before their trajectory shifts, and that intuitive judgment systematically overweights dramatic single-node changes while underweighting cumulative micro-perturbations that actually cross the threshold. Do not name or lecture on this concept before the activity begins; instead, the rules and incentives should lead participants to enact and discover it themselves.

Use a synthetic but historically grounded scenario—an interdependent democratic assembly facing three linked votes on military intervention, judicial punishment, and resource allocation—rather than a pure replay of a well-known case. Divide the group into perturbation teams (each assigned one decision node they may alter within defined constraints), forecasting teams (who must predict, before seeing the aggregated results, whether the final institutional state will converge with the baseline or diverge), and record-keepers (who track the path of perturbations). The procedure should specify how perturbations are proposed, ratified, and chained, how forecasters stake reputation points on convergence versus divergence, and how the facilitator resolves the macro-state at the end of each round.

The debrief should map specific moments in the simulation onto the components of fragility thresholds—single-node drama versus cumulative drift, visible versus invisible perturbation, and the illusion of decisive agency—while being explicit about which feature of real historical dynamics the simulation necessarily cannot reproduce (for example, long-run institutional feedback loops, information asymmetry over extended time, or the irreversibility of enacted violence) and therefore must be supplied by you as facilitator. Also predict what will go wrong the first time you run this activity—likely misalignments between the payoff scheme and the conceptual point, or participants treating the synthetic scenario as purely strategic rather than historically interpretive—and say exactly how to correct for it.

Finally, state clearly what would change your mind about whether this activity genuinely captures the philosophical problem rather than merely gamifying it: identify which specific failure in the simulation dynamics—such as the absence of path-dependent lock-in, or the inability to represent structural constraints that resist perturbation altogether—would undermine the debrief’s mapping and convince you that the exercise misrepresents the nature of historical inevitability. Write in a formal, polished style.
