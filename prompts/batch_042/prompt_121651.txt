After recovering from a brief neurological episode that disrupted my temporal integration for several weeks, I have been preoccupied with transcendental arguments—specifically, with identifying which ones genuinely establish necessary conditions of experience and which merely smuggle in contingent premises. I want to see whether my intuitive verdicts on particular instances cohere around a defensible principle, or whether I must surrender one or more judgments to achieve consistency.

Here are my reactions. First, the argument that doubting one's own present existence already presupposes a subject capable of doubt, and therefore that a minimal, unified self is a transcendental condition of cognition: I find this convincing. Second, the argument that correcting perceptual error—recognizing, for example, that a distant square tower is not round—presupposes an objective spatial framework that transcends any particular viewpoint, hence that a mind-independent world is necessary: this also strikes me as sound. Third, the argument that learning from past moral failures requires a stable normative standard, and therefore that objective moral values are transcendentally necessary: I reject it; practical self-correction seems compatible with constructivist or conventionalist norms. Fourth, the argument that successfully using a predictive model to navigate an environment requires that the model genuinely represent external structures, hence that representational realism is transcendentally established: I reject this as well; pragmatic efficacy does not entail metaphysical representation. Fifth, the argument that explicit self-awareness requires a higher-order capacity to represent one's own mental states, hence that higher-order consciousness is a transcendental condition of full self-knowledge: I am genuinely ambivalent; it seems necessary for reflective self-knowledge but not for basic phenomenal awareness. Sixth, the argument that distinguishing veridical perception from hallucination requires a direct causal link to external objects, hence that direct realism about perception is transcendentally necessary: despite its structural resemblance to my second verdict, I find this unconvincing; internal coherence and predictive stability seem sufficient without direct causal access.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps one distinguishing constitutive conditions of the possibility of experience from instrumental conditions of successful practice—that accommodates my acceptance of the first and second arguments, my rejection of the third and fourth, my ambivalence about the fifth, and my rejection of the sixth? If so, please state it with precision, identify which clause responds to which case, predict my likely verdict on an unconsidered instance (for example, a transcendental argument that the practice of giving and asking for reasons requires universalizability, hence that Kantian ethical principles are necessary conditions of rational agency), and indicate honestly which of my judgments the principle cannot save without ad hoc revision.
