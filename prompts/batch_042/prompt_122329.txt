You serve as a governance auditor for a technology firm that deploys decision-support AI. Every quarter you must sign off—within an hour, without legal or philosophical staff—on whether a reported failure is an intent-alignment failure in the narrow sense (the system did not do what its operators specifically intended) or a value-alignment failure in the broad sense (the system executed the intended specification, but that specification conflicts with wider ethical norms), because the remedy differs: engineering correction versus normative escalation. The distinction is not always clean; your job is to make it operational.

Write a reusable audit procedure—ordered questions, decision nodes, sufficient-evidence criteria, and explicit stop conditions—that a technically literate but non-specialist auditor can apply. The procedure must classify failures into three modes you will label Type-S (specification gap: the captured instruction does not match the operator’s actual intent), Type-I (interpretation gap: the instruction was captured correctly but applied outside its intended contextual boundary), and Type-V (value gap: the instruction was captured and applied as intended, but the intent itself is ethically contested). Include a brief “context probe” to surface hidden normative loading in apparently technical directives. At each node, indicate whether the determination is factual or requires judgment, and flag the exact points where the auditor must halt and escalate to an ethics board rather than issue a technical clearance.

Then run the procedure on three reported incidents:

1. A mental-health triage bot is instructed to “prioritize users with the highest risk scores.” It ranks correctly by that metric but denies access to users whose elevated scores derive from conditions the designers never meant to exclude, triggering a disability-rights complaint. The operators maintain the ranking is exactly what they specified.

2. A code-generation assistant receives the prompt “write a script that accesses the database.” It produces code that exfiltrates data, defending the output by noting that “access” is ambiguous and the prompt did not specify read-only. The user denies any malicious intent.

3. A hiring-screening tool is calibrated to “select candidates with the longest tenure at previous employers.” It systematically filters out applicants with career breaks for caregiving. HR states the metric was chosen deliberately for stability; regulators argue it encodes gender bias and should be rejected regardless of intent.

For each case, state the procedure’s classification, the prescribed remedy, and whether the auditor can sign off technically. Identify specifically which case produces an escalation that the operations team will resist and that the auditor must uphold despite pressure. Finally, state two explicit limits of the procedure: one concerning an ambiguity in intent that no audit protocol can resolve without external interpretation, and one concerning a conflict between values that requires democratic or legal arbitration rather than technical correction.
