So I’ve been volunteering at this local archive tagging protest ephemera—flyers, zines, old meeting minutes—and we keep fighting over keywords. One person tags something 'direct action,' another says 'civil disobedience,' and there's no rulebook that settles it. That mess got me back into Wittgenstein, specifically language games and family resemblance, and I’ve definately come around to the view that philosophical concepts don't have hidden essences; they just have overlapping uses across different practices, and asking for 'the real meaning' of justice or art is like asking for the essence of 'game.' I think this applies even to scientific classification—like psychiatric diagnosis being more like family resemblance clusters than natural kinds—so I'm treating this as a pretty general framework.

But I want to stress-test it. What would actually count against this position? I don't mean the standard 'some concepts do have necessary conditions' objection—that feels like playing a different language game already. I mean: are there cases where family resemblance breaks down because the overlaps are too thin to hold the concept together? Could there be genuinely incommensurable language games where translation fails not just practically but conceptually, and if so, does that undermine Wittgenstein's picture or just illustrate it? I'm also worried the view is insulated—any counterexample gets re-described as 'just another game'—so I want an honest take on whether it's been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that's actually a problem for a view like this. Rank the defeaters for me: what's fatal, what's merely awkward, and which ones defenders of Wittgenstein would concede hurt them. And specifically, does applying family resemblance to archival tagging and DSM-style diagnosis expose weaknesses that the classic 'game' example hides?
