Read Akira Kurosawa’s Rashomon (1950) through the lens of the debate over objectivity in history, taking seriously Leopold von Ranke’s ideal of recovering the past as it actually was, E. H. Carr’s scepticism about bare facts and his emphasis on the historian’s interpretive selection, and Hayden White’s claim that historical writing is structured by deep narrative tropes—romance, tragedy, comedy, and irony—rather than by archival fidelity alone. The film presents four contradictory accounts of a bandit’s encounter with a samurai and his wife in a forest: the bandit Tajomaru, the wife Masako, the dead samurai speaking through a medium, and the woodcutter who claims to have witnessed the events from hiding. Treat the film as cinema, not merely as allegory: attend to the framing of testimony inside the ruined Rashomon gate, to the contrast between the sunlit forest and the rain-soaked gate, and to the direct address of witnesses to an unseen listener.

Your task is to produce a critical reading that asks whether the film dramatizes Carr’s claim that facts never speak for themselves but only acquire meaning through the framing consciousness of the historian—or, here, the witness. Do the four testimonies illuminate, or strain under, White’s four master tropes? Where does the film seem to preserve a Rankean residue—perhaps in the woodcutter’s final, more modest version, or in the camera’s apparent visual neutrality—only to undermine it? Be precise about where the philosophical lens illuminates a scene or a character’s choice, and be honest about where it distorts the work: for instance, does the film’s primary concern lie in moral psychology and self-deception rather than in epistemology, and if so, where must the critic concede that?

End by saying what Rashomon adds to the philosophical debate about historical objectivity that a purely conceptual argument cannot supply—particularly regarding the affective and ethical stakes of competing narratives, and whether the refusal to privilege any single account is itself a historical judgment with its own unacknowledged tropes. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; work only from the film and from the philosophical positions named.
