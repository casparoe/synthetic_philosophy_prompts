I'm trying to map the conceptual upstream-downstream structure of a problem that sits between philosophy of history, philosophy of science, and macro-causal reasoning. The specific area is the treatment of non-human forces—pathogen dynamics, climate shifts, and volcanic aerosol forcing—as genuine historical actors rather than mere background conditions, without collapsing into the kind of environmental determinism that erases intentional agency. The familiar reference points are the thesis that infectious disease reshaped imperial boundaries and the argument that continental ecology explains long-run political divergence, but I'm not asking for a literature survey; I'm asking for an agenda.

Suppose you are advising a small interdisciplinary group—five researchers with backgrounds in philosophy of social science, historical methodology, and causal modeling—who have three years and no empirical laboratory obligations. They want a philosophically defensible framework that allows germs, climate, and volcanic events to carry explanatory weight in macro-historical narratives, while avoiding two failures: anthropomorphizing these forces by importing psychological predicates, and allowing them to "causally exclude" human decisions in the way mental-causation debates worry about in philosophy of mind. In other words, they need a non-competitive pluralism of historical causation.

Within that framing, what are the main open conceptual questions, and which should they tackle in what order? I'm especially interested in three candidate lines, though I want your own ranking and reasoning, not just an evaluation of these.

First, whether manipulationist accounts of causation can apply to climate or pathogen explanations when the required "intervention" on a global climate parameter or viral evolutionary trajectory is physically undefined, or operates in a different modal register than local social interventions.

Second, whether "agency" for non-human actors can be reconstructed purely in terms of counterfactual robustness and structural constraint—something like "an entity functions as an agent in history if its presence systematically alters the decision-space available to human actors across a range of counterfactual scenarios"—without importing intentions or strategic reasoning.

Third, whether there is a dependency relation analogous to mental-causation exclusion: if human intentional explanations are sufficient for some event, do environmental explanations get automatically excluded, or can they be genuinely co-causal in a non-overdetermining way?

But these are only illustrations. What I actually want is a defended prioritization: for a group with this profile and timeline, which one or two conceptual cruxes are most upstream—meaning that settling them restructures how the others are approached—versus which are tractable but downstream, and which should be deprioritized because resolving them wouldn't change much for either historiography or policy? Give me the criteria you're using: tractability for philosophers rather than climate scientists, neglect in current conceptual literature, dependency ordering, and what kind of result over the next few years—a formal distinction, a case-study failure, or a model—would move a question up or down. I care less about completeness than about the reasoning behind the ordering: what makes a question worth years of a philosopher's career in this space?

Also, for the highest-priority item, describe what concrete success would look like: not a bibliography, but a principle or distinction that, if accepted, would change how a specific macro-historical case is narrated—say, the interaction between a well-documented late-antique volcanic winter and the subsequent political fragmentation in the Mediterranean alongside the rise of new imperial formations—without either reducing human actors to climate puppets or ignoring the aerosol forcing altogether. Which of the other questions depend on this one being settled first, and which could be pursued in parallel without waste?
