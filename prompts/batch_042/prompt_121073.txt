I am a high school senior enrolled in an advanced moral philosophy seminar, and after a recent discussion with my peers about whether character traits are genuine psychological structures or merely convenient labels we apply to situation-driven behavior, I want to design an assessment that pushes beyond standard summaries. Rather than asking students to rehearse the well-known situationist experiments or to list Aristotelian virtues, I want the exam to center on a specific conceptual tension that is not usually treated in introductory texts: whether virtue ethics can be defended by retreating to a "domain-indexed" conception of virtue, according to which traits like courage or practical wisdom are locally stable within particular spheres of activity—such as professional, domestic, or civic life—without being globally consistent across every context in an agent's life.

Please write a rigorous essay exam question suitable for an upper-level undergraduate course, together with a model answer of approximately 600 to 800 words and a detailed marking scheme. The question should require students to evaluate whether this domain-indexed retreat genuinely preserves Aristotelian virtue ethics or whether it collapses into a form of situationism that undermines the global moral assessments—praise, blame, and character attribution—that virtue theories traditionally require. In particular, students must distinguish trait possession from trait expression, analyze how phronesis operates when practical domains are narrowly circumscribed, and address whether moral evaluation can still be meaningful if an agent displays a virtue in one domain but its absence in another. The marking scheme should separate an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific philosophical moves each must contain—such as recognizing that situationists attack cross-situational consistency rather than local reliability, correctly applying the concept of practical wisdom to domain-specific reasoning, avoiding the error of treating virtue as a simple behavioral disposition, and constructing an original argument about whether domain-indexing saves or abandons virtue ethics—together with the marks each move carries and a brief note on how candidates typically go wrong on each point.
