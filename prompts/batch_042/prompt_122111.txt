As a retired biologist who has recently begun studying classical Chinese philosophy of language, I have become preoccupied with Gongsun Long’s argument that a white horse is not a horse—the Mingjia distinction that horse selects for form (xing) while white selects for color (se), so conjoining them yields a different selection. For the closing session of a small reading group, I want a sonnet that enacts this separation structurally rather than merely describing it. Use the octave to establish the taxonomist’s conviction that kindhood belongs to morphology—the stable equine form—then let the volta introduce a leucistic mutation that seems to split the kind, and use the sestet to ask whether the addition of the chromatic property excludes the individual from the category. Make the rhyme scheme do philosophical work: anchor the word horse in a fixed rhyme position throughout all fourteen lines, while allowing white to land in shifting or broken rhyme slots, so that form remains constant and color interrupts it. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with the School of Names recognizes the xing/se distinction, yet fully intelligible to listeners with no background in it; please do not include citations, textual references, or bibliographic notes, only the conceptual tension itself.
