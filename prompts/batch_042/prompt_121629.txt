For a seminar on cross-cultural metaphysics of mind, I want to specify what any adequate theory of personal identity must deliver if it takes the Buddhist doctrine of anatta—no permanent self—not as a mere denial of the atman but as a positive constraint on accounts of moral continuity, autobiographical coherence, and rebirth. Write the desiderata: the cases and phenomena the theory must accommodate—such as the first-person stability of attention across deep meditation stages, the ethical logic of karma without an enduring owner, and the retrospective coherence of experience after states in which subject-object structure is said to collapse—the distinctions it must preserve (e.g., between causal continuity and numerical identity, between conventional self-reference and ultimate non-self), the results from cognitive science or clinical psychology it cannot flatly contradict, and the conceptual puzzles it must dissolve rather than dismiss. For each requirement, give the motivating consideration or counterexample behind it. Flag which desiderata are genuinely non-negotiable within a Buddhist framework and which are prejudices imported from Western personal-identity debates. In particular, identify any subset of requirements that appears jointly unsatisfiable—above all, whether strict causal discontinuity between lives can be reconciled with a robust notion of moral responsibility across them. Keep the focus on the specification; only after laying out the full list should you briefly indicate how reductionist, narrative, and Abhidharma-style stream-continuity accounts stand against it.
