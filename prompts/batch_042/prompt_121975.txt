In discussions of acausal cooperation, the interaction is often analogised to two merchants stranded on separate islands who exchange messages in sealed bottles washed ashore, agreeing to trade goods they each value even though the bottles carrying the agreement never reach the other party. The image is used to license the claim that rational agents ought to cooperate whenever their decision procedures are sufficiently correlated, irrespective of causal influence. Audit this analogy: provide an explicit correspondence table mapping the islands, the merchants, the bottles, the goods, and the failed delivery onto the formal elements of acausal trade — correlated decision algorithms, shared priors, and interdependent utility functions. Then identify the points of breakdown: does the bottle-image import a physical medium and temporal ordering that have no counterpart in logical correlation; does it obscure the absence of enforcement or reputation mechanisms that ordinarily stabilise trade; and does it treat correlation as a kind of hidden communication rather than a mathematical fact about decision theory? Finally, assess whether the normative conclusion — that high correlation alone generates an obligation to cooperate — survives once these disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was doing the persuasive work all along.
