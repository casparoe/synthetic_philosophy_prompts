Henry Sidgwick—utilitarian moral philosopher, co-founder and first president of the Society for Psychical Research, and advocate of exceptionally stringent evidential standards—died in 1900. Imagine that a present-day research group publishes a large-scale, pre-registered study of anomalous cognition conducted under sensory-deprivation conditions, using Bayesian sequential analysis with declared stopping rules; critics concede the registration but charge that the prior distributions were effectively calibrated after preliminary data inspection. Drawing on Sidgwick’s documented insistence that psychical inquiry must be held to stricter, not looser, canons of proof than conventional science, and on the epistemic discipline he tied to ethical impartiality in his moral philosophy, predict how he would assess this dispute. Would he regard formal pre-registration as sufficient protection against the experimenter-bias he feared, or would he identify a deeper failure of intellectual virtue in the framing of the priors? Focus on his conceptual commitments—his view of evidence, bias, and moral-epistemic duty—rather than biographical speculation, and do not supply citations or a reading list.
