I take this to show that rationality demands coherence, but I am unsure exactly how far the argument reaches.

Here is my confusion. In my philosophy seminar we recently completed an exercise in which each pupil assigned a numerical confidence between 0 and 1 to various propositions—some empirical, such as "it will rain tomorrow", and others normative, such as "it is wrong to break a promise merely for minor convenience". When we examined the results for coherence, many students' normative credences violated the probability calculus just as severely as incoherent empirical guesses would. Yet nobody treated this as proof that the underlying moral views were false; we regarded it as a quirk of how people hold ethical commitments.

This led me to construct what I shall call a "moral Dutch book". Imagine someone who simultaneously commits to two incompatible moral rules in different contexts—say, that one must never deceive, and that one must always protect the vulnerable—while knowing that future circumstances will eventually force a single choice between them. By distributing their practical commitments inconsistently, they guarantee a kind of practical defeat no matter which rule they ultimately invoke. If an ordinary Dutch book demonstrates not mere foolishness but an objective failure of belief, then should not this moral analogue demonstrate that incoherent normative commitments are objectively mistaken about what ought to be done?

Does the possibility of a moral Dutch book show that ethical beliefs must obey the calculus of probability if they are to avoid guaranteed practical defeat?
