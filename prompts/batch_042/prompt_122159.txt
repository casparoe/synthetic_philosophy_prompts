Consider Johann Gottfried Herder, writing in the late eighteenth century, who defended the idea that each people possesses a distinctive collective spirit shaped primarily by climate, language, and historical tradition, and who rejected hierarchical racial classifications of humanity. Now imagine he is confronted with two twenty-first-century developments: first, large-scale behavioral-genetics studies that report small but statistically significant differences in polygenic scores for personality-related traits across populations mapped onto modern nation-states; second, political movements that invoke national character to justify restrictive immigration and citizenship policies. Based on Herder's philosophical commitments, how do you think he would respond? Would he dismiss genetic population differences as irrelevant to his concept of collective spirit, or would he feel compelled to revise his framework to account for biologically mediated group variation? In your answer, reconstruct the reasoning he might employ, paying particular attention to the tension between his insistence on cultural and environmental formation and the apparent determinism of inherited genetic profiles. Conclude with an assessment of whether his position, consistently applied, could accommodate modern genetic discourse without collapsing into the very essentialism he sought to avoid.
