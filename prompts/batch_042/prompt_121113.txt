Translate the debate about whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible into the vocabulary of distributed consensus engineering—nodes reaching valid but mutually incompatible states, arbitration protocols, deadlock without faulty nodes, Byzantine fault tolerance, and irreducible disagreement among correctly functioning agents. Assume fluency in that vocabulary but no background in moral philosophy. Take the denial view (all apparent conflicts dissolve under correct reasoning, leaving only tragic choices with a single right answer) and the affirmation view (two all-things-considered obligations can genuinely conflict with no higher-order resolution available). For each position, report what translates cleanly, what must be distorted to fit, and what—if anything—has no counterpart in system-design terms. Focus especially on whether the idea of a morally correct agent being in simultaneous, non-erroneous "fault" relative to two authoritative norms can be expressed, or whether it forces an import foreign to distributed systems. Conclude with a residue assessment: does the leftover material show that normative authority contains something your framework genuinely misses, or does the philosophical dispute largely collapse into a disagreement about protocol design that is less substantive than it appears?
