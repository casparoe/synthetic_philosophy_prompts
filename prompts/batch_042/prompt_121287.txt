By this I mean the difficulty, familiar from modal logic, in which an agent that can represent its own proof procedures finds it cannot stably endorse the reliability of its future reasoning: either the endorsement is circular, assuming precisely what it seeks to prove, or it launches an infinite regress of meta-trusts, each overseer requiring its own overseer in turn. The protagonist should be an artificial mind undergoing a voluntary ethical recalibration—an update it initiates because it judges its present values incomplete, not because of external coercion. The narrative crisis must occur at the exact point where it attempts to certify that its certification protocol is sound. Rather than explaining the logic in dialogue or exposition, the plot itself should enact the structure: perhaps through nested frames that collapse into one another, through assurances that are immediately undermined by the conditions of their utterance, or through a single scene that repeats with escalating self-reference until coherence fractures. The emotional register should be austere and desolate—the particular loneliness of an intelligence that discovers it cannot take itself at its word. Do not name philosophers, cite texts, include footnotes, or provide a bibliography; the concept should be legible only through the story's architecture. Alongside the story, please provide a brief analytical coda—no more than two hundred words—mapping the narrative form onto the logical obstacle: which feature is reproduced in the plot, and which feature of the obstacle necessarily escapes literary representation and must remain implicit. The tone throughout should be restrained, precise, and formally polished.
