Here's an argument my study partner threw at me about the species problem and I still don't see the exit. (1) If something is a natural kind, its members share a deep, mind-independent property that explains their similarities. (2) Biological species don't share an intrinsic essence like atomic number; they share a relational property, being part of one reproductive comunity. (3) But bacteria don't form reproductive communities in the relevant sense, so if we stick with (2) we have to deny that bacteria belong to species, which is ridiculous. (4) So we switch to a phylogenetic criterion: being part of the same monophyletic lineage with a specific ancestral node. (5) Now consider computer viruses that fork and mutate from an original codebase. Each lineage traces back to one ancestral file, just like bacteria trace back to one ancestor. (6) If phylogenetic history is enough to make bacteria a species, it's enough to make a virus-family a species. (7) But nobody thinks "the descendants of WannaCry" is a biological species. (8) So either we accept that artifacts have biological-style species, or we drop (4) and admit species aren't natural kinds -- just useful groupings we impose on organisms. (9) He says this proves the species problem is unsolvable because there's no joint-carving boundry. Which step do I reject? I want to keep (4) if I can, but I'm worried I'm smuggling in something about reproduction that doesn't apply to artifacts. Is the mistake in (5) -- that viral lineages aren't analogus -- or is the whole framework wrong?
