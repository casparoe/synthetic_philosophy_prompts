I am preparing a feature on the philosophy of narrative and synthetic media, and I want to pressure-test the pretense-theory against a scenario that blends familiar conceptual territory with an unfamiliar technological framing. Here is my opening challenge: consider a procedurally generated interactive drama in which a character’s psychology and narrative arc are co-constructed in real time by the audience member’s own decisions together with an algorithmic system that the audience knows possesses no consciousness, memory, or subjective experience. When the user’s choice tree leads to the character’s irreversible loss, many report sustained grief that feels specifically directed at this counterfactual figure rather than at their own agency or at a generic sad event. If your view is correct, then recognizing that the character is a bracketed mental representation—indeed, one whose properties the user herself partially authored—should allow her to suspend the grief at will, just as one stops being frightened by a cinematic monster upon leaving the theater. Yet the affect often persists and carries a moral weight that seems to treat the counterfactual figure as genuinely mattering, even while the ontological bracket remains fully transparent. Does this not indicate that fictional emotion is not merely pretended belief in a represented reality, but rather a form of valuing counterfactual possibilities that retains its force independently of any make-believe frame?
