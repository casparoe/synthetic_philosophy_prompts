Consider two structurally parallel ways of exposing a defect in an agent's attitudes.

A Dutch book argument shows that if someone's degrees of belief violate the probability axioms, a book can be constructed whose individual bets each look fair by that person's own lights but whose joint payoff is a sure loss — and the whole book can be laid at once.

A money pump argument shows that if someone's preferences form a strict cycle (A preferred to B, B to C, C to A), a sequence of individually acceptable trades can leave them with their original holdings minus a fee.

Both arguments move from exploitability to a charge of irrationality. Yet the two arguments lead different rhetorical lives. Dutch-bookability is standardly presented as decisive: the bookie is treated as a mere diagnostician whose trap reveals a contradiction already present in the agent's credences. Money-pumpability is presented far more cautiously: the design of the sequence, the agent's foresight, and the trader's intentions are all treated as part of what has to be argued about, and the trader often reads as a manipulator rather than a referee. The same inference — a structural flaw makes you exploitable, therefore you are irrational — is treated as obvious in the credal case and as contestable in the preferential case.

Is there a principled difference that justifies this asymmetry? The most promising candidate is that a Dutch book is simultaneous and surveyable: the full set of bets can be placed at one time, so the agent's state exhibits its contradiction all at once. A money pump, by contrast, works across time, so no single moment displays the cycle; the agent may never face the global pattern. But surveyability looks like a psychological fact about how contradictions are detected, not obviously a normative fact about whether one is irrational. Alternatives to weigh: that belief aims at truth while preference aims only at satisfaction; that the probability axioms are mandatory for any truth-tracking representational state whereas transitivity is merely a local matter of ordering; that preferences allow sophistication and resolute choice in a way beliefs do not; that the right diagnosis depends on whether the exploiter arranged the options.

Test whatever principle you defend against the nearest destabilizing cases:
- A Dutch book whose bets are spread across a month, one piece at a time.
- A money pump offered to the agent as a single binding booklet of trades, seen all at once.
- An agent with cyclic preferences who sees the full cycle in advance and still accepts each swap.
- An agent with intransitive indifference rather than strict preference cycles.
- An agent who says: I stand by each choice at the time I make it and reject the global pattern.

Then give your honest verdict: keep the asymmetry, collapse it (condemn both vulnerabilities as irrational, or excuse both as exploitation), or replace it with a finer distinction. End your answer with exactly one line of the form:

\answer{keep the asymmetry} or \answer{collapse it} or \answer{replace it with a finer distinction}.
