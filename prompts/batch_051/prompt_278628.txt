In a seminar on the philosophy of language we have kept returning to the following tangle, and I would like to think through it more carefully. Sentences like "ducks lay eggs" and "mosquitoes carry malaria" are accepted by ordinary speakers as true, even though most individual ducks never lay eggs and the overwhelming majority of individual mosquitoes never transmit the malaria parasite. At the same time, sentences like "ducks are female" and "mosquitoes are infected" are rejected, even though their statistical profiles are not obviously worse. The standard diagnosis is that the accepted sentences are generics: they make a claim about the kind rather than about most of its members, and their truth depends on something like whether the predicate characterizes the kind, not on how many members satisfy it. But then there is a further complication. Speakers appear readier to accept a generic when the property is dangerous or striking: "ticks carry Lyme disease" is endorsed far more readily than "ticks carry harmless bacteria," even when the imagined prevalence is held identical. It is unclear whether this striking-property effect belongs to the semantics of generics or is a pragmatic effect of what a speaker is doing when they utter a sentence about a low-probability danger.

I want to know how much of this can be put to an empirical test. Consider the specific claim: the exception tolerance displayed by "ducks lay eggs" and "mosquitoes carry malaria" is built into the meaning of the generic itself, rather than being a pragmatic inference or a judgment bias that speakers bring to certain interesting properties. What would a genuine test of that claim look like? Please design a concrete investigation—for example, a controlled set of judgment tasks, a corpus study, or some combination—that could separate the semantic account from the pragmatic one. Be precise about which pattern of results would count for each side, and about what would have to be held fixed for the test to be probative. Then say what even a favorable result would leave open: which part of the distinction between "true of a kind" and "true of most members" is not the sort of thing any experiment can settle, and what conceptual residue would remain after the best possible evidence is in.
