I sit on my university's academic senate committee charged with selecting the voting rule for an upcoming faculty election with four candidates and one seat. The current plurality rule is widely disliked; one faction wants instant-runoff voting so the winner ultimately clears a majority threshold. Another faction objects that the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem shows that, with three or more candidates, any strategy-proof ranked voting rule must either be dictatorial, effectively exclude some candidates from winning from the outset, or apply only to a restricted class of preference profiles; since instant-runoff voting has none of those features, it is gameable—voters can sometimes do better by misreporting their true ranking. They urge us to adopt approval voting, where voters mark every candidate they find acceptable, on the ground that it is substantially less manipulable. Draft the short decision memo I will circulate before next week's vote: recommend a concrete rule (or a defensible compromise), say how heavily the strategic-voting objection should count against ranked methods, and engage the strongest argument for the option you reject.
