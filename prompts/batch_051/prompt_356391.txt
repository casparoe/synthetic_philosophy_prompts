Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book III, chapter 1, in the W. D. Ross translation, opens with the following claim:

"Since virtue is concerned with passions and actions, and on voluntary passions and actions praise and blame are bestowed, on those that are involuntary pardon, and sometimes also pity, to distinguish the voluntary and the involuntary is presumably necessary for those who are studying the nature of virtue, and useful also for legislators with a view to the assigning both of honours and of punishments."

I have been trying to settle a dispute about whether a "constrained agent" — an AI system that can form judgments but cannot act on them — should count as a moral agent. The case is this: a hospital uses a triage algorithm that examines patient records and clinical data and produces a ranked list of who should receive surgery next, with a written justification for each ranking. The system has no effectors, no authority over hospital resources, and no way to pause, refuse, or override anything. It only produces judgments; a clinician decides whether to follow them.

One colleague argues that the quoted passage settles the case. He reconstructs the argument this way:

(1) Praise and blame attach only to voluntary passions and actions.
(2) An action is voluntary in the relevant sense only if its moving principle is in the agent, so that it is in the agent's power to perform it or not perform it.
(3) The system's only output is a judgment. Every overt act that follows that judgment — operating, withholding treatment, scheduling a patient, overturning a ranking — is initiated by a human being, and its moving principle is in that human, not in the system.
(4) Therefore, for any overt act downstream of the system's output, that act is voluntary on the part of the human, not on the part of the system.
(5) Therefore, no overt act is voluntary on the part of the system.
(6) A thing is a moral agent only if it is an apt object of praise or blame for what it does.
(7) Since the system performs no voluntary overt act, there is nothing it does for which it can aptly be praised or blamed.
(8) Therefore, the system is not a moral agent.

Is this argument valid? If it is not, identify the first step that fails, state the most plausible correct version of that step, and say whether the argument with that correction still yields the conclusion. In particular, examine the slide from step (5), which concerns overt acts, to step (7), which concerns what the system does: does the system's producing of a judgment amount to an act, and could it be voluntary in Aristotle's sense? Does the fact that Aristotle speaks of passions as well as actions matter here? And if the argument is repaired by adding a premise denying that acts of judgment can be objects of praise and blame, does the same reasoning then show that a human advisor, who can recommend but not implement, is not a moral agent?
