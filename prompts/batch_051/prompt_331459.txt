I teach a once-a-week AI governance seminar for undergrads, about 18 people, 75-minute sessions, mix of CS and philosophy majors. I want to run a game that makes them feel the competitiveness constraint in AI development: the way even teams that genuinely care about safety get pushed toward cutting corners on alignment because whoever doesn't will reach the finish line first. By the end I want them to see the bad outcome as structural, not just a story about bad actors. I don't want a lecture up front; I want the incentive structure of the game to produce the bad outcome, so the group feels the pull before I name it. Design a runnable in-class simulation with clear rules, materials I can put together in an afternoon, and a payof scheme that doesn't use real money. Each team is an AI lab deciding each round how much of a fixed budget to spend on capabilities versus safety, and the scoring should be set up so safety is individually expensive—the alignment tax—but everyone going hard on capabilities makes the whole group worse off in a predictable way. Then the debrief should map specific moves in the game onto the formal idea: the alignment tax as the private cost of safety work, and the competitiveness constraint as the reason unilateral safety loses. Also be honest about what a tabletop game can't capture about actual AI risk, so they don't walk out thinking the simulation is the argument. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. Give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) materials and setup, (2) how a round works, (3) the payoff structure and why it should trap even safety-minded teams, (4) the debrief mapping, and (5) what will likely go wrong plus what the game can't reproduce.
