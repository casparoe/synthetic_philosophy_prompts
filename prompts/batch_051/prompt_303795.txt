I am a physician and a member of my hospital's clinical-decision-support committee, and we are drafting guidance on how clinicians should revise a working diagnosis as imperfect information arrives during a single visit. The information is uncertain in two distinct ways at once: a test may be inaccurate even when its result is unambiguous, and the result itself may be uncertain — a rapid antigen line too faint to read with confidence, a radiology report that hedges about whether an infiltrate is present, a colleague relaying a tentative impression over the phone. Current practice is to treat hedged findings as though they were firm or to adjust probabilities by feel, and different clinicians reach materially different probabilities from the same inputs. The theory I take to fit the situation is Jeffrey conditionalization: an observation need not make any proposition certain; instead it shifts the probabilities assigned to a partition of possible findings, while the conditional probabilities of each diagnostic hypothesis given each possible finding are held fixed, after which probabilities of interest are recomputed. This guidance will be adopted as department policy, so it must be usable by clinicians with no formal training in epistemology, runnable within the time of an ordinary visit, and explicit about which steps are judgment calls rather than arithmetic. I would like you to write the procedure itself: the steps, in order, for choosing the partition of possible findings, attaching new probabilities to its elements, checking whether the holding-fixed assumption is plausible, combining two uncertain items of evidence that arrive at different times, and deciding when the procedure should stop and hand the decision to a clinician because the step involves values or cannot be quantified. Then run it on three cases: a patient with cough and fever whose rapid flu test shows a line so faint that a positive result is itself uncertain while background prevalence is low; the same patient, whose chest X-ray then returns with hedged wording that raises the probability of pneumonia without making it certain; and a third case of your own choosing in which the procedure delivers a posterior probability the treating clinician will find clinically unacceptable — and say whether the procedure is nevertheless right to deliver it or should defer. Finally, state plainly what this procedure cannot do: the forms of evidence, uncertainty, and clinical reasoning for which Jeffrey conditionalization is the wrong instrument.
