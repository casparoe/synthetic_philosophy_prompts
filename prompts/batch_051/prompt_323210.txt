I got into an argument with my housemate about whether being excellent at something is good even if no one ever benefits from it. He said no: excellence is just a disposition or capacity, and it only gets value from the effects it produces, so an unused or unobserved excellence isn't good in any robust sense. I wanted to push back, but I realized I couldn't say exactly where the value comes from if I don't just assert that "it's good to be excellent." We kept coming back to this passage, from Book I, Chapter 7, where Aristotle says: "Perhaps then we may arrive at this by ascertaining what is man's function. For the goodness or efficiency of a flute-player or sculptor or craftsman of any sort, and in general of anybody who has some function or business to perform, is thought to reside in that function; and similarly it may be held that the good of man resides in the function of man, if he has a function." I think I understand the rough analogy: the goodness of a flute-player is a matter of performing the function of flute-playing well, and Aristotle wants to say something parallel about human beings. But the more I stare at it, the less sure I am what the argument is supposed to establish. Is this an argument that human excellence is good in itself, independently of any benefit it produces? Or is it really an argument that if you accept that human beings have a function—and that's a fairly large if—then the phrase "good human being" means something like "performs that function well," which wouldn't by itself answer the question about whether that excellence is choiceworthy when it doesn't produce any downstream goods? I'm also confused about whether the "good" here is attributive (good as a human, like a good knife) rather than good in the sense that a life containing that excellence is more worth living, and whether this distinction matters for my argument with my housemate. Could you explain exactly where the normative force is supposed to come from in this passage, and what the strongest way is to defend the thought that excellence that never benefits anyone is still good, on Aristotle's own terms?
