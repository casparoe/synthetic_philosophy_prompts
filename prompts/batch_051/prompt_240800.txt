I am a biostatistician by training and now work on causal inference in epidemiology, so my native vocabulary is potential outcomes, consistency, SUTVA, exchangeability, identifiability, transportability, and estimands. My reading in philosophy of biology is patchy: I can follow the shape of the old debate about whether natural selection is a tautology, but I do not have a confident view about what would settle it. As I understand it, the objection runs that if fitness just means actual reproductive success, then the claim that the fitter survive is only the claim that those who reproduce more reproduce more, and natural selection explains nothing. The propensity interpretation replies that fitness is a probabilistic disposition rather than an actual outcome: an organism can be fit in the relevant sense and still fail to leave many offspring, so natural selection is an empirical, causal claim. I cannot tell whether this reply works, and my instincts keep trying to formalise the whole exchange in the way I would formalise a non-deterministic treatment effect.

Please restate the tautology objection and the propensity interpretation of fitness in my vocabulary as systematically as you can. Spell out what the potential outcomes would be, what plays the role of treatment or exposure, what the estimand is, and what identifiability assumptions would be needed to recover fitness from observed reproductive histories. I want a translation, not an analogy: fit the two positions into potential-outcome machinery where they genuinely belong, flag the places where you have to bend or distort them, and say when a claim has no expression at all. Do not smuggle assumptions from causal inference into the biology and present them as if they were part of the original view.

Then give me the residue report. In particular, I want to know whether the propensity interpretation is best reconstructed as claiming that fitness is a latent parameter identifiable across environments, whether the tautology objection reappears as an identifiability or transportability problem, and whether anything in the philosophical debate is lost in that reconstruction. A colleague recently insisted that the propensity interpretation merely relabels actual success as an unobservable parameter and pretends the tautology has vanished; my hunch is that this is really a disagreement about identifiability, but I want to know whether my causal-inference lens is clarifying the debate or quietly changing the subject.
