A few months ago I sat in on a discussion group — mostly people who read the same blogs and forums I do — where the topic was the Neolithic transition, the shift from foraging to farming, and whether it should count as a mistake. Two of the regulars, Priya and Elias, ended up in an argument I still cannot settle, and I would like you to adjudicate it.

Priya opened with the empirical case for the "mistake" view. She said the skeletal record from the Levant and elsewhere shows early farmers were shorter than the foragers who preceded them in the same regions, with more enamel defects, more tooth decay, and more signs of chronic nutritional stress and infection. The diet narrowed to a handful of staples; time-budget estimates suggest work hours rose rather than fell; and the stored surplus made possible durable forms of inequality — forced labor, taxation, and coercive hierarchy — of a kind that mobile, sharing bands had mostly suppressed. So the transition looked less like progress than like a long decline in the welfare of the median person. Elias conceded the archaeological pattern but denied that "mistake" follows. There was no act of choice, he said: the transition happened piecemeal across many millennia, so there is no agent whose error we are identifying. He added that the claim that foragers were affluent rests heavily on modern hunter-gatherers pushed onto marginal land, and that a low-density, mobile way of life does not scale. The actual alternative for a given band, he said, was usually foraging on a deteriorating land base under climate stress, with neighbors who were already farming and outbreeding them. Priya answered that this is not a refutation of the mistake claim but its strongest form. Locally sensible decisions can compose into a collective trap — an arms race in which each group must farm or shrink and be absorbed, leaving an equilibrium that is worse per capita for nearly everyone than the pre-transition baseline. The interesting fact, she said, is exactly that the path into the trap can be rational at each step and still be something we call, after the fact, a miscalculation. Elias then separated explanation from evaluation. If the transition is an attractor that no locally available policy could have avoided, then "miscalculation" does no work; it is a constraint, not a mistake. He also pushed on the welfare arithmetic: if farming lowered average welfare while multiplying the population, the total-welfare ledger is ambiguous, and anyone who says it is clearly negative owes an account of why additional lives have no value. Priya replied that his population move commits him to something close to the repugnant conclusion — the view that enough lives barely worth living can outweigh a smaller number of good lives — which he would not accept in a human population. And the inequality argument, she added, does not require a central planner: sedentism, storage, and inheritable land claims were enough to make some households' claims on other people's labor durable and transmissible. Elias closed by granting the narrow empirical claim — he conceded that early farming probably worsened the typical person's health and workload for a very long time. But he said the word "mistake" still smuggles in a counterfactual: a stable, better path of foraging that populations in that time and place could actually have stayed on. And the inequality half of Priya's argument overclaims, he said, because some non-farming societies that stored wild foods already had hereditary rank and forms of servitude; farming is one route to durable hierarchy, not its only origin.

At that point the group split, and I found myself changing sides every time I replayed the exchange. Could you go through it move by move, rate each of Priya's and Elias's claims from clearly mistaken to clearly right, say who is right about which specific sub-issue, and say what both of them are missing? In the end, can the conclusion that the agricultural revolution was a mistake survive Elias's counterfactual challenge once the missing distinctions are made explicit?
