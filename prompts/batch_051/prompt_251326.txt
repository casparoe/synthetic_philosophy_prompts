I have been trying to decide how strongly to defend a deflationary view about truth-seeking, and before I present it at a reading group I want to know how much it actually risks. The view, roughly, is this: believing the truth and the practices aimed at it -- inquiry, argument, corrigibility, open-mindedness -- are valuable only as means. A true belief is good insofar as it helps you act effectively, coordinate with others, build reliable technologies, or secure some other practical or moral good; there is no further, distinctively epistemic value that accrues to truth for its own sake. I am drawn to this because it seems to dissolve the so-called value problem in epistemology: instead of explaining why knowledge is more valuable than mere true belief, I can say it isn't, except through the practical advantages that reliably produced true beliefs tend to bring. The view also handles the counting-blades-of-grass intuition: if truth had intrinsic value, then every true belief, however trivial, would add a little value to the world, and that seems false; on my view most trivial true beliefs rightly count for nothing because they are good for nothing. But I am aware the view has to swallow a lot. It owes an account of why we so persistently prefer knowledge to lucky true belief -- the swamping worry -- and of why understanding seems worth having even when it never pays. It also seems committed to saying that a useful false belief can be, all things considered, a better belief than a useless true one; I am prepared to defend that in some cases, but many interlocutors treat it as a reductio.

What I would like from you is a defeaters audit: a map of what would have to be true for the instrumentalist position I just stated to be false, and an honest assessment of how exposed it currently is. I am not mainly asking for the standard objections one at a time; I want to know which considerations would actually move a sophisticated instrumentalist, and which would be dismissed as trading on exactly the intuition under dispute. Please structure the response as follows:

- "The position, restated" (one paragraph): restate the view with enough precision to fix its commitments, especially what it says about knowledge, understanding, trivial truths, and useful false beliefs.
- "Decisive defeaters" (one to two paragraphs): the developments that, if accepted, would show the view to be false or untenable -- for instance, a convincing case for distinctively epistemic value, or a demonstration that some value we are unwilling to abandon cannot be explained by practical payoff. Say what would have to be true for each to land.
- "Mere pressures" (one to two paragraphs): considerations that would not refute the view but would make it costly -- stubborn intuitions, cases, or explanatory burdens. Say how a committed instrumentalist could absorb each, and at what cost in plausibility.
- "Concessions a defender should make" (one paragraph): what the strongest version of the view ought to concede up front rather than deny, so that it does not insulate itself dishonestly.
- "Exposure assessment" (one paragraph): your verdict on whether the view as stated is well insulated, whether it has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that insulation is a vice here or a feature of this kind of axiological position.
- "Where I should worry most" (one short paragraph, optional): if you had to pick the single line of objection I, as someone already sympathetic to the view, should take most seriously, what would it be?
