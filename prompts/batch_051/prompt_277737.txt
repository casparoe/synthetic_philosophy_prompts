I have spent the past month arguing, on and off, with a close friend about whether human extinction is bad beyond the deaths it involves. He defends the strong view that a future in which the species quietly dies out — say, because people simply stop having children — would be a catastrophe, even if the last generation lives out full, decent lives and no one is made to suffer. I have kept pressing the obvious objection: if the last generation’s lives are complete and decent, who is left to be wronged? Last night he introduced what he called Scheffler’s afterlife conjecture, where “afterlife” means only the continuing existence of humanity after our individual deaths. His summary was roughly this: many of the things we care about — our work, our immersion in the arts and sciences, even the point of trying to pass something on — depend on the background assumption that human life will continue after we are gone. If we all knew the species were about to end, those things would come to seem pointless to us, and they would stop motivating us the way they normally do.

I am now puzzled about what this move is supposed to show. The conjecture explains why the last generation would be demoralized, disoriented, or unable to sustain its projects. But demoralization is a psychological cost borne by people who already exist; it is not a wrong done to people who never come into existence. So the afterlife conjecture, taken at face value, seems to work against the original claim rather than for it. It appears to relocate the supposed extra badness of extinction inside the lives of current people: extinction would matter only because it would make our remaining lives feel meaningless. And that turns concern for future generations into an indirect form of concern for our own motivation.

That is the part I cannot get past. If the afterlife conjecture is doing the real work in my friend’s argument, has he quietly abandoned his original position — that extinction is bad for a reason that goes beyond the lives it extinguishes — in favor of the much weaker claim that it would make the last generation unhappy in a distinctive way? Or am I misunderstanding what the afterlife conjecture is meant to explain? I am no longer sure whether it is a point about the badness of extinction, a point about our current values, or somehow both at once, and I would like a careful account of where my reasoning goes wrong, if it does.
