I got into this with a friend after we both visited the same city and disagreed about whether we'd been rude, or actually wrong, to skip tips in places where servers are paid almost nothing on the assumption that tips make up the wage. He later sent me a cleaned-up version of the argument, and now we're stuck because it seems to show either skipping the tip wasn't wrong after all, or tourists are on the hook for every local habit they can't possibly know. It goes:

(1) If a community has a standing practice that almost everyone follows and that people materially rely on, then deliberately flouting it in normal cases wrongs the people whose reliance you disappointed.
(2) In a place where tipped workers are paid below the ordinary minimum because tips are assumed, tipping is exactly that kind of practice: servers rely on it to make a normal wage.
(3) So a visitor who quietly doesn't tip in such a place does something wrong.
(4) Nothing in (1)–(3) turns on the visitor having consented, having grown up there, or identifying with the practice; the reliance is doing all the work.
(5) So if reliance is what does the work, then any established, relied-upon practice in a place imposes the same sort of obligation on a visitor.
(6) But there are established, relied-upon practices that are invisible to visitors, that point in opposite directions in different places, or that are themselves pretty shabby: which side of the sidewalk people walk on, whether everyone queues single-file or just clusters, whether jaywalking is nothing or a genuine scandal, whether haggling is expected or insulting, forms of address, who gets served first.
(7) So a visitor is morally obligated to follow local conventions they mostly can't know about, including some they'd find demeaning or unjust.
(8) But a supposed obligation you can't know about and would be repelled by can't be a genuine moral obligation.
(9) So (1) is false: even in the original case, skipping the tip wasn't a moral wrong, just a breach of etiquette.

We both want to say the conclusion is bonkers, and I in particular want to keep (1), because the tip case really doesn't feel like mere etiquette to me. But it's embarassing that neither of us can find the step where the argument overextends. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd like the diagnosis to say why the step looked obviously true from inside the argument, and what the standard ways of thinking about conventional obligations—consent, fair play, expectation-based views, practice-based views—each have to give up or stretch in order to take that way out.
