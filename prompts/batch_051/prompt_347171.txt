I have been turning over a parallel that I cannot decide is deep or merely seductive. Cartography starts from a fact that is easy to state and hard to live with: no flat map of the earth can simultaneously preserve area, shape, distance, and direction, so every projection must choose which properties to honor and which to distort. A map is therefore never a neutral picture of the globe; it is a selection, organized around a purpose and, in an extended sense, a standpoint. This looks to me structurally similar to the problem that now surrounds the writing of world history. A narrative that aspired to be genuinely global — to refuse any privileged center, to escape Eurocentrism by telling the past from everywhere at once — would still have to choose scales, periodizations, agents, and causal threads, and those choices would seem to function like a cartographer's choice of projection: foregrounding some regions and letting others fall into silence, compressing some histories and inflating others. On that reading, the view from everywhere is not an ideal asymptotically approached by more inclusive scholarship; it is an impossibility built into the very form of a narrative, just as the perfect map is impossible on any flat surface. But I can also credit the suspicion that this is metaphor working without a license. Cartographic distortion follows with necessity from the geometry of mapping a curved surface onto a plane, whereas the historian's distortions are contingent, corrigible, and shaped by politics rather than mathematics, so perhaps there is no deep constraint here — only an evocative analogy that borrows its rigor from one side. Is this parallel deep, a shared condition of finite representation from a situated standpoint, or superficial, an artifact of using words like perspective and distortion loosely across two fields? In particular: what would have to be true of historical narrative for the cartographic analogy to carry weight rather than merely charm, and does the analogy predict anything specific about history-writing — say, a principled trade-off between global coverage and local adequacy, or the impossibility of a single master narrative — where it then goes wrong?
