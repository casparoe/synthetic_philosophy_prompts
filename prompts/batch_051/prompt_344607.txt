Suppose a state's cruelty-to-animals statute, passed in 1955 and never amended, has one operative line: "Whoever knowingly or recklessly causes an animal to suffer pain or death not justified by a reasonable and lawful purpose is guilty of animal cruelty; provided that nothing in this section prohibits practices generally accepted in agriculture." Someone is at the grocery store deciding how to feed her household for the week. She can go with chicken (birds bred so fast their legs give out, killed at six weeks), beef (a small fraction of a grass-finished steer, then a slaughterhouse), or wheat and lentils (no farmed animal, but the combines kill the mice, voles, and insects in the field as a matter of course, and over a year of meals that adds up). She knows all this — she has read the supply-chain studies. Dont just say the statute wasnt written for grocery shoppers; that's exactly the question. Does her buying the chicken or the beef "cause" pain or death to an animal? Are crowded sheds and combine harvesting "generally accepted in agriculture" so the whole thing is carved out? Work through the interpretive approaches — the words, the purpose, what the drafters would have said if asked, what a reasonable reader in the store would take the law to mean today — and then say which approach is most defensible and what it actually implies about her dinner.
