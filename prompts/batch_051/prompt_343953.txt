I taught chemistry for thirty-some years and retired a while back, and I've spent the last couple years reading philosophy of time and persistance. I've written down the package of views I've ended up with, and I'd like you to audit it: tell me which parts pull against which, how badly, and which apparent tensions I can safely ignore.

Here's the list:

(1) I'm an endurantist. An ordinary object — a molecule, a leaf, a person — is wholly present at each moment of its existence. It doesn't have temporal parts; it isn't spread through time the way a long polymer chain is spread through space.

(2) I'm a B-theorist. Past, present, and future are all equally real, and 'now' is just an indexical like 'here.' I read this as what relativity is pushing us toward, and I basically take presentism to be a non-starter.

(3) I think genuine change is everywhere and it's consistent: one and the same molecule can be cis at noon and trans at one o'clock, a leaf can be green on Tuesday and red on Friday, and there's no contradiction as long as we say the properties hold at different times.

(4) I also want to say the properties involved in change are intrinsic. Being cis or trans, bent or straight, green or red is a way the thing itself is, not a relation to a time. If I have to relativize every property to a moment, chemistry starts talking about ordered pairs of molecule and instant instead of the molecule itself.

(5) I hold Leibniz's law without exception: if a thing at t1 is strictly identical with a thing at t2, then they share all properties.

(6) And I reject coincident objects. If the same lump of matter in the same place can be described as a statue and as a piece of clay, it's one thing, not two. The statue just is the clay.

So: is this a consistent set, or am I quietly helping myself to something? Which pairs are in genuine tension and which are only apparently in tension? If I have to give something up, what's the cheapest fix and what does it cost? Please answer in American English.
