Here's one I keep coming back to when I teach On the Uses and Disadvantages of History for Life (the second Untimely Meditation). Early in the essay Nietzsche tells us the past is needed for life and action, and in the discussion of monumental history he gives that mode a pretty warm write-up: the person who strives for something great needs exemplars, and monumental history shows him that greatness was possible once and so can be possible again, that the great moments of the past form a chain across the ages. But then, almost in the same breath, he says monumental history does violence to the past. It deals in approximations and analogies, smudges the differences between motives and circumstances, and can push bold spirits into recklessness and enthusiasts into fanaticism precisely because it hands them misleading parallels with the past. So the same practice gets described both as a source of strength for the active person and as a deceptiive, potentially destructive distortion of the past.
