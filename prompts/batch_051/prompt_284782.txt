A friend of mine who teaches political theory gives one-word verdicts, "free" or "unfree," on whether a given situation makes a person unfree. Her record so far:

- A slave whose master is benevolent and never actually interferes in her life: unfree.
- A citizen compelled by a general, democratically enacted law to serve on a jury: free (that is, not unfree).
- A driver held at a police checkpoint without any legal basis: unfree.
- A retiree with no dependents, no outstanding obligations, and no one holding power over her: free.

Next week's case is a person with a severe addiction whom no one interferes with and no one has power over, but whose compulsion stops her from acting on values she endorses. Would the friend say the addiction leaves her free or unfree?

I care less about the prediction itself than about the theory of liberty she is implicitly using. Lay out whether the record best fits a negative, positive, or republican conception of freedom, and which of these views fit her earlier verdicts equally well. Say what each surviving view predicts about the addiction case, give a best guess about her verdict with the reasons that favor it, and name one further case that would settle which view she is running.

Separate what is settled about these concepts from what is genuinely contested among scholars of liberty, and avoid presenting one contested definition of freedom as the consensus. Structure the response in four sections, in this order: "The candidate views" (roughly 150 words), "What the record already rules out" (roughly 150 words), "The addiction case" (roughly 200 words), and "The remaining test" (roughly 100 words).
