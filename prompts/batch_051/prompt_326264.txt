I am working through Leibniz's Monadology (1714), and I have gotten stuck at the move where he argues that no two monads can be exactly alike. Here is the passage as it appears in the translation I am using (section 9):

"Each monad, indeed, must be different from every other monad. For there are never in nature two beings which are exactly alike, and in which it is not possible to find a difference either internal or based on an intrinsic property."

Just before this, in section 8, he has argued that monads must have qualities at all; otherwise, he says, there would be no way to distinguish one state of things from another, and the monads would be indistinguishable from one another.

I understand the surface claim — roughly, no two things are perfectly alike. But I am trying to get clear on exactly what kind of difference Leibniz is demanding, and how the principle fares against certain quantum-mechanical cases. Help me work through the following.

First, what is the force of "internal or based on an intrinsic property"? Is Leibniz thereby excluding differences that consist only in relations to other things, or only in position in space and time? If I said that two drops of water differ because one is here and the other is there, or because one is to the left of the other, would that count as the sort of difference section 9 requires, and why or why not?

Second, I want to test the passage against identical quantum particles. Two bosons — for example, two helium-4 atoms in a Bose–Einstein condensate — can occupy the same quantum state. They have the same mass, the same charge, the same spin, and the formalism treats them as strongly indiscernible: there is no intrinsic property in the standard list that one has and the other lacks. Is this a counterexample to Leibniz's principle as stated? Or does a Leibnizian have room to say that the case falls outside the scope of the claim, because Leibniz is talking about monads — simple substances — and not about whatever particles turn out to be in quantum field theory?

Third, could a Leibnizian push further and say that the two bosons are not two distinct substances at all; that quantum statistics, with its loss of individual labels and its symmetric states, is actually closer to the spirit of sections 8–9 than to a refutation of the principle? I would like you to lay out which replies are available, what each would commit me to, and how one would tell the difference between a genuine counterexample to the principle and a case that merely changes the subject.

Please keep the discussion tied closely to the wording of the passage; I am less interested in a general history of Leibniz's metaphysics than in a careful reading of what "internal" difference is supposed to rule out and what the quantum case does and does not show about it.
