I have been working through the debate over biological individuality, and I find myself pulled in two directions. The theory I find hardest to resist says that 'organism' is not a natural kind fixed by common sense but a term of art tied to whatever process we are tracking, and that the relevant criteria—physiological integration, reproduction through a bottleneck, and being a unit of selection—systematically pull apart. Follow that view and it becomes true in a principled way that a human being plus her gut microbiota is one organism (a holobiont), that an ant or termite colony is an organism in the evolutionary sense, and that a Portuguese man o' war is a single organism in one sense and a colony of specialized zooids in another; a sterile worker ant, by contrast, may not count as a full individual at all. I like this view because it replaces a vague folk category with explicit criteria, handles lichens and corals (with their zooxanthellae) cleanly, and is honest about the fact that nature has no fixed line between one living thing and many. And yet I cannot shake the firm intuition that a man o' war is simply a colony and not an organism, that an ant colony is only metaphorically a superorganism, that I am one organism while my gut bacteria are other organisms, and that a sterile worker ant is an organism full stop. I do not want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that gives, what it would take for the theory to be amended or abandoned—for instance by distinguishing physiological from evolutionary individuality and refusing to grant any one of them the word 'organism'—and whether there is a reading of 'organism' on which both survive. Which resolution is most honest, and why?
