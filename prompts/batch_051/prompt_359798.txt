I have been reading about ordinary people who take part in mass atrocities, and I keep noticing an asymmetry in how we judge the perpetrators, and I cannot tell whether it is defensible. Compare two people who commit the same act, with the same knowledge and the same result. The first is an unremarkable person: he seems to have hated no one, he was ordered and pressured, he feared what would happen if he refused, and he slid into the violence in small steps. The second is an eager believer: he was under no special pressure, but he had absorbed an ideology that told him the victims were not fully human, and he acted out of conviction. Most people, myself included, feel that the first is less blameworthy than the second — we call the first a man overtaken by his situation and the second a man acting from character. But I cannot find the principle behind that feeling. Both men were shaped by forces outside their control; both made choices along the way; both knew the act was wrong by any ordinary standard. Does the difference between situation and conviction really carry that much moral weight? If it does, I want the strongest version of the principle, tested against the hard case: a person who joins in reluctantly and out of fear, but in a culture that has already taught him the victims are less than human, so that the two explanations cannot be pulled apart. If it does not, I want to hear that the asymmetry is something we keep only because it is comforting. Finally, tell me how confident you are in your answer, and identify the point you are least sure of.
