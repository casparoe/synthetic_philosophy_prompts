I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm drafting a long feature about the Daoist notion of wu-wei — roughly, acting effortlessly and in tune with a situation rather than forcing things. The bit that keeps snagging me is the paradox of trying not to try: the more you aim straight at being spontaneous or unforced, the more self-concious and artificial you get. In the books and interviews I've been going through, people keep reaching for one analogy to make this vivid: its like trying to fall asleep. Insomniacs know that the harder you try to will yourself to sleep, the more awake you stay; sleep only arrives once you stop trying. The analogy is doing real work for them — it licences the conclusion that there is no direct route to wu-wei, that any technique for becoming effortless backfires, and that all you can do is set up the conditions and then get out of your own way. That conclusion already takes a side in a dispute I keep coming across in the material: whether wu-wei is what you get after years of drilling a skill until it becomes second nature, or only after you drop the drilling entirely.

I want you to audit how far that analogy actually holds. Map out what is meant to correspond to what (sleeplessness to self-consciousness, sleep to wu-wei, the insomniac's effortful striving to deliberate trying, and whatever else you see), then show where the mapping breaks down. I especially want to know which disanalogies matter for the conclusion the analogy is being used to support, and which are just superficial. Also flag what the falling-asleep picture quietly imports — about passivity, about whether we are to blame for failing to achieve it, about skill and effort and the self — that might distort the Daoist idea. Does the conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and is there a better analogy for wu-wei if this one fails? Please write the whole answer in British English.
