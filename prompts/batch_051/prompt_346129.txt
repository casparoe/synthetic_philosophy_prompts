I'm a novelist, and I've been trying to write a character who insists that no machine could ever make the kind of intuitive leap that produces a genuinely new plot twist — connecting two things in a way the training data didn't already contain. I've been collecting the usual arguments for the "no" side:

(1) The interpolation argument: a model learns a function over its training data, so every output stays somewhere inside the region that data covers; it can recombine and interpolate, but it can't jump outside the distribution.

(2) The recombination argument: what looks like a new idea is just a blend of existing elements, and reshuffling pieces is not a leap — a collage is not a discovery.

(3) The no-feeling argument: human leaps are usually driven by a felt sense of a gap or of rightness, a pressure to resolve something, and a model has no such feeling guiding it; it picks tokens by probablity, not by the click of a solution.

(4) The no-stakes argument: novelists get their leaps partly from having a life — deadlines, anxieties, specific memories, the need to surprise a real reader — and a model has no situation to leap from and no skin in the game.

I have a nagging sense I'm missing a different kind of argument, one that isn't just a restatement or special case of these four. Can you give me one? It should be genuinely new relative to this list — not a reworded version of any of them — and it would help if you could say whether it's actually any good as an argument or just clever-sounding.
