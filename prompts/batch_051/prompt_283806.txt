I run a small wildlife rehabilitation clinic, and over the years I have come to believe something my own clinic keeps failing to live up to: species membership, by itself, should not decide which animal gets help. The fact that a squirrel is familiar while a pigeon is "dirty" or a starling is common looks to me like the same habit of mind as preferring one breed of dog over another. And yet every spring, when the intake room is full, I watch myself and my staff do exactly that. The mammals get the incubator, the songbirds get hand-fed, and the starlings and house sparrows get set aside or quietly euthanized because they are common, no one brought them in, and no one will ask questions.

I want you to turn the principle I keep fumbling toward into an actual triage procedure. The principle, as I understand it: what matters is not which species an animal belongs to, but what its life is like from the inside—whether it can suffer, how much, and how likely our help is to relieve that suffering—together with the cost in money, time, and cages. Species membership may enter only if it is a reliable proxy for one of those things, not as a separate reason of its own.

Here is the situation the procedure has to work in. I run the clinic with two part-time staff and rotating volunteers, none of them with academic training in this. In intake season we make three to ten of these decisions a day. Most decisions have to be made in under twenty minutes, from a quick exam, phone advice from a vet, and an honest guess about the odds of release. We have a fixed weekly budget and limited space, so a raccoon we keep for a month is genuinely a robin, a mouse, or a turtle we cannot take. Local law puts some cases outside our discretion: certain species are protected and must be transferred or given priority, and some introduced species cannot legally be released and may have to be euthanized. I want the procedure to treat those legal constraints as fixed inputs, not as disguised moral preferences. Donations and volunteer effort are also partly species-driven: the public complains if we spend money on "pests" and stops giving if we ignore the photogenic ones. We cannot pretend that pressure does not exist, because a clinic that goes broke helps no one, but I also do not want public sentiment to become the criterion by accident.

Please give me a step-by-step decision procedure—a sequence of questions or tests, in order—that a volunteer could actually work through in twenty minutes. In doing so:

- Show how equal consideration survives even when treatment is unequal. I keep being told that treating species equally does not mean treating every animal the same, and I need to see how that distinction works out in the actual decisions.
- At the step where you compare suffering and the prospects of relieving it, say plainly how much of that comparison is evidence and how much is guesswork, and how to proceed when we cannot compare two animals' inner states on a single scale.
- Tell me how to handle numbers: one animal with a good chance versus many animals with poorer chances. Is adding up many small expected recoveries legitimate, and if so, where does the procedure draw the line?
- Address whether an animal's species being locally rare or endangered should ever count, and on what grounds. I can see an argument that rarity is about future suffering or legal duty, and I can also see rarity becoming speciesism in a lab coat.
- Flag each step where a human judgment is doing the work under a procedural-looking cover, and say what a volunteer should do when two people disagree at one of those steps.

Then apply the procedure to these three intake decisions, all of which have happened to me in one form or another:

1. A juvenile raccoon with a clean foreleg fracture, a very good chance of full recovery and release after a month, at about $400 in care; versus a common painted turtle with a cracked shell, survivable and cheaper at about $250, but unlikely to be releasable and likely to become a long-term education animal.
2. A red fox with moderate injuries, perhaps a 60 percent chance of eventual release at about $600; versus thirty nestling house mice needing weeks of hand-feeding, perhaps 40 percent surviving to release at about the same total cost—and they are not an animal our volunteers or donors like.
3. A bat from a locally endangered species with a wing injury, maybe a 30 percent chance of release and expensive care; versus a raccoon with a painful but very fixable injury and a high chance of returning to the wild. I already suspect your procedure will tell me to treat the raccoon, and I admit I hate that answer.

Finally, say what the procedure cannot do—where it will systematically misfire or what question it is not even trying to answer. Then tell me how confident you are in the procedure overall, and single out the one step or claim you are least sure of.
