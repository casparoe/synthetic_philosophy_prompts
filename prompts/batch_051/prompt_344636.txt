I am a retired physicist who, late in life, has been working through the foundations of probability and the philosophy of decision theory. Please act as a professional philosopher of decision theory who is comfortable with the relevant mathematics. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the several distinct ways in which the single change below might matter before you commit to a worked analysis.

Take the standard St. Petersburg game: a coin is tossed until it lands heads for the first time, and if that first head occurs on toss n, the bank pays 2^(n−1) dollars. Now change exactly one feature. The coin is not fair; it is known to land heads with probability p = 0.51 and tails with probability q = 0.49 on every toss, independently.

I would like a rigorous worked analysis of what this change does to the paradox, structured as follows.

1. For a general p, derive the condition under which the expected monetary value of the game converges, and find its closed form. Specialize to p = 0.51.

2. Show that at p = 0.51 the second moment of the payoff diverges, so the game has a finite mean but no finite variance. State the general threshold on p for the second moment to converge.

3. Apply Bernoulli's logarithmic utility, u(x) = log₂ x. Compute the expected utility of the payoff and the certainty equivalent — the amount m such that u(m) equals that expected utility — as functions of p. Show that this quantity remains finite for every p strictly between 0 and 1 and is continuous through the fair-coin case p = 0.5, even though the monetary expectation blows up as p approaches 0.5 from above. Compare the certainty equivalent at p = 0.51 with the fair-coin value.

4. Assess which of the standard resolutions of the original paradox still have real work to do in the biased-coin version, and which become partially or wholly superfluous. Consider at least: logarithmic utility, bounded utility, a payoff cap or a finite bankroll, general risk aversion, and discounting of delayed or uncertain payments.

5. Finally, take a position and argue for it: is the St. Petersburg paradox best understood as an artifact of an infinite mathematical expectation, or as a deeper problem about heavy-tailed payoffs, unbounded utilities, and the gap between mathematical expectation and what a finite agent should pay? What does the continuity of the Bernoulli valuation across p = 0.5, in contrast to the blow-up of the mathematical expectation, show about where the paradox really lives?
