I'm pulling together my position on the grit/resilience/fortitude debate for a short essay and I'd rather not have someone point out after the fact that I'm holding an inconsistent set of views. Here's where I've landed, and I want a check on wether it hangs together.

1. I think fortitude is a real virtue in the old-fashioned sense: a stable disposition to withstand hardship for worthwhile ends, lying somewhere between softness and a joyless, brittle endurance.
2. I think the contemporary resilience discourse—the HR, public-health, and education version—is ideological in the pejorative sense: it repackages structural problems as individual deficits and moralizes coping.
3. But I don't think the skills taught under that banner (emotion regulation, realistic goal adjustment, help-seeking) are worthless. On the contrary, I think they're generally good for the people who acquire them, and I wouldn't want my own students to lack them.
4. I think Duckworth-style grit is not a well-formed psychological kind. It's a fuzzy blend of conscientiousness and interest stability, and the scale can't distinguish virtuous persistence from self-destructive stubbornness.
5. I'm also drawn to the view that whether a stretch of endurance counts as a virtue depends on its social context and purpose: the same pattern of dogged persistence can be noble in a political prisoner and servile in an overworked employee.
6. And yet, to take a concrete case, I think the overworked employee who stays in a hostile job because she is the family's only income is acting admirably, not servilely.

I'd like an audit rather than a lecture. Go through these and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, and how severe each tension is. Then tell me what the cheapest fix would be and what it costs.
