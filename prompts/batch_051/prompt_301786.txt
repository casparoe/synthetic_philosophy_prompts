I've been mulling over the 2013 decision to drop the bereavement exclusion from the DSM-5's major depression criteria, and I keep thinking it's really a philosophical fight wearing a diagnostic-manual costume. Under DSM-IV, if you met the symptom bar for a major depressive episode within two months of losing a loved one, the diagnosis was normally withheld unless there were extra alarm features—marked functional impairment, suicidal ideation, psychotic symptoms, morbid worthlessness, that sort of thing—because your state was chalked up to ordinary mourning. DSM-5 removed that carve-out, so the same symptoms can get you a depression diagnosis two weeks after the funeral, with only a footnote distinguishing normal grief from major depression. The official rationale was mostly that bereavement is just one loss among others (divorce, losing a job), that the exclusion was doing more harm then good, and that it kept people from a diagnosis they needed; the critics said it was medicalizing grief. I know the clinical epidemiology is messy; I'm interested in the philosophical side.

What I want is an analysis of that episode through the lens of the debate about whether negative emotions like sadness and grief have value—whether a life without them would be better, or whether they're doing something that a flattened, symptom-free life would lose. On one side: grief is a fitting and even informative response that tracks a real loss, and folding it into a disease category treats an appropriate part of a whole life as a malfunction. On the other side: grief is suffering, suffering is bad, and if we can intervene early and blunt it, the talk about "value" is just romancing a cluster of symptoms.

Run the mapping in both directions. What would each side say the APA should have done, and what does the actual episode do to each position? Be careful about which features the value-of-negative-emotions lens actually explains—the red flags for impairment and suicidality, the two-month-versus-two-week timing, the cultural resistance to treating mourning with antidepressants, the genuine suffering the diagnosis was meant to catch—and which features it misdescribes or stays mute on. Then give an honest verdict: is DSM-5 a confirmation of one side, an anomaly for the other, or a case neither position was built to handle? And does the episode tell us anything about the abstract question of whether a griefless life would be better, or does it mostly show that the abstract debate was asking the wrong question?
