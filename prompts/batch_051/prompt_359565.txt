Here's the task. Draft the joint statement that a committed conditionalist about dispositions and a committed dispositional realist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The conditionalist is working with something like the simple conditional analysis: a thing is disposed to M in response to S just in case it would M if it underwent S. The realist thinks dispositions are intrinsic powers, and that conditionals like that are at best fallible symptoms of the power, not what being disposed consists in. Both already agree the classic cases are decisive against the bare conditional analysis. Spell them out or take them as given: a fink case, where the stimulus removes the disposition (a fragile glass that would not break if struck, because a device detects the incoming strike and renders it shatterproof before impact); a mask case, where the disposition remains but something extrinsic blocks the manifestation (the glass stays fragile but is packed in shock-absorbing foam, so a strike doesn't break it); and a mimic case, where the thing lacks the disposition yet the conditional holds for extrinsic reasons (a non-fragile block wired so that any impact detonates a charge and shatters it).

Now write the document: a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table. The agreed claims should be substantive enough that neither side has to cross their fingers while signing, and the disagreement rows should put each side's view in wording its own holder would accept. At minimum the table should include a crux about whether a non-circular 'normal conditions' or ceteris paribus clause can repair the conditional analysis, and one about whether the fink/mask distinction is carrying real theoretical weight or is just two labels on the same counterexample. For each crux, both sides should agree on the argument, case, or test that would bear on it, and each should record there prediction about which way it cuts. Both are also suspicous that part of the argument has been verbal — around 'is disposed' and 'causal basis' — so say plainly which residual disagreements you'd count as verbal and which as substantive. Don't referee the dispute: produce the statement they could both put their names to, with the remaining disagreement left visible and made exact.
