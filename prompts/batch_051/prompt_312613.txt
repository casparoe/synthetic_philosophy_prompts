I'm an undergraduate writing a term paper on how two-dimensional semantics bears on conceivability arguments against physicalism, and I'm trying to sort out which parts of the framework I actually need to accept before I trust its more ambitious applications. A friend sent me a short entry from a philosophy encyclopedia, written by someone whose name I recognized, so I read it carefully. It summarized the view roughly as follows. On a two-dimensional account, each expression has two meanings: a primary meaning, which is essentially the description a competent speaker associates with the term and which is the same in every possible world, and a secondary meaning, which is the term's actual referent and depends on how our world happens to be. Because the primary meaning of water says only something like clear, drinkable liquid that fills lakes and rivers, the entry inferred that the claim that water is H2O comes out only contingently true: in another possible world, the word water would simply pick out whatever watery stuff that world contains. The entry then drew its main payoff: once meanings are split this way, being necessary and being knowable a priori line up again, so every statement that is true in all possible worlds can be known from the armchair, and vice versa. The entry closed by saying that this is what lets Chalmers move from the conceivability of zombies to the claim that physicalism is false.

Something about the transition from the machinery to the payoffs feels off to me, because I have repeatedly seen philosophers call the claim that water is H2O a paradigm of the necessary a posteriori and describe two-dimensional semantics as a way of explaining how that combination is possible, not as a way of denying it. But the entry is by a professional, and I don't want to reject it on a hunch. Could you give me an accuracy audit of that summary the way a working philosopher of language would?
