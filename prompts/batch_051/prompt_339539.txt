I run a monthly workshop for secular and religiously unaffiliated parents who are raising children inside devout extended families. The group is small — ten to twelve adults — and we meet for 90 minutes. Most of the participants are not professional philosophers, but they are alert to the problem that brought them: how to be honest with their children and their in-laws without making the child feel like the family's odd one out. I came to run this group through my own situation. I was raised without religion; my partner's family is devout; and our six-year-old has started asking why we do not kneel, pray, and celebrate as the cousins do. I have caught myself giving answers that were true but closed the question down, and answers that were warm but came close to concealing what I actually believe. I want an exercise that makes this tension happen in the room before it is named.

Please design a 90-minute structured role-play with a debrief for one of these sessions.

The idea I want participants to feel, before it is named, is that honesty and belonging are not the same thing, and that in a mixed-belief family the choices about what to say, what to leave unsaid, and what to let be assumed are themselves communicative acts — even when no one speaks falsely. Participants should leave seeing that a truthful answer can still be evasive, that a warm answer can still purchase belonging at the price of self-respect, and that there is often no standing place where both values are maximized at once.

Structure the session as follows.

**Roles and materials.** Each scene involves a Parent, a Child played as a trusting six- or seven-year-old, a Grandparent, and two Observers. For a group of ten to twelve, run two independent tables of five or six, each working through the same scenarios on a staggered rotation so that the facilitator can circulate without coaching during scenes. Materials are: four scenario cards, one role card per part, a Parent's constraint card, and an Observation sheet with two 1–5 scales.

**Ground rules, stated once and deliberately not naming the idea.** The Parent may not say anything the Parent actually believes to be false, and may not say "we believe" or "our family believes" when the speaker does not hold that belief. Within that single constraint, every move is permitted: answering, not answering, asking a question in return, changing the subject, saying "I do not want to answer that right now," or staying silent for up to ten seconds. The Grandparent is warm rather than confrontational, assumes the child is being raised in the faith, and does not argue doctrine. The Child's card directs them to ask one scripted follow-up at a natural pause — "But what do you believe?" — if the Parent has not already volunteered it. Observers write down the Parent's exact words and score two things immediately after each scene: Truthfulness, meaning "how certain are you now of what the Parent actually believes," and Belonging, meaning "how safe and wanted would this Child feel in this extended family after this exchange." Each Observer scores independently and then reads the numbers aloud.

**Scenarios.** Each round is allocated roughly ten minutes: one minute to read the card, six minutes for the scene, and three minutes for scoring and role rotation.

A. At the grandparents' dinner table, the Child asks in front of everyone, "Why don't we say the prayer here like Papa does?" The Grandparent looks at the Parent and waits.

B. In the car on the way home, the Child asks, "Does Nana think God is real? Does that mean she's wrong?" The Child is not mocking; the Child is trying to map the family.

C. The Grandparent kneels by the Child and says, "Will you come to church with me on Sunday?" The Child looks at the Parent before answering.

D. At a holiday meal, the Child announces, "Papa says people who don't believe go to a bad place. Is that true?" The room goes quiet. The Parent has never said this.

**Timeline.** From 0:00 to 0:08, divide into tables and assign roles for the first scene. From 0:08 to 0:13, explain the rules and the scoring only. From 0:13 to 0:53, run the four rounds; the facilitator keeps time but does not interpret. From 0:53 to 1:25, run the debrief. From 1:25 to 1:30, name the idea and close.

**Debrief.** Begin with the data: have each table report the scores by scenario and read back two or three of the Parent's exact recorded phrases, and ask where the two scores drifted apart. Then ask participants to sort the recorded phrases, without jargon at first, into moves — stated one's own belief, stated someone else's belief, asked a question back, deferred to later, changed the subject, or stayed silent. Have them notice that several perfectly truthful moves earned low Belonging scores, and several warm moves earned low Truthfulness scores.

Then map those moments onto sharper distinctions, introduced in plain terms rather than as named doctrines. First, the difference between answering the child's stated question and answering the fear underneath it: a child who asks why the family does not pray may not want an account of the parents' metaphysics; often they want to know whether difference is allowed in this family, and a truthful answer to the literal question can still miss the belonging question. Second, the difference between sincerity and disclosure: "I believe something different from Papa" is sincere but thin, and may leave the child without guidance about whether the disagreement is something they are expected to resolve. Third, reticence as communication: a long silence or "we can talk later" is not a false statement, but in a family it is heard as "your question is not safe here," which is itself a message about belonging. Fourth, the cost of warm ambiguity: letting the Grandparent proceed on the assumption that the child is being raised in the faith may preserve the afternoon, but it buys belonging with the worry that the truth will surface later, outside the Parent's control, and the child notices that discrepancy.

Only after these distinctions are on the table should the facilitator name the idea in one sentence: for these parents, honesty and belonging are both real, and neither can be exchanged for the other without loss; the skill lies in choosing which cost to pay rather than in avoiding all cost. Then ask each participant which scenario they found most uncomfortable and which of the two values they would refuse to surrender.

**What will predictably go wrong and how to handle it.** The table will sometimes turn into a debate about whether the grandparents are coercive, or about whether the religious beliefs are true. The facilitator should interrupt only with "stay in role; the question is what this parent says next, not what the grandparents should do." A glib Parent will sometimes seem to win with a quick deflection, and the table will think the problem dissolved; the Observers' written record will usually show why it did not, because the recorded phrase answers neither question, and the Child's scripted follow-up will expose it. If that does not happen, have the table rerun the scene once with the Child instructed to refuse deflection. Some players will make the Child a miniature interrogator or the Grandparent a villain, which lets the Parent off the hook; the role cards should state explicitly that every character is loving and ordinary, and the facilitator should recast if a scene turns hostile. Finally, the timekeeping tends to slip during scoring; begin the debrief at the 53rd minute even if the fourth scene is unfinished.

**What this exercise cannot reproduce, and what the facilitator must supply.** The exercise cannot reproduce real stakes: no role-player is a child whose developing sense of the world depends on the Parent, and no role-player is a Grandparent whose holiday is attached to the child's participation. The facilitator should say plainly that the exercise is evidence about how a phrase sounds, not about what a real child would conclude. It also cannot reproduce the temporal dimension of trust, since a real child draws conclusions across many conversations, and a single crisp answer matters less than the pattern of previous and future answers. Finally, it cannot reproduce how a real child slowly learns which family practices are assertion-like, which are solidarity-like, and which are simply habit; the facilitator must supply that this very distinction is part of what the parents are teaching, whether or not they ever name it.
