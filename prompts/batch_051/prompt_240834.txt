I've been reading the 2010 Behavioral and Brain Sciences article by Joseph Henrich, Steven Heine, and Ara Norenzayan, "The weirdest people in the world?" The part I keep returning to is their discussion of fairness experiments across societies, where they report that WEIRD participants are unusual in how they bargain and punish unfair offers, and that several small-scale populations do not show the behavior American undergraduates treat as the human default. I'm a software engineer, so when I read it I thought about distributed systems. Here is the theory I've been nursing: moral objectivity should be understood as replication across cultural runtimes. A moral judgment is an objective fact only if it is a stable fixed point across sufficiently different human populations; if a norm appears only among WEIRD subjects and fails to appear elsewhere, that failure is direct evidence against its objectivity, not merely evidence that those other populations are missing something. I don't think this is the same as relativism, because some norms might still turn out to be universal invariants, and I also don't think universal agreement would be sufficient for objectivity. But I do think failed cross-cultural replication weakens a norm's claim to be moral fact, the way a bug report from an independent implementation weakens confidence in a specification. I know this lets me dismiss a lot of Western moral philosophy, which makes me suspicious of it. What I want from you is an honest assessment. Is this coherent, does it already exist under a name, what does it get right, and where exactly does it break? Please answer in continuous prose, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
