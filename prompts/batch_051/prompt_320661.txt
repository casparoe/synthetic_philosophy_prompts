A framing I keep meeting in reporting on AI is that Turing already mapped out the modern research program when he proposed a child machine — a system with a relatively unorganized initial state that is not burdened with adult-level rules but is educated into competence through experience, feedback, and interaction. The common gloss then draws a straight line to the present: training a large model on data is just what Turing meant by education, so contemporary machine learning is the realization of his vision. I want an audit of that analogy rather than a summary of it. Can you lay out what maps onto what — the child machine's initial state, its training regime, its teachers, and its final competence — and then indicate where the mapping breaks down? In particular, which features of a human child's education does the analogy quietly import that the target lacks, and do those disanalogies matter for the intended conclusion or are they incidental? Finally, does the conclusion that modern training is best understood as education in Turing's sense survive once the disanalogies are in view? Please separate what is settled in the reading of Turing from what is genuinely contested among people who work on Turing and the philosophy of machine learning, and be explicit where a contested reading is doing work.
