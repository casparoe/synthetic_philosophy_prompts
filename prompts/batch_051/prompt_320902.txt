I am a retired geochemist who has spent the past few years reading philosophy of science, and I have been drawn especially to the recent literature on causality. My old field dealt mostly with observational records—ice cores, sediment columns, long-term monitoring series—where controlled experiments were seldom possible, so Pearl's distinction between seeing and doing struck me as obviously important. I recently came across a piece in a popular science magazine that summarized his framework roughly as follows:

Pearl's central achievement is the do-calculus, which at last lets one calculate the effect of an intervention from purely observational data whenever one has a causal diagram. The key tool is the do-operator, P(y|do(x)), which is essentially a rigorous way of conditioning on x while holding the confounders fixed. Pearl organizes causal knowledge into three rungs: association, prediction, and explanation. The headline result is that the do-calculus lets one climb from the lowest rung to the highest using data alone, provided the diagram is complete. The framework rests on structural causal models, which assume that causation is acyclic—cycles are ruled out—and that each variable is a deterministic function of its direct causes. Because the top rung concerns what would have happened, Pearl regards counterfactual claims as empirically untestable.

The article then drew the further moral that machine-learning systems which merely fit conditional probabilities are in principle incapable of genuine causal reasoning, and that no amount of additional data can close that gap.
