I've got a policy for whenever I argue about animal minds, and I can't tell anymore whether it's a genuine epistemic rule or just my own bias wearing a lab coat. The policy is: when an animal does something that looks clever or emotional, I refuse the rich interpretation unless someone shows me the behavior can't be produced by something lower down the scale — a reflex, a fixed action pattern, associative learning, a hardwired subroutine. So when my friend tells me her dog looks guilty because it knows it did wrong, I say the guilty look is just submissive body language triggered by her posture and tone, and it shows up weather or not the dog actually broke a rule. When someone shows me the study where scrub jays who cached food in view of another bird later re-hide it, I say the bird isn't tracking "that other bird knows where my food is"; it's just sensitive to the cue of being watched. The rule is basically Morgan's canon: never attribute a higher psychological process if a lower one can explain the behavior. It's saved me from anthropomorphizing plenty of times. But lately I've noticed two things. First, "a lower explanation can explain it" is a very low bar — there's almost always some stimulus-response story you can sketch, so the rule may amount to: never attribute animal minds, no matter what. Second, I don't apply the rule to humans. If you wince after I kick the table, I accept you hurt; if you hide your chips when I come in, I accept you know I want your chips. So is my policy defensible? Could it be restated so it keeps the don't-get-fooled part without the never-count-the-evidence part, and is the human exemption itself justified rather than just another bias? And what sort of evidence, if any, should actually get an animal across the line?
