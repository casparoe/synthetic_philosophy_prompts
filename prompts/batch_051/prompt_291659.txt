hey, so I'm in a seminar on animal minds and moral status and we keep fighting about whether brain size itself should matter for how much an animal counts, or whether it's cognitive sophistication, or something else entirely. I wrote down my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and they definately don't seem to hang together. Could you try to find the principle behind them—or if there isn't one, tell me which verdicts I should drop? (don't worry about citing the actual readings, I'll match it back up myself.)

1. A normal adult human vs an adult human with a severe intellectual disability. I treat them as full moral equals, no hesitation. The disability doesn't lower anyone's moral status in my book.

2. A newborn human vs an adult pig. The pig is plainly better at planning and problem-solving than the newborn, and I still put the baby's moral status above the pig's. I'm not even tempted to rank the pig higher.

3. An elephant vs a chimpanzee. An elephant's brain is maybe three times the size of a human's; a chimp's is around a third. If I'm being honest, I want to say the elephant matters at least as much as the chimp—but I also think the chimp is more cognitively similar to us, and I can't actually defend the elephant intuition.

4. A sperm whale vs an elephant. The whale's brain is something like five times a human's, and I have this feeling it might be the most morally significant nonhuman animal there is, but I'd never put it above a human, and I have no clue whether it's actually smarter than a chimp or just wired for echolocation and deep diving.

5. Einstein's brain was, famously, on the small side of normal. That doesn't make me care about him any less, and I don't think a person with a bigger brain matters more. So within humans, raw size is clearly irrelevant to me.

6. An alien or a robot with a brain the size of a walnut that passes every test of human-level general intelligence. I want to give it full moral status without knowing anything about how big or dense its brain is. So size isn't doing the work here either—which is why cases 3 and 4 bug me so much.

7. A human in a permanent vegetative state (brainstem intact, no sign of consciousness for years) vs a healthy dog. In a forced choice, I think I'd save the dog—it has a richer mental life. But this seems to flat-out contradict cases 1 and 2, where I gave humans full status even when their capacities were low (or not here yet).

The principle I keep almost believing is: moral status tracks mental capacity, and brain size is only a rough proxy for that. But then 1, 2, and 7 pull in other directions. Maybe it's something about the normal capacities of the species? But I can't see how that isn't just species prejudice with a lab coat on. Is there a single principle that gets all or most of these? And if not, which of my verdicts should go first?
