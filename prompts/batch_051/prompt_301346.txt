I am drafting the semantics-and-paradoxes question for our PhD comprehensive exam in philosophical logic, and I want a question that separates candidates who can work the mechanics of Curry's paradox and Yablo's paradox from those who only know the slogan version of the Liar story. Please write the exam question itself, a model answer, and a marking scheme.

The exam question should be a single 90-minute essay, aimed at candidates who have taken a graduate seminar on truth and the semantic paradoxes, and the essay should run roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words. The crux to build the question around is this: the Liar is standardly diagnosed by pointing to two features, self-reference and negation; Curry's paradox is widely taken to show that negation is not needed for paradox, and Yablo's paradox is widely taken to show that even direct self-reference is not needed. Both morals are contested, however, and the question should press candidates to decide how much of each survives contact with the derivations.

The question should require the candidate to do four things: (1) define the Yablo sequence precisely and prove, using the T-schema, that it admits no classical assignment of truth values — that no sentence in the sequence can be true, and that if none is true then each is true; (2) state a Curry sentence for an arbitrary claim P and derive P, flagging each point at which the T-schema, modus ponens, and the structural rule of contraction are used; (3) assess the two morals — whether Curry really shows that negation is not the culprit, and whether Yablo really shows that self-reference is not the culprit — making clear whether rejecting the relevant feature would block the paradox, and whether Yablo's sequence is circular in some less direct sense; (4) state the strongest consequence this pair of paradoxes has for non-classical treatments of the Liar, and in particular why a paraconsistent logic that tolerates contradictions may still be in trouble.

The exam question should be self-contained. It should restate, in its own words, the following working definitions rather than assuming recall of any named article or author: the T-schema, namely the schema T(A) ↔ A for each sentence A; contraction, namely the structural rule that one and the same assumption may be used more than once in a conditional proof, equivalently that from A → (A → B) one may infer A → B; the Yablo sequence, namely, for each natural number n, a sentence S_n saying that for every m > n, S_m is false; and a Curry sentence for P, namely a sentence C that says that if C is true, then P. Do not require any bibliography, and do not require candidates to identify named positions by author.

The model answer should give the derivations in full and then state the dialectic: which of the two suspected culprits survives, what rejecting contraction buys and costs, and what a non-classical treatment must say about the T-schema and the conditional to block Curry while still having something substantive to say about truth. Where a named view would naturally come up, present the view and its argument on the merits rather than as an attribution to be recalled.

The marking scheme should contain five must-hit points, each with its mark, summing to 20, and a one-line note on how candidates typically mishandle that point. Weight the marks toward the mechanics — for instance, correctly locating where contraction does the work, and correctly saying whether Yablo's sequence, though free of direct self-reference, is nonetheless circular — rather than toward erudition.
