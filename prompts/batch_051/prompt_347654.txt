I have been teaching a unit on inverse reinforcement learning, and I keep coming back to the underdetermination problem: when one tries to recover an agent's reward function from observed behavior, infinitely many reward functions typically rationalize the same behavior, so the behavior by itself does not pin down the preferences that generated it. I want one concrete, tightly specified case that makes this problem vivid without leaning on any literature, built to the following requirements.

Construct, in full detail, a deterministic gridworld — states, actions, transitions, start state, terminal state — together with two reward functions R₁ and R₂ such that: (1) the source gridworld contains at least one genuine choice point, and yet an agent optimizing R₁ and an agent optimizing R₂ yield the same demonstrated trajectory, with unique optimal policies and no hidden tie-breaking; (2) R₁ and R₂ are not rescalings or relabelings of one another, and are not related by potential-based shaping (adding a state-potential difference that cancels along every trajectory and would leave every optimal policy unchanged); they genuinely disagree about the desirability of at least one state on the demonstrated path, and they correspond to two recognizably different hypotheses about what a human cares about — for example, one reward attributes the observed behavior to a preference for the object reached, the other to a preference for the route taken or the states avoided; (3) after one minimal alteration to the environment — one wall moved, one transition changed, or the goal shifted by one cell — the optimal policies under R₁ and R₂ diverge, and each resulting policy remains intuitively interpretable as a different underlying preference.

Then carry out the check: show, via Bellman-optimality reasoning or a rigorous informal equivalent, that both rewards make the demonstrated trajectory optimal in the source environment and that the two policies are distinct in the modified environment; state the two modified-environment trajectories and explain what each reveals about its reward. Finally, draw the lesson: explain why a learner that observes only the source demonstration, knows the source dynamics, and has no prior over reward functions cannot in principle distinguish R₁ from R₂, identify what additional ingredient (a regularizer, a prior, demonstrations in the modified environment, something else) would break the tie, and say which feature of the construction is doing the real work and what happens if it is removed.
