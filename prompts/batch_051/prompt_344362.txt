my officemate in the history of political thought programme keeps coming back to the man-of-his-time defence and insisting it isnt a blanket excuse: it only does exculpatory work, he says, when the relevant moral view wasnt reachable from the person's actual historical-epistemic position, not merely rare. His line is that Columbus gets more excuse than Jefferson, because by Jefferson's moment an articulate antislavery position was live in his own circles, and Churchill gets almost none on some colonial questions because the better view was right there, defended, and he had access to it. He calls this moral reachability. I suspect reachability is doing sneaky work: it sounds like a testable historical claim but might just be a story we tell after we've already decided whom to exonerate.

So here's what I want. Pick one case (Jefferson and slavery, Churchill and empire, Columbus, your call) and tell me how you'd actually test the claim that the better view was reachable for this person in the exculpatory sense. What would you operationalize, what evidence would you look for, and what pattern of evidence would count for or against excusing them? Then be honest about what the test cannot settle even if it comes out cleanly — is the leftover just missing data, or is there a conceptual gap, e.g. between being able to arrive at a norm and being blameworthy for not arriving at it, that no amount of history could touch? And give me a confidence read on your proposed test and the point your least sure of.
