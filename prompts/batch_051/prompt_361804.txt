I run a monthly discussion group in a rationalist-adjacent network, and next month's two-hour session is mine. The topic is the relation between collective memory and history, narrowed to the practical question of what should happen when a community's remembered past and the historiographical record conflict. The audience is mostly engineers and scientists with a strong epistemic-hygiene ethos, and I expect their prior to be nearly unanimous: the record is the truth, memory is error plus motivated distortion, and the only serious answer is deference to the record. I want to move them past that reflex without handing them the opposite slogan that memory is a deeper truth about experience. The understanding I am aiming at is that these conflicts are often between two different kinds of authority over the past — an epistemic authority about what happened and a practical, identity-constitutive authority about what the past means and requires now — and that the interesting cases are the ones where the two cannot be cleanly separated, because what is taken to have happened changes what a community can now be, or because deferring to the record would itself be a political act.

Please do not give me the explanation itself. Give me a teaching plan.

In particular: what has to be in place first, before the conflict is even intelligible as a conflict rather than as a mismatch between fact and fiction, and what each later step depends on; the single carrying example that should organize the whole session, and the properties it must have and must lack — including why the canonical cases of national founding myths, Holocaust denial, and the Alamo would be wrong for this audience, and whether I should use a real, lightly fictionalized, or wholly constructed case; the misconceptions the attendees will bring or form, in the order they will actually surface, and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off rather than argued down later (I expect at least the ideas that collective memory is just defective history and that the archive is neutral); the one question, task, or scenario at the end that would show me the distinction has landed rather than that I have introduced new vocabulary; and what I should openly set aside or refuse to settle at this level. Please write the plan in American English.
