I got into a back-and-forth with a friend about the golden rule after reading the bit in Kant's Groundwork, Second Section, where he takes a swipe at it. I don't have the book in front of me, but the footnote I'm thinking of says roughly this: the rule cannot be a universal law because it gives no ground for duties to oneself, nor for duties of love to others, since someone might gladly agree that others should leave him alone if only he could be excused from helping them, and a criminal could use it to argue against the judge who punishes him. That last example got under my skin, so I tried to turn it into a tighter argument. Here is what I came up with:

(1) If the golden rule, read literally, entails a false moral conclusion about a clear case, then it is not an adequate fundamental moral principle.
(2) The golden rule, read literally, says: do not do to another what you would not want done to you.
(3) A judge would not want to be imprisoned.
(4) Therefore, from the golden rule, the judge should not imprison the convicted criminal standing before him.
(5) But the judge's imprisoning that criminal is not wrong.
(6) So the golden rule, read literally, entails a false moral conclusion.
(7) Therefore the golden rule is not an adequate fundamental moral principle.

I half-suspect the move from (3) to (4) is where the trick hides, maybe because would not want is doing two different jobs, but I can't make the repair precise. Is the argument valid? If not, identify the first step that fails and say what the corrected version of the golden rule would need to be to avoid that failure. If it is valid, tell me which premise a defender of the golden rule should deny and why.
