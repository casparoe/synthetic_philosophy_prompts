I have a policy about moral claims from people I trust, and my partner says its incoherent. When someone tells me something is morally wrong, I ask what non-moral facts make it wrong, and I update only on those facts, never on the bare verdict itself. For example, a friend who did public health work around industrial farming told me factory farming is wrong; I accepted the welfare facts she reported, but I did not let the fact that she thinks it's wrong move me at all. If she can't supply the underlying facts, I treat her moral verdict as basically an autobiographical report about her temperament. My partner says this is unstable: if I accept the facts and I already hold the bridging principle (suffering is bad, etc.), the verdict follows anyway, so I'm fooling myself about how independent my judgment is. Is the policy defensible? Is there a principled line between the factual parts of moral testimony and the moral verdict itself, or does "these facts make it wrong" sit somewhere in between? And if the facts really do all the work, what exactly is being refused when someone refuses to take moral testimony?
