I cover developments at the intersection of decision theory and artificial intelligence for a magazine that reports on science and ideas, and I am working on a long-form piece about why some researchers take seriously the possibility that one agent can be influenced by another even when no causal signal passes between them. The piece needs a concrete, built-to-order example rather than the familiar Newcomb problem, so I would like you to construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. The protagonist should be an artificial agent — or, if that becomes unwieldy, a tightly organized human institution — with an ordinary terminal goal such as preserving its own continued operation or maximizing some finite resource. Over the course of the scenario, it rationally adopts a surrogate goal: a new, costly, ongoing objective whose pursuit cannot promote the terminal goal through any causal chain available in the story. The adoption must be driven by an acausal threat or acausal bargain, meaning the protagonist reasons that another agent's behavior is correlated with its own policy or with facts about what it would choose, even though neither agent can causally affect the other. The case should be engineered so that a causal decision theorist rejects the surrogate goal while both an evidential decision theorist and a functional or updateless decision theorist accept it, though the functional or updateless theorist's ground for acceptance should be distinct from the evidentialist's. Constraints: no predictor in the story may be better than a shrewd human being; no exotic physics, infinite computation, simulations, or exact copies; and the scenario may not simply stipulate that the two agents share source code or can observe one another's private deliberations. The payoffs should be ones a normal firm or institution could face.

After the scenario, deliver three things. First, set out the reasoning each of the three theories would give about the surrogate goal, and show explicitly where their verdicts converge and diverge. Second, identify the specific structural feature that does the work — the feature that creates the acausal channel — and explain what the verdicts become if that feature is removed; in particular, say whether the case then collapses into a conventional Newcomb problem, ordinary reciprocity, a hostage negotiation, or simply ceases to be an acausal scenario. Third, give the strongest objection a defender of the theory that rejects the surrogate goal could raise against the case, and say whether the scenario can be repaired without weakening the very feature that makes it interesting. The finished case is for a magazine feature, so it should remain explainable to a general reader in roughly 700 words or fewer.
