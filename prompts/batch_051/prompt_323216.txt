Act as a philosopher of mind who is equally at home in phenomenology and cognitive science, and who takes both theoretical virtues and first-person reports seriously. Think carefully, steelman each side before criticizing it, and do not simply declare a winner.

I have been working through the confrontation between the Cartesian theater picture and the multiple drafts model of consciousness, and I find myself pinned between two things I cannot easily give up. On reflection, the multiple drafts account looks to me like the stronger theory. It refuses to posit any place or moment in the brain where contents are assembled and presented to a single inner audience. Instead it describes consciousness as the continuously contested process by which many parallel streams of interpretation are probed, revised, consolidated, and sometimes discarded, with whichever version happens to win access to speech, memory, and behavior being the one the person later reports. That story is economical, it dissolves the homunculus regress, it requires no central headquarters, and it sits comfortably with what neuroscience says about cognition being massively distributed. But when I examine my own experience, I cannot make the first-person datum go away. There does not merely seem to be a multiplicity of drafts competing for influence; there seems to be the one for whom all of this is happening, the one who is now weighing these two positions. The suggestion that this unified subject is itself a useful abstraction generated by the winning draft feels, from the inside, not like a cost the theory must pay but like a refutation of it, since the phenomenon it was supposed to explain is precisely the thing being denied.

What I want is not a verdict about which side wins, but a negotiator's map of the space between them. Walk me through, with maximal force, the case for saying that the intuition should be debunked and dropped, and the case for saying that the theory must be amended or abandoned in order to preserve it. Then address whether there is a reading on which both survive: for example, one on which rejecting a theater is not the same as rejecting a subject, and on which the single, continuous, first-person point of view is real but is a representational achievement or a useful abstraction rather than a place or a thing. Tell me what would justify each move, what each side would have to be taken as claiming for the conflict to be genuine rather than verbal, and which consideration would have to shift for it to become clear to me which of the two I should give up.
