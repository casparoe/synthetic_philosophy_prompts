I've been writing a short paper about what rationality requires when an agent has no reason to prefer one option over another, and I find that I want to hold all of the following at once. (1) Buridan's ass is not a genuine paradox: a rational agent facing two exactly similar bales of hay can simply take one of them, and is neither irrational nor stuck. (2) Intentional action is action performed for a reason, and the relevant reasons are contrastive: a reason that would justify taking the left bale must favor the left bale over the right bale, so a consideration that applies equally to both is not by itself a reason for taking either. (3) Revealed preference theory captures something importantly right: when an agent freely takes one of two available options in the absence of coercion or mistake, we are entitled to attribute to that agent a preference for the taken option over the alternative. (4) Indifference is not the same as incomparability: two options can be equal in value, in which case taking either is rationally unproblematic, or they can fail to stand in any evaluative relation at all, in which case choosing either would misrepresent the agent's values and is rationally unavailable. (5) A tie-breaking mechanism's selecting one of two options does not by itself make taking that option any more rational; principles of rational choice and descriptions of selection mechanisms answer different questions.

I'd like an audit of this set rather than a lecture on it: go through the commitments pair by pair, say which pairs are in genuine tension and which only appear to be, rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would be. Then say which single revision would leave me with the most coherent overall position, and what that revision would cost.
