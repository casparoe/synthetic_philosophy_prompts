I have recently taken over the copy-editing of an academic humanities journal, and for the first time I have to decide, case by case, what counts as "bad grammar". I find that the phrase is being used to cover several quite different kinds of thing, and I have begun to doubt whether it names one property at all. I want a working definition of "grammatical error" — or several definitions, if the phrase turns out to do more than one job — before I impose it on authors.

Here are the cases I need it to classify, all drawn from material that has actually crossed my desk this month.

1. A four-year-old native speaker says "I goed to the park." I regard this as an error, but not as the same kind of error as an adult's.
2. A postgraduate student who learnt English as an adult writes "I have 30 years old." Native speakers find this plainly wrong, yet it is not a slip: it follows a rule of her first language.
3. An undergraduate whose home variety of English uses negative concord writes "The poem didn't have no regular metre." I am inclined to say this is not an error in the undergraduate's own variety of English, but it is not standard academic prose.
4. A cover letter contains "between you and I." I would correct it under the traditional rule, though it is common among educated speakers and seems deliberate rather than careless.
5. An author writes "to better understand the data" in an article. The house style guide would have me change it to "to understand the data better" to avoid the split infinitive, but I suspect the guide is enforcing a taste rather than a rule.
6. An author writes "Each applicant should bring their passport to the interview." I would defend this, and would not alter it to satisfy the older rule requiring "his or her" instead of "their".
7. A philosophy paper contains "The committee dissolved into a flavour." The word order and morphology are perfectly normal, but the sentence seems to be about nothing at all. I would say that whatever is wrong with it is not a grammatical error.
8. A colleague types "Would you like a cup of teh instead?" I want to call this a typo, not a grammatical error.
9. In an internal meeting, a native speaker says "Me and Sarah went to the cinema." I would not correct him aloud, but I would write "Sarah and I went to the cinema" in the minutes without even deciding that I was doing anything to the grammar.

I would like you to propose a definition — or definitions — of "grammatical error" that gets these nine cases right, run the definition through each case, and then say which of my own judgements above the definition forces you to challenge and which of your own classifications is least secure.
