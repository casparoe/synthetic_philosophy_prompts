A country alarmed by what it calls the instrumentalization of free time adopts a Leisure Dividend. Every adult may claim a modest monthly payment for up to twelve hours per week of certified autotelic activity. To qualify, an activity must be freely chosen rather than compulsory; produce nothing sold, graded, scored, listed on a CV, or used to meet any obligation; involve no competition; and be pursued for its own sake rather than for health, profit, status, or self-improvement. A National Board of Autotelic Activity maintains the schedule of eligible practices, adjudicates appeals, and audits claims by interview and observation; the payment attaches to certified hours and is not taxed. The Board defends its criteria by appeal to the classic account of play as a free activity standing outside ordinary life, bounded by rules internal to itself and indifferent to material result, and to the idea that leisure is a receptive condition of doing things for their own sake rather than time reserved for recovery or self-cultivation. Analyze the Dividend as a mechanism rather than as a moral ideal. What does it incentivize among claimants — the genuine player, the contemplative gardener, the runner who logs every mile, the amateur whose hobby occasionally produces something salable — and among the Board and the communities it accredits? Where does the line between autotelic and instrumental first give way, and what gaming strategies emerge (coached self-description, activities packaged to hit the written criteria, auditing that turns play into performance)? Once a payment attaches to certified play, does the scheme protect activities done as ends in themselves or corrupt the very quality it set out to preserve? And which of its failure modes could be repaired — self-certification, a universal grant with no certification at all, or public funding of festivals rather than individual hours — without gutting the policy?
