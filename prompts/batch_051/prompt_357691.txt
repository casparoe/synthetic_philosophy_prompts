I have been trying to get clear about whether Levinas's ethics can make the transition from the one-to-one encounter to the scene of justice without undermining its own starting point, and I have an argument that seems to show it cannot. For Levinas the face of the Other is not an object of perception or classification; it is an address that commands me before any comparison or calculation, and it imposes an infinite, asymmetrical responsibility to this singular, unsubstitutable Other. The complication is that no actual situation contains only me and one Other, and this is where the problem begins. Consider the following steps:

(1) The ethical command issues from the face of the singular Other, and the command is nontransferable: to substitute a different face for this face would be to lose the relation itself.
(2) The third party, being another person, has a face and therefore addresses me with a demand of the same singular, incomparable kind.
(3) To respond to two such demands in a single situation, I must determine whose claim to answer, in what order, and by what rule; any such determination involves comparing the claimants.
(4) Comparison brings persons under a common measure and thereby treats them as interchangeable instances of a kind — the very movement Levinas identifies with totalization, in which the Other is thematized and its alterity is effaced.
(5) Therefore Levinasian ethics faces a dilemma: whenever more than one other person is present, either it gives no determinate guidance, because it forbids the comparison that action requires, or it permits the totalizing stance it took itself to precede and interrupt, in which case it ceases to be ethics as first philosophy.

Levinas's own move, as I understand it, is to deny that the third party arrives only after the dyad: he claims the third is already present in the face of the Other, and that justice is the comparison of what cannot be compared, a necessary translation of the ethical relation into the political and legal order. But this has always struck me as renaming the problem rather than resolving it. Is the argument sound? If not, identify the first step that fails, spell out any ambiguity or suppressed premise it turns on, and say whether Levinas can genuinely dissolve the dilemma or must accept one of its horns.
