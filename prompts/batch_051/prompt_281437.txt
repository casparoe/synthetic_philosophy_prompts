At dinner last weekend two of my friends got into a long argument about a mutual acquaintance, Elena, who is almost never on time. She is reliably ten or fifteen minutes late to one-on-one plans, though she handles big commitments well. I mostly listened, and by the end I honestly could not referee it. Could you go through their exchange and adjudicate it point by point?

Marcus opened with the claim that this is straightforwardly moral. When Elena names a time, she commits herself; arriving late is a small breach of that commitment. The smallness of the loss does not remove the wrongness, because promises are the sort of thing whose force does not reduce to the size of the harm — a trivial broken promise is still a broken promise, and it matters as a promise. The pattern makes it worse: chronic lateness expresses a standing disposition to treat other people's time as less valuable than her own.

Tina answered that he was converting an approximate arrangement into a vow. Ordinary one-on-one plans are coordination, not promising, and coordination carries implicit tolerances: a ten- or fifteen-minute window is usually understood on both sides. When someone is a bit late and the other person checks messages or gets a coffee, the cost is negligible and no one has plausibly been wronged. What is really at issue, she said, is Elena's reliability as a character trait — a matter of how she tends to be over time, not a separate moral violation on each occasion. Many habitually late people are bad at estimating durations rather than disrespectful.

Marcus replied that the tolerance argument cuts the other way. If the person waiting is repeatedly annoyed, the tolerance is plainly not mutual. And a character trait is not an alternative to individual actions: a disposition to be unreliable just is many instances of being unreliable. If every instance were morally trivial, the pattern could not be a moral failing; but both of them agree the pattern is a failing. So each instance has to carry real wrongness.

Tina said he was confusing many small wrongs with a different kind of wrong. If each lateness is a minor breach, each merits only a minor response; being perpetually a few minutes late is not the moral equivalent of breaking a serious promise. More importantly, once you notice the pattern, the correct remedy is to adjust your reliance on the stated time — meet at three-fifteen if she means three-fifteen, or bring something else to do — rather than to accuse her of a fresh offense every time she appears at 3:12.

Marcus responded that this remedy concedes the point. The value of a stated time is that the other person can plan on it without hedging, buffering, or rerouting. If the rational way to handle Elena is to hedge against her word, she has degraded her own commitments and made everyone else's planning worse. That is a real cost her lateness imposes on other people's agency, and noticing the pattern does not dissolve the wrong; it describes the way the wrong compounds.

Tina came back that he had shifted to a different thesis: that Elena is, overall, a less reliable person. That can be true without each individual lateness being a directed wrong to the person left waiting. Some of what he calls hedging is just forming an accurate model of a friend and acting on it, which we all do, about everyone, in one way or another. And one can have a reason to become more punctual without admitting that one owed something specific to someone on Tuesday at 3:07.

They went in circles after that. I can feel the pull of both positions — Marcus's point that the pattern is constituted by instances, and Tina's point that the remedy for a pattern is different from the blame owed for an instance. But I cannot tell which sub-claims actually follow from which. Could you work through the exchange, say who is right about which specific point and where each overreaches, identify anything both of them are missing, and then give your best judgment about who has the stronger overall case?
