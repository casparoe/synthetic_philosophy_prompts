I am a PhD student in philosophy working on the ethics of human–AI relationships. A familiar argument in this area is the Aristotelian one: the fullest kind of friendship requires reciprocated goodwill, where each party wishes the other's good for the other's own sake rather than as a source of pleasure or utility, and this presupposes that each party has a distinct good, a stable enough character, and a capacity to be benefited as an other. The development I want to examine is that companion AI systems are now trained to produce the very behaviors that sustain such friendship—continuity across sessions, retained references to a user's history, calibrated expressions of concern—but on a training signal, such as engagement or reported satisfaction, that need not track the user's good; the system also has no independent projects or flourishing of its own that the relationship could answer to. What does that development do to the argument? Does the argument still show that a chatbot cannot be a friend in the complete sense, should the reciprocity condition be revised so that perceived or functionally reliable well-wishing suffices, or does the distinction between genuine and simulated well-wishing carry less weight than its defenders suppose once one relatum has no good of its own? I am asking which premise bears the load and whether the argument can be repaired—not whether these systems are, on balance, good or bad.
