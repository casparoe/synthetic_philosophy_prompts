I'm putting together the episode rundown for a small technology-governance podcast, and next week's guest is a political scientist who has argued that Fearon's rationalist explanations for war transfer more cleanly than people admit to competitive races to build advanced AI. His core claim is that the three standard rationalist mechanisms—private information with incentives to misrepresent, commitment problems, and issue indivisibility—explain why otherwise reasonable labs and states keep sliding toward unsafe corners even when everyone involved says they would prefer coordination. For an audience that knows Fearon's framework but has not thought carefully about the AI application, I'd like eight to ten interview questions that do more than invite him to restate the analogy. In particular, I want at least one question each on the following, with a follow-up keyed to the reply he is most likely to give: (a) whether firms racing to deploy actually hold meaningful private information about their capabilities or intentions, or whether the relevant asymmetric information lies between labs and regulators instead; (b) whether the binding commitment problem is between labs, between states, or between a lab and its investors, and why that distinction should matter for the analogy; and (c) whether "indivisibility" is doing real work when the prize is something like leadership in a general-purpose technology, or whether that part of the framework is metaphor stretched past its breaking point. He tends to answer all three by saying the unit of analysis is not war between states but a coordination failure with safety externalities, so please build in a follow-up that makes that deflection harder to hide behind. Finally, add one question asking what the framework would have correctly predicted about the last decade of AI development, and one asking what single observation about the field would most embarrass the analogy, written so that he cannot shrug it off as a question about prediction rather than explanation.
