I've been turning over a weird asymmetry in how I think about my own decisions and I cant shake it. If a friend is deciding whether to quit her job, I can happily put odds on it: maybe 60% she stays, 40% she goes, based on how she talks, her savings, what people usually do. That's just a prediction and it doesn't feel rude or strange. But if I'm the one deciding whether to quit my job, and someone asks "so what do you think you'll actually do?", the same kind of answer won't stay a prediction. The minute I say "I'd say 60% I stay" while I'm still choosing, the number stops being a forecast about the world and turns into something else: a lean, a preference, or a decision I've sort of already made. I'm not finding out what I'll do; I'm doing it, or half-doing it. And if I force myself to make it a real prediction — by stepping outside myself and asking what people like me usually do in the end — then I'm no longer inside the decision; I've turned myself into somebody else I'm managing.

That's the asymmetry: I can assign probabilities to other people's open choices, and I can even assign them to my own future choices when the future me feels far enough away (I'm maybe 80% sure I'll hit snooze tomorrow at 6am, and I can plan around it). But I can't assign a probability to the choice I'm making right now, not without the prediction turning into a decision or collapsing back into a hunch about what I want.

I want to know whether there's a real principle that says this is how it should be, or whether it's just a glitch in how people are wired. Give me the strongest justification you can find for treating other people's choices and my own current choice differently, test it against the snooze-button case and against cases where I ask someone else to predict me in order to help me decide, and then tell me straight whether the asymmetry should be kept or given up. Give me your actual view, not a list of possibilities.
