I cover decision theory and the science of rational choice, and I've gradually talked myself into imprecise probabilities: when evidence is ambiguous or conflicting, you shouldn't assign a single credence but a set of probability functions, and you should only bet when every function in the set agrees. But I now worry the position is built to be unfalsifiable — every counterexample gets met with the claim that the agent's credences were simply imprecise in that respect. Give me a damage assessment, not a polite list of open questions. What would actually count against imprecise probabilism? I'm especially interested in the sharpest versions of these pressure points: (a) dilation, where conditioning on an event widens your interval no matter what you observe, so an agent can apparently prefer not to recieve a free piece of information; (b) sequential choice, where someone accepts each bet in a series on its own but rejects the combined bet; (c) the deflationary idea that Ellsberg-style ambiguity aversion is a confusion about the setup, not evidence for set-valued beliefs; and (d) the lack of a principled stopping point — once you allow some imprecision, what blocks a slide to a maximally mushy interval? Rank these, and any stronger ones, from 'this should make an honest defender walk away' to 'irritating but survivable,' and say which costs a defender would themselves concede.
