This stopped being an abstract problem for me earlier this year. A former mentor, who died some months ago, left me a gift I never asked for: a sum of money that, I have since learned, came mostly from an investment he himself regarded as an unearned piece of luck. The practical position is further tangled because the costs of accepting the gift may leave me little better off than I would otherwise have been, and because he is no longer alive to answer the questions I would now put to him. In trying to work out what I owe, I have arrived at the following set of commitments about gratitude. Before I act on them, I want to know whether they hang together.

1. Gratitude is owed only where there is benevolence toward me. If someone improves my life merely as a side-effect of pursuing his own ends — his renovation raises the value of my flat — I may be glad, but I am not grateful to him, and he has no claim on me.

2. A benefit need not have been solicited. An unsolicited benefit grounds just as much gratitude as one I requested, provided the benefactor acted from good will; the absence of social pressure may make the good will more salient, not less.

3. The weight of what I owe tracks the value the benefit actually has for my life, not the effort or sacrifice the benefactor incurred. Great effort that misfires and leaves my situation unchanged gives him little claim; a small favour that transforms my circumstances gives him a larger one.

4. Gratitude is not a debt to be discharged. A debt can be settled and the books closed, but gratitude is a standing orientation of good will and readiness to help, and no particular act of reciprocation can complete it.

5. Luck in the background does not undercut gratitude. If the benefactor was able to help only because of an inheritance, a fortunate investment, or good timing, my gratitude is undiminished; its object is his good will, not the history of the resources through which it reached me.

6. Gratitude can lapse, or be substantially revised, when I was mistaken about what mattered. If the benefactor's motives were far less benevolent than I supposed, or if what he gave me turns out on balance to have worsened my life, I am no longer grateful in the way I was.

7. The benefactor's death does not cancel what I owe, but it changes its form. What remains is something like a duty of honouring and rededicating the benefit, not the reciprocation one would direct at a living person.

I would be grateful for an audit of the set. Could you tell me which of these commitments are in genuine tension with one another, which pairs only appear to conflict and dissolve on closer reading, and what the least costly revision would be for each genuine tension? Please rank any tensions by severity rather than giving an undifferentiated list.
